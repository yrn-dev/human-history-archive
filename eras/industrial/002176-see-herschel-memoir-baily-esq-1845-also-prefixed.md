# Francis Baily

Francis Baily (1774–1844) was an English astronomer who made significant contributions to the fields of celestial observation, planetary measurement, and the compilation of astronomical catalogues. Born in Newbury, Berkshire, on April 28, 1774, Baily's early career was not focused on science. Between 1796 and 1797, he toured the unsettled regions of North America, and in 1799, he entered the London Stock Exchange.

## Professional Transition and Early Works
Baily established a high reputation as a writer on life-contingencies through the publication of several works: *Tables for the Purchasing and Renewing of Leases* (1802), *The Doctrine of Interest and Annuities* (1808), and *The Doctrine of Life-Annuities and Assurances* (1810). Through his integrity and diligence, he amassed a fortune, which allowed him to retire from business in 1825 to devote himself entirely to the study of astronomy.

## Contributions to Astronomy and the Royal Astronomical Society
Baily was a central figure in the institutionalization of astronomy in England, having taken a leading role in the foundation of the Royal Astronomical Society in 1820. In 1827, he was awarded the society's gold medal for his preparation of the Astronomical Society's Catalogue of 2881 stars.

His work in cataloging was extensive. He revised the catalogues of Hevelius, Edmund Halley, Tycho Brahe, Ulugh Beg, Ptolemy, and Tobias Mayer. Furthermore, he superintended the compilation of the British Association's Catalogue of 8377 stars, published in 1845, and executed the reduction of the catalogues of Nicolas de Lacaille and Joseph de Lalande, which contained approximately 57,000 stars, following a recommendation to the British Association in 1837. Baily also initiated the reform of the *Nautical Almanac* in 1829 through his protests.

## Solar Eclipses and "Baily's Beads"
Baily is noted for his observations of solar eclipses. During an annular eclipse on May 15, 1836, at Inch Bonney in Roxburghshire, he described a phenomenon now known as "Baily's Beads," which is caused by inequalities in the limb of the moon. His vivid description of this event drew unprecedented attention to the totality of the eclipse on July 8, 1842, which Baily observed at Pavia.

Additionally, Baily contributed to historical astronomy. As early as 1811, in his first contribution to the Transactions of the Royal Society, he settled the date of a specific eclipse to September 30, 610 B.C. Sir John Herschel later approved these calculations, noting that Baily was the first to solve this disputed question, and expressed surprise that subsequent French and German astronomers had failed to notice the paper.

## Geophysics and Standards of Length
Beyond celestial observation, Baily applied his skills to the study of the earth. Between 1838 and 1842, he used Henry Cavendish's method to determine the mean density of the earth, arriving at the authoritative value of 5.66. He also discussed and completed the pendulum-experiments of H. Foster, from which he deduced an ellipticity for the earth of 1/289. In this work, he introduced a neglected element of reduction to correct the length of the seconds-pendulum. In 1843, Baily was entrusted with the reconstruction of the standards of length.

## Final Years and Legacy
Baily's scholarly output included the *Account of the Rev. John Flamsteed* (1835), a work of fundamental importance to the scientific history of its era that included a republication of the British Catalogue. He died in London on August 30, 1844. His life and work were further documented in a memoir by J. Herschel (1845), which was prefixed to Baily's *Journal of a Tour* and included a list of his writings.
