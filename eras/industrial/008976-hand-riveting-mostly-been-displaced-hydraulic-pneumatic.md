# Riveting and Mechanical Fastening

Riveting is a method of joining materials that has evolved from manual labor to the use of specialized machinery. In modern industrial applications, hand-riveting has been largely replaced by pneumatic and hydraulic machines. This transition has resulted in a significant reduction in costs and the benefit of more uniform and trustworthy results.

## Machinery and Applications
The type of machinery used for riveting depends on the nature of the project:
*   **Boiler Work:** Machines used for this purpose are mostly of a fixed type.
*   **Bridge and Girder Work:** Portable machines are utilized, which are slung from chains and supplied with compressed air or pressure water via systems of flexible pipes.

In addition to riveting, welding is used extensively in boiler work, primarily for the edges of plates. Because these edges are long and thin, they are united in short lengths through successive "heats." While much of this remains hand work—with "gluts" or insertion pieces generally preferred over overlapping joints—large shops employ steam-driven power hammers to close the welds. Common welded parts include angle rings, conical cross-tubes, and furnace flues.

## Technical Processes and Material Standards
The process of preparing holes for rivets has undergone significant changes to improve safety and structural integrity. Historically, holes were punched, but this often led to a lack of exact coincidence between holes in different plates. To correct this, workers used a "drift"—a tapered rod driven by hammer blows to force holes into alignment. This practice caused permanent strains in the plates and enlarged incipient cracks, which were clearly linked to many boiler explosions.

Subsequent specifications required holes to be enlarged by reamering after plates were positioned, though this was sometimes ineffective if the metal was removed unevenly. Consequently, drilling rivet-holes became the standard for high-class boiler work. For average bridge and girder work, the practice of reamering and punching is still used due to less severe service conditions.

Regarding materials, soft steel is always used for rivets. Other steel classifications include:
*   **Medium Steel:** Used generally for angle bars and plates to reduce bridge weight by approximately 7% for a given factor of safety.
*   **Moderately Hard Steel:** Used for larger members in long-span bridges.
*   **Hard Steel:** Used only for compression members to minimize the risk of flaws extending.

When using moderately hard or medium steel, holes must be drilled or punched 1/8 inch smaller than the rivet and then reamed out to remove material strained by the punch.

## Structural Engineering and Component Use
In the construction of girders, those with spans exceeding 100 feet now utilize bracing bars for the web rather than solid or plate webs. Connection methods vary:
*   **Riveted Connections:** There is a general tendency to prefer riveted connections over pin joints.
*   **Pin Joints:** Once common in early English bridges (such as Saltash, Newark Dyke, and Chepstow), pin joints are now rarely used in Europe, though they remain a distinctive feature of American construction. Pin joints save some weight and ease erection, but early versions often worked loose due to insufficient bearing area, necessitating the substitution of riveted covers. Currently, pins are generally used only for tension members.

## Riveting in Other Applications
Riveting is also employed in the manufacture of various mechanical components:
*   **Wheels:** Rims for pneumatic tires, made of aluminium alloy or sheet steel, may be jointed by riveting or brazing.
*   **Chains:** In "roller" chains, the outside link consists of two steel side plates united by two shouldered rivets. These rivets, along with the rollers and bushes, are case-hardened.
