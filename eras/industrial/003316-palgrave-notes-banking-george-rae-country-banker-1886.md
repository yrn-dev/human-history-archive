# Palgrave, Notes on Banking and George Rae, The Country Banker

The study of banking and financial systems is documented through a variety of historical texts, manuals, and statistical records. Among these are the works of Sir Robert Harry Inglis Palgrave and George Rae, which contribute to the broader literature on the practice and theory of banking in the 19th and early 20th centuries.

## Sir Robert Harry Inglis Palgrave
Sir Robert Harry Inglis Palgrave, F.R.S., was a prominent figure in the banking and financial sectors. He served as the Director of Barclay & Co., Ltd., Bankers, and held the position of Editor of the *Economist* from 1871 to 1883. His contributions to the field include the authorship of *Notes on Banking in Great Britain and Ireland, Sweden, Denmark and Hamburg*, as well as *Bank Rate and the Money Market in England, France, Germany, Holland and Belgium, 1844-1900* (published in 1903). Additionally, he served as the editor of the *Dictionary of Political Economy*, published by Macmillan & Co. between 1894 and 1906.

Palgrave's expertise was utilized in official capacities; he provided evidence before the House of Commons committee during the banking inquiry of 1875. During this inquiry, he stated that provincial banks in England and Wales, whose power to issue notes had been restricted by the Act of 1844, possessed a property in their issues that they could use but were unable to sell.

## George Rae and Country Banking
George Rae authored *The Country Banker* in 1886, a work that has seen several later editions and is noted for providing sound hints on banking practice. This text sits alongside other specialized manuals of the era, such as J. George Kiddy's *The Country Banker's Handbook* (4th ed., 1903).

The context of "country banking" involves the operation of banks outside of major financial centers. For example, in France, a large number of banks—primarily in the provinces—conducted business with prudence and discretion. By 1898, 182 of these firms were members of the French Country Bankers' Association, operating in 66 of France's 86 departments. Some of these banks followed the English method of establishing small branches in nearby towns.

## Historical Context of British Banking
The works of Palgrave and Rae emerged from a banking system that underwent significant structural changes. Early British banking was characterized by the use of goldsmiths' notes, which were receipts for deposits payable on demand. This practice continued in London until approximately 1781, when cheque-books were introduced. Prior to this, the issuance of notes was considered the primary feature of banking.

The Bank of England, established to provide the government with a loan of £1,200,000, became the government's banker and the manager of the national debt. It held a monopoly as the only joint-stock bank allowed in England and Wales until 1826 and possessed the exclusive advantage of limited liability for shareholders until 1862.

## The Bank Act of 1844 and Note Circulation
A pivotal event in the history of banking was the Act of 1844. As described by Sir Robert Peel, this act divided the Bank of England into two departments: one for the transaction of ordinary banking business and an Issue Department. The Issue Department's note issuance was restricted to £14,000,000 upon securities, with the remainder governed by fluctuations in bullion stocks.

This act also restricted the power of provincial banks to issue notes. Consequently, the proportion of note circulation relative to banking deposits diminished greatly in the following decades. For instance, data from twenty-one banks showed that in some cases, circulation fell from one-third of deposits to 9%, and in another instance, from 22% to 1.5%.

By 1906, the banking landscape in England had shifted. While the number of individual banks had decreased to approximately ten private and sixty joint-stock banks, the number of banking offices had grown significantly, increasing from 976 in 1844 to more than 5,880 in 1906.
