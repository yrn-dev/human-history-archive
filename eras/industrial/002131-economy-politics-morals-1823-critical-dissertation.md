# Politics, Morals, &c. (1823) and the Development of Political Economy

*Politics, Morals, &c.* (1823) is a work authored by Samuel Bailey, a British philosopher and author born in Sheffield in 1791. Bailey was a merchant who established trade connections in the United States before retiring from business with an ample fortune, though he continued to serve as the chairman of the Sheffield Banking Company for many years.

## Samuel Bailey and His Economic Writings
Samuel Bailey's literary career was characterized by lucidity, originality of treatment, and elegance of style. His first work, *Essays on the Formation and Publication of Opinions* (1821), gained significant attention and was followed by a sequel in 1829 titled *Essays on the Pursuit of Truth*. 

Between these two publications, Bailey produced several works focused on economic and political subjects. In addition to *Politics, Morals, &c.* (1823), he wrote *Questions in Political Economy* and a *Critical Dissertation on the Nature, Measure, and Causes of Value* (1825). The latter work was specifically directed against the opinions of Ricardo and his school of thought. Bailey continued to explore these themes in later publications, including *Money and its Vicissitudes* (1837) and the *Rationale of Political Representation* (1835), as well as various pamphlets such as *Defence of Joint-Stock Banks*, *Right of Primogeniture Examined*, and *Discussion of Parliamentary Reform*.

Despite his intellectual output and his identity as an "ardent liberal," Bailey took little part in political affairs. He unsuccessfully stood for Sheffield as a "philosophic radical" on two occasions.

## Contemporary British Political Economists
The era of Bailey's writing saw the contributions of several other prominent British economists and statisticians.

### John Ramsay M'Culloch
Born in 1789, M'Culloch began his career in journalism with the *Scotsman* and later the *Edinburgh Review*, where he wrote an 1818 article on Ricardo's *Principles of Political Economy*. In 1823, the same year Bailey published *Politics, Morals, &c.*, M'Culloch was chosen for a lectureship established in honor of Ricardo. He later became a professor of political economy at London University in 1828 and served as the comptroller of the stationery office. While not regarded as an original thinker who introduced new ideas to the dominant school of his day, M'Culloch is noted for his extensive statistical compilations, including the *Statistical Account of the British Empire* (1837) and the *Dictionary of Commerce and Commercial Navigation* (1832).

### Francis Horner
Francis Horner was a master of political economy who exerted intellectual and moral influence over the House of Commons. He focused on coinage, currency, and finance. Horner was a promoter of the *Edinburgh Review* (1802) and played a key role in the *Bullion Report* as chairman of the parliamentary committee investigating the depreciation of bank-notes in 1810. His efforts contributed to the checking of the English banks' paper-issue and the reinstatement of silver and gold as circulating media.

### John Elliott Cairnes
Born in 1823, Cairnes entered Trinity College, Dublin, and later became a professor of political economy in a chair founded by Archbishop Whately. His 1857 work, *Character and Logical Method of Political Economy*, expanded upon the treatment of J.S. Mill. Cairnes is recognized for his precision of language and his exploration of the term "law" within economics. His later works include *Essays in Political Economy, Theoretical and Applied* (1873) and *Some Leading Principles of Political Economy, newly Expounded* (1874), the latter of which serves as a commentary on the English school of economists regarding wages, capital, labour, and the cost of production.

## International and Philosophical Perspectives
Beyond Britain, the study of political economy included figures such as Luigi Cossa (1831–1896), an Italian economist and professor at Pavia. Cossa authored *Scienza delle finanze* (1875) and *Guida allo studio dell' economia politica* (1876).

In a broader philosophical context, the term "political economy" has sometimes been viewed with antipathy. Some have regarded the principle of reciprocity in human dealings as "egoism" or "calculation," associating it with the label of political economy. This sentiment is contrasted with the belief that those who possess wealth should give it gratuitously to those who do not.
