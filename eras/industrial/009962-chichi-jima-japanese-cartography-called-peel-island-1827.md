# Chichi-jima

Chichi-jima, known in English as Father Island, is one of the islands within the Bonin Islands chain. These islands, referred to by the Japanese as Ogasawara-jima, consist of twenty small islands stretching nearly due north and south, located approximately 500 miles from the mainland of Japan. Chichi-jima is the second largest of these islands.

## Geography and Nomenclature
The Bonin Islands are volcanic in nature and are situated between 26 deg. 35' and 27 deg. 45' N. and a little east of 142 E. While Japanese investigations identify twenty islands, only ten are of appreciable size. These include Muko-shima (Bridegroom Island), Nakadachi-shima (Go-between Island), Yome-shima (Bride Island), Ototo-jima (Younger-brother Island), Ani-shima (Elder-brother Island), Chichi-jima (Father Island), Haha-jima (Mother Island), Mei-jima (Niece Island), Ani-jima (Elder-sister Island), and Imoto-jima (Younger-sister Island).

European geographers have historically divided these islands into three groups: the Parry Islands in the north, the Beechey Islands in the center, and the Coffin or Bailey Islands in the south. In 1827, Captain Beechey utilized different nomenclature in Japanese cartography, naming Chichi-jima "Peel Island." He also named Ototo-jima "Stapleton Island" and Ani-jima "Buckland Island." Additionally, Beechey identified Fitton Bay as a large bay on the south of Peel Island.

## Port Lloyd
Located on Peel Island (Chichi-jima), Port Lloyd serves as the chief anchorage. In 1853, Commodore Perry visited the islands and suggested that the United States establish a coaling station at this location. Perry observed that Port Lloyd appeared to have formerly been the crater of a volcano, with the surrounding hills having been thrown up from it and the harbor entrance serving as a fissure through which lava once flowed into the sea.

## History and Settlement
Japanese annals indicate that the islands were discovered toward the end of the 16th century and were added to the fief of a Daimyo named Ogasawa Sadayori, which provided the name Ogasawara-jima. The islands were also called Bunin-jima (which foreigners corrupted into "Bonin") because they were without inhabitants (*bu* meaning without, *nin* meaning inhabitants).

Effective occupation of the islands was inconsistent. Following a Japanese government veto against the construction of sea-going vessels, communications ceased entirely in 1635. Communication was fitfully restored in 1728 by a representative of the Ogasawara family, but was interrupted again until 1861, when an attempt to establish a Japanese colony at Port Lloyd failed.

During the intervening period, Captain Beechey visited the islands aboard the "Blossom" to publish descriptions and assign names. In 1830, a small party consisting of a Dane, two American citizens, and two British subjects sailed from the Sandwich Islands to Port Lloyd with several Hawaiian natives. This group settled on Peel Island and hoisted the British flag.

By the time of Commodore Perry's visit in 1853, Peel Island had thirty-one inhabitants, including four Americans, four English, one Portuguese, and the remainder being natives of the Ladrones and the Sandwich Islands. By 1875, a visit by Mr. Russell Robertson revealed the colony had grown to sixty-nine people, though only five were pure whites. Robertson noted that the settlers lived comfortably on cultivated land but lacked laws, religion, education, and a system of government. The settlers spoke English and viewed themselves as a British colony.

## Administration and Modern Status
In 1861, the British government renounced all claims to the islands, recognizing Japan's right of possession. Following this, the population grew to approximately 4,500, and the islands were duly administered with the establishment of regular steam communication.
