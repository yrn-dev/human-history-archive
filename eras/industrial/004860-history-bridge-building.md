# History of Bridge Building

The construction of bridges has evolved from early timber and stone structures to complex metal designs guided by mathematical theory. Throughout history, bridges have served as critical infrastructure for urban connectivity, military movement, and the expansion of railway networks.

## Early and Ancient Bridge Construction
In ancient urban centers, bridges were often developed to replace the use of boats for crossing rivers. In one instance, a bridge was constructed across a branch of the Euphrates river to connect two quays. This bridge measured a furlong in length and thirty feet in breadth. To address the challenge of a sandy river bottom, the builders turned the course of the river to lay the channel dry and establish foundations. The structure featured arches made of huge stones, which were secured using melted lead and iron chains.

In England, some of the earliest known stone bridges include the bridge at Crowland near Peterborough, which is mentioned in a charter from the year 943 and was likely built by abbots. In London, the first bridges over the Thames were timber. William of Malmesbury noted the existence of a bridge in 994. By approximately 1176, Peter of Colechurch began the foundation of the first stone bridge, known as Old London Bridge, located to the west of the timber bridge. Old London Bridge supported timber houses that frequently burned, though the main structure remained until the early 19th century. Its arches spanned between 10 and 33 feet, with a total waterway of 337 feet.

## Medieval and Early Modern Developments
During the latter part of the 12th century, a confraternity known as the Bridge-building Brotherhood (*Fratres Pontifices*) emerged in southern France. Recognized by Pope Clement III in 1189, this group built bridges, managed ferries, and maintained hospices at the primary fords of major rivers.

Other early stone examples include Devorgilla's bridge, built in 1280. Originally consisting of nine arches, it was later reduced to three and eventually reserved exclusively for foot passengers. In other regions, medieval bridges were eventually replaced by newer structures, such as the Pont du Change over the Saône and the Pont de la Guillotière over the Rhône, the latter of which retained a portion of its original medieval structure.

## Engineering Principles and Theory
Modern bridge engineering relies on the calculation of loads and stresses to ensure safety. Engineers must proportion bridge parts so that stresses do not exceed limits established by experience, and in many countries, these limits for railway and public bridges are mandated by law.

The development of design theory has progressed alongside the demand for more complex designs and greater spans. In modern metal bridges, every member has a calculated straining action and a specific function. This theoretical approach has reduced the need for empirical safety allowances. However, as the span of a bridge increases, the dead weight of the superstructure becomes a larger fraction of the total load. Eventually, a limit is reached where the dead weight prevents any further increase in the span.

## Modern Materials and Specialized Structures
The substructure of a bridge—comprising the foundations, abutments, and piers—is typically constructed from concrete, brickwork, or stone masonry, though woodwork or metal is occasionally used for intermediate piers.

In the United States, railway bridges are often designed by expert bridge-building companies rather than the railway companies themselves. These designs utilize standard patterns to ensure accurate manufacture. The choice of material and design typically depends on the span:
* **Riveted plate girders:** Used for spans up to 50 feet.
* **Riveted braced girders:** Used for spans between 50 and 75 feet.
* **Pin-connected girders:** Used for spans longer than 75 feet.

Following the construction of the Forth bridge, cantilever bridges became extensively used, alongside various steel arch and suspension bridges. Additionally, overhead railways are considered virtually continuous bridge constructions.

## Military and Urban Integration
In military fortification, a "bridge-head" (*tête-du-pont*) is a work designed to protect the passage of a river. Because moving an army across a bridge is a slow and complicated process, these fortifications are extended far enough to keep enemy artillery out of range and provide space for troops to form on the opposite bank.

In urban environments, bridges often integrate with other city features. In some historical contexts, bridges were connected to city fortifications by walls. In other cities, such as those spanning the river Lee, drawbridges were used in the 18th century to connect islands broken up by navigable channels. Modern urban bridges also support public transit, such as electric tramways.
