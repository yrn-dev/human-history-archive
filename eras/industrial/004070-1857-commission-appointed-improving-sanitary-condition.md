# Commission for Improving the Sanitary Condition of Barracks and Hospitals

In 1857, a commission was appointed for the purpose of improving the sanitary condition of hospitals and barracks. This body conducted an exhaustive inspection of barracks located within the United Kingdom, eventually issuing its report in 1861. Following this initial domestic inquiry, similar commissions were established to examine military barracks in India and in Mediterranean stations.

## Objectives and General Principles
The commissions provided valuable recommendations for the improvement of nearly every barrack they inspected. Beyond specific site improvements, they established general sanitary principles regarding the construction and arrangement of military hospitals and barracks. These reports continue to be considered useful for those interested in sanitary science as applied to the construction and improvement of such buildings.

Prominent contributors to this work included John Sutherland, M.D., Captain (later Sir Douglas) Gallon, R.E., and Sidney Herbert, who later became Lord Herbert of Lea.

## Standards of Accommodation
The commission addressed significant deficiencies in soldier accommodation. A general order from 1845 had previously directed that new barracks at home stations provide 450 to 500 cubic feet of space per man. However, this standard had not been applied to existing buildings or those appropriated for use as barracks. 

Detailed examinations revealed that some men had less than 250 cubic feet of space. Out of a nominal accommodation for 76,813 soldiers, only 2,003 men had 600 cubic feet per man. The royal commission of 1857 established 600 cubic feet as the new minimum scale. Implementing this standard for every soldier would have required reducing the total accommodation of the barracks by nearly one-third. Consequently, some buildings were condemned as entirely unsuitable, while others were improved through site opening and alterations.

## Evolution into the Army Sanitary Committee
In 1862, the commission was constituted as a standing body known as the Army Sanitary Committee. This committee continues to advise the secretary of state for war on several matters, including:
* The selection of sites for new hospitals or barracks.
* Type plans, with a specific focus on sanitary details.
* Principles of fitments and sanitary construction.

A definite standard of accommodation was established by the committee, which served as the basis for the first issue of the *Barrack Synopsis* in 1865. Members of the committee, such as Yule, worked in communication with Miss Florence Nightingale.

## Impact on Military Health and Infrastructure
The systematic progress following the 1857 inquiry led to significant improvements in the condition of soldiers, despite being hampered by a lack of funds. Various legislative acts facilitated these improvements:
* **Military Forces Localization Act of 1872:** Led to the construction of numerous other barracks and regimental depots.
* **Barracks Act of 1890:** Replaced permanent buildings with sanitary and convenient ones at Shorncliffe, Colchester, Aldershot, and Curragh, replacing worn-out huts.
* **Military Works Acts (1897, 1899, 1901):** Provided further improvements and additions.

The practical results of these expenditures are reflected in mortality rates. In 1857, the annual rate of mortality for the army at home was 17.5 per 1,000 men (compared to 9.2 for the civil male population of the same age). By 1897, forty years later, the army mortality rate had dropped to 3.42 per 1,000. Improved barrack accommodation is credited as a great contributor to this result.

## Administration of Construction
Until 1904, the construction of barracks remained under the control of the Corps of Royal Engineers. In 1904, a civil department was re-established. This department was led by an architect titled the "director of barrack construction," who was responsible for the maintenance and construction of military hospitals and barracks at home stations.
