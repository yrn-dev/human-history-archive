# Terminating Building Societies and "Starr Bowkett" Mutuals

Building societies are organizations established for the purpose of raising a fund through member subscriptions to provide advances to members upon leasehold, copyhold, or freehold estate via mortgages. According to the Building Societies Act 1874 (§ 13), these organizations are classified as either "permanent" or "terminating." A terminating society is defined as one that, by its own rules, is intended to terminate upon the attainment of a specified result or at a fixed date. In a more general sense, these societies allow individuals to become their own landlords by collecting small periodical subscriptions until each member can receive a sum sufficient to assist in the purchase of a dwelling-house.

## The Starr Bowkett and Mutual Societies
A specific variety of the terminating class of societies, known as "mutual" societies or "Starr Bowkett" societies, gained considerable favor, resulting in the establishment of more than a thousand such organizations. These societies differed from typical building societies in several key ways: the advance provided to a member was much larger, while the contributions required from members who had not yet received an advance were much smaller. Furthermore, advances were made without any calculation of interest.

For example, a society might issue 500 shares with weekly contributions of 1s. 3d. Once a sum of £300 accumulated, it was allotted to a shareholder via a ballot. The recipient was required to repay the sum without interest in installments over 10 or 12½ years while continuing to pay their share contributions. While the recipient of such an appropriation could sell it, often for a profit, those who did not succeed in the ballot earned no profit except from fines. Because the number of successful members in the early years was necessarily small, many members became discontented and retired.

## Financial Limitations and Modifications
Because Starr Bowkett societies did not receive interest, they were unable to pay it and therefore could not borrow money. To address the discontent of investing members, the plan was eventually modified to grant appropriations through a combination of ballot and sale. This allowed the society to earn profits for investing members via premiums paid on sales, which functioned similarly to interest payments on the advanced amount.

A further modification involved making all appropriations via sale. This shifted the mutual society back toward the ordinary form of building society. For instance, paying 10s. a month on a £60 loan for 14 years is functionally identical to borrowing a nominal sum of £84 for the same period and allowing a "bidding" of £24 off the loan at a sale. The formation of this class of societies ended following the passing of the act of 1894, which prohibited the use of balloting for advances in any societies established thereafter.

## Evolution Toward the Permanent Plan
Around 1846, a significant modification known as the "permanent" plan was introduced and adopted by many societies. This system recognized that members fell into two distinct classes: investors (those who had not yet received or did not desire an advance) and debtors (those who had received advances). 

The permanent plan allowed members to enter the society at any time without the need for back-payment and permitted advances for any agreed-upon term of years. This created a continuous inflow of funds and a steady means of profitable investment. Under this model, a member's interest in the society terminated only when their advance was paid off or their share was realized, while the society itself continued to operate using the accruing subscriptions of other members to fund further advances.

## Historical Context and Global Variations
The early history of building societies is not clearly traceable, though "building clubs" were mentioned in Birmingham in 1795. A society was established by deed in Greenwich in 1809, and another was founded in 1825 in Kirkcudbright, Scotland, under the auspices of the earl of Selkirk; similar Scottish societies used the title "menages."

Outside the United Kingdom, different systems exist. In Belgium, the Government Savings Bank provides advances to societies of construction or credit at interest rates of 2½% or 3%, while the borrower pays 4%. In many Belgian cases, borrowers maintain insurance with the savings bank so that repayments terminate upon their death. As of December 31, 1903, nearly 25,000 such advances were being repaid. In Germany, building societies are recognized as self-help societies, though they are fewer in number due to the influence of the credit societies founded by Schulze-Delitzsch.
