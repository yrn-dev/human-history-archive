# Bank of England

The Bank of England serves as a central financial institution in the United Kingdom, managing a significant portion of the nation's note circulation and maintaining reserves of specie. Its operations are governed by specific legal frameworks, most notably Peel's act of 1844, which regulates its note circulation.

## Note Circulation and Capital
In 1906, the average circulation of the Bank of England was £28,890,000. When combined with the circulation of private banks (£124,000) and joint-stock banks (£429,000), the total circulation in England amounted to £29,443,000. This contributed to a total United Kingdom circulation of £43,372,000, which included Scotland (£7,477,000) and Ireland (£6,452,000).

The Bank of England's note circulation is smaller than that of other major international banks; for comparison, the note circulation of the Bank of France was £186,300,000 and the Imperial Bank of Germany was £69,000,000 in 1906. However, the Bank of England possesses a higher capital of £18,000,000, which exceeds the capital of the Bank of Germany (£12,200,000) and the Bank of France (£9,000,000).

Since 1844, there has been an increase in the circulation of Bank of England notes. This growth is attributed in part to the expansion of banking offices in England and Wales, which grew from 976 in 1844 to more than 5,880 in 1906. Because these offices must hold till-money, a large portion of the Bank of England's increased circulation is likely held within these tills.

## Specie Reserves and Stability
The Bank of England maintains a reserve of specie, though this basis has been described as narrow for the country's business requirements. Decennial averages of these reserves show fluctuations:
* 1845: £8,500,000
* 1855: £8,400,000
* 1865: £8,000,000
* 1875: £11,600,000
* 1885: £15,100,000
* 1895: £29,900,000
* 1906: £23,500,000

These figures have not increased uniformly. For instance, the £15,100,000 reserve in 1885 dropped to £12,700,000 by 1888, and the £29,900,000 in 1895 was followed by £34,600,000 in 1896 and £21,200,000 in 1899.

The Bank of England's gold holdings are generally smaller than those of the Bank of France. This disparity was evident during the Baring crisis in the autumn of 1890, when the Bank of England was required to borrow £3,000,000 in gold from the Bank of France. Furthermore, the Bank of England experiences more frequent fluctuations in the rate of discount compared to the Bank of France, where a larger specie reserve has provided greater trade stability.

## Crisis Management and Legal Framework
The strictness of the Bank Act has historically created challenges during financial panics. In October 1847, the Bank of England's reserve of specie dropped rapidly from £3,070,000 on October 16th to £1,600,000 by October 30th. During this period, widespread hoarding of bank notes and coin occurred. Despite there being more than £6,000,000 of specie in the issue department, the banking department faced the possibility of being compelled to stop payment.

To resolve the crisis, the prime minister, Lord John (afterwards Earl) Russell, provided a relaxation of the Bank Act on October 22nd or 23rd. An official letter dated October 25th recommended that the directors enlarge their advances and discounts upon approved security, charging a high rate of 8% to keep operations reasonable. The government promised a bill of indemnity for any breach of law resulting from this arrangement, and the resulting profits were designated for the benefit of the public.

Regarding statutory limits, the English act differs from the German law of the Reichsbank. While the German bank is permitted to overpass its limit of issue by paying 5% on the surplus, the English act allows no such automatic statutory power.

## Financial Contributions
The note circulation provides revenue for the government. In a typical year ending April 5, 1904, the government received £184,930 2s. 2d. from the profits of issue from the Bank of England. Additionally, composition for duties on the notes and bills of country bankers and the banks of Ireland and England amounted to £120,768 18s. 6d.
