# Wilson v. Brett (1843)

The legal case of *Wilson v. Brett* (1843, 11 M. & W. 113) is cited within the context of the law of bailment, specifically regarding the classification and responsibilities associated with gratuitous loans.

## Legal Context of Bailment
Bailment is defined in law as the delivery of goods from a "bailor" to a "bailee" for a specific purpose. This delivery occurs under a contract, which may be express or implied, with the understanding that once the purpose is fulfilled, the goods will be redelivered to the bailor, dealt with according to the bailor's directions, or kept until the bailor reclaims them.

The classification of bailments generally follows the system established by Chief Justice Holt in the 1704 case of *Coggs v. Bernard*. Under this system, different types of bailments carry different levels of liability for the bailee.

## Commodatum and Wilson v. Brett
The case of *Wilson v. Brett* falls under the category of *commodatum*, which is defined as a loan of useful goods or chattels lent to a bailee *gratis* (without payment) for the bailee's own use.

In this specific type of transaction, the bailee is considered to be representing themselves to the bailor as a person possessing the competent skill necessary to care for the item being lent. Because the transaction is a gratuitous loan and is conducted solely for the advantage of the bailee, the legal obligations placed upon the bailee are stringent.

## Standards of Care and Liability
Under the principles applied in *Wilson v. Brett*, a bailee in a *commodatum* arrangement is bound to exercise great diligence in protecting the bailed item. Consequently, the bailee is held responsible even for slight negligence.

This standard of care differs from other forms of bailment. For example, in *depositum* (bailment without reward for the purpose of keeping goods), the bailee is liable for gross negligence but not for ordinary negligence. In contrast, *locatio rei* (lending for hire) requires the bailee to exercise the level of diligence that a prudent man would exercise toward his own property.
