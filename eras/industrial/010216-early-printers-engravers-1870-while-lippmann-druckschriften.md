# Early Printers and Engravers

The history of printing and engraving is documented through various specialized bibliographies and the study of early printed works. While the appreciation of finely printed books has seldom extended much beyond the 15th century, several key texts provide insight into the development of the craft. These include Humphrey's *Masterpieces of the Early Printers and Engravers* (1870) and Lippmann's *Druckschriften des XV. bis XVIII Jahrhunderts* (1884-1887), the latter of which covers the later period, although not very fully.

## Early Printing and Typefounding
The invention of printing coincided with the development of illustration. In the Netherlands, Laurens Janssoen-Koster (c. 1370-1440), a city treasurer and official of the parish church of Haarlem, is recognized by many investigators as one of the inventors of the art. 

In England, the Caslon family became famous typefounders. William Caslon (1692-1766) began his career in London in 1716 as a bookbinder's tool-cutter and an engraver of barrels and gun locks. Encouraged by William Bowyer, he established a type foundry. His type was noted for its legibility and distinction, earning him patronage from leading printers in England and on the continent. Although the use of Caslon types ceased around the beginning of the 19th century, they were revived circa 1845 for the printing of the *Diary of Lady Willoughby* by the Chiswick Press. William Caslon's son, also named William (1720-1778), continued the business after his father's death on January 23, 1766.

## Early Illustration and Woodcuts
Early illustrated books, particularly those containing primitive woodcuts in incunabula, are highly valued. Notable examples include the *Nuremburg Chronicle* (1493) and the *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili* (Venice, 1499), the latter of which was printed by Aldus in the final year of the 15th century and is noted for its fine outline illustrations. 

The development of engraving on wood and metal attracted many Renaissance painters. In Italy, artists such as Titian, Michelangelo, Mantegna, and Pollajuolo contributed to the expansion of illustrative art. The German school of the 16th century was even more vigorous in its commitment to illustration; artists such as Holbein, Altdorfer, Burgmair, and Dürer produced engraved illustrations on copper or wood that remain unrivaled.

## Engraving Techniques and Counterfeiting
Engraving evolved into different technical systems, including line-engraving (where lines are cut into copper or steel plates) and relief-engraving (where lines stand above the plate, similar to wood-engraving). Line-engraving is used for plate-printing, which produces a sharper impression, while relief-engraving is used for surface-printing, similar to typography.

Historically, the use of copper plates for early bank-notes presented challenges, as the plates wore out quickly. This resulted in a variation in appearance between notes printed from new plates (sharp and clear) and those from old plates (pale and blurred), a discrepancy that often assisted forgers.

In the realm of artistic engraving, Marcantonio was a prominent figure in Italy between 1505 and 1511. His work initially drew inspiration from Francia and northern masters, particularly in the use of light and shadow and landscape backgrounds. Marcantonio eventually produced sixty-nine direct copper counterfeits of Albert Dürer's woodcuts, including thirty-seven from the "Little Passion" and seventeen from the "Life of the Virgin." According to Vasari, this led Dürer to travel to Venice to lodge a complaint with the Senate to prohibit the counterfeiting of his monogram. However, this account is considered mistaken, as Dürer's journey occurred in 1506, while the woodcuts in question were not published until 1511.

## Bibliographic Authorities
The study of early printing and engraving is supported by numerous specialized works:
*   **Italy:** Lippmann's *Wood Engraving in Italy in the 15th Century* (1888), Kristeller's *Early Florentine Woodcuts* (1897), and the duc de Rivoli's *Bibliographie des livres a figures venitiens 1469-1525* (1892).
*   **Germany:** Muther's *Die deutsche Bucherillustration der Gothik und Fruhrenaissance* (1884).
*   **Netherlands and Belgium:** Sir W.M. Conway's *The Woodcutters of the Netherlands in the 15th Century* (1884).
*   **France:** Claudin's *Histoire de l'imprimerie en France* (1900).
*   **England:** Colvin's *Early Engraving and Engravers in England* (1905) and Martin Hardie's *English Colour Books* (1906).

Other authorities include W. J. Linton, Walter Crane, and Gleeson White, who documented English illustration. Additionally, printers' marks have been cataloged by Berjeau, W. Roberts, and Silvestre.
