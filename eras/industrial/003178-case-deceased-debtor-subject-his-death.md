# Bankruptcy and Insolvency Law

The legal frameworks governing bankruptcy and insolvency have evolved from primitive, drastic measures focused on the interests of creditors to complex administrative systems that provide relief to debtors. These laws vary significantly across different jurisdictions and historical periods.

## Historical Development and Early Practices
In primitive communities, formal bankruptcy systems did not exist. Creditors pursued remedies through community-sanctioned practices that were often severe. Under the Roman law of the Twelve Tables, creditors possessed the legal power to sell a debtor and their family into slavery. In extreme cases, the law was understood to allow creditors to divide the body of the *addictus debitor* among them, though there is no known example of this being carried into effect.

Similar practices were common in Greece and Rome. In Attic law, a person who failed to repay money used to redeem a citizen from captivity became the slave of the party who provided the funds. Even among the Bani Israel, whose legislation is described as comparatively humane, the Old Testament records a woman seeking help from Elisha because a creditor had come to take her two children as bondmen following her husband's death.

As humane ideas grew and political tensions rose between the creditor class (patricians) and the debtor class (plebeians), these laws shifted. The right to sell a debtor was eventually replaced by imprisonment in public prisons. In feudal Europe, the state often prohibited enforcement against the person of the debtor to ensure subjects remained free for military service. However, as trade developed, English courts gradually evolved the practice of imprisoning debtors to compel the meeting of obligations.

## English Bankruptcy Law
The earliest English statute on bankruptcy, 34 & 35 Henry VIII. c. 4 (1542), targeted fraudulent debtors. It allowed high officers and the lord chancellor to seize and divide the estates of such debtors among creditors, but provided no relief to the debtor from their liabilities. Over time, legislation shifted toward granting relief to bankrupts who conformed to the law, and by 1825, debtors were permitted to initiate their own proceedings.

Under current English and Welsh procedure, a receiving order may be made by a court in the district where a debtor resides or does business. This can occur via the debtor's own petition or a petition from creditors whose claims total at least £50. A creditor's petition requires proof of the debt and an "act of bankruptcy" committed within the previous three months. Acts of bankruptcy include:
* Failing to satisfy a claim upon a bankruptcy notice.
* Making an assignment for the benefit of general creditors.
* Absconding or keeping house.
* Giving notice of suspension of payments.
* Having goods seized or sold under execution.
* Filing a declaration of inability to pay debts.
* Granting a fraudulent conveyance or preference.

## Scottish Bankruptcy Law
The Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act of 1856 primarily regulates bankruptcy in Scotland. "Notour" (legally declared) bankruptcy is constituted through sequestration or insolvency. Insolvency may concur with a duly executed charge for payment, the sale of effects under poinding or sequestration for rent, or an application for the benefit of *cessio bonorum*.

Sequestration may be awarded for the estate of a living debtor subject to Scottish jurisdiction if they petition with the concurrence of qualified creditors, or if qualified creditors petition provided the debtor is a notour bankrupt who had a place of business or dwelling-house in Scotland within the previous year. In the case of a deceased debtor subject to the court's jurisdiction, sequestration may be awarded upon the petition of qualified creditors or the debtor's mandatory.

The Debtors Act 1880 and the Bankruptcy and Cessio Act 1881 modified the process of *cessio bonorum*. Previously, this was primarily a means to avoid imprisonment for debt via the formal surrender of all goods, though it did not grant a discharge. Following the abolition of imprisonment for debt (except for public rates, assessments, and crown debts), the 1881 Act extended the right to petition for a decree of *cessio* to any creditor of a notour bankrupt. The court may award sequestration if the debtor's liabilities exceed £200.

## International Variations
Bankruptcy laws differ across various British territories and other regions:
* **Canada:** The British North America Act 1867 gave the Dominion parliament exclusive power over bankruptcy, but no Dominion act currently exists. A 1875 act was repealed in 1880, likely due to differing provincial systems, such as the English law in Ontario and the French code based on *cessio bonorum* in Quebec.
* **Transvaal:** Roman-Dutch law prevails, governed by the Insolvency Law, No. 13 of 1895, which provides for compulsory sequestration and voluntary surrender.
* **Other Regions:** English law is substantially followed in Natal and Cape Colony. The laws in Hong Kong, Mauritius, Trinidad, Tobago, Jamaica, Grenada, British Guiana, and the Straits Settlements are modelled on the English pattern. In India, the Indian Insolvency Act 1848 (extended in 1889) regulates the subject.
