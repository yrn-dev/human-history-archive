# RULE VII.--DAMAGE TO ENGINES IN REFLOATING A SHIP

## Definition and Application
Rule VII governs the treatment of damage caused to a ship's boilers and machinery during efforts to refloat a vessel. According to the rule, such damage is allowed as General Average (G.A.) provided the ship is ashore and in a position of peril. To qualify for G.A., it must be demonstrated that the damage resulted from an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety, accepting the risk of such damage.

## Nature of the Sacrifice
Efforts to force a stranded ship off the ground often involve an abnormal use of the vessel's appliances. This abnormal use is likely to cause damage to the engines and boilers, as well as to sails and spars (the latter of which is covered under Rule VI). Because these efforts are undertaken for the common safety, the resulting damage is treated as an act of sacrifice.

## Legal Precedent and English Law
The application of Rules VI and VII is considered to be in accord with English law. This was demonstrated in the 1895 case of "The Bona" (P. 125), where the court of appeal ruled that certain costs and damages were subjects for G.A. contribution at common law. Specifically, the court held that both the coal and stores consumed, and the damage sustained by the engines while they were worked to get the ship off the ground, were allowable.

## Context within General Average Rules
Rule VII exists as part of a broader framework of rules regarding maritime sacrifice and common safety. While Rule VII focuses on machinery, other related rules address different aspects of stranding and recovery:
* **Rule V:** Addresses the conditions under which a voluntary stranding of a ship is considered a G.A. act.
* **Rule VI:** Covers damage to or loss of sails and spars caused by driving a ship higher up the ground or forcing her off the ground for common safety.
* **Rule VIII:** Allows for G.A. in the case of damage sustained by cargo when it is discharged or lightered to assist in getting a ship off a strand.

## Contrast with Ancient Maritime Codes
The modern approach to refloating a ship differs significantly from ancient regulations. Under an earlier Code, the captain bore total responsibility for the ship and the freight and was required to replace all losses. In that legal system, even if a captain successfully refloated a ship, he was required to pay a fine equal to half the ship's value for having sunk it.
