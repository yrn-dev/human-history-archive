# The Publication of Logic and Related Logical Works

## The Logic of 1870
In 1870, the work titled *Logic* was published. This text was specifically designed for the use of students. While it was based on the work of J. S. Mill, it differed from Mill's views in many particulars. The work is characterized by two distinctive features: the detailed application of the principles of logic to various sciences and the treatment of the doctrine of the conservation of energy in connection with causation.

The author of *Logic*, Bain, had been appointed by the crown in 1860 to the new chair of logic and English at the university of Aberdeen. This chair was created following the 1858 Scottish Universities Commission, which amalgamated Marischal and King's colleges. Upon his appointment, Bain sought to address the lack of adequate attention given to English and logic in Aberdeen. He established a school of philosophy and influenced the teaching of English composition and grammar.

## Other Works by Bain
Prior to the publication of *Logic*, Bain focused on English textbooks, producing *Higher English Grammar* (1863), the *Manual of Rhetoric* (1866), *A First English Grammar* (1872), and the *Companion to the Higher Grammar* (1874). 

Regarding his philosophical contributions, Bain had previously published *The Senses and the Intellect*, to which *The Study of Character* (including an *Estimate of Phrenology*) was added in 1861. Because these earlier writings were too large for effective classroom use, he published the *Manual of Mental and Moral Science* in 1868. This manual served as a condensed version of his treatises, featuring re-stated doctrines, new illustrations, and important additions. Following the publication of *Logic*, Bain published *Mind and Body* (1872) and *Education as a Science* (1879) as part of "The International Scientific Series." In 1871, the university of Edinburgh recognized his services to Scottish education by conferring upon him an honorary degree of doctor of laws.

## Parallel Developments in Logic
Other significant contributions to logic were made by Boole and De Morgan. Boole is noted as perhaps the first English mathematician since John Wallis to write on logic. In the spring of 1847, he published a pamphlet titled *Mathematical Analysis of Logic*, though he later viewed this as an imperfect exposition. His mature views were presented in the 1854 work, *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, on which are founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities*. 

Boole's system utilized a general symbolic method of logical inference, treating premises symbolically to draw conclusions. He identified a deep analogy between the symbols of algebra and those representing logical forms and syllogisms, suggesting that logic is mathematics restricted to the quantities 0 and 1. In his system, unity represents the universe of thinkable objects, and literal symbols (such as x, y, z) act as elective meanings. For example, if x represents "horned" and y represents "sheep," the combination of x and y represents "horned sheep."

## The Work of Augustus De Morgan
Augustus De Morgan also produced a principal logical treatise in late 1847 titled *Formal Logic, or the Calculus of Inference, Necessary and Probable*. This work included the *First Notions*, a development of his doctrine of the numerical definite syllogism and the syllogism, as well as chapters on induction, probability, and fallacies. 

Between 1850 and 1863, De Morgan published four elaborate memoirs on the "Syllogism" in the *Cambridge Philosophical Transactions*. These papers developed a new onymatic system of logical expression and a new logic of relations, utilizing improved systems of notation. In 1860, he published a *Syllabus of a Proposed System of Logic* to make these contents better known, and he wrote the article "Logic" for the *English Cyclopaedia* in the same year.

De Morgan believed that mathematicians had neglected logic and logicians had neglected mathematics. He sought to reconcile the two branches of exact science. While he attempted to show how a mathematician could detect errors in logical writings, some suggest that the real analogy between qualitative and quantitative reasoning is better found in the works of Boole than in De Morgan's system.
