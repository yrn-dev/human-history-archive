# Internal Construction of the Aneroid

The internal construction of an aneroid, as illustrated in Figure 4, is revealed when the face of the instrument is removed while the hand remains attached. This specific design differs only slightly from the form developed by Vidie.

## The Exhausted Chamber
The core of the instrument is a flat circular metallic box, designated as *a*. This chamber serves as a spring and is characterized by upper and under surfaces that are corrugated in concentric circles; these corrugations are designed to increase the elasticity of the box. The chamber is partially exhausted of air via a short tube, labeled *b*, which is then soldered to ensure it is air-tight. Because of this partial vacuum, the box is affected by every variation in the external atmospheric pressure, causing the surface of the box to be either depressed or elevated.

## The Lever System and Motion
The motion originating in the corrugated elastic box is communicated to the index through a series of levers:

*   **Principal Lever:** A solid cylindrical projection, *x*, is located at the center of the upper surface of the exhausted chamber. The principal lever, *cde*, is attached to the top of this projection. This lever is supported by two vertical pins to allow for perfect freedom of motion and rests partly on a spiral spring at point *d*.
*   **Secondary Lever and Transmission:** The end of the principal lever (*e*) is attached to a smaller lever, *f*. From this second lever, a chain (*g*) extends to point *h*.
*   **The Hand and Drum:** At point *h*, the chain works on a drum attached to the axis of the hand. This mechanism, which is connected with a hair spring at *h*, changes the motion from vertical to horizontal to regulate the hand. The hand's attachments are secured to a metallic plate, *i*.

## Temperature Compensation and Magnification
To ensure accuracy, the spiral spring located at *d*, upon which the lever rests, is intended to compensate for the effects of alterations in temperature.

The instrument is designed to multiply the smallest degrees of atmospheric pressure to make them sensible on the index. While the actual movement at the center of the exhausted box is very slight, the action of the levers multiplies this motion 657 times at the point of the hand. Consequently, a movement of the 220th part of an inch within the box results in the point of the hand moving three inches across the dial.

## Related Instruments
The principle utilized in this construction is the same as that used in the aneroid developed by Eugene Bourdon. Additionally, the instrument designed by Vidie has been improved by Hulot and Vaudet.
