# Piston Combinations and Intonation in Brass Instruments

In the construction of brass wind instruments, the use of pistons and valves allows for a chromatic compass by altering the length of the tubing. While individual pistons lower the pitch by specific intervals, the use of these pistons in combination is necessary to achieve a complete chromatic scale. However, this method introduces a persistent acoustic challenge regarding intonation.

## Mechanics of Pitch Alteration
The fundamental principle of the valve system is to provide additional lengths of tubing to deepen the pitch of the instrument and transpose it into different keys. On instruments like the cornet, three pistons open valves leading into supplementary wind-ways that lengthen the original tube, lowering the pitch by one tone, half a tone, and one tone and a half, respectively. Similarly, the French horn utilizes valves to lower the pitch by a tone (first valve), a semitone (second valve), and a tone and a half (third valve).

On the bombardon (or bass tuba), which was the first bass wind instrument fitted with valves, the pistons typically lower the pitch by 1, 1/2, 1-1/2, and 2-1/2 tones, though Belgian models may differ, lowering the pitch by 1, 1/2, 2, and 3 tones. To obtain a complete chromatic scale throughout the compass—similar to the seven positions available on a slide-trombone—the bombardon utilizes combinations of these pistons. For example, the simultaneous use of pistons No. 2 and 3 lowers the pitch by two tones; using Nos. 1, 2, and 3 lowers it by three tones; and the combination of Nos. 1, 2, 3, and 4 lowers the pitch by five and a half tones.

## The Problem of Intonation
A significant inherent defect in the valve system is that a combination of pistons fails to produce intervals with absolutely correct acoustic intonation. This occurs because the length of tubing thrown open by a combination is not the theoretical length required to produce the specific interval.

This defect is illustrated by the B[flat] cornet, which has a tube approximately 4 feet long. To lower the pitch by a semitone, an additional length of about 3 inches is required. However, if the cornet has already been lowered by one tone to the key of A[flat], the total tube length increases by some 6 inches. In this state, the 3-inch semitone piston no longer provides sufficient additional tubing to produce a semitone of correct intonation.

## Corrective Measures and Technical Evolutions
Because of these shortcomings, performers must often conceal the instrument's defects by varying their lip tension to correct the intonation. Historically, cornets were supplied with numerous crooks for keys such as A, A[flat], G, F, E, E[flat], and D, but most were abandoned as they proved unpractical for valve instruments.

Instrument makers have developed various ingenious contrivances to remedy these defects:
* **Independent Valve Systems:** Adolphe Sax designed a six-valve independent system around 1850, using a separate valve for each position to avoid piston combinations. However, this was abandoned due to practical difficulties, as the valves were "ascending" rather than "descending," cutting off lengths of the wind-way rather than adding to them.
* **Besson Systems:** The Besson *Registre* provided eight independent positions, while the Besson compensating system *Transpositeur* and the Besson enharmonic valve system (featuring three pistons and six independent tuning slides) were developed to improve accuracy.
* **Other Innovations:** These include the Boosey automatic compensating piston invented by D.J. Blaikley, V. Mahillon's automatic regulating pistons, and a new bore patented by Klussmann (produced by Rudall, Carte & Company) that is conical throughout the open tube.

These advancements have produced instruments that leave nothing to be desired regarding intonation, effectively overcoming the faulty intonation that previously required manual correction by the performer.
