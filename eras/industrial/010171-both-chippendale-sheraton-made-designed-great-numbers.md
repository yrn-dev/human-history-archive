# Bookcases and the English Cabinetmaking School

## The Work of Chippendale and Sheraton
Thomas Chippendale and Sheraton were prominent figures in a brilliant school of English cabinetmakers that emerged in the middle of the 18th century. Both designers produced great numbers of bookcases. These pieces were mostly glazed, featuring little lozenges encased in fret-work frames characterized by elegance and charm. Sheraton is specifically noted for the alluring grace of his satinwood bookcases, a quality that has very rarely been equalled. Examples of Sheraton's style include glass-fronted bookcases and cabinets made of mahogany, dating to approximately 1790.

## Materials and Construction
The construction of high-quality bookcases during this period involved various luxury materials. Mahogany, rosewood, and satinwood were commonly used, along with other choicer exotic timbers. These pieces were frequently mounted with chased and gilded bronze and inlaid with marqueterie. In some instances, dwarf bookcases were finished with a slab of choice marble on the top.

## Evolution of Cabinetmaking
The middle of the 18th century marked a shift in English furniture production. Prior to this time, Andre Charles Boulle was the only cabinetmaker to gain sufficient personal distinction for his name to be preserved. Following this period, England and France produced designers whose renown was comparable to artists in other media. Alongside Chippendale, the most outstanding names in the English school included Sheraton, Heppelwhite, Shearer, and the Adams.

However, this school was short-lived. By the 19th century, the rise of machinery and large factories in England and on the continent changed the nature of production. The traditional relationship between the designer and the work was severed; whereas a master and a few artificers previously superintended each piece, furniture began to pass through numerous distinct workshops. This subdivision of labour and the use of machinery resulted in a loss of artistic effect, as the "sensitive touch of the human hand" no longer guided the knife and chisel.

## Related Designers and Styles
Other figures influenced or imitated the dominant styles of the era. William Ince, an 18th-century designer and cabinetmaker, was a successful imitator of Chippendale. Ince's work was generally lighter, serving as a bridge between the florid and massive style of Chippendale and the "boudoir-like" forms of Hepplewhite. Ince was also influenced by Gothic and Chinese tastes in the second half of the 18th century.

Additionally, Matthias Darly, an 18th-century engraver and designer, produced many engravings for Thomas Chippendale. Darly also published *A new Book of Chinese Designs* in 1754 to cater to the period's interest in Chinese-style household decorations.

## Public Library Systems and Modern Bookcases
By the 20th century, the materials used for bookcases in great public libraries shifted toward industrial metals and stone. The British Museum utilized iron shelves covered with cowhide, the library of Congress in Washington used steel, and the Fitzwilliam library at Cambridge employed slate.

The archive identifies three primary systems for arranging these bookcases:
* **Flat:** Positioned directly against the wall.
* **Stacks:** Ranges arranged parallel to one another with only enough space for a librarian to pass. This system is used in public libraries where economy of space is essential.
* **Bays (or Alcoves):** Cases that jut out into the room at right angles to the wall-cases. This system is considered handsome and utilizes space to great advantage, as seen in the library of the city of London at the Guildhall.
