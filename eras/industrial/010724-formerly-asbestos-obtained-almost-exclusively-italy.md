# Asbestos

Asbestos is a mineral widely distributed across the globe, though it is found in sufficient purity and abundance for commercial work in only a few localities. Historically, it was obtained almost exclusively from Corsica and Italy, the latter of which continues to yield large quantities.

## Mineralogical Varieties and Characteristics
Asbestos is primarily categorized into two mineralogical types: amphibole and serpentine.

**Amphibole-Asbestos**
Also known as hornblende-asbestos, this variety is typically grey or white. It can produce exceptionally long fibres, with some Italian specimens reaching 5 or 6 feet in length, though these are often brittle and harsh. Specific types include:
* **Byssolite (or asbestoid):** A green or blue fibrous amphibole originating from Dauphiny.
* **Crocidolite:** A blue fibrous mineral known as Cape asbestos, found in the Asbestos Mountains of Griqualand West, Cape Colony. It occurs in veins within slaty rocks and is associated with quartzites and jaspers rich in brown iron-ore and magnetite.
* **Other forms:** The asbestos used in the arts historically included fibrous amphiboles such as anthophyllite or tremolite, and occasionally pyroxene.

**Serpentine-Asbestos**
Known mineralogically as chrysotile, this variety has become the most common type on the market in recent years. It occurs in narrow veins and produces shorter fibres (2 or 3 inches) that are elastic, flexible, and possess a delicate silky lustre. These fibres are typically greenish or yellowish in colour.

## Global Distribution and Extraction
Beyond Italy and Corsica, asbestos is found to a limited extent in Russia, Hungary, and Tirol. In the British Isles, it occurs in parts of Ireland and among the old rocks of North Wales.

**North America**
Canadian asbestos is currently the most industrially important variety. It is found in a small belt of serpentine in Quebec, specifically near Thetford and Black Lake, where its commercial value was recognized around 1877. The extraction process involves quarrying the rock, hand-cobbing, drying, and crushing it in rock-breakers. It is then passed through rollers and "fiberizers" to achieve a finer division before being sorted on a shaking screen. 

In the United States, asbestos is worked to a limited degree. Serpentine asbestos is found in Vermont, while amphibole-asbestos is obtained from Sall Mountain, Georgia. It is also present in Virginia, Massachusetts, Arizona, and South Carolina. Dr G.P. Merrill has indicated that some asbestos is the result of a shearing process within the rocks.

**South Africa**
In Griqualand West, asbestos is worked in the south-western districts. The mineral is found in the Griqua Town series, which are categorized as Pre-Cape rocks.

## Physical Properties and Industrial Applications
The economic value of asbestos is derived from its low thermal conductivity, its ability to withstand high temperatures, and its partial resistance to acid attacks. Depending on the texture of the fibres, it is given various trivial names:
* **Mountain flax:** Silky and flexible fibres.
* **Amianthus:** Finer kinds of fibres.
* **Mountain leather, paper, or cork:** Naturally interwoven, felted masses.

**Industrial Uses**
Asbestos is utilized as an electric insulator and a filtering medium for corrosive liquids. It is used for jacketing steam-pipes and boilers, and as packing for stopcocks, glands, and joints in machinery. It is manufactured into felt, millboard, and yarn. Other applications include:
* **Construction:** Fire-proof roofing and flooring (felt and sheathing), as well as fire-proof paints, plasters, and cements.
* **Safety equipment:** Clothing for firemen and fire-proof stage curtains.
* **Miscellaneous:** Packing for safes and balls made with fire-clay for gas-stoves.

A Canadian product called "Asbestic" is created by mixing lime with crushed serpentine rock containing thin asbestos seams to create a plaster. Other trade preparations include salamandrite, gypsine, asbestolith, and uralite.

## Historical and Rare Uses
In antiquity, asbestos was used in some sacred lamps as a wick; a similar use was adopted by the Eskimos of Labrador. Because of its fire-resistant properties, it was once spun into fabrics as a rarity. A merchant from China exhibited a handkerchief of *linum asbesti* (or "salamander's wool") to the Royal Society in 1676. Additionally, Charlemagne is said to have owned an asbestos tablecloth that could be purified by being thrown into a fire when soiled.
