# Würzburg and Vienna

The cities of Würzburg and Vienna appear in the archive's records primarily as centers of medical and scientific study, as well as hubs for the development of specific medical doctrines.

## Medical Education and Philosophy
Würzburg and Vienna served as key locations for the academic training of Friedrich Karl Christian Ludwig Büchner (1824–1899). A German physician and philosopher born in Darmstadt, Büchner studied in Vienna and Würzburg, along with Strassburg and Giessen. Following these studies, he became a lecturer in medicine at the university of Tübingen in 1852. 

Büchner is noted for his commitment to materialism, a philosophy he defended in several works, including *Kraft und Stoff* (1855). In this text, he argued for the finality of physical force and the indestructibility of matter and force. Büchner's materialistic monism posited that all spiritual and natural forces dwell within matter; he compared the production of mind, soul, and thought in an animal organism to the way a steam-engine produces motion. Due to the opposition caused by the extreme materialism of his work, he was forced to leave his post at Tübingen and return to Darmstadt to practice medicine and write for physiological and pathological magazines.

## Homoeopathy in Vienna
Vienna was the center of a significant movement regarding the introduction and growth of homoeopathy in Austria. While homoeopathy entered Austria around 1817, it was legally forbidden by 1819. However, the practice gained a foothold when the physician to Archduke John became a homoeopath, and the doctrine was introduced to Vienna in 1825.

To evaluate the system, Francis I ordered experiments using homoeopathic medicines in a dedicated ward of twelve beds. The results were considered satisfactory, leading to the rapid expansion of the system in Vienna. This growth was further accelerated during the cholera epidemic of 1836, following reports of brilliant successes in treatment. The movement led to the publication of journals and the founding of societies, including the Society of Homoeopathic Physicians in 1873. 

The infrastructure for homoeopathy in Vienna expanded through the founding of a second hospital in 1846 and a third in 1850, the latter of which provided clinical lectures on the system. Although the practice declined between 1873 and 1893, it persisted into the 20th century. By 1901, approximately fifty physicians and two hospitals practiced homoeopathy across thirty-seven cities and towns, with an estimated seventy-five additional practitioners in the coast provinces, Salzburg, Tirol, Bohemia, and Moravia.

## Other Scientific Contributions in Vienna
Vienna also served as a site for professional employment and publication for other scholars. Gustav Adolph Kenngott (1818–1897), a German mineralogist, was employed at the Hofmineralien Cabinet in Vienna before becoming a professor of mineralogy at the university of Zürich. 

Additionally, the city was a location for the publication of works by Karl Gustav Carus (1789–1869). Carus, a German psychologist and physiologist who spent much of his life in Dresden, published *Natur und Idee* in Vienna in 1861 and *Vergleichende Psychologie* in Vienna in 1866.
