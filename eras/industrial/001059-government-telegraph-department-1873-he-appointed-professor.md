# Government Telegraph Departments and Related Appointments

The administration of telegraph services and the appointment of professors in technical and academic fields during the late 19th century spanned various global regions, including India, Burma, and Japan.

## Telegraph Administration in Burma and Japan

In Burma, the telegraph service is managed through a structured hierarchy of officials. The administration includes a chief superintendent and four superintendents of telegraphs, as well as a deputy postmaster-general. These officials operate within a broader public service framework where commissioners of division are responsible for the working of every department, with the exception of the military department and branches under the direct control of the supreme government.

In Japan, the telegraph services were extended as part of a series of reforms carried out by Tokyo statesmen. These reforms, which included the reorganization of State departments on European lines in 1885 under Premier Ito Hirobumi, also saw the extension of postal services and the institution of a system of postal savings-banks.

## William Edward Ayrton

William Edward Ayrton (1847–1908) was an English physicist born in London. His career involved significant service in government telegraphy and academia. In 1868, he entered the service of the Indian Government Telegraph department in Bengal. 

In 1873, Ayrton was appointed professor of physics and telegraphy at the Imperial College of Engineering in Tokio. Following his return to London six years later, he held several academic positions:
* Professor of applied physics at the Finsbury College of the City and Guilds of London Technical Institute.
* Professor of electrical engineering at the Central Technical College, South Kensington, a post he was chosen for in 1884.

Ayrton was noted for his work on electrical subjects and, alongside Professor John Perry, invented a long series of electrical measuring instruments. He was assisted in his researches by his wife, Mrs. Hertha Ayrton, who became known for her scientific work on other subjects and the electric arc, receiving a Royal medal from the Royal Society in 1906.
