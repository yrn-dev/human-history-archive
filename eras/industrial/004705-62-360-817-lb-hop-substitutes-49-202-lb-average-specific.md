# Brewing and Hop Production in the United Kingdom and United States

## The United Kingdom Brewing Industry
The brewing trade in the United Kingdom reached vast proportions by the turn of the 20th century. Production peaked in 1900 at 37,090,986 barrels. Although a steady decline followed that peak, production for 1905-1906 remained at 34,109,263 barrels, a figure higher than any year prior to 1897. This growth contradicted earlier industry expectations; in 1875, it was believed that the industry—then producing roughly 25,000,000 barrels—had already reached its maximum development.

By the year ending September 30, 1905, the exchequer received £13,156,053 in beer duty. During this period, there were 5,180 brewers for sale. The industry was characterized by a few very large firms; Messrs Guinness owned the largest brewery in the world, producing over two million barrels and paying approximately one million sterling to the revenue. Three other firms each produced close to or more than one million barrels.

The consumption of beer was heavily concentrated in England, where 28,590,563 barrels were consumed in 1905-1906, compared to 3,265,084 in Ireland and 1,648,463 in Scotland. Trade figures for imports (57,194 barrels) and exports (520,826 barrels) remained almost stationary for thirty years.

## Industry Capital and Materials
The financial scale of the liquor trade grew significantly over the late 19th century. In 1871, Professor Leone Levi calculated the invested capital at £117,000,000, a figure that may have safely doubled by 1908. Specifically, the *Brewers' Almanack* for 1906 estimated that capital invested in limited liability breweries alone was £185,000,000.

The materials used in production for the 1905-1906 period included:
*   **Malt:** 51,818,697 bushels
*   **Hops:** 62,360,817 lb
*   **Sugar:** 2,746,615 cwt.
*   **Rice, flaked maize, and similar materials:** 1,348,558 cwt.
*   **Unmalted corn:** 125,671 bushels
*   **Hop substitutes:** 49,202 lb

The average specific gravity of beer produced in 1905-1906 was 1053.24. In the industry, "gravity" is expressed as 1,000 times the physicist's specific gravity. For example, a wort with a specific gravity of 1.045 is referred to as having a gravity of 1045, or 45 "degrees of gravity."

## Hop Cultivation and Substitutes
Hops have been cultivated since the 8th and 9th centuries in Germany and France, where gardens were known as "humuleta" or "humularia." The plant was introduced to England from Flanders in 1525. While cultivation was fitful and limited until the 16th century, it increased rapidly after the 17th century. In the United States, cultivation was encouraged by legislative enactments in 1657.

Historically, other plants were used to season ale, including sage (*Salvia officinalis*), sweet gale (*Myrica Gale*), "alecost" (*Balsamita vulgaris*), and "alehoof" (*Nepeta Glechoma*). Modern hop substitutes, often containing quassia, gentian, or camomile, are rarely used because they impart an unpleasantly rough and bitter taste.

In modern brewing, preservatives such as the bisulphites of potash and lime are almost universally used for draught ales and sometimes for stock ales. These preservatives shorten brewing time and replace some of the hop extract and alcohol, which is necessary because modern light beers are more lightly hopped and less alcoholic than those of previous generations.

## Hop Production and Trade
In Great Britain, approximately 70% of hops used for consumption are home-grown, while 30% are imported. Between 1897 and 1906, the annual average of imports was 186,362 cwt., with the largest proportion coming from the United States. The highest import volume occurred in 1885 (266,952 cwt.), and the lowest in 1887 (145,122 cwt.), the latter following the largest home-grown crop on record.

In the United States, the center of production shifted from New York toward the Pacific coast (Washington, California, and Oregon) during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. From 1900 to 1906, the average annual produce in the U.S. was 423,471 cwt., with 80% grown on the Pacific coast. Yields in Oregon were 9 to 10 cwt. per acre, and 12 to 14 cwt. in California and Washington, whereas New York yields did not exceed 5 or 6 cwt. per acre.

## Economics of Hop Farming
The cost of cultivating hops has risen due to higher wages, the need for intense cultivation, and the fight against fungi and insects. In 1893, the average annual cost for an acre of hop land was estimated at £35 10s., which included expenses for manure, digging, poling, picking, and rent. By 1900, the average cost per acre in Kent had risen to £37.

In Kent, a historical trend of replacing fruit land with hops has reversed; farmers are now increasingly grubbing up hop plants to plant cherry trees, plums, or apples.
