# Glazed Bricks

Glazed bricks are a specialized category of masonry characterized by a surface coating of glaze or enamel. These materials are produced through distinct technical processes and have been utilized globally for decorative, sanitary, and architectural purposes.

## Manufacturing Processes
There are two primary methods for producing glazed bricks: salt-glazing and enamelling.

*   **Salt-glazed bricks:** These bricks are brown in colour. The glazing process is achieved by throwing salt onto the bricks while they are still in the kiln.
*   **Enamelled bricks:** These are produced by dipping the bricks into a slip of a required colour before they are burnt. Enamelled bricks are employed for sanitary purposes, decorative work, and in environments where reflected light is necessary.

## Historical Development in Asia
The art of brickmaking was practiced by all civilized nations of antiquity, with the earliest known burnt bricks found in the ancient cities of Babylonia. It is probable that the method of burning blocks of dried clay to create durable bricks was discovered in this region of Asia. Well-burnt bricks were produced by the Babylonians more than 6,000 years ago and were used extensively during the time of Sargon of Akkad (c. 3800 B.C.).

The Babylonians and Assyrians achieved a high level of proficiency in the manufacture of bricks featuring coatings of enamel or coloured glaze, which were used largely for the decoration of walls. It is believed that this art of making glazed bricks traveled eastward from the west, passing through northern India and Persia before reaching China, where the craft was later highly developed. While the Great Wall of China utilized both burnt and unburnt brick, this occurred at a comparatively late period (c. 210 B.C.); there is no evidence that the Chinese possessed knowledge of burnt bricks during the era when the art flourished in Babylonia.

## Architectural Applications in Europe
Glazed bricks have been used in significant European architecture, particularly in Germany. In the city of Lübeck, the capital of the free state and former head of the Hanseatic League, glazed brick is a prominent feature of the city's ancient grandeur. The city's five chief churches are described as stately Gothic edifices constructed of glazed brick, featuring lofty spires. Additionally, the *Rathaus* (town hall) of Lübeck is constructed from red and black glazed brick and dates from various epochs of the middle ages.

## Related Ceramic Arts
The technology of glazing extends to other earthenware and pottery. In China, specimens of glazed earthenware in buff, green, and brown have been discovered in tombs dating to the Tang Dynasty (616-906). Additionally, green-glazed earthenware jars from the Han Dynasty (contemporary with the early Roman Empire and late Roman republic) are preserved in the Victoria and Albert Museum.

In England, the development of enamel glazes was advanced by Sir Henry Doulton (1820-1897), an inventor and manufacturer of pottery. Doulton's experiments led to the production of good enamel glazes, and he later superintended the manufacture of sanitary appliances and drainage pipe works in Lambeth starting in 1846.

## Comparison with Other Brick Types
Glazed bricks differ in function and properties from other specialized bricks:
*   **Blue bricks:** Chiefly made in North Wales and South Staffordshire, these are vitrified throughout, heavy, and impervious. They are used for engineering work, coping, channelling, and paving due to their high compressional resistance.
*   **Fire-bricks:** These are used in connection with furnaces because they can withstand great heat; they are laid with fire-clay rather than cement mortar or lime.
*   **Moulded bricks:** Used for plinths, labels, string courses, and cornices, these are often preferred over rubbers due to their durability and the reduction in cutting labour. Bricks shaped as voussoirs are supplied for arches and sewer work.
