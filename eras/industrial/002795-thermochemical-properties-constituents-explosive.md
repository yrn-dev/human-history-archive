# The Thermochemical Properties of the Constituents of an Explosive

## Overview of Thermochemical Properties
The thermochemical properties of the constituents within an explosive serve to assign an upper limit to the pressure, temperature, and volume of the gas that is produced during combustion. While these properties provide a theoretical limit, the determination of these factors requires extensive additional experimentation. Sir Andrew Noble published some of his findings regarding these results in the *Phil. Trans.* during 1905-1906 and the years following.

## Cordite and Stability Testing
Cordite is an explosive adopted for use in the British service. It is named for its appearance as short lengths of cord, a form achieved by squeezing the composition through a die hole while in a viscous state. The size of the cordite is designated by the diameter of the hole in hundredths of an inch; for example, cordite size 30 is produced using a hole with a diameter of 0.30 inches.

To examine the stability of explosives such as cordite, a method known as the vacuum silvered vessel process is utilized. This process involves heating 50 grams of finely divided explosive within a Dewar's silvered vacuum glass bulb. The material is held at a constant temperature of 80 degrees C for several hours. A sensitive thermometer is placed in the center of the cordite to monitor for any temperature rise above 80 degrees C. Such a rise, accompanied by the evolution of nitrogen dioxide ($\text{NO}_2$)—which produces red fumes visible through a side tube—indicates decomposition or internal oxidation. Because explosives of this class will eventually decompose, specific time limits have been established to determine relative instability or stability based on the production of fumes and the rise in temperature.

## Chemical Properties of Explosive Acids and Salts
Certain chemical substances and their derivatives exhibit extreme explosive properties. For instance, a pure acid obtained through the reaction of hippuric acid with hydrazine hydrate (forming hippuryl hydrazine), followed by conversion into diazo-hippuramide via nitrous acid and subsequent hydrolysis with caustic alkalis, results in salts of hydrazoic acid. 

The free acid is a colorless liquid with a very unpleasant smell that boils at 30 degrees C and is extremely explosive. This acid is soluble in water and can dissolve metals such as iron and zinc, which liberates hydrogen and forms salts known as hydrazoates, azides, or azoimides. All of these salts are explosive and interact readily with alkyl iodides. These metallic salts crystallize in an anhydrous condition and, upon heating, decompose to leave a residue of the pure metal.

## Related Explosive Materials and Applications
The archive notes various other explosive applications and materials:
*   **Bombs:** Historically, the term "bomb" referred to an explosive shell fired by artillery, derived from the Greek *bombos* (a buzzing or hammering noise). In modern contexts, it refers to incendiary or shattering grenades, or explosive vessels triggered by trip mechanisms or clockwork to destroy property or life. In naval history, before shell guns were introduced, "bomb-vessels" (also called bombs or bombards) were used to carry explosive projectiles.
*   **Mercuric Fulminate:** This substance has been applied as a detonator.
*   **Petroleum Constituents:** While not an explosive in the same sense as cordite, the volatile constituents of petroleum, such as benzene and naphtha, must be removed via distillation to prevent premature ignition and the subsequent danger of explosion.
