# Legal and Institutional Prohibitions and Modifications in the 19th Century

The early 19th century saw significant shifts in the legal frameworks governing financial institutions, public gambling, and social organizations. Various prohibitions that had been in place for decades were modified or removed to accommodate economic growth, public opinion, and changing institutional relations.

## Banking and Limited Liability
The establishment of joint-stock banks in England was heavily restricted by acts from 1694, 1697, and 1708. These laws ensured that no joint-stock bank could be established during the continuance of the Bank of England and prohibited any partnership exceeding six persons from borrowing or taking up money on bills or notes payable on demand (or in less than six months). These restrictions were confirmed by the act of 1800.

A significant modification occurred in 1826. One act from that year prohibited bank notes under £5, while a second Banking Act allowed the establishment of co-partnerships of more than six persons—which were necessarily joint-stock companies—provided they were located beyond 65 miles from London. Further changes followed in 1833, when the establishment of joint-stock banks was permitted within the 65-mile limit, and restrictions on notes for less than £50 were removed. However, joint-stock banks within the 65-mile radius were still not allowed to issue notes.

Despite these changes, the privilege of limited liability remained exclusive to the Bank of England until the act of 1862. Prior to this, every shareholder in a joint-stock bank was liable for the whole of his means. This lack of limited liability deterred many wealthy individuals from becoming partners. The risks of unlimited liability resulted in great hardship for many when the Western Bank of Scotland failed in 1858 and the City of Glasgow Bank failed in 1878. The latter failure led to the Companies Act of 1879, which allowed unlimited companies to adopt limited liability. Under this system, shareholders who paid the nominal amount of their holding are not liable for further amounts, unless the company issues bank notes, in which case they remain liable as if the company were unlimited.

## Government Lotteries
From 1709 to 1824, the English government raised substantial sums through lotteries authorized by acts of parliament, despite a 1698 prohibition on lotteries as "common nuisances" that had ruined servants, children, and other unwary persons. Between 1793 and 1824, the government earned an average yearly profit of £346,765.

These lotteries operated via contractors who bought £10 tickets at a premium of approximately 40% and resold them in retail, sometimes in one-sixteenth parts, through "morocco men" who traveled with red leather books. To combat a "pernicious system" of insuring tickets during the forty-day drawing period, the government passed the Little Go Act of 1802 and Perceval's Act in 1806, the latter of which limited the drawing of each lottery to a single day. While initially used for general loans, lotteries were later restricted to specific objects, such as the purchase of a picture gallery, the disposal of a museum, or the improvement of London. Following a shift in public opinion led by Lord Lyttleton and others, lotteries were finally prohibited in 1826.

## Religious and Academic Restrictions
In the realm of religious distribution, the British and Foreign Bible Society maintained relations with various continental societies. Up to 1816-1817, these societies printed 436,000 copies of the Scriptures. However, a decision made by the British and Foreign Bible Society in 1826 regarding the circulation of the Apocrypha modified its relations with the most influential of these continental societies. While some societies were suppressed or dissolved due to ecclesiastical or political opposition—particularly from the Roman Church—many continued to flourish. By 1905, German Bible Societies (including those in Berlin, Stuttgart, Eberfeld, Dresden, Nuremberg, and Halle) and societies in Switzerland, the Netherlands, Denmark, Norway, and Sweden continued to circulate hundreds of thousands of copies of the Scriptures.

In academia, the German psychologist Friedrich Eduard Beneke faced professional prohibitions. In 1822, his lectures at Berlin were prohibited, which Beneke believed was due to the influence of Hegel on Prussian authorities. This influence also prevented him from securing a chair from the Saxon government, leading him to retire to Gottingen for several years before he was eventually allowed to return to Berlin.
