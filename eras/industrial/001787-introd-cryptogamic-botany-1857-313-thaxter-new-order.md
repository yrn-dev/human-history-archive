# Cryptogamic Botany and Related Biological Studies

Cryptogamic botany involves the study of plants and organisms that reproduce via spores rather than seeds, including ferns, horsetails, and various bacteria. This field encompasses a wide range of organisms, from vascular cryptogams to specialized bacterial groups.

## Vascular Cryptogams: The Horsetail
The genus *Equisetum*, commonly known as the horsetail, is the sole genus within the botanical natural order Equisetaceae. These are vascular cryptogamous plants characterized by a vegetative structure that resembles the flowering plants *Ephedra* and *Casuarina*. Horsetails are herbaceous plants that grow from a much-branched underground rootstock. From this rootstock, green, ribbed aerial shoots emerge, featuring a whorl of leaves reduced to a toothed sheath at each node.

The genus consists of 24 known species distributed universally. These plants produce both sterile and fertile shoots. Fertile shoots bear a fructification at the apex—a cylindrical, oblong, oval, or dense conical spike. This spike is composed of shortly-stalked peltate scales, with a circle of spore-cases (sporangia) attached to the under surface of each scale. These sporangia open via a longitudinal slit on the inner side. 

The spores of *Equisetum* differ from those of ferns because their outer coat, the exospore, is split into four hygroscopic club-shaped threads called elaters. These elaters straighten when dry and curl when moist. In most species, fertile and sterile shoots appear similar; however, in *E. arvense*, the fertile shoots are unbranched, not green, and appear first in the spring. Because any broken portion of the underground rhizome can produce a new plant, horsetails are difficult to eradicate.

## Myxobacteriaceae and Bacterial Divisions
Bacteria are categorized into several divisions, including Haplobacterinae, Trichobacterinae, and Myxobacterinae. The group Myxobacteriaceae was founded by Thaxter in 1892, who rediscovered *Chondromyces crocatus*. Although Berkeley had described *C. crocatus* as early as 1857, it was originally misclassified as belonging to the Hyphomycetes. Another form described by Thaxter as *Myxobacter* was later identified as *Polyangium vitellinum*, a form described by Link in 1795.

The Myxobacterinae are distinguished by their ability to form plasmodium-like aggregations and build sporogenous structures of a definite form, which superficially resemble the cysts of the Mycetozoa. These bacteria are frequently found in the air and the alimentary canals of herbivorous animals, as well as growing on the dung of such animals. They are most effectively obtained by keeping dung that has been exposed to air for at least eight days at a temperature of 30-35°C in the dark. This high temperature promotes bacterial growth while remaining inimical to the fungi common on that substratum.

## Notable Botanists and Contributions
Several figures have contributed significantly to the documentation and study of botany and cryptogams. Sir William Jackson Hooker (1785-1865), an English botanist, produced numerous works including *British Jungermanniae* (1816) and *Muscologia Britannica* (1818), the latter a comprehensive account of the mosses of Ireland and Great Britain prepared with Dr. T. Taylor. His extensive bibliography includes *British Flora Cryptogamia* (1833), *Species filicum* (1846-1864), which is considered the standard work on the subject, and *The British Ferns* (1861-1862).

John Ball (1818-1889), an Irish naturalist and politician, contributed papers on botany and Swiss glaciers to scientific periodicals. In his capacity as under-secretary for the colonies, Ball used his influence to further natural science, specifically supporting Sir W. Hooker's efforts to obtain systematic knowledge of colonial floras and assisting with the equipment of the Palliser expedition in Canada.

## General Botanical Distribution
In rainy regions, herbaceous vegetation includes ferns and other Cryptogams, alongside the Orchidaceae, Scitamineae, Cyperaceae, and Orontiaceae. Some of these forms, including various Cyrtandraceae, Scitamineae, epiphytical orchids, *Osbeckia*, and *Begonia*, extend beyond the tropics and ascend into the temperate zones of mountains.
