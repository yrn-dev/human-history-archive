# BAND

The term "band" refers generally to something that binds or fastens one object to another, such as a tie, rope, or cord. Etymologically, the word is a variant of "bond" and is derived from the Teutonic stem *bindan*, meaning to bind.

## Linguistic Origins and Variations
The word "band" has several related forms and origins depending on its application. The term "bend" shares the same source as *bindan* and properly refers to the act of fastening a string to a bow to curve and constrain it. When "band" is used to describe a flat strip of material for fastening, it entered the English language directly from the French *bande*.

In a different context, the word "bond" is a phonetic variant of "band" derived from the same Teutonic root. However, the archive notes a distinction between this and an obsolete meaning of "bond" (Old English *bonda*), which originally referred to a householder. Over time, due to an early confusion with the "bond" meaning "to bind," the term *bonda* transitioned in meaning toward serfdom and slavery. This confusion also affected the word "bondage," which properly meant "tenure in villeinage" but became synonymous with slavery.

## Applications in Bookbinding
In the craft of bookbinding, bands are used to fasten sheets to the back of a book. In medieval "half-binding," quires were sewn together, creating projecting bands at the back. To protect these, strips of leather were fastened over the back. Because vellum leaves tended to curl, wooden boards were added to each side; the loose ends of the bands were fastened to these boards, which then hinged upon them. As the craft evolved into "whole" binding, the leather strip expanded to cover the entire board, providing a surface for artistic decoration.

In the 19th century, the binder Charles Lewis utilized bands at the back of his bindings that were consistently broad, flat, gold-tooled, and often double.

Modern commercial machine-binding differs from hand-sewing. While hand-sewn books have band ends drawn through and strongly fastened to the substance of the boards, machines typically only paste the loose ends of the sewing tapes to the inner surfaces of the boards. Consequently, "cased" books are more likely to slip out of their covers once the paste perishes.

## Architecture and Clothing
In architecture, a band is a type of fascia or flat frieze that runs horizontally around parts of a building or a tower. This is particularly common in the base tables of perpendicular work used with long shafts in the 13th century. These bands generally feature bold projecting mouldings above and below and may be carved with quatrefoils, cusped circles, or shields of arms, and sometimes foliages.

In dress, "bands" refer to two small strips of white linen worn at the neck as part of academic, clerical, and legal attire. These are survivals of 17th-century falling collars. While usually white, the secular clergy of the Roman Church wear black bands with white edges. The "band-box," originally used to carry these neck-bands, is now a term for light chip or cardboard boxes used for millinery.

## Groups and Organizations
The use of "band" to describe a troop or company is likely connected to *bindan*. This meaning originated in Romanic languages (such as Spanish, Portuguese, and Italian *banda*) before entering Teutonic languages. It may have derived from a sash of a specific color worn by soldiers as a distinctive mark, or from the medieval Latin *bandum* (banner), referring to a strip of cloth fastened to a pole. This application is most commonly seen today in "military bands," referring to companies of musicians in the navies or armies.

## Technical and Other Uses
In seamanship and industry, a "bend" (from the same root as "bind") is a method of fastening ropes together or to spars, such as the Carrick bend or the Fisherman's Bend. Unlike knots or seizings, which are intended to be permanent, a bend can be undone quickly by pulling the ropes in the reverse direction of the strain.

In early gunpowder artillery, primitive machine-guns were created by the binding or grouping of several small-calibre guns to allow for rapid succession of shots or volleys.

Finally, the concept of binding is found in the origin of the word "religion," from the Latin *religare*, meaning to bind. This refers to the complex growth of beliefs and traditions that began to bind humans together mentally and emotionally in common action.
