# George Boole and the Philosophical Transactions of 1844

George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician born in Lincoln on November 2, 1815. The son of a studious tradesman who provided his first lessons in mathematical science, Boole did not show extraordinary mathematical powers early in life, initially favoring classics. He began studying higher mathematics at age seventeen, though his progress was hindered by a lack of efficient help. Throughout his life, Boole remained in the scholastic profession, serving as an assistant-master at a private school in Doncaster at age sixteen, establishing his own school in Lincoln (later moved to Waddington), and eventually becoming the professor of mathematics at Queen's College in Cork in 1849.

## Contributions to Mathematical Analysis
Boole is recognized as one of the most eminent mathematicians to perceive that symbols of operation could be treated as distinct objects of calculation, separate from symbols of quantity. He possessed an unrivalled power in tracing results based on the primary laws and conditions of symbols. 

A pivotal moment in his career occurred in 1844, when he published a famous memoir in the *Philosophical Transactions*. This memoir described a general method in analysis, for which Boole received a medal from the Royal Society. This work laid the foundation for his later discoveries and is discussed in the 16th and 17th chapters of his *Treatise on Differential Equations* (1859), where he provides a lucid account of the general symbolic method.

## Logical Reform and the Laws of Thought
Alongside contemporaries such as Sir W. R. Hamilton and Augustus de Morgan, Boole was an independent discoverer of the principle of the quantification of the predicate. While De Morgan was a logical reformer who stressed logical training, Boole applied his confidence in symbolic reasoning to create a novel logical method.

In his work *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, on which are founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities* (1854), Boole detailed a general symbolic method of logical inference. He demonstrated how to draw conclusions logically contained within premises through purely symbolic treatment. He also attempted to discover a general method in probabilities to determine the probability of an event logically connected to a system of given events.

Boole did not view logic as a branch of mathematics, but rather identified a deep analogy between algebraic symbols and those representing logical forms. He proposed that logic is essentially mathematics restricted to the quantities 0 and 1. In this system, unity (1) represents the universe of thinkable objects, while literal symbols (such as x, y, z) act as elective symbols. For example, if x represents "horned" and y represents "sheep," the combination of x and y represents "horned sheep." Boole showed that these elective symbols follow the same primary laws of combination as algebra, allowing them to be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided.

## Published Works and Academic Reach
Boole's output consisted of approximately fifty scattered articles and several separate publications. His earliest paper, "Theory of Analytical Transformations," appeared in the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* in 1839, leading to a friendship with the journal's editor, D.F. Gregory, which lasted until Gregory's death in 1844. 

His publications include:
* **Treatises:** The *Treatise on Differential Equations* (1859) and the *Treatise on the Calculus of Finite Differences* (1860).
* **Logic:** The pamphlet *Mathematical Analysis of Logic* (1847), which Boole later considered a hasty and imperfect exposition, and the mature *Laws of Thought* (1854).
* **Articles:** Twenty-two articles in the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* and its successor, sixteen papers in the *Philosophical Magazine*, and six important memoirs in the *Philosophical Transactions* of the Royal Society.
* **Other Journals:** Contributions to the *Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh*, the *Transactions of the Royal Irish Academy*, *Crelle's Journal*, the *Mechanic's Magazine* (1848), and the *Bulletin de l'Academie de St-Petersbourg* (1862, published under the name G. Boldt).

## Personal Life and Interests
Boole was known for his modesty and a single-minded pursuit of truth. Despite his discoveries, he did not seek the ordinary rewards associated with such work, though he did receive an honorary LL.D. from the University of Dublin. He married Miss Mary Everest in 1855; she later wrote educational works based on his principles.

Beyond mathematics and logic, Boole possessed a deep acquaintance with general literature. He studied the ethics of Spinoza, the metaphysics of Aristotle, and the philosophical works of Cicero. His favorite poet was Dante, specifically preferring the *Paradiso* over the *Inferno*. He also delivered four printed addresses on the social and scientific aspects of intellectual culture, including *The Genius of Sir Isaac Newton* and *The Claims of Science*.

George Boole died on December 8, 1864, following an attack of fever that resulted in suffusion on the lungs.
