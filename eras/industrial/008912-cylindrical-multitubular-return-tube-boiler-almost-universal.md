# Cylindrical Multitubular Return Tube Boiler

The cylindrical multitubular return tube boiler is a type of steam generator in almost universal use within merchant steamers. These boilers are manufactured in various sizes, reaching diameters of up to 17 feet. While the usual working pressure for these units ranges from 160 to 200 lb per sq. in., some specific cases utilize pressures as high as 265 lb. per sq. in.

## Types and Configurations
These boilers are categorized into two primary types: single-ended and double-ended.

**Single-ended Boilers**
The single-ended variety is the most generally used. In this configuration, furnaces are fitted at only one end of the boiler. The number of furnaces varies depending on the size of the boiler, ranging from one in the smallest units to four in the largest, although three furnaces are the most common practice. 

The arrangement of combustion chambers in single-ended boilers varies:
* Each furnace generally possesses its own separate combustion chamber.
* In boilers with four furnaces, a single chamber may be shared by the two middle furnaces, or a single chamber may be fitted to each pair of side furnaces.

**Double-ended Boilers**
In double-ended boilers, furnaces are fitted at both ends. While some of these boilers utilize separate combustion chambers for each furnace, it is more common for one chamber to serve two furnaces, with one furnace located at each end of the boiler.

## Furnace Design and Construction
The design of the furnaces depends on the size of the boiler and the required working pressure. Smaller boilers often employ plain cylindrical furnaces, with plate thicknesses ranging from 3/8 in. to 3/4 in. 

For larger furnaces or those operating under high pressures, corrugated or ribbed furnaces are typically adopted because they are stronger and more resistant to collapse than plain furnaces of the same thickness. Some specific types of corrugated sections include the Morison, Fox, and Deighton types; these are made from plates originally rolled to a uniform thickness, formed into a cylinder with a welded longitudinal joint, and then corrugated. The primary difference between these three is the shape of the corrugations. Other types utilize plates rolled with thickened portions or ribs at distances of 9 inches. Additionally, "Adamson" joints, similar to those found in Lancashire boilers, are occasionally employed.

## General Boiler Classifications
The cylindrical multitubular return tube boiler falls under the broader category of "tank" boilers. Boilers are generally divided into two distinct classes:
1. **Tank Boilers:** These contain relatively large quantities of water. Their outer shells are wholly or partially cylindrical to obtain necessary strength without requiring a large number of stays. They are generally internally fired, with furnace plates surrounded by water. Products of combustion leave the furnace and pass into a chamber, then through flues or numerous small tubes to transmit heat to the water.
2. **Water-tube Boilers:** In these, water is mainly contained in numerous small tubes, and the fire is usually placed under these tubes.

## Maintenance and Water Quality
The efficiency and safety of boilers are heavily influenced by the quality of the feed-water. Impurities such as lime or magnesia salts do not evaporate with the steam and instead concentrate in the boiler water, precipitating as mud or scale on heating surfaces. 

Scale is particularly dangerous as it obstructs heat transmission, which can lead to plates becoming overheated and weakened. While mud can be removed via blow-off cocks, scale requires periodical cleaning. Because thorough cleaning is more difficult in multitubular or water-tube boilers than in types like the Lancashire, Galloway, or Cornish boilers, impure feed-water is often treated using a "softener." This process involves mixing the water with chemical reagents and raising the temperature to precipitate impurities before the water is filtered and pumped into the boiler.
