# Lord Stanley and the Election of 1830

## Political Ascent and the 1830 Appointment
In November 1830, the arrival of Lord Grey to power provided Stanley with his first opportunity to demonstrate his capacity for responsible office. He was appointed to the position of chief secretary of Ireland. This role offered him significant scope to utilize both his debating skills and his administrative abilities. During his tenure as Irish secretary, Stanley was instrumental in passing the first national education act for Ireland, which established a board composed of Presbyterians, Episcopalians, and Catholics to harmoniously administer an education scheme. Additionally, he was chiefly responsible for the Irish Church Temporalities Act, a measure that abolished eight bishoprics and two archbishoprics to remedy abuses regarding church revenues. Although the bill was not introduced into parliament until after he had left the Irish secretaryship, Stanley and other cabinet members had strongly opposed a clause that would have allowed surplus revenues to be used for non-ecclesiastical purposes, leading to its withdrawal before the bill reached the Lords.

## The Preston Contest of 1830
Upon accepting his official appointment in 1830, Stanley was required to vacate his seat for Preston and seek re-election. This resulted in a particularly keen contest in which Stanley suffered the mortification of being defeated by Hunt, who was described as a Radical "orator." The election turned specifically on the question of the ballot, a measure that Stanley refused to support. 

Stanley's connection to Preston had begun at the general election of 1826, when he renounced his connection with Stockbridge to become the representative of the borough. In Preston, the influence of Derby was paramount. This shift in representation was advantageous for Stanley, as it allowed him to speak with force against the system of rotten boroughs during the Reform Bill debates without being accused of personal inconsistency. This was particularly relevant given that, according to Gay, cobblers in the place he previously represented used to "feast three years upon one vote."

## Subsequent Parliamentary Career
Following his defeat in Preston, Stanley re-entered the House of Commons as one of the members for Windsor, after Sir Hussey Vivian resigned in his favour. He changed his seat once more in 1832, when he was returned for North Lancashire.

Stanley's early official life began in 1827 as under-secretary for the colonies, following a coalition made by several distinguished Whigs with Canning. This coalition ended with Canning's death in August of that year. While Lord Goderich succeeded as premier, he resigned after a few months without ever being truly in power. During the subsequent administration of the duke of Wellington from 1828 to 1830, Stanley and his associates remained in opposition. During this period, he exhibited a robust and assertive Liberalism that appeared curious to later generations who viewed him as the embodiment of Conservatism.

## Transition to the Conservative Party
By the end of 1834, Stanley—now styled as such by courtesy because his father had succeeded to the earldom in October—was invited by Sir Robert Peel to join a short-lived Conservative ministry formed after Lord Melbourne's resignation. Although he declined the offer for reasons detailed in a letter in the Peel memoirs, he acted with the Conservative party from that date forward. 

In 1841, upon the party's next accession to power, he accepted the office of colonial secretary, a post he had previously held under Lord Grey. Despite his party affiliation, Stanley's temperament and position made him a thoroughly independent supporter. In 1844, due to health issues caused by late hours in the Commons, he sought elevation to the Upper House in the right of his father's barony. Sir Robert Peel acceded to this request, which strengthened the Conservative debating power in the Upper House and freed Peel from the effects of Stanley's "candid friendship" in the House of Commons.

## Conflict over Free Trade
A definitive breach occurred between Lord Stanley and Sir Robert Peel in 1846 when Peel accepted the policy of free trade. The split was instant and irreparable. Stanley established himself as the uncompromising opponent of free trade and became the recognized leader of the Protectionist party. In the Commons, he was supported by lieutenants Disraeli and Lord George Bentinck. Despite their efforts, the logic of events worked against them, and Protection remained only a watchword for their cause.
