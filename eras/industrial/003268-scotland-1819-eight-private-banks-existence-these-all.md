# Banking in Scotland

## Early Development and Institutional Growth
The early history of Scottish banking was marked by the establishment of major chartered institutions and a period of experimentation with branch banking. The Bank of Scotland began issuing notes—then referred to as tickets or bills—in denominations of £100, £50, £20, £10, and £5, later introducing £1 notes in 1704. Around the year 1700, the Bank of Scotland attempted to establish branches in Montrose, Dundee, Aberdeen, and Glasgow, but these were closed because expenses exceeded profits. A second attempt to establish agencies in Dundee, Aberdeen, and Glasgow in 1731 lasted only two years before being discontinued; branches were not successfully re-established until 1774.

The Royal Bank of Scotland was established in 1727 via a charter of incorporation, which provided the institution with a common seal and perpetual succession. A significant rivalry developed between the Royal Bank and the Bank of Scotland. Additionally, the British Linen Company was incorporated in 1746; while originally intended for linen manufacture, by 1763 the company shifted its operations to focus on banking transactions.

## Note Circulation and Deposit Habits
Note circulation was a primary component of Scottish banking. For example, the Bank of Dundee operated from 1764 to 1792 exclusively through the issue of notes, with circulation values ranging between £23,000 and £56,000. It was not until 1792 that the Bank of Dundee began receiving money from the public. By the time the Bank of Dundee amalgamated with the Royal Bank of Scotland in 1864, its note circulation was £41,000 and its deposits totaled approximately £700,000.

While deposits in Scottish banks increased rapidly after 1792, the practice of hoarding savings in chests in amounts of £10 or £20 persisted for a significantly longer period.

## Private and Joint-Stock Banking
Private banking did not maintain a considerable hold in Scotland. In 1819, there were eight private banks in existence, all of which had disappeared by 1844. The banking landscape later shifted toward larger entities. By 1906, the vast majority of the country's banking business was carried out by ten banks of issue, supplemented by two small, more recently established banks. These ten banks operated 1,180 branches by 1906.

The sector also experienced significant failures. The Western Bank of Scotland failed in 1858, and the City of Glasgow Bank failed in 1878. The latter event led to the Companies Act of 1879, which allowed unlimited companies to adopt limited liability. Prior to the act of 1862, shareholders in joint-stock banks were liable for the full extent of their means, which caused great hardship during the aforementioned bank failures.

## Regulatory Framework and Circulation
The regulatory environment in Scotland differed from that in England. Under the acts of 1844-1845, Sir Robert Peel implemented a policy allowing Scottish and Irish banks to exceed their authorized issues, provided they held specie equal to the excess. Furthermore, no restrictions were placed on bank amalgamations in Scotland. Unlike in England, notes for amounts less than £5 continued to be permitted in Scotland. This regulatory flexibility supported the development of a large proportion of local branches.

In terms of circulation and infrastructure:
*   **1844:** The combined circulation of chartered, private, and joint-stock banks in Scotland was £2,987,665. There were 368 banking offices, with one office for every 7,120 inhabitants.
*   **1906:** The average circulation in Scotland had risen to £7,477,000. The number of banking offices increased to 1,180, resulting in one office for every 3,790 inhabitants.
