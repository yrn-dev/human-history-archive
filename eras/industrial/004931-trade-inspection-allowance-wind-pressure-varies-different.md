# Wind Pressure and Atmospheric Pressure

## Wind Pressure in Structural Design
Wind action has received significant attention following the 1879 disaster of the Tay bridge. In the design of structures, the allowance for wind pressure varies based on the exposure of the site and the size of the surfaces involved. In England, the maximum wind pressure is considered to be 56 lb per sq. ft., a figure applied to the most exposed positions or sea fronts. However, for most cases, an allowance of 40 lb is sufficient, and in areas protected by buildings or surrounding trees, only 28 lb per sq. ft. is required.

Observations using anemometers show that pressures of 30 lb per sq. ft. occur annually in many localities during storms. In exceptionally exposed positions, such as Bidstone, Liverpool, pressures of 80 lb per sq. ft. have been recorded. During the 1896 tornado in St Louis, calculations based on the stability of overturned structures suggest pressures reached between 45 and 90 lb per sq. ft.

There is a difference of opinion regarding whether average pressure on large surfaces is as great as on small anemometer plates. Experiments conducted by Sir B. Baker at the Forth bridge indicated that the intensity of pressure on a surface measuring 30 ft. × 15 ft. was less than that on a similarly exposed anemometer plate. Additionally, in bridge construction, some surfaces may partially shield others, such as one girder shielding the girder behind it.

In 1881, a Board of Trade committee decided that 56 lb per sq. ft. should be the assumed maximum wind pressure on vertical surfaces in Great Britain for structural design. For braced girder bridges, wind pressure is calculated as acting on a continuous surface from the rails to the top of the carriages, plus the vertical projected area of any girder exposed below the rails or above the train. For plate girder bridges shorter than the train, the wind is assumed to act on the projected area of one girder and the exposed part of the train. A scale is also used to make an allowance for pressure on the leeward girder.

## Atmospheric Pressure and Wind Generation
Atmospheric pressure is measured using an instrument called a barometer. Various types exist, including the mercurial barometer (a tube approximately 36 in. long closed at the top), the "cistern barometer," and the "siphon barometer." The "aneroid" barometer uses an exhausted metallic chamber rather than liquid, while "baroscopes" indicate variations without quantitative data and "barographs" automatically record pressure changes.

Periodical changes in pressure result in winds, which generally blow from areas of greatest pressure to areas of least pressure. These are modified by the earth's west-to-east revolution on its axis. For example, the south-west monsoon occurs when atmospheric pressure over Asia diminishes due to rising temperatures in April and May, while pressure remains relatively higher over the equator. As barometric pressure is restored and equalized after the October equinox, these winds are succeeded by the north-east monsoon, driven by relatively greater atmospheric pressure over Asia during winter.

The "Horse Latitudes" are belts of calms and variable breezes located at the polar edges of the S.E. and N.E. trades. This name is commonly applied to the permanent belt of high atmospheric pressure encircling the globe between 30 and 35 degrees from the equator.

## Wind Pressure in Musical Instruments
In the production of sound from a horn, wind-pressure must be maintained in a specific ratio with lip-tension. The performer blows into a funnel-shaped mouthpiece, and the vibrating lips communicate pulsations to a stream of air known as the exciting current. 

The relationship between wind-pressure and lip-tension affects the resulting sound:
* **Pitch and Frequency:** If the aperture between the lips is narrowed (increasing tension) without a proportional increase in wind-pressure, the intensity of the tone is reduced or the intonation suffers because the force is insufficient to give the vibrations the required amplitude and frequency.
* **Loudness and Amplitude:** If the wind-pressure is excessive—more than the maximum required for the increased frequency—the superfluous energy increases the amplitude of the vibrations, producing a note of higher pitch and greater loudness.
* **Harmonics:** Victor Mahillon attributes harmonics to increased wind-pressure alone. D. J. Blaikley suggests the ratio of increase in breath and lips follows the harmonic series. 

A parallel is drawn with organ-pipes: if the mouthpiece is fixed proportional to the tube length and even wind-pressure is maintained, the pipe produces one note of unvarying dynamic intensity.
