# Boiler and Plating Shop Practices

The boiler and plating shops are characterized by the use of similar materials and methods to those found in bridge and girder work, primarily utilizing iron and steel. While these departments share commonalities, they diverge in specific applications: boiler work involves bending plates into cylindrical shapes for furnaces and shells and employs welding more frequently, particularly for stand pipes and furnace flues. Conversely, bridge and girder work rarely utilizes flanging and typically involves drilling or punching plates and bars individually rather than through the seams of adjacent plates.

## Materials and Structural Requirements
The transition from malleable iron to mild steel has significantly influenced shop practice. While iron is less susceptible to irregular heating and overheating, steel offers higher ductility. However, steel is more prone to cracking if treated improperly. 

Because boilers are subjected to high pressures and temperatures, they must be constructed to allow for freedom of movement and elasticity to prevent grooving or fractures. This is achieved through the selection of steel or iron with high percentage elongation, the use of judicious staying, the limitation of tube lengths, and the imposition of hot and cold bending or temper tests. In land and marine boilers, plates and stays are almost always mild steel. Shell plates and long stays typically possess a tensile strength of 28 to 32 tons per sq. in., while flanged plates, furnaces, flues, and short-screwed stays use a softer steel with a strength of 26 to 30 tons per sq. in. 

## Riveting and Drilling
A defining feature of boiler and plating shops is the enormous volume of rivets that must be inserted and the corresponding number of rivet holes that must be created. To increase efficiency, machines are designed to produce many holes simultaneously rather than drilling them singly. These machines are specialized; some are designed for shells, others for furnaces, and some are specific to one type of boiler. Modern drilling machines can be adjusted to an exact pitch, removing the need to mark out holes as guides.

While hand-riveting was once common, it has been largely replaced by pneumatic and hydraulic machines, which provide more uniform and trustworthy results at a lower cost. In boiler shops, these machines are generally of a fixed type, whereas bridge and girder shops use portable machines slung from chains and powered by compressed air or pressure water via flexible pipes.

## Flanging and Welding
Flanging—the bending or turning over of plate edges to allow union with other plates—is a major component of boilermaking. It is used for tube-plates, end-plates, furnace flues, the crowns of vertical boilers, and the fire-boxes of locomotive boilers. This method replaced the older system of using angle iron rings, which lacked elasticity and were a source of explosions and grooving. Flanges with a large radius provide the necessary elasticity to withstand temperature changes.

Historically, flanging was performed by a skilled anglesmith who heated and hammered the metal around an iron block in successive "heats." In modern shops, hydraulic presses with massive dies can bend entire flanges for plates 4 to 8 feet in diameter in a single squeeze. Special machines are used for Adamson seams, where a flue is rotated on a table and the flange is turned over by revolving rollers within a minute. Machine-flanging is preferred not only for time savings but because it causes less injury to the material.

Welding is also prevalent, particularly for the edges of plates. Furnace flues and conical cross-tubes are commonly welded. In large shops, steam-driven power hammers are used to close welds, though much of this remains hand work performed in successive heats. "Gluts" or insertion pieces are generally preferred over overlapping joints.

## Specialization and Standardization
The industry has moved toward specialized manufacture, with firms restricting their product range to increase volume. The growth of machine tools and the diminution of hand work mean that single shops no longer produce multiple classes of boilers alongside bridge work. 

Repetitive operations are now managed through a system of templeting. For large production runs, every plate, stay, flange, or hole is made identical. Dimensions are marked from a sample templet plate, or in some cases, the self-acting mechanism of specialized drilling machines pitches and drills the plates without any marking. Similarly, bracing bars for girders are cut and punched from a templet bar to ensure they fit without further adjustment.

## Boiler Design and Components
Different boiler types employ various structural solutions:
*   **Locomotive Boilers:** These feature a cylindrical barrel and a nearly rectangular fire-box. The fire-box sides are stayed to the shell by stays approximately 1 inch in diameter, pitched 4 inches apart. The crown may be supported by vertical stays or girder stays.
*   **Marine Boilers:** These may use different end-connections. The "Gourlay back end" features a furnace contracted into an oval conical form and flanged outwards, allowing the furnace to be replaced without disturbing the end plates, though it requires more longitudinal stays for stiffness.
*   **Reed Boilers:** These are characterized by tubes fitted with screw and nut connections at each end for quick replacement, rather than being expanded into the tube plate.
*   **Staying and Tubes:** Combustion chambers are stayed using screw stays, strong girders, or vertical stays attached to T bars. Tube plates are strengthened by stay tubes. Depending on the draught used, tubes vary in diameter (typically 2-1/2 to 3-1/4 inches) and length (7 to 8-1/2 feet), usually maintaining a clear space of 1-1/4 inches between them.
