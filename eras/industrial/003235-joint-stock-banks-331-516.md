# Joint-Stock Banks

Joint-stock banks are banking institutions organized as joint-stock companies, distinguishing them from private banks. Their development in England and Wales was characterized by a long period of legal restriction followed by a gradual transition toward larger, more powerful corporate structures during the 19th century.

## Legal Evolution and Establishment
The early legal framework in England heavily favored the Bank of England, which was incorporated by the act of 1694. Subsequent legislation in 1697 and 1708 prohibited the establishment of joint-stock banks within England during the continuance of the Bank of England. The act of 1708 specifically forbade any company or partnership exceeding six persons from borrowing or taking up money on bills or notes payable on demand or in less than six months. This restriction was confirmed by the act of 1800.

Significant changes began with the acts of 1826, which prohibited bank notes under £5 and allowed the establishment of joint-stock co-partnerships beyond 65 miles from London. In 1833, the law was further modified to allow joint-stock banks to be established within the 65-mile limit, although they were still prohibited from issuing notes within that radius.

## Liability and Corporate Structure
Until the act of 1862, joint-stock banks did not possess the privilege of limited liability, which was reserved solely for the Bank of England. Consequently, every shareholder in a joint-stock bank was liable for the whole of his means. This lack of protection deterred many wealthy individuals from becoming partners and led to severe hardships during bank failures, such as those of the Western Bank of Scotland in 1858 and the City of Glasgow Bank in 1878.

The failure of the City of Glasgow Bank prompted the Companies Act of 1879, which allowed unlimited companies to adopt limited liability. Under this act, a shareholder who paid the nominal amount of his holding was no longer liable for further amounts, unless the company issued bank notes, in which case the shareholders remained liable as if the company were unlimited. Most joint-stock banks in the United Kingdom utilized these facilities, except those already limited by special act or charter.

## Note Issuance and Circulation
Historically, the power to issue notes was essential for the existence of banks outside of London. Under the Bank Charter Acts of 1844 and 1845, the Bank of England was granted an absolute monopoly on note issuance within London and a 3-mile radius. Between 3 and 65 miles, the right was concurrent for banks of six or fewer persons established before 1844. Beyond 65 miles, the privilege remained for all banks established and issuing notes before 1844, provided they had not lost the right through bankruptcy or abandonment.

In October 1844, there were 72 joint-stock banks in England with an authorized issue of £3,478,230 and an actual circulation of £3,331,516. By May 1907, the number of joint-stock banks with authorized issues had decreased to 17, with an actual issue of £437,693. The actual circulation of these banks remained far below their authorized limits because their notes typically only circulated in the immediate neighborhood of their issuance.

## Growth and Integration
The banking system shifted toward larger entities in the latter half of the 19th century. While the act of 1844 initially favored small banks, it eventually necessitated the formation of larger, more powerful banks. When joint-stock banks amalgamated, the continuing bank retained only its own issue; if a private issuing bank joined a joint-stock bank, the issue lapsed.

The number of banking offices grew substantially. In England and Wales, there were 976 offices in 1844, increasing to over 5,880 by 1906. By 1906, the English banking business was primarily conducted by approximately sixty joint-stock banks and ten private banks.

## The Clearing House
The "Clearing House" was originally composed of purely city banks, with a system of settlement existing as early as 1773 and a dedicated house established in 1810. Initially, only private banks were permitted to be members. Joint-stock banks gained entry to the Clearing House in 1854. By 1906, the membership consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank.
