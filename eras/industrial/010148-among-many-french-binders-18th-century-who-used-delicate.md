# French Bookbinding in the 18th Century

The 18th century was a period of transition and specific artistic developments in French bookbinding. While the best binders of this era were generally considered inferior to those of the 17th century—until the later arrival of Roger Payne—the overall technique of average work saw improvement. This period was characterized by the emergence of distinct styles, the use of specialized decorative elements, and the influence of prominent binding families.

## Notable Binders and Styles
Several French binders achieved prominence during the 18th century through their technical skill and unique decorative approaches. Jean Charles le Monnier is noted as perhaps the most skilled among the many French binders of this period who utilized delicate inlays of coloured leathers. Le Monnier frequently signed his work by impressing his name in small capitals in gold near the inlaid sections.

The Deromes family also stood as one of the great French binding families. The most celebrated member of this family was Nicholas Denis, known as "Le Jeune," who was born in 1731. His work featured "dentelles a l'oiseau," which were dentelle borders resembling those created by Padeloup, but distinguished by the inclusion of little birds interspersed among the arabesques.

Padeloup le Jeune, belonging to a family of excellent workmen active across the 17th and 18th centuries, produced bindings often characterized by inlaid work and borders of lace-like gold tooling referred to as "dentelle." His bindings frequently included small tickets bearing his name.

## Technical Context and Materials
The 18th century saw a general improvement in presswork, type, and paper within France and England, leading to books that were more elegant and neat. In the realm of trade bindings, there was a shift in materials; the use of calf and sheepskin became much less common, with books being mostly cased in paper boards.

The artistic landscape of the era also saw a revival in book illustration. This was evidenced by the French *livres-a-vignettes*, and the work of French artists such as Gravelot, who illustrated English books.

## Comparison with Earlier Periods
The foundations for 18th-century styles were laid by masters of the 16th and 17th centuries. For instance, the "fanfare" style—a delicate tracery of leafy stem spirals over book boards—was credited to Clovis Eve. Additionally, Le Gascon invented a dotted work technique by scoring his small gilding tools (arabesques and curves), ensuring a dotted line appeared instead of a right line during impression. This style was followed continuously and imitated by Dutch binders such as Magnus and Poncyn in Amsterdam during the 17th century.
