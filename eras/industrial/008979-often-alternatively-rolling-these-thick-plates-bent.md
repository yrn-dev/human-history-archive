# Bending of Thick Plates in Boiler Construction

In the production of modern marine boilers, the transition from iron to steel has necessitated a significant increase in the proportions of machine tools. This development is primarily due to the increased thickness of the materials used; for example, the steel shell-plates of the largest boilers reach a thickness of 1-1/2 inches.

## Methods of Bending
To achieve the required cylindrical forms for these large boilers, various methods are employed. In the era of iron boilers, the capacity of rolls was limited, never exceeding plates of approximately 3/4 inch in thickness. For the thicker plates used in modern construction, rolling is not always the preferred method. 

Alternatively to rolling, these thick plates are bent by squeezing them in successive sections between huge blocks. These blocks are operated by hydraulic pressure acting on toggle levers. Because of the immense size of the plates used in modern marine boilers, other machines besides the rolls have been made more massive than in the past to handle the material.

## Flanging Processes
Flanging is the process of bending or turning over the edges of a plate to provide a means of union with other plates. This practice is essential in boilermaking for components such as:
* Front and back plates of marine boilers
* Back end-plates of Cornish and Lancashire boilers
* Fire-boxes of locomotive boilers
* Crowns of vertical boilers
* Ends of conical cross-tubes
* Adamson seams of furnace flues

Flanging replaced the older system of using angle iron rings, which formed two sides of a rectangular section. Those older rings were a source of explosions and grooving in steam boilers because their sharp angular form lacked the elasticity needed to counteract changes in temperature. A flange turned with a large radius provides the necessary elasticity.

## Evolution of Flanging Techniques
The method of flanging has evolved from manual labor to mechanized processes:

**Hand-Flanging:** In small establishments, a skilled workman known as an angle ironsmith performs the work. This involves heating a length of the edge (approximately one foot or 10 inches) and hammering it around the edge of an iron block of a suitable shape. This process is repeated in "heats" until the work is finished.

**Machine-Flanging:** In modern boiler shops, hand work is largely replaced by hydraulic presses. Plates with diameters of 4, 6, or 8 feet, as well as locomotive fire-box plates, have their entire flanges bent in a single squeeze between massive dies. For the ends of marine boilers that are too large for this method, a special press is used to bend the edges over in successive heats.

**Specialized Rolling:** The flanges of Adamson seams are produced using a special machine where a length of flue is rotated on a table while the flange is turned over by revolving rollers within a minute. Machine-flanging is preferred not only for the enormous saving of time but also because it causes far less injury to the material than hand-flanging.

## Applications in Boiler Types
Different boiler designs utilize these bending and flanging techniques in specific ways:

* **Cylindrical Multitubular Return Tube Boilers:** These are common in merchant steamers and can reach 17 feet in diameter. Their furnaces may be plain cylindrical types with plate thicknesses ranging from 3/8 to 3/4 inch. Some furnaces (such as Morison, Fox, and Deighton types) are made from plates originally rolled to a uniform thickness, bent into cylindrical forms with welded longitudinal joints, and then corrugated.
* **Lancashire and Galloway Boilers:** These large stationary boilers use cylindrical furnace flues made in sections of 3 to 3-1/2 feet. Each section consists of a plate bent into a cylindrical form with a welded longitudinal joint and flanged at both ends. These sections are joined by "Adamson" joints, which act as stiffening rings to prevent the flue from collapsing and ensure that rivets or double plate thickness are not exposed to the fire.
