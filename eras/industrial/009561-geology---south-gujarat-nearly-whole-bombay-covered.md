# Geology of the Bombay Presidency

The geological composition of the Bombay presidency is characterized by a diverse array of rock formations, ranging from ancient lava flows to recent alluvial deposits. The region's structure varies significantly across its different divisions, particularly in the areas of Gujarat, Cutch, and the Deccan.

## The Deccan Trap and Lava Flows
South of Gujarat, nearly the entire region of Bombay is covered by the Deccan Trap series, which consists of horizontal lava flows. These flows are not limited to Bombay but spread across the majority of the Kathiawar peninsula and extend into Cutch.

## Stratigraphy of Cutch and Kathiawar
In the regions of Cutch and Kathiawar, the Deccan Trap flows are underlaid by Neocomian and Jurassic beds. The Jurassic beds are marine in nature and contain numerous Ammonites. The beds identified as Neocomian consist of a series of shales and sandstones containing plant remains; several of these plants are identical to forms found in the upper portion of the Gondwana system. 

Overlying the Deccan Trap in Cutch are Tertiary shales, sandstones, and limestones. While these Tertiary deposits are present in Cutch, their greatest development is found on the western side of the Indus.

## Alluvial Plains and Surface Deposits
The surface of eastern Gujarat and the plain of Sind is covered by wind-blown sand and alluvium. Geographically, the presidency is divided into several tracts, including the lower valley of the Indus (Sind), which is very flat. Gujarat, with the exception of its northern parts, consists of rich alluvial plains that are highly cultivated and watered by the Nerbudda and Tapti rivers.

## Lacustrine Features
The presidency contains several notable lacustrine features. The Manchar Lake, located on the right bank of the Indus, reaches a breadth of 10 meters and a length of 20 meters during inundations, covering an estimated total area of 180 square meters. 

A more peculiar feature is the Runn or Lake of Cutch. Depending on the season, this area—which covers 8000 square meters—functions as an inland lake, a salt marsh, or an arm of the sea. The Runn forms the western boundary of the province of Gujarat. During the rains, it floods and unites the Gulfs of Cambay and Cutch, effectively converting the territory of Cutch into an island.
