# J. H. Speke's Map of the Nile Sources (1863)

The map of the Nile sources produced by J. H. Speke in 1863 is a historical cartographic record of the equatorial African lake regions. This map is noted in archival records for a specific geographical error regarding the identification of Lake Baringo.

## Confusion of Lake Baringo
In his 1863 map, J. H. Speke confused Lake Baringo with the Kavirondo Gulf of Victoria Nyanza. This misidentification occurred during a period of evolving geographical understanding of the region. Lake Baringo had been reported to Europe around 1850 by J. Rebmann and Ludwig Krapf, who were German missionaries based at Mombasa. 

The inaccuracies associated with Lake Baringo persisted in later cartography; for example, Sir H. M. Stanley's 1877 map depicted the lake as a large sheet of water located to the northeast of Victoria Nyanza. It was not until 1883 that Joseph Thomson, during a journey through Masai country, became the first white man to see the lake and correct these exaggerated notions regarding its size. Despite these corrections, native tradition maintains that the lake once covered a significantly larger area.

## Context of Speke's Explorations
The mapping of the Nile sources was the result of extensive exploration in equatorial Africa. In 1856, the foreign office commissioned Richard Burton to search for the sources of the Nile, a mission supported by the Royal Geographical Society. Burton was accompanied by Speke, and together they explored the lake regions. In February 1858, they discovered Lake Tanganyika. While Burton was ill, Speke continued forward, acting on indications provided by Burton, and discovered Victoria Nyanza.

This separate discovery of the lakes led to a bitter dispute between the two men. However, the expedition served as the incentive for subsequent explorations by other figures, including Stanley, Baker, Grant, and Livingstone.

## Cartographic Accuracy and Challenges
The work of explorers like Speke highlights the difficulties inherent in compiling chorographical and general maps when official surveys are unavailable. In such cases, cartographers must rely on miscellaneous sources, including astronomical observations, route surveys, and information from native sources.

The archive notes that while latitudes from traveler observations are generally reliable, longitudes often require caution. For instance, despite being a competent observer, Captain Speke recorded the longitude of the capital of Uganda as 32° 44´ E., whereas more trustworthy observations determined the true longitude to be 32° 26´ E., resulting in an error of 18´.

## Geography of the Baringo Region
Lake Baringo is situated within a rift-valley characterized by a generally level floor divided by transverse ridges into a series of basins. These basins contain lakes without outlets. Lake Baringo is part of a sequence of such basins; it is preceded by Lakes Hannington and Elmenteita, and followed to the north by Lake Sugota (approximately 35 m long) and the larger Lake Rudolf.
