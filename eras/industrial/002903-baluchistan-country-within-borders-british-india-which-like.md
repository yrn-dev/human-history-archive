# BALUCHISTAN

Baluchistan is a country located within the borders of British India, deriving its name from its dominant race of inhabitants. Geographically, it extends from the Gomal river to the Arabian Sea, and from the borders of Afghanistan and Persia to the borders of Sind and the Punjab.

## Administrative Divisions
The territory is divided into two primary divisions: British Baluchistan and foreign territories. Both are under the authority of a chief commissioner, who also serves as the agent to the governor-general.

British Baluchistan is an official province of British India and a portion of the Baluchistan Agency. It consists of an irregularly shaped block of upland territory including the Toba plateau, the basins of the Nari and Bolan, the Thal Chotiali valley, and all upper Lora tributaries. This region covers 9,403 sq. m. and includes the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. These districts were originally Afghan but were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, though they had been nominally handed over to Kalat forty years prior. British Baluchistan was formally declared part of British India in 1887.

The foreign territories are categorized into three subdivisions:
*   **Directly Administered Districts:** These include the eastern portion of the Quetta tahsil, the Bolan Pass, and the entirety of the Chagai and Zhob political agencies, totaling 36,401 sq. m. Some of these areas are tribal lands where revenue is collected, while others are held on lease from the khan of Kalat.
*   **Native States:** Covering 79,382 sq. m., these include Las Bela and the state of Kalat, which encompasses Kharan and Makran.
*   **Tribal Areas:** These cover 7,129 sq. m. and are in the possession of the Bugti and Marri tribes.

## Political Status and Governance
While some districts within the Baluchistan Agency are as independent as any in Afghanistan, the province as a whole is subject to British control, similar to the political status of the native states of the Indian peninsula. The agent to the governor-general of India and his staff of political assistants exercise supreme control. The degree of independence enjoyed by various districts generally varies in proportion to their distance from Quetta.

Nominally, all districts and provinces—excluding the ceded territory of British Baluchistan—fall under the suzerainty of the khan of Kalat. However, it is noted that this suzerainty may not have been complete or maintainable without the assistance of the British government.

## Strategic and Frontier Position
Baluchistan serves as an intervening state and buffer between British India and Afghanistan. The boundary between Baluchistan and Afghanistan begins at the Gomal river and follows the Kundar stream for approximately 40 m. south-west. The line then diverges northwards toward Lake Ab-i-Istada before striking the Kadanai river and turning the northern spurs of the Toba plateau. The boundary continues along the western edge of the Toba plateau (reaching 8,000 ft.) near the New Chaman railway station, then descends to the plains and follows hill ranges southwards to Nushki.

The eastern boundary of this northern section is defined by the "red line" at the foot of the frontier hills, which marks the border of British India. This creates a buffer system of independent tribes. While the Indian government interferes little with the internal jurisdiction of tribal chiefs in the Suliman Range, the occupation of a line of posts on the Zhob river allows the British to command the hills and control communications with Afghanistan.

Strategically, the occupation of the Zhob valley keeps the Pathan tribes of the Suliman hills in check. Additionally, the central position at Quetta secures the peace of the Baluch hills (occupied by the Bugtis and Marris) and Kalat, while simultaneously blocking any advance toward India via Kandahar.

## Geography and Geology
The mountain ranges of Baluchistan are primarily composed of Tertiary and Cretaceous beds arranged in folds. These folds form a system running from Mount Ararat to Peshawar, with a subsidiary festoon forming the Bugti and Suliman Hills. Jurassic rocks are also present in the hills of British Baluchistan, and Triassic beds and *Fusulina* limestone are found along the south side of the upper Zhob. Most deposits are marine, except for volcanic conglomerates and tuffs in the lower Tertiary and upper Cretaceous of north-western Baluchistan.

The region features a line of recent volcanic cones stretching from eastern Persia into western Baluchistan; these are considered extinct, though the Koh-i-Tafdan beyond the Persian frontier still emits vapours. Mud "volcanoes" are found on the Makran coast. The interior of north-western Baluchistan consists of stony desert, while the Makran coast features horizontal deposits.

The climate of British Baluchistan is described as dry, bracing, and exhilarating, and is generally favorable for Europeans, although extreme temperatures can lead to severe forms of fever.
