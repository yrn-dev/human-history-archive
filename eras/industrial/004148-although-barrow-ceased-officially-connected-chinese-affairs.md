# Sir John Barrow (1764–1848)

Sir John Barrow was an English statesman whose career spanned diplomatic missions, colonial administration, and high-level government service. Born on June 19, 1764, in the village of Dragley Beck within the parish of Ulverston, Lancashire, he began his professional life as a superintending clerk for an iron foundry located in Liverpool. He later transitioned into education, teaching mathematics at a school in Greenwich.

## Diplomatic Service in China
Barrow's entry into diplomatic affairs was facilitated by Sir George Staunton, to whose son he had taught mathematics. Through this connection, Barrow was attached to the first British embassy to China, serving as the comptroller of the household to Lord Macartney. During this tenure, he acquired a proficient knowledge of the Chinese language. Following the return of the embassy in 1794, Barrow ceased to be officially connected with Chinese affairs, though he remained deeply interested in the subject. He contributed interesting articles regarding the Chinese language to the *Quarterly Review*, and the account of the embassy published by Sir George Staunton notes Barrow's valuable contributions to science and literature related to China. Despite his official departure from the role, the British government frequently consulted him on critical occasions.

## Administration in South Africa
In 1797, Barrow accompanied Lord Macartney once again, this time serving as private secretary during a delicate mission to settle the government of the Cape of Good Hope, a newly acquired colony. During this assignment, Barrow was tasked with reporting on the interior of the country and reconciling the Kaffirs and the Boers. After visiting all parts of the colony, he was appointed auditor-general of public accounts. 

Barrow intended to settle permanently in South Africa; he purchased a house in Cape Town in 1800 and married Anne Maria Trueter. However, these plans were disrupted by the peace of Amiens in 1802, which resulted in the surrender of the colony.

## Admiralty and Later Public Life
Barrow returned to England in 1804, where Lord Melville appointed him second secretary to the admiralty. He held this position for forty years, during which time he earned the confidence and esteem of the eleven chief lords who presided over the admiralty board. He was particularly regarded by King William IV, who served as lord high admiral and provided Barrow with tokens of personal regard.

In addition to his government service, Barrow was a fellow of the Royal Society. In 1821, Edinburgh University awarded him the degree of LL.D. He was further honored in 1835 when Sir Robert Peel conferred a baronetcy upon him.

## Retirement and Death
Sir John Barrow retired from public life in 1845. In his retirement, he focused on writing, producing an autobiography published in 1847. He was also a great promoter of modern Arctic voyages of discovery and wrote a history of these voyages, published in 1846. He died suddenly on November 23, 1848.
