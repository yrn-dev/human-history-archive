# The Skull of Sir Charles M'Carthy and the Royal Drinking-Cup of Kumasi

The use of the skull of Sir Charles M'Carthy as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi was the result of a military conflict between the British crown and the Ashanti kingdom in the early 19th century. This event occurred during a period of shifting diplomatic relations and escalating tensions between the British settlements on the coast and the powerful African kingdom of Ashanti.

## Diplomatic Prelude and Conflict
In 1817, the British sent a mission to Kumasi consisting of T.E. Bowdich, W. Hutchinson, and Frederick James, the commandant of Accra fort, following repeated Ashanti invasions of Fantiland. The mission encountered conflict when the king of Ashanti demanded payment of quit rents for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort. The king became enraged, labeling the emissaries liars and cheats because the Fanti had induced the British to pay the majority of these rents to them, leaving only a nominal sum for Kumasi. Although a treaty was eventually concluded to satisfy the king's demands and recognize British control over coast town natives, tensions remained.

In 1819, the British government sent Mr. Joseph Dupuis to Kumasi as consul to cultivate intercourse with the kingdom. Dupuis arrived in Kumasi on February 28, 1820, and drew up a treaty acknowledging Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory. This treaty was disowned by Mr. J. Hope Smith, the governor of Cape Coast, who viewed it as a betrayal of natives under British protection. By 1821, the government in London assumed direct control of the British settlements.

## The Battle of Essamako
Sir Charles M'Carthy was appointed as the first governor by the crown. He espoused the cause of the Fanti, which led to a military confrontation with the Ashanti. On January 21, 1824, at a location beyond the Prah called Essamako, Sir Charles was defeated in battle. The Ashanti forces were significantly larger, numbering 10,000 men compared to the 500 men under Sir Charles.

Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed during the engagement. Following the battle, the skull of the governor was taken to Kumasi, where it was used as a royal drinking-cup. Regarding the cause of the defeat, it was asserted that Sir Charles lost the battle because his ordnance-keeper brought up kegs filled with vermicelli instead of ammunition; however, records indicate that this mistake, if it occurred, merely hastened an inevitable catastrophe.

## Aftermath and the Decline of Ashanti Power
The day of the defeat coincided with the death of Osai Tutu Quamina, who was succeeded by Osai Okoto. A period of chronic warfare followed until August 7, 1826, when the Ashanti suffered a signal defeat at Dodowah. This defeat marked the beginning of the waning of Ashanti power over the coast tribes. By 1831, the king was forced to purchase peace from the administrator of the Gold Coast, Mr. George Maclean, paying 600 oz. of gold and sending his son as a hostage to Cape Coast.

## The City of Kumasi
The capital, Kumasi, was described as having a handsome appearance with a high state of culture before its destruction by the British in 1874. The city featured broad, regular streets—the main avenue being 70 yards wide—and a king's palace built of red sandstone, believed to be modeled on Dutch buildings at Elmina. The city also contained a large market-place and a "Spirit House" in a grove of trees, which served as the place of execution.

The city suffered several waves of destruction. On February 6, 1874, the forces of Sir Garnet Wolseley blew up the royal palace. Later, in 1896, several ancient houses in the priestly and royal suburb of Bantama were destroyed by fire, and the town was further injured during a revolt in 1900. Despite these destructions, the arrival of the railway in September 1903 led to a trade revival, and by 1906, Kumasi had become the distributing centre for the whole of Ashanti, supplanting the coast towns.
