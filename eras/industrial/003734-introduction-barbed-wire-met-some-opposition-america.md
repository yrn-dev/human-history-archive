# Barbed Wire Fencing

Barbed wire is a fencing material that revolutionized the manufacture of wire and became widely adopted by farmers in the United States and across the world. Its development was primarily driven by the needs of the cattle-raising industry in the western United States, where timber was scarce.

## Origins and Development
Prior to the invention of barbed wire, No. 9 round or oval iron wire was commonly used for fencing on the frontier of the United States and in South America. However, iron was found to be unsuitable due to a lack of homogeneity and insufficient tensile strength. Furthermore, single strands of iron wire were prone to breaking because they contracted in cold weather and stretched in warm weather. Cattle also tended to rub against smooth fences, which broke the wire and loosened the posts.

To solve these defects, farmers and other ingenious individuals sought a way to confine animals without the fence being destroyed. This led to the invention of barbed wire. The industry's growth was made possible by the availability of Bessemer and open-hearth steels, which provided the necessary strength and homogeneity that iron lacked.

The history of the industry includes several early patents. In 1867, patents were taken out by William B. Hunt of Scott, N.Y., and Lucien B. Smith of Kent, Ohio; Michael Kelly of New York followed a year later. The practical foundation of the industry was established in 1874 with patents issued to Joseph F. Glidden of De Kalb, Ill., for barbed fence wire, and a joint patent with Phineas W. Vaughan for a machine to manufacture it.

## Manufacture and Technical Specifications
Modern barbed wire is produced from soft Siemens-Martin or Bessemer steel. The manufacturing process involves drawing the wire in a mill and using a continuous galvanizing process to protect the material from atmospheric corrosion and provide a bright finish. This process involves passing the wire through an annealing medium (heated fire-brick tubes or red-hot lead), a muriatic acid bath to remove scale, and a bath of spelter heated slightly above its melting point. For those desiring painted fencing, the galvanizing step is omitted, and the finished wire is submerged in paint and dried.

The barbing and twisting of two longitudinal strand wires are performed by automatic machinery. For example, in the production of 2 and 4 point Glidden wire, cross wires are fed at right angles to the strand wires, cut diagonally to form sharp points, and wrapped firmly around the strand. This two-strand design allows the fence to adjust automatically to temperature changes; the twist loosens during heat expansion and tightens during cold contraction without altering the relative lengths of the wires or causing a sag. A single machine can produce between 2,000 and 3,000 lb of wire in a ten-hour day.

## Adoption and Opposition
The introduction of barbed wire in America was met with some opposition based on supposed humanitarian grounds. These objections were eventually silenced following extended tests regarding the humanity and economy of the material. Consequently, barbed wire became the standard for American farmers, particularly in the west.

While widely used in the United States, its use in other countries has been less extensive. In Australia and Argentina, plain wire fences are used in larger quantities. Argentina specifically utilizes a strong high-carbon oval fence wire to meet its local conditions.

Production statistics in the United States show rapid growth:
*   1874: 5 tons
*   1875: 300 tons
*   1880: 40,000 tons
*   1890: 125,000 tons
*   1900: 200,000 tons
*   1907: 250,000 tons

Since 1899, there has been an increasing demand for meshed wire field fencing, especially along railway lines and in thickly settled areas, as it is considered a better enclosure for small animals like hogs and sheep.

## Installation and Regulation
Barbed wire is typically shipped on wooden spools holding approximately 100 lb (or 80 to 100 rods). It is attached to posts using galvanized steel wire staples made from No. 9 wire. While wooden posts were traditional, steel and reinforced concrete posts have become popular due to the rapid decay of wood.

The use of the material is regulated by law in some American states, typically concerning its placement on highways, its use as a property line marker between owners (which may require mutual agreement), or requirements that the fence include a lumber top rail. In England, the Barbed Wire Act 1893 allows local authorities to require the removal of barbed wire fences adjoining highways if they are deemed a nuisance or injurious to persons and animals.

## Military Applications
Barbed wire is utilized in field fortifications as "high wire entanglements." This involves planting 4 ft. high pointed stakes (pickets) in rows and connecting them at the top and bottom with lengths of barbed wire. Historical accounts from the west describe fortifications surrounded by barbed-wire entanglements, such as those at the fort of Merxem, and defensive preparations in harbours where barbed wire was installed, sometimes with the intended (though unfulfilled) purpose of electrification.
