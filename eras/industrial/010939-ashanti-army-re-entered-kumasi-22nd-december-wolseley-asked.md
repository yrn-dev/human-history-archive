# The Ashanti Wars and the City of Kumasi

The history of the Ashanti kingdom and its capital, Kumasi, is marked by periods of high culture and prosperity interspersed with violent conflicts with British forces and internal political instability.

## Causes of the 1873-74 War
The war of 1873-74 was triggered by two primary events. The principal cause was the transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti regarded the Elmina as their subjects and the king of Ashanti held the "custom-note," receiving an annual payment from the Dutch that the Ashanti viewed as ground rent for the fort. The British occupation of this territory was greatly resented. A secondary cause was the captivity of four Europeans: Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, his wife and infant son (who died shortly after capture), and Mr. J. Kuhne—all members of the Basel mission—along with a French trader, Monsieur M.J. Bonnat. These individuals had been captured in June 1869 during an Ashanti invasion of Krepi and taken to Kumasi.

## The Campaign of Sir Garnet Wolseley
On January 22, 1873, an Ashanti force invaded the British protectorate by crossing the Prah, defeating the Fanti and encamping at Mampon. In response, the British parliament voted £800,000 for an expedition led by Sir Garnet Wolseley. Upon landing at Cape Coast on October 2, Wolseley found the Ashanti forces decimated by fever and smallpox and preparing to return home. Despite this, Wolseley determined to march to Kumasi, while Captain John Glover cooperated from the east with native levies to take the Ashanti in the rear.

The city of Kumasi, before its destruction in 1874, was a center of high culture with broad, regular streets and a main avenue 70 yards wide. The king's palace was constructed of red sandstone and modeled after Dutch buildings at Elmina. On February 6, 1874, Wolseley's forces blew up the palace. Other notable features of the city included a large market-place and the Spirit House, which served as the place of execution.

## Political Transition and Internal Strife
Following the 1873-74 war, several Ashanti-dependent states sought British protection, though this was refused. The Ashanti deposed King Kofi Karikari and placed his brother, Mensa, on the golden stool, the symbol of sovereignty. Mensa's reign was characterized by tyranny, human sacrifices, and a failure to pay the stipulated indemnity of 50,000 oz. of gold, of which only 4,000 oz. were paid. 

After a revolution in 1883 and the subsequent death of Kwaka Dua II in 1884, a desolating civil war occurred. Prince Prempeh (Kwaka Dua III) was chosen as king on March 26, 1888, with the sanction of a Gold Coast government officer. While peace initially returned, tensions rose again in 1893 when Prempeh closed roads to traders. After Prempeh declined to accept a British protectorate or a resident at Kumasi by October 31, 1895, he was deported.

## The Rebellion of 1900
Following Prempeh's deportation, Captain Donald W. Stewart was installed as British resident at Kumasi. A fort was built and garrisoned by Gold Coast constabulary. In 1900, a serious rebellion broke out involving the Kumasi, Kokofu, and Adansi tribes, who commanded a force reported at 40,000. The conflict was sparked in part by Governor Sir F. Hodgson's demand for 4,000 oz. of gold annually and his reproaches regarding the hidden golden stool.

The rebels attacked the fort, where Sir F. and Lady Hodgson and Basel missionaries took refuge. The garrison was eventually reinforced by Lagos constabulary and forces under Major A. Morris, bringing the total to 700 men. Colonel Willcocks later led a force of 3,368 natives (including Sikhs and Yaos from Central Africa) and 169 British officers and non-commissioned officers to crush the rebellion. After the Kumasi were beaten at Obassa on September 30, the final rebel chiefs were captured on December 28. The campaign resulted in 1,007 total casualties, including nine British officers killed in action.

## Geography and Economy of Kumasi
Kumasi is situated on a low rocky eminence in a clearing of dense forest, located 120 miles north-northwest of Cape Coast. By 1906, the population including suburbs exceeded 12,000. The town's name is derived from the okum trees found in its streets. 

Economically, the region is known for gold-mining, particularly in Obuassi, and the production of rubber, kola-nuts, and cocoa. The Ashanti are noted for their skill in pottery, gold work—including beaten gold masks—and cotton weaving. The arrival of the railway from the coast in September 1903 led to a rapid expansion of trade, and by 1906, Kumasi had become the distributing center for the entire Ashanti region.
