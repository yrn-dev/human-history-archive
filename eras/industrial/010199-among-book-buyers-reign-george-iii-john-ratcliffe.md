# John Ratcliffe

## Book Collecting in the Reign of George III
During the reign of George III, book-collecting was practiced by various individuals with differing motivations and methods. While King George III himself accumulated a magnificent library of 440 manuscripts and over 80,000 pamphlets and books—a collection characterized by a literary and catholic spirit that included English classics and works from the first presses of Venice, Rome, and Mainz—other buyers focused on more specialized acquisitions.

Among these book-buyers was John Ratcliffe, an ex-coal-merchant. Ratcliffe, along with James West, devoted his collecting efforts specifically to the products of early English presses and the works of Caxton. Ratcliffe possessed 48 Caxtons, while West possessed 34.

## Trends in Book Acquisition
The era saw a transition in how collections were formed and preserved. Many large buyers of the 16th through 18th centuries collected with a public object in mind, often bequeathing their treasures to public institutions. For example, the library of George III passed to the British Museum in 1823. Other figures of the period, such as Garrick and Capell, maintained smaller, homogeneous collections.

By the mid-19th century, a "cabinet theory" of collecting became popular in England, France, and the United States. This approach, exemplified by Henry Perkins (who died in 1855), favored small, compact collections that epitomized the owner's specific tastes rather than the large, miscellaneous libraries of the past. This shift was driven by the increasing cost of books and limitations regarding house-room.
