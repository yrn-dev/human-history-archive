# Cape Coast Castle

Cape Coast Castle is a British fortification located on the coast of West Africa. It has served as a central point of interaction and conflict between the British administration and the Ashanti Empire, particularly regarding the control of the coast and the surrounding territories.

## Relations with the Ashanti Empire

The Ashanti power, founded by Osai Tutu, expanded through conquests to the east and west, subduing the state of Denkera in 1719 as well as the Banna and Gaman (Mahommedan) countries. Following the death of Osai Tutu in 1731, his successor, Osai Apoko, continued to make acquisitions toward the coast. By 1800, Osai Tutu Quamina became king; he was an ambitious man who sought to open communications with white nations.

In 1807, after defeating two chiefs of the Assin, Osai Tutu Quamina invaded the country of the Fanti—the ruling people on the coast—because the Fanti refused to deliver the fugitives. The Ashanti reached the coast near Anamabo, where a British fort was located. While the Ashanti failed to storm the English fort, they destroyed the town and slaughtered 8,000 inhabitants, losing over 2,000 of their own men.

## The Administration of Colonel G. Torrane

Following the conflict at Anamabo, a truce was established. The Ashanti king refused to negotiate with anyone other than the governor of Cape Coast, Colonel G. Torrane, who served as governor from 1805 to 1807. Torrane traveled to Anamabo, where he was received with great pomp. 

During these negotiations, Torrane decided to surrender the fugitive Assin chiefs. While one chief escaped, the other was handed over and executed by the Ashanti. Torrane concluded an agreement that acknowledged the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland. As part of this arrangement, Torrane delivered half of the fugitives in the Anamabo fort to the Ashanti; the majority of the remaining fugitives were sold as slaves by Torrane and his council members. Additionally, the governor agreed to pay rent to the Ashanti for both the Anamabo fort and Cape Coast castle. Colonel Torrane died on the coast in 1808.

## Diplomatic Missions and Treaties

In 1817, the British sent a mission to Kumasi consisting of W. Hutchinson, T.E. Bowdich, and Frederick James, the commandant of Accra fort. This mission was prompted by repeated Ashanti invasions of Fantiland. During the mission, the Ashanti king demanded the payment of quit rents for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort. It was revealed that the Fanti had induced the British to pay the majority of these rents to them, leaving only a nominal sum for Kumasi. After Frederick James failed to provide a satisfactory explanation, the king became enraged. Bowdich and Hutchinson subsequently took over negotiations, resulting in a treaty that satisfied the king's demands and recognized the British right to control the natives in the coast towns.

Later, in January 1819, the British government sent Mr. Joseph Dupuis, formerly the consul at Mogador, to Kumasi as consul to cultivate intercourse with the kingdom. Dupuis arrived at Cape Coast in January 1819 and reached Kumasi on February 28, 1820. He drew up a treaty that acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of their enemies. This treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, who viewed it as a betrayal of the natives under British protection.

## British Crown Control and Conflict

In 1821, the government in London assumed direct control of the British settlements. Sir Charles M'Carthy was appointed as the first governor by the crown. Sir Charles supported the Fanti, but he was defeated by the Ashanti on January 21, 1824, at Essamako. The Ashanti force of 10,000 men overwhelmed Sir Charles's 500 men. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed; the governor's skull was later used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. This defeat occurred on the same day that Osai Tutu Quamina died, leading to the succession of Osai Okoto.

## Decline of Ashanti Influence

A period of chronic warfare followed until the Ashanti suffered a significant defeat at Dodowah on August 7, 1826. Following this event, Ashanti power over the coast tribes diminished. In 1831, the king was forced to purchase peace from the administrator of the Gold Coast, Mr. George Maclean, paying 600 oz. of gold and sending his son to Cape Coast as a hostage. The payment of ground rent for the British forts, including Cape Coast castle, ceased after the battle of Dodowah.
