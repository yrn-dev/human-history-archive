# BARRICADE

A barricade, also referred to as a barricado, is an improvised fortification constructed to hinder the advance of an enemy. The term is derived from the Spanish *barricada*, which comes from *barrica*, meaning a cask. This etymology reflects the early practice of using casks filled with earth to form these defensive structures.

## Composition and Construction
Barricades are typically thrown up across streets using materials that are readily available at hand. Common materials used in their construction include:
* Earth
* Paving-stones
* Trees
* Cobble-stones
* Sheet-iron

In some instances, defenders may establish supporting infrastructure near these fortifications, such as huts constructed from tarpaulins, boards, and other materials.

## Military and Urban Application
In urban warfare, barricades are often used to block long, narrow streets. Historical accounts from the insurrection of June 25, 1848, in Paris describe a sophisticated use of these structures. Insurgents established a series of barricades extending from the Hôtel de Ville to the Rue St. Antoine. To protect themselves and maintain mobility, the insurgents created interior communications between houses, allowing them to move through a covered passage from distant points to the center of the barricade line. Such obstructions can force opposing forces, such as the troupe de ligne or the garde nationale mobile, to conduct sieges of individual houses.

## Naval Use
The term barricade also applies to a specific feature of old wooden warships. In this context, a barricade is a wooden rail or fence that extends across the quarter-deck. This structure is supported by stanchions and strengthened with various materials to provide protection during naval action.
