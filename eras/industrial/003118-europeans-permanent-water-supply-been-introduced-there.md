# Europeans

## Early Settlements and Neolithic Life
In the early stages of human development, the transition from a transient existence as hunters or herdsmen to permanent settlement required a trustworthy, year-round supply of water, building materials, and food. While such conditions were found on a massive scale in Mesopotamia and Egypt, they were also present in various Asiatic and European valleys. In Europe, these favorable conditions allowed Neolithic communities to settle from a very early date, as evidenced by the Swiss lake-dwellings. The homes of these European Neolithic communities around 6000 B.C. are considered counterparts to contemporary lake villages found in Borneo.

## Nineteenth-Century Worldview and Imperialism
During the nineteenth century, the European mind was characterized by a shallow historical background and a lack of penetrating criticism regarding political systems. Following the mechanical revolution in the west, European great powers gained temporary advantages over the rest of the old world. These advantages were interpreted by Europeans as evidence of a permanent and assured leadership of mankind.

This perspective was fueled by a belief in an innate intellectual drive within the west, contrasted with a perceived innate conservatism and indolence in the east. Consequently, Europeans failed to recognize the transferability of science, believing that individuals from India or China could not carry out research as ably as Englishmen or Frenchmen. This mindset extended to the "headlong painting of the map of Africa in European colours," which was accepted by many as a permanent new settlement of world affairs.

## Science, Industry, and Technology
European scientific and industrial progress varied by field and region. In the realm of illumination, Felice Fontana observed the reaction of steam on heated carbonaceous matter in 1780. Later, in 1830, Donovan introduced carburetted water gas for illumination. While the town of Narbonne used incandescent platinum wire heated by water gas for nine years, this process was abandoned in 1865, leading to a period of stagnation in the field within Europe. 

Progress resumed after 1882 when the apparatus of Lowe and Dwight was introduced in Essen. Further improvements by E. Blass allowed water gas to establish a firm footing for heating purposes. By 1890, the London Gas Light and Coke Company introduced the American process for making carburetted water gas as an auxiliary to ordinary coal gas on a large scale.

## Agriculture and Trade
European settlers in British Central Africa introduced coffee from abroad and cultivated wheat. In other regions, Europeans interacted with various botanical products for medicinal or ceremonial use. For example, John Fothergill introduced kino, a West African astringent drug, into European medicine in 1757.

The date palm (_Phoenix dactylifera_) was introduced along the Mediterranean shores of Europe. Because the fruit does not ripen in the northern latitudes, European plants are used primarily for the celebration of the Passover by the Israelites and for the Christian festival of Palm Sunday. While most Europeans at home only know dates as dried specimens from shop windows, the fresh fruit is noted for being delicious, healthy, and lacking the "heating" defect found in preserved fruit.

## Infrastructure and Urban Development
In certain European regions, infrastructure was developed to support industry and population. In Lucca, the river Serchio provided water-power for factories producing cottons, tobacco, silks, and jute goods. To maintain the water supply for the area, an aqueduct featuring 459 arches was constructed between 1823 and 1832, sourcing water from the Pisan mountains.
