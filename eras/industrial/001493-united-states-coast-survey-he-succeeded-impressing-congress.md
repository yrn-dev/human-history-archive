# United States Coast Survey

The United States Coast Survey is a governmental organization dedicated to the mapping of the American coastline and the collection of scientific data. Over its history, it has evolved into the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey, providing the foundational triangulation upon which other national surveys, such as the U.S. Geological Survey, are based.

## Leadership and Administration
Following the death of Professor F. R. Hassler (1770–1843), Alexander Dallas Bache was appointed as the superintendent of the United States Coast Survey in 1843. Bache, an American physicist and the great-grandson of Benjamin Franklin, was instrumental in the growth of the organization. He succeeded in impressing Congress with the great value of the survey's work, which resulted in the granting of liberal aid.

Under Bache's leadership, the survey implemented a comprehensive plan characterized by a skillful division of labour. By erecting numerous observing stations, the organization was able to map the entire coast simultaneously under the supervision of the general director. In addition to coastal mapping, the survey collected a vast mass of meteorological and magnetic observations.

## Personnel and Scientific Contributions
The survey employed officers with strong mathematical backgrounds to ensure precision. For example, John Adolf Dahlgren, an admiral in the U.S. Navy, was employed by the coast survey in 1834 due to his knowledge of mathematics.

The organization has also contributed to the field of cartography and map projections. T. Craig of the United States Coast and Geodetic Survey published *A Treatise on Projections* in 1882. The survey utilizes the "simple polyconic" projection, a method where meridians are obtained by measuring the true lengths of the degrees of longitude outwards from the central meridian along each parallel. This projection is considered a sufficiently close approximation to better forms of conical projection when used for large scales and single sheets. Additionally, the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey has published useful tables based on Clarke's spheroid of 1866.

## Integration with Other Surveys
The work of the Coast and Geodetic Survey serves as a critical baseline for other mapping efforts:

*   **U.S. Geological Survey:** Organized in 1879 under director Clarence King and later Major J. W. Powell, the geological survey relies on the triangulation provided by the U.S. Coast and Geodetic Survey to produce its topographical maps.
*   **Canadian Surveys:** The lake and coast surveys of the United States are connected with the cadastral surveys carried out in Canada.
