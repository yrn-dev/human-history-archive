# The Electric Nerve: The Telegraph and the Shrinking World

## Overview
During the Industrial Revolution, the speed of information was tethered to the speed of physical transport—be it a galloping horse or a steamship. The advent of the electric telegraph in the early 19th century fundamentally decoupled communication from transportation, initiating what historians often call the "annihilation of space and time."

While various pioneers experimented with electricity, the commercial breakthrough came in the 1830s and 1840s. Samuel Morse in the United States and Cooke and Wheatstone in Britain developed systems that allowed complex messages to be sent via electrical pulses. However, the most ambitious leap occurred in the mid-19th century: the attempt to lay cables across the Atlantic Ocean. After several failed attempts and cables that snapped or went silent due to insulation failure, the first enduring Transatlantic cable was successfully completed in 1866. This achievement reduced the communication time between London and New York from ten days (by ship) to mere minutes, revolutionizing global diplomacy, finance, and journalism.

## Key Facts
*   **Economic Impact:** The telegraph birthed the modern financial market. For the first time, commodity prices (like cotton or wheat) could be synchronized globally, reducing arbitrage but increasing the volatility of market crashes.
*   **Political Control:** Imperial powers, particularly Great Britain, used the "All Red Line" (a network of cables touching only British soil) to maintain tighter administrative control over colonies in India and Africa.
*   **The Technical Debate:** Historians of technology often debate the "Great Man" theory regarding the telegraph. While Samuel Morse is the household name, scholars emphasize that the telegraph was an incremental achievement relying on the prior work of scientists like Hans Christian Ørsted and André-Marie Ampère.

## Did You Know?
1. **The First "Bug":** Early telegraphers often referred to the clicking sound of the apparatus as a "bug," a term that likely influenced the modern computing term for a technical error.
2. **The 1858 Failure:** The first Transatlantic cable of 1858 worked for only three weeks. The chief electrician, Wildman Whitehouse, attempted to increase the voltage to boost the signal, which effectively "fried" the cable's insulation.
3. **Morse Code's Efficiency:** Morse Code was designed using frequency analysis; the most common letter in English, "E," was assigned the shortest signal (a single dot) to maximize transmission speed.
