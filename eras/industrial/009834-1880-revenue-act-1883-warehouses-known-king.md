# Bonded Warehouses and the Revenue Act 1883

## Definition and Purpose of Bonded Warehouses
A bonded warehouse is a facility established by either private enterprise or the state. Its primary function is to serve as a location where goods liable to duty are lodged until the required duty upon them has been paid. 

Before the implementation of the bonded warehouse system in England, importers were required to pay duties at the time of importation or provide a bond with security to revenue authorities for future payment. This previous system created several inconveniences:
* Importers often struggled to find sureties.
* Importers were frequently forced to sell goods immediately to raise the necessary funds for duty, often during periods of low market prices.
* The requirement to pay duty in a lump sum increased the price of goods due to the interest on the capital required.
* Competition was hindered because the importation of heavily taxed articles required large amounts of capital.
* There were difficulties in granting an exact equivalent drawback to exporters for goods that had already paid duty.

## Evolution of the Warehousing System
To address these difficulties and prevent frauds on the revenue, Sir Robert Walpole proposed an "excise scheme" in 1733 focusing on wine and tobacco. Although this proposal was initially unpopular, the system was officially adopted in 1803. Under the act of 1803, imported goods were placed in warehouses approved by customs authorities, and importers provided "bonds" for the payment of duties upon the removal of the goods. The terms "bonded" or "bonding" are derived from this practice.

## Legislative Framework and King's Warehouses
The Customs Consolidation Act 1853 removed the requirement for giving bonds and established various provisions to secure the payment of customs duties on warehoused goods. These provisions were later contained within the Customs Consolidation Act 1876 and several amending statutes, specifically the Customs and Inland Revenue Act 1880 and the Revenue Act 1883.

Under these regulations, these facilities are known as "king's warehouses." According to section 284 of the act of 1876, a king's warehouse is defined as any place provided by the crown or approved by the commissioners of customs for the deposit of goods to ensure the security of the goods and the duties due upon them.

## Other Legislative Acts of 1880
The year 1880 saw other significant revenue-related legislation, such as the Free Mash-Tun Act of 1880. This act shifted the duty from malt to beer (specifically the wort). It repealed maltsters' and brewers' licences, replacing them with an annual licence duty of £1 for every brewer for sale. Starting October 1, 1880, a beer duty was imposed at a rate of 6s. 3d. per barrel of 36 gallons (at a specific gravity of 1.057). This act allowed brewers greater freedom in choosing materials—such as corn, sugar, or other saccharine substitutes—for the manufacture or coloring of beer, provided they were not drugs or deleterious articles.
