# The Brontë Sisters

The Brontë sisters—Charlotte (1816–1855), Emily (1818–1848), and Anne (1820–1849)—were English novelists and poets whose lives and literary achievements are characterized by intensity and a lasting tradition. Their story, as recounted by their biographer Mrs. Gaskell, is noted for its exceeding pathos and interest.

## Family Background and Early Life
The sisters were three of the six children of Patrick Brontë, a clergyman of the Church of England. Born at Emsdale, Co. Down, Ireland, on March 17, 1777, Patrick Brontë worked as a schoolmaster and weaver in his native country before attending St John's College, Cambridge, in 1802. Following his education, he served as a curate in Wethersfield, Essex (1806), and Wellington, Salop (1809), before moving to Dewsbury and Hartshead-cum-Clifton in Yorkshire.

In 1812, at Hartshead, Patrick married Maria Branwell, a Cornishwoman. They had two children, Maria and Elizabeth, both of whom died in 1825. The family later moved to Thornton, where Charlotte, Patrick Branwell (1817–1848), Emily Jane, and Anne were born. In April 1820, three months after Anne's birth, Patrick Brontë accepted the living of Haworth, a village near Keighley in Yorkshire. This location remains permanently associated with the romantic story of the family. Maria Brontë died in September 1821; she is remembered through letters to her lover, "dear saucy Pat," and a manuscript essay titled *The Advantages of Poverty in Religious Concerns*.

## Literary Works and Achievements
The sisters initially attempted poetry, but the failure of these works led them to pursue novels. Each sister had a manuscript to dispose of: Charlotte's *The Master* (later retitled *The Professor*), Emily's *Wuthering Heights*, and Anne's *Agnes Gray*.

### Charlotte Brontë
Charlotte's *The Professor* was submitted to the firm Smith, Elder & Co. In August 1847, the firm's reader, R. Smith Williams, and his employers informed her that while the work had merit, it was too short for the required three-volume form. In response, Charlotte provided a longer novel, *Jane Eyre*, which was published in October 1847 to wild acclaim, though it later received a critical review in the *Quarterly* in December 1848 by Miss Rigby (Lady Eastlake). Along with *Villette*, *Jane Eyre* is recognized for its independence, rough individuality, and intensity. While Charlotte is considered undistinguished as a maker of verse, her novels contain passages of magnificent pure poetry.

### Emily Brontë
Emily is regarded as a great poet and novelist. Her poetry, specifically "Old Stoic" and "Last Lines," is cited as some of the finest poetry produced by any woman in English literature. Her novel *Wuthering Heights* is described as a monument of intensity—passionate, haunting, and grim—that stands apart from the achievements of earlier writers and tradition.

### Anne Brontë
Anne's two novels survive primarily due to the vitality of the Brontë tradition. Additionally, she maintains a place in most religious communities as a hymn writer.

## Biographies and Documentation
The primary biographical account, *The Life of Charlotte Brontë* by Mrs. Gaskell, was first published in 1857. The third edition was significantly revised due to controversies regarding the identity of Lowood in *Jane Eyre* (linked to Cowan Bridge school), the relationship between Branwell Brontë and his employer's wife, and the peculiarities of Mr. Brontë. The 1902 Haworth edition, published by Smith, Elder & Co., is considered the most satisfactory version and includes letters from Charlotte to her publisher, George Smith.

Other significant contributions to the Brontë archive include:
* *Charlotte Brontë: a Monograph* (1877) by T. Wemyss Reid.
* *A Note on Charlotte Brontë* (1877), a forcible essay by A.C. Swinburne.
* *Life of Charlotte Brontë* (1887) by Augustine Birrell.
* *Charlotte Brontë and her Circle* (1896) and *Charlotte Brontë and her Sisters* (1906) by Clement Shorter.
* *The Brontës in Ireland* (1893) by William Wright, D.D.
* Publications of the Brontë Society (1895–1907), edited by Butler Wood.
* A separate biography of Emily Brontë (1883) and an essay in *Grands Écrivains d'outre-Manche* by Madame Duclaux (A. Mary F. Robinson).
* *The Brontës: Life and Letters* (1907) by Clement Shorter, which organizes Charlotte Brontë's letters chronologically.
