# Calculus of Finite Differences

The calculus of finite differences is a branch of mathematics that provides tools for analyzing data and functions. It has been utilized in both theoretical mathematical treatises and practical scientific applications, such as the study of ballistics.

## Applications in Ballistics
Formulae of the calculus of finite differences are employed to derive critical data from chronograph records. In the field of ballistics, these formulae enable the inference of the retardation and velocity of a shot, which in turn allows for the determination of the resistance of the air.

This application was central to the series of experiments conducted by the Rev. F. Bashforth, B.D., between 1864 and 1880. Bashforth sought to measure the resistance of the air and the velocity of shot using a chronograph and equidistant electric screens equipped with vertical wire or threads. His work established that the resistance of the air cannot be represented by a simple algebraical law over a large range of velocity, leading him to rely on experimental measurement rather than a priori theoretical assumptions.

## Mathematical Treatises and Theory
The subject was the focus of systematic study by Boole, who authored a *Treatise on the Calculus of Finite Differences* in 1860. This work was designed as a sequel to his 1859 *Treatise on Differential Equations*. Boole's contributions to these branches of mathematics are noted for combining profound philosophical investigation with elementary exposition.

Boole was a prominent figure in the recognition that symbols of operation could be treated as distinct objects of calculation, separate from symbols of quantity. His work in these treatises embodies many of his important discoveries, characterized by a high degree of skill in tracing results obtained through the treatment of symbols according to their primary conditions and laws.

## Related Mathematical Concepts
The calculus of finite differences exists alongside other analytical methods used to describe physical and mathematical phenomena:

*   **Infinitesimal Calculus:** The analytic operations of the infinitesimal calculus are used to derive results from the mathematical expressions of Newton's first two laws of motion. These equations are considered a perfect statement of the laws of motion because they unambiguously express naked phenomena without relying on concepts not associated with actual phenomena, such as force.
*   **Differential Coefficients and Continuity:** In the general theory of functions, a function is continuous throughout an interval if it has a finite differential coefficient at all points of that interval. However, a function may be continuous without being differentiable. This distinction was advanced by G.F.B. Riemann, following an initial attempt by Ampere.
*   **Taylor's Theorem:** This theorem states that if a function $[f](x)$ and its first $n$ differential coefficients are continuous in the interval between $a$ and $a + h$, then $h^2 [f](a + h) = [f](a) + h[f]'(a) + \dots \frac{f''(a)}{2!} h^{n-1} + \dots \frac{[f]^{(n-1)}(a)}{(n-1)!} + R_n$.
