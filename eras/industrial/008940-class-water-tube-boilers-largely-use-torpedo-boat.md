# Express Boilers

Express boilers are a specific class of water-tube boilers utilized primarily in cruisers and torpedo-boat destroyers. They are designed for installations where the maximum amount of power is required relative to the total weight of the equipment. To achieve this, express boilers employ tubes of a smaller diameter than those found in other boiler types and are engineered to support a high rate of fuel combustion.

## Technical Design and Operation
The high combustion rates in express boilers are obtained through a high degree of "forced draught." While natural draught—produced by a chimney—typically allows for the combustion of 15 to 20 lb. of coal per square foot of grate area per hour, forced draught significantly increases this capacity. In express boilers used in torpedo boats and similar craft, combustion rates can reach as high as 70 or 80 lb. per square foot.

In war vessels, this is often achieved via a "closed stokehold" system. In this arrangement, the stokehold compartment is sealed so that air can only exit through the fires. Fans drive air into the compartment to maintain an air pressure above that of the outside atmosphere. While large vessels typically use an air pressure corresponding to a height of 1 to 1-1/2 inches of water, torpedo boats may use as much as 4 inches.

## The Yarrow Boiler
The Yarrow boiler is widely used in the British navy and several other navies. Its construction consists of two lower water-chambers and a large cylindrical steam chest, all connected by numerous straight tubes. The tubes are arranged with their centers forming equilateral triangles, a spacing that allows for external cleaning from the front of the boiler and cross-ways in two directions.

The diameter of the tubes varies based on the boiler's application:
* **Large vessels:** All tubes have an external diameter of 1-3/4 inches.
* **Large express boilers:** The two rows nearest the fire on each side have a diameter of 1-1/4 inches, while the remaining tubes are 1 inch in diameter.

To improve circulation, some models connect the lower part of the steam chest to the water chambers using large pipes located outside the casings. In larger versions, access to the interior is provided via manholes in the water-chamber ends and steam chest. However, in smaller versions fitted for torpedo boats, the water chambers are too small for manholes; instead, they are constructed in two parts joined by a bolted joint to make the tube ends accessible.

## Other Express and Water-Tube Types
Several other designs are utilized to manage heat and water circulation:

* **Thornycroft Boiler:** This design features three chambers connected by tubes that are straight for most of their length but bent at the ends. The outer rows of tubes form "water-walls" at their lower parts, while the inner rows form "water-walls" at their upper parts, forcing combustion products to pass over all heating surfaces. The fire-rows and remaining tubes are 1-3/8 inches in outside diameter.
* **Thornycroft-Schulz Boiler:** Used extensively in the German and British navies, this type utilizes four chambers and a fire-grate divided into two separate portions. It employs large outer circulating pipes connecting the steam and water chambers, and several nearly vertical down-comers in the center of the boiler for the middle water chamber.
* **Normand Boiler:** This boiler uses three chambers connected by bent tubes. The two outer rows of tubes touch for nearly their entire length to create a "water-wall" protecting the outer casing. The two inner rows are also bent to form a water-wall for much of the boiler's length, directing the products of combustion.
* **Blechynden and White-Foster Boilers:** Both utilize three chambers connected by bent tubes. The curvature is designed so that tubes can be removed through small doors in the upper steam chest (Blechynden) or through the steam-chest end manhole (White-Foster).

## Materials of Construction
In water-tube boilers designed for naval purposes, seamless steel tubes are invariably used. For general marine and land boilers, the plates and stays are typically made of mild steel. Shell plates and long stays generally utilize steel with a tensile strength of 28 to 32 tons per square inch, while furnaces, flues, and short-screwed stays use a softer steel with a strength of 26 to 30 tons per square inch.
