# Sugars in Brewing and Chemistry

In the process of brewing, various sugars are utilized as adjuncts or substitutes for barley malt. The primary sugars employed for these purposes are cane sugar, glucose, and invert sugar.

## Cane Sugar
Cane sugar is frequently used in the production of stouts and heavy mild ales. Its application in these beverages is credited with providing a full and peculiarly sweet flavour, which contributes to the popularity of these specific beer classes. 

In the United States, cane sugar production is concentrated in several regions. Between 1907 and 1908, Louisiana produced 335,000 long tons and Texas produced 12,000 tons. During the same period, Hawaii produced 420,000 tons, Porto Rico produced 217,000 tons, and the Philippines produced 135,000 tons. The industry in the United States dates practically from 1796, when Étienne Boré first succeeded in clarifying and crystallizing syrup.

## Invert Sugar
Invert sugar, commonly referred to as "saccharum," is a mixture of glucose and fructose. It is termed "invert" because the mixture is laevo-rotatory, whereas cane sugar is dextro-rotatory to the plane of polarized light. The conversion of cane sugar into invert sugar is represented by the chemical equation: $\text{C}_{12}\text{H}_{22}\text{O}_{11} + \text{H}_2\text{O} = \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$ (cane sugar + water = glucose + fructose).

There are two primary methods for preparing invert sugar:
*   **The Acid Process:** This involves treating a cane sugar solution with a small amount of mineral acid. The excess acid is removed using chalk, and the solution is concentrated into a thick syrup. This method is used almost exclusively in special sugar works.
*   **The Yeast Process (Tompson's):** This method utilizes invertase, an enzyme found in ordinary yeast. A cane sugar solution is pitched with yeast at approximately 55° C. At this temperature, inversion occurs rapidly while fermentation remains practically impossible. Once complete, the liquid and yeast are run into the boiling contents of the copper. This method is better suited for use within the brewery itself.

## Glucose and Fructose
Glucose is one of the constituents of invert sugar and is used independently in brewing. For brewing purposes, it is not prepared from invert sugar but is produced directly from starch using dilute boiling acid. This process converts starch first into maltose and dextrine, and then into glucose. The raw materials used for glucose manufacture vary by region: sago, rice, and purified maize are used in England; potatoes are common in Germany; and purified Indian corn is used in America.

Fructose (also known as laevulose or fruit-sugar) is a carbohydrate with the formula $\text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6$. It is an oxyketone or ketose. Fructose can be obtained from inulin or from the hydrolysis of cane sugar with sulphuric acid to create invert sugar. To isolate fructose from invert sugar, the mixture is freed from acid, concentrated, mixed with water, cooled in ice, and treated with calcium hydroxide to precipitate it as a saccharate. This is then decomposed by carbon dioxide, filtered, concentrated, and washed with cold alcohol.

Fructose is sweeter than cane sugar and more easily assimilated. It has been used as a sweetening agent for diabetics under the name diabetin because it does not increase the sugar content of urine. It is also used in medicinal applications for scrofula, children with tuberculosis, and phthisis (when mixed with other bitters like quassia).

## Enzymatic Processes
Several enzymes facilitate the breakdown and conversion of sugars:
*   **Invertase:** Found in yeasts, moulds, and fungi, this enzyme splits cane sugar and other complex sugars into simpler sugars like levulose and dextrose.
*   **Zymase:** Extracted from yeast, zymase splits sugar into alcohol and carbonic acid (or carbon-dioxide).
*   **Glucase:** This enzyme splits maltose into glucose.
*   **Diastase:** Converts starches into sugars.

## Occurrence in Honey
Honey contains laevoglucose and dextroglucose, and according to some sources, cane sugar. It is suggested that cane sugar is gradually transformed into inverted sugar through a species of fermentation. Analysis by Dr. J. Campbell Brown of various samples showed average percentages of 36.45% laevulose and 36.57% dextrose. Honey may be adulterated with manufactured glucose, cane sugar, or common syrup.
