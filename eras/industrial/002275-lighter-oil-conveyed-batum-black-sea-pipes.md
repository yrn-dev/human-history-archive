# Petroleum Production and Transport in Baku

## Geography and Administration
Baku is the chief town of the government of the same name, located in Russian Transcaucasia. It is situated on the south side of the Apsheron peninsula, positioned at 40deg 21' N. and 49deg 50' E. The government of Baku stretches along the west coast of the Caspian Sea from 41deg 50' to 38deg 30' N. lat. It is bounded to the south by Persia, to the west by the province of Daghestan and the government of Elisavetpol, and includes the Kuba plain on the north-east slope of the Caucasus. The region's area is 15,172 sq. m.

The landscape includes the eastern extremity of the Caucasus range, featuring the Bazardyuz (14,727 ft.) and the Shad-dagh (13,960 ft.), and the steppes of the lower Aras and Kura. While the slopes of the Caucasus are fertile and well irrigated, the Apsheron peninsula is dry and lacks vegetation.

## Petroleum Extraction and Production
The Apsheron peninsula contains famous petroleum wells that reach depths between 700 and 1,700 ft. These wells yield crude naphtha, which is then distilled to produce kerosene or petroleum. The heavier residue from this process, known as *mazut*, is utilized as fuel—such as for locomotives on the Transcaspian railway—and as lubricating oil.

Production levels saw a massive increase between the mid-19th and early 20th centuries. In 1863, the output of crude naphtha was only 5,500 tons, but by 1904, it had risen to 9,833,600 tons. However, business suffered due to a serious fire in 1905.

The oil-fields are located around the town of Baku. Key fields include:
* **Balakhany-Sabunchi-Romany**: The largest field, covering 6 sq. m. and located 8-1/2 m. north of the town.
* **Bibi-Eybat**: Located 3-1/2 m. south of the town.
* **The "black town"**: Associated with Nobel, located 2 m. south-east.
* **The "white town"**: Associated with Rothschild.

In 1904-1905, general political anarchy led to serious conflicts between Armenians and Tatars, resulting in the burning of two-thirds of the Bibi-Eybat and Balakhany oil-works.

## Transport and Export
The transport of oil from Baku to the Black Sea is divided by the weight of the oil:
* **Lighter oil**: This is conveyed via pipes to Batum on the Black Sea, where it is shipped for export.
* **Heavier oils**: These are transported in tank railway-cars to the ports of Poti, Novorossiysk, and Batum.

Batum and Poti serve as the two chief ports of Caucasia. A railway connects the Caspian Sea to these ports via the Suram tunnel and Tiflis. Specifically, the rail distance from Baku to Poti is 536 m. and to Batum is 560 m.

## The Town of Baku
Baku serves as a major depot for Russian manufactured goods and merchandise from Transcaspia and Persia, including silk, raw cotton, fish, wine, timber, dried fruit, and rice. The town's infrastructure includes petroleum refineries, oil-works for fuel, tobacco factories, flour-mills, and sulphuric acid works. Its harbor features dry docks, engineering works, and stores belonging to the Russian Caspian fleet.

The town is divided into a "new town" with a long stone quay and an "old town" characterized by narrow, winding streets and an oriental appearance. Historical sites in the old town include:
* A 16th-century palace of the native khans.
* Mosques built by Persian shahs in 1078, which now serve as an arsenal.
* The "maidens' tower," which has been converted into a lighthouse.
* 9th and 10th-century Arabic architecture visible in ancient walls projecting above the sea.

Baku was mentioned by the Arab geographer Masudi in the 10th century. It was possessed by the Persians from 1509 until it was captured by the Russians in 1723. Though restored to the Persians in 1735, it was incorporated into the Russian empire in 1806.
