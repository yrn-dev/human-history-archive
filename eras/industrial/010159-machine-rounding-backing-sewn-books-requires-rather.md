# Machine Rounding and Backing of Sewn Books

The process of rounding and backing sewn books is a technical operation in commercial bookbinding that has been transitioned from labor-intensive hand work to the use of ingenious machinery. While traditional hand methods for sewing and rounding were considered safer, stronger, and less likely to bruise or injure the paper, they were also slow and required highly skilled labor. Consequently, larger trade binders have adopted machines to execute these tasks efficiently.

## The Rounding and Backing Process
The machine used for rounding and backing requires a very careful and elaborate setting of several parts to meet the exact requirements of the specific book size being processed. The operation begins with a sewn book that has a glued back; this book is caught in a clip and forced between two tight rollers. This action automatically transforms the previously flat back into a rounded shape.

Following this, the book is drawn forward by a continuous onward movement until it reaches the rounding plate. This plate consists of a block of steel featuring a polished groove slightly larger than the required size. The rounding plate moves within a small arc, powered by heavy counter-weights. As the back of the book is pressed strongly against this plate, it acquires the permanent form of the groove. Simultaneously, a strong grip on each side of the book causes a ledge to rise along each outer edge of the back.

## Significance of the Ledge
The creation of this ledge is a critical technical advancement. This ledge allows the boards of the book to be fixed so that they hinge on a line outside the actual and natural boundary of the book. Before this method was discovered, book boards hinged on a line that coincided with the inner edges of the back. This older method resulted in a destructive and disagreeable peculiarity: when the book was opened, there was a constant tendency for the book to open and pull away the vellum or paper of the outer sections.

## Machine Capacity and Application
Once properly set, these machines are capable of rounding and backing approximately 750 volumes of the same size within a single hour. Because most modern bookbinding machines are driven by power and must be accurately set to a particular size, they are not suitable for binding books of varying sizes. The full advantage of such machinery is realized only when producing a large edition of a single book.

## Related Binding Processes
Rounding and backing is part of a broader suite of mechanized binding processes. Other technical developments include:

*   **Sewing:** Books may be sewn by machines on bands (either round or flat) or via a chain stitch. Band-working machines pull the return thread through the upper and lower edges of the back of each section, which can weaken the section, though this may be neutralized by careful head-banding. The chain stitch system brings the return thread inside each section, which prevents the cutting of edges but can cause a flattening of the back, creating difficulties during the covering process. The Martini book-sewing machine represents an improvement by sewing on tape without cutting the head or tail and forming complete Kettle stitches.
*   **Case-making:** A large and complicated machine is used to create "case" covers. This machine utilizes over fifty springs and a series of rollers, gouges, and raisers to glue cardboard boards and a cardboard back to a roll of cloth, finishing the corners and edges. This machine can produce 1,200 cases per hour.

While these machines offer great savings in time and labor, high-class trade binders often continue to use older hand methods, and private binders generally do not use machines at all.
