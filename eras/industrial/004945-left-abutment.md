# Abutments in Bridge and Arch Construction

In the context of structural engineering and architecture, an abutment serves as a primary support structure at the ends of a bridge or arch. Its function varies depending on the type of superstructure employed, ranging from simple vertical load-bearing to the management of complex horizontal thrusts and the facilitation of mechanical movement.

## Structural Functions by Superstructure Type

The requirements for an abutment are determined by the nature of the superstructure it supports:

*   **Girders:** When girders form the superstructure, the resultant pressure exerted on the abutments or piers is vertical. Consequently, the dimensions of these supports are regulated based on their ability to bear this vertical load.
*   **Arches:** Arches consist of wedge-shaped blocks that transmit superimposed weight through the blocks to the abutments upon which the arch ends rest. Because arches create an outward thrust, the abutment must be designed to distribute this resultant thrust to the foundation in a safe direction to prevent undue compression. The stability of these supports is critical, as even slight movement in the abutments can lead to deflection and failure.
*   **Suspension Bridges:** For these structures, the abutment acting as the anchorage must be designed for thorough stability to withstand the greatest pull exerted by the chains.

## Calculation of Reactions and Stresses

In the analysis of beam girder or truss bridges, the abutments are the points where reactions to loading forces are measured. For a beam with a system of loads ($W_1, W_2, \dots W_n$), the reaction at the right abutment ($R_2$) is calculated as $W_1x_1/l + W_2x_2/l + \dots$. The reaction at the left abutment ($R_1$) is then determined by subtracting $R_2$ from the sum of all loads ($W_1 + W_2 + \dots$).

When considering a girder of span $l$ with a fixed load $W$ located at distance $m$ from the right abutment, the reactions are $R_1 = Wm/l$ and $R_2 = W(l-m)/l$. In cases involving a uniform load $w$ per foot run, the total load is $wl$, and the reactions at both abutments are equal: $R_1 = R_2 = \frac{1}{2}wl$.

## Bending Moments and Travelling Loads

The position of a load relative to the left abutment significantly affects the bending moment and shear of a girder. For a travelling live load of $w$ per foot advancing from the left abutment, with its center at distance $x$ from that abutment, the reaction at $B$ is $2wx^2/l$. The bending moment at any section $C$ (at distance $m$ from the left abutment) is $2wx^2/(l-m)/l$. This moment increases as $x$ increases, reaching its maximum when the span is completely covered.

In the case of a series of travelling loads at fixed distances passing from the left, where $W_1$ and $W_2$ are resultants at distances $x$ and $x+a$ from the left abutment, the reaction at $B$ is $W_1x/l + W_2(x+a)/l$.

## Specialized Bridge Types

Certain bridge designs utilize abutments for mechanical movement or balance:

*   **Rolling Bridges:** In these designs, the girders are longer than the span. The portion overhanging the abutment is counter-weighted so that the center of gravity remains over the abutment when the bridge is rolled forward.
*   **Draw or Bascule Bridges:** In the original fortress draw-bridge type, a single leaf (bascule) turns around a horizontal hinge located at one abutment. More common versions feature two bascules, one hinged at each abutment. These bascules are prolonged backwards beyond the hinge to maintain balance; this prolongation sinks into the piers during opening.

## Interdependence in Masonry

From a construction standpoint, a masonry arch is not a complete piece of construction in itself because it requires abutments to prevent it from falling to pieces. In a series of arches, each arch may rely on collateral arches to serve as its abutments, with the entire series ultimately depending on the bridge's ultimate abutments for stability. While older bridges used widely distended masses to make each pier sufficient to abut the springing arches, this often choked the river or led to the destruction of the structure or the river banks.
