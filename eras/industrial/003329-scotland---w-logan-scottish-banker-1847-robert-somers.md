# SCOTLAND

## Banking and Financial Systems
The banking system in Scotland was influenced by policies established by Sir Robert Peel in the 1844-1845 acts, which differed from those applied in England. Under these acts, Scottish banks were permitted to exceed their authorized issues provided they held specie equal to the amount of the excess. Additionally, no restrictions were placed on the amalgamation of banks within the country, and the use of notes for amounts less than £5 continued to be allowed. Consequently, ten large banks in Scotland possess the power to issue notes, a capability that has significantly assisted the large proportion of local branches.

## Nobility and Earldoms
The earldom of Mar and Buchan was among the seven original Scottish earldoms. Over time, Buchan was separated from Mar. Early earls of Buchan included Henry Beaumont (d. 1340) and the constables of Scotland, John Comyn (d. c. 1313) and Alexander Comyn (d. 1289). Isabel, the wife of John Comyn, crowned Robert the Bruce as king at Scone in 1306. By approximately 1382, the earldom was held by Sir Alexander Stewart, known as the "wolf of Badenoch," a son of King Robert II. The Stewarts held the earldom for roughly a century and a half, including the Scottish regent Robert, duke of Albany, and his son John, who was killed at the battle of Verneuil in 1424. In 1617, the earldom passed to James Erskine (d. 1640), and has been retained by the Erskines since.

The Scottish earls of March descended from Crinan. His grandson Cospatrick was welcomed by King Malcolm Canmore and granted Dunbar and adjoining lands after being deprived of the earldom of Northumberland. Waltheof, a descendant, was the first to be styled "Comes de Dunbar" around 1174 and served as a hostage for the Treaty of Falaise in 1175. Later, Patrick, 7th earl, helped liberate King Alexander III in 1255 and signed the Treaty of Perth in 1266, through which the Hebrides and the Isle of Man were ceded to Scotland by Magnus VI of Norway.

Other prominent families include the Kennedys of Ayrshire, whose chief seat is at Culzean or Colzean. John Kennedy of Dunure obtained lands in Ayrshire around 1350. His descendant, Sir James Kennedy, married Mary, a daughter of King Robert III. Another descendant, James Kennedy (c. 1406-1465), served as bishop of St Andrews from 1441 and founded St Salvator's college.

## Political and Religious History
The 17th and 18th centuries were marked by religious and political strife. Robert Baillie (1602-1662), a divine born in Glasgow, was a member of the Glasgow Assembly in 1638 and served as a chaplain to the Scottish army. He was one of five Scottish clergymen sent to the Westminster Assembly in 1643 and later served as principal of Glasgow University. Baillie's letters provide a historical record of this period, which saw the establishment of episcopacy in Scotland.

During the same era, Robert Burnet (1592-1661), a lord of session, refused to sign the Scottish Covenant, leading to his retirement and two periods of exile. His son, Gilbert Burnet (1643-1715), was born in Edinburgh and educated at Marischal College. Gilbert Burnet's religious views were influenced by Robert Leighton, who sought peace in the church.

The Jacobite movement saw leaders such as William Gordon, 6th viscount Kenmure. On October 12, 1715, Kenmure raised the royal standard of Scotland at Lochmaben. He joined forces with Thomas Forster and James Radclyffe, 3rd earl of Derwentwater, at Rothbury. After crossing the English border to join Lancashire Jacobites, Kenmure was captured at Preston on November 13. He was executed on Tower Hill on February 24 following a trial before the House of Lords.

## Literature and Scholarship
Scotland has produced various notable writers and historians. Robert Michael Ballantyne (1825-1894), born in Edinburgh, was a writer of fiction who wrote based on personal knowledge of the scenes he described. His works include *The Young Fur-Traders* (1856), *The Coral Island* (1857), and *The World of Ice* (1859), among over a hundred other books. He also exhibited water-colours at the Royal Scottish Academy.

John Hill Burton (1809-1881), born in Aberdeen, was a jurist and historical writer. He practiced as an advocate and served as editor of the *Scotsman*. His major works include the *Manual of the Law of Scotland* (1839), *Life of David Hume* (1846), and a *History of Scotland* completed in 1870. He also served as secretary to the prison board and editor of the *National Scottish Registers*.
