# The Electric Nerve: The Telegraph and the Shrinking World

## Overview
During the Industrial Revolution, the speed of information was limited by the speed of physical transport—horses, trains, and steamships. The invention of the electrical telegraph in the early 19th century fundamentally decoupled communication from transportation, allowing data to travel nearly instantaneously across vast distances. While terrestrial lines quickly linked cities, the true geopolitical shift occurred with the laying of submarine cables, which effectively "shrank" the planet and integrated global markets and imperial administrations in real-time.

## Timeline of Connectivity

| :--- | :--- | :--- |

## Key Facts
The telegraph relied on the transmission of electrical pulses over wires, translated into text via codes—most notably **Morse Code**. This system transformed several sectors of society:

*   **Economics:** Before the telegraph, commodity prices varied wildly between cities. The telegraph created a synchronized global market, allowing traders in London and New York to coordinate prices instantly.
*   **Governance and War:** Empires, particularly the British Empire, used the "All Red Line" (a network of cables touching only British soil) to maintain tighter control over distant colonies. In warfare, the Crimean War demonstrated the telegraph's power to bring news of the front lines to the home public almost immediately.
*   **Journalism:** The "inverted pyramid" style of writing emerged because telegraph lines were expensive and prone to failure; journalists learned to put the most important facts first in case the connection dropped.

**Historical Debate:** Historians often debate the extent to which the telegraph truly "globalized" the world. Some argue it was the primary catalyst for modern globalization, while others suggest it merely reinforced existing imperial power structures, serving as a tool for colonial surveillance rather than a bridge for international cooperation.

## Did You Know?
1. **The 1858 Failure:** The first transatlantic cable worked for only three weeks. The chief engineer, Wildman Whitehouse, attempted to increase the voltage to boost the signal, which accidentally fried the cable's insulation.
2. **The "Victorian Internet":** Historians often call the telegraph the "Victorian Internet" because it created the first global social network and introduced the concept of "instant messaging."
3. **The Cable Ships:** The *Great Eastern*, the largest ship in the world at the time, was the only vessel capable of carrying the massive weight of the cable required to reach the ocean floor.
