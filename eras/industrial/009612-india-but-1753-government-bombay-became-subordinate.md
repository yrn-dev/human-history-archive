# Bombay Presidency and Administration

## Early English Settlements and Acquisition
The English presence in the Bombay presidency began in 1618 with the establishment of a factory at Surat by the East India Company, which operated under a charter from Emperor Jahangir. Early attempts to secure the island of Bombay were unsuccessful, including a joint effort by the English and Dutch in 1626. In 1653, proposals were made to purchase the island from the Portuguese. The island was eventually ceded to the English crown in 1661 as part of the dower for the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II.

Due to the poor administration of crown officers and a low estimation of the acquisition's value in England, Bombay was transferred to the East India Company in 1668 for an annual payment of £10. With this transfer, the Company was granted powers for the administration of justice and defense. The Company enrolled a European regiment and constructed fortifications, which successfully deterred a Dutch attack in 1673.

## Administrative Evolution
In 1687, Bombay was designated as the head of all East India Company possessions in India. However, this status changed in 1753 when the government of Bombay became subordinate to that of Calcutta. By 1773, an act of parliament established that the presidency of Bengal would exercise control over other Company possessions, with the chief of that presidency styled as governor-general. In 1834, the governor-general of Bengal became the governor-general of India.

The government of Bombay maintained authority over several areas and states, including Cutch, Bhaunagar, Kolhapur (and its dependencies), and Khairpur (Sind). Additionally, the settlement of Aden, along with Sokotra Island and Perim, formed part of the government of Bombay.

## Territorial Expansion and Conflict
The British engaged in conflicts with the Mahratta power, the first of which began in 1774. This resulted in the 1782 treaty of Salbai, where Salsette was ceded to the British and Broach was handed to Sindhia. The second Mahratta war ended in 1803; prior to this, Surat had been annexed in 1800. Following the war, the East India Company received the districts of Kaira and Broach. 

Broach (or Bharuch), located on the right bank of the Nerbudda, has a long history as the Barygaza of Arrian and Ptolemy and the Barakacheva of the Chinese traveller Hsüan Tsang. It was part of the Gujarat kingdom under Mahommedan conquest, then annexed to the Mogul empire by Akbar in 1572 and governed by a Nawab. The Mahrattas took control in 1685, holding it under the peshwa until 1772, when it was captured by General Wedderburn. After being ceded by the British to Sindhia in 1783, it was stormed by Colonel Woodington in 1803 and finally ceded to the East India Company.

Other administrative divisions included East and West Khandesh in the central division of Bombay, which were formed in 1906 from a single district. As of 1901, East Khandesh (headquartered at Jalgaon) had a population of 957,728, and West Khandesh (headquartered at Dhulia) had a population of 469,654.

## Economic Development and Infrastructure
The annexation of the Deccan and victory over the Mahrattas led to a period of unrestricted development for Bombay. Under the influence of Mountstuart Elphinstone in 1819, the city grew significantly; its population rose from 236,000 in 1838 to 821,000 by 1891. This growth was driven by the foundation of cotton factories, the opening of the Suez Canal, and the development of railways. The first railway line was completed in 1853.

By 1905, the Bombay government had 7,980 miles of railway open for traffic, though this excluded the system in Sind. Most lines concentrated at Bombay City, including the Indian Midland, Great Indian Peninsula, Southern Mahratta, Rajputana-Malwa, and Bombay, Baroda & Central India systems. The North-Western line served as an exception, terminating at Karachi. Regarding irrigation, the water supply in the Bombay Presidency, with the exception of Sind, was not suited for large irrigation works.

The city's prosperity faced setbacks, including a financial crisis in 1866-1867 caused by falling cotton prices after the American war. Later, in the decade following 1891, plague caused a 6% decline in population and a 7% decline in exports.

## Notable Figures and Military Movements
Sir George Christopher Molesworth Birdwood (1832– ) was a prominent Anglo-Indian official born in Belgaum, Bombay presidency. He served in the Bombay Medical Service starting in 1854 and the Persian War of 1856-57. He held various roles, including sheriff at Bombay and professor at Grant Medical College. He authored a work on the *Economic Vegetable Products of the Bombay Presidency* and contributed to the conversion of the *Standard* into the *Times of India*.

In September 1914, Bombay served as a critical transit point for military movements. Plans were made to send transports carrying Territorial Divisions and batteries of artillery to Bombay to facilitate the exchange of British Indian regular artillery and infantry battalions for Territorial units being dispatched to France and German East Africa.
