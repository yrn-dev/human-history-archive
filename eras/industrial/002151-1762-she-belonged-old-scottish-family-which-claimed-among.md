# Joanna Baillie

Joanna Baillie (1762–1851) was a British dramatist and poet. She was born on September 11, 1762, at the manse of Bothwell, located on the banks of the Clyde. She was a member of an old Scottish family that claimed Sir William Wallace as one of its ancestors.

## Early Life and Education
Baillie received an excellent education and began writing poetry at a very early age. In her youth, she moved to London with her sister, Agnes, to join their brother, Dr. Matthew Baillie, who had already settled there. The two sisters eventually took up residence at Hampstead, which was then on the outskirts of London, after inheriting a small competence from their uncle, Dr. William Hunter. Baillie spent the remainder of her life living in Hampstead.

## Literary Career and Poetry
Baillie's first published work was a volume titled *Fugitive Verses*, released anonymously in 1790. However, she is most noted for her later contributions to drama and the study of human emotion.

## Plays on the Passions
In 1798, Baillie produced the first volume of *A Series of Plays*. This work was designed as a study of the "plays on the passions," intended to illustrate the deepest and strongest passions of the human mind—including love, fear, jealousy, and hate. Her method involved using both a comedy and a tragedy to exhibit how an individual acts under the influence of these specific passions.

Although the first volume was published anonymously and its authorship was initially attributed to Sir Walter Scott, Baillie was soon discovered as the author. The work achieved considerable success and was expanded through subsequent publications:
* A second volume was published in 1802.
* A third volume followed in 1812.
* Three volumes of *Dramas* were released in 1836.

In addition to this series, she published *Miscellaneous Plays* in 1804 and *The Family Legend* in 1810.

## Stage Success and Critical Reception
While Baillie intended her plays for the stage rather than for private reading ("the closet"), they met with varying degrees of success. *The Family Legend*, published in Edinburgh in 1810 under the enthusiastic patronage of Sir Walter Scott, enjoyed a brief but brilliant success. *De Monfort* had a short run in London, aided primarily by the performances of Mrs. Siddons and John Kemble. Other works, such as *The Separation* and *Henriquez*, were received coldly.

Critics have noted that, with few exceptions, Baillie's plays are unsuited for stage exhibition. This is attributed to a flaw in her fundamental concept—the idea of an individual serving as the embodiment of a single passion—as well as a lack of incident and a focus on a single point, which created insuperable obstacles for their success as acting pieces.
