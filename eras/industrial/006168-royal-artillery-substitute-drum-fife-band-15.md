# Royal Artillery Brass Band

The Royal Artillery Brass Band evolved from a specialized bugle-band created as a substitute for the traditional drum and fife band. Its development reflects broader 19th-century trends in military instrumentation, specifically the transition from signal instruments to chromatic musical ensembles.

## Origins and the Bugle-Band
The use of the bugle in the English military was closely tied to the creation of light infantry, where it gradually replaced the drum as an instrument for duty and signals. This transition began in the 17th century, following improvements in firearms that led infantry to abandon armor. By the end of the 18th century, the bugle was the recognized signal instrument for light infantry, while the trumpet remained the standard for the cavalry.

Within the Royal Artillery, a bugle-band was established to replace the drum and fife band. The responsibility for the training and organization of this ensemble was given to Trumpet-major James Lawson. Under Lawson's leadership, the band reached a high standard of excellence.

## Technical Evolution and Instrumentation
Trumpet-major Lawson, an accomplished cornet player, found the scale of the standard service bugle to be too restricted for his musical needs. He obtained permission to add a valve attachment to the instrument. This modification transformed the bugle into a chromatic instrument, making it practically a saxhorn and similar to a cornet.

Following this innovation, the ensemble expanded its instrumentation to include several copper instruments:
* Tenor horns in B flat
* Horns in E flat
* Euphoniums
* Bass tubas

This development mirrored a wider trend during much of the 19th century, where the bugle—developed via valves into tubas and saxhorns, or via keys into the ophicleide and Royal Kent bugle—served as the nucleus for brass bands across many countries.

## Transition to the R.A. Brass Band
As the ensemble evolved from a signal-based group into a full musical unit, its official designation changed. In 1869, the "bugle band" was renamed the R.A. Brass Band. The ensemble continued to operate under this name until 1877, at which point it was merged into the Mounted Band.

## Historical Context of Artillery Music
The history of music within the Royal Artillery also includes the use of kettledrums. Before the formal establishment of the Royal Regiment of Artillery, the master-general of ordnance was responsible for raising artillery trains, which included a kettledrummer and a trumpeter in his retinue during wartime. These kettledrums were mounted on a carriage drawn by six white horses. 

Kettledrums appeared in an artillery train during the Irish rebellion of 1689, with records showing a cost of £158, 9s. for large drums with cloths marked "I.R." They were later used in the Vigo expedition and the 1748 Flanders campaign. During these periods, the drums were mounted on a gilt, ornamented triumphal car that bore the ordnance flag. The kettledrummer was required to beat the drums from the carriage every night for half an hour before sunset until the firing of the guns. However, by 1759, kettledrums were removed from the Royal Artillery establishment and deposited in the Tower.
