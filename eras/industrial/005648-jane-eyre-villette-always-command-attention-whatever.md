# The Brontë Sisters and Their Literary Works

The Brontë sisters—Charlotte, Emily, and Anne—produced a body of work characterized by intensity and a deep connection to their own forlorn lives. Their achievements in English literature are viewed as standing on their own merits, though their life story, as recounted by biographer Mrs. Gaskell, is considered as interesting as any novel.

## Charlotte Brontë and Her Novels
Charlotte Brontë (1816–1855) is recognized primarily as a novelist. While her work as a maker of verse is described as undistinguished, her novels contain passages of magnificence and pure poetry, particularly *Villette*. Her literary convictions centered on the belief that the mutual passionate love between one man and one woman is sacred, creating a center of joy, energy, and the highest life in the world.

Her path to publication began with a novel titled *The Master*, which was later retitled *The Professor*. This manuscript was sent to the firm Smith, Elder & Co., where the reader R. Smith Williams and his employers were impressed, though they noted the work lacked "varied interest" and was too short for the then-imperative three-volume form. In response to a request for a longer novel, Charlotte provided *Jane Eyre*, which was published in October 1847. The novel was wildly acclaimed, though it shocked the primness of certain coteries due to the unconcealed ardour of its love passages. It also received a critical counterblast in December 1848 from Miss Rigby (later Lady Eastlake) in the *Quarterly*.

In *Jane Eyre*, Charlotte used the "thin disguise" of a school called "Lowood" to criticize the Clergy Daughters' school at Cowan Bridge, which Charlotte and Emily had attended in 1824. The school's principal, the Rev. William Carus Wilson, is accepted as the counterpart to the novel's Mr. Naomi Brocklehurst.

Following *Jane Eyre*, Charlotte wrote *Shirley*, which was published in October 1849. She worked hard on *Villette* during 1852, and it was published in January 1853. Both *Jane Eyre* and *Villette* are noted for their rough individuality, independence, and intensity, qualities that ensure they will continue to command attention regardless of the future of English fiction.

## Emily and Anne Brontë
Emily Brontë is regarded as a great novelist and poet. Her poetry, specifically "Last Lines" and "Old Stoic," is cited as perhaps the finest achievement of poetry by any woman in English literature. Her novel, *Wuthering Heights*, is described as a monument of intensity—passionate, haunting, and grey in its melancholy—that owes nothing to earlier writers or tradition.

Anne Brontë's two novels survive primarily through the vitality of the Brontë tradition, though they are constantly reprinted. She also held a place in most religious communities as a hymn writer. Her novels include *Agnes Gray* and *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall*.

## Publication History and Personal Tragedy
The novels of Emily (*Wuthering Heights*) and Anne (*Agnes Gray*) were accepted by T. C. Newby and published together in three volumes in December 1847. Newby later published *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall* in June 1848, attempting to persuade the public that these works were written by the author of *Jane Eyre*. This prompted Charlotte and Anne to visit London to establish their separate identities.

The sisters suffered a series of rapid losses in 1848 and 1849. Their brother Branwell died on September 24, 1848, followed by Emily on December 19, 1848. Anne died on May 28, 1849, in Scarborough, where Charlotte had taken her in hopes that the sea air would revive her. In a span of eight months, Charlotte lost all three of her youth companions and returned to Haworth to care for her father, who was becoming blind.

## Biographical Records
The *Life of Charlotte Brontë* by Mrs. Gaskell was first published in 1857. The third edition was revised due to controversies regarding the identity of Lowood, the peculiarities of Mr. Brontë, and the relations of Branwell Brontë with his employer's wife. The Haworth edition of 1902 is considered the most satisfactory version and includes letters from Charlotte to George Smith. Other significant biographical contributions include works by T. Wemyss Reid, A.C. Swinburne, Clement Shorter, Augustine Birrell, William Wright, and Madame Duclaux.
