# Bombay Presidency

The Bombay Presidency was a province of British India consisting of a combination of British districts and native states, all under the administration of a governor. Geographically, the territory extended from 13 deg. 53' to 28 deg. 45' N. and from 66 deg. 40' to 76 deg. 30' E. It was bounded by the Arabian Sea to the west, Madras and Mysore to the south, Indore, Hyderabad, and the Central Provinces to the east, and Rajputana, the Punjab, and Baluchistan to the north. The total area of the presidency, including Sind but excluding Aden, was 188,745 sq. m., with 122,984 sq. m. under British rule and 65,761 sq. m. under native rule. As of 1901, the total population was 25,468,209.

## Territorial Evolution and Administration
In 1803, the presidency was limited to Surat, Bankot, Salsette, and the islands of the harbour. Between 1803 and 1827, the presidency expanded into its primary shape. The Gujarat districts were acquired in 1805 and enlarged in 1818, while the settlement of Mahi Kantha and Kathiawar began between 1807 and 1820. Following the defeat and capture of Baji Rao, the last of the peshwas, in 1817-1818, large portions of his dominions—including Nasik, Poona, Ahmednagar, Sholapur, Belgaum, Dharwar, and Kaladgi—were incorporated into the presidency. 

The settlement of these areas was completed by Governor Mountstuart Elphinstone (1819-1827), who sought to rule on native lines and avoid changes for which the population was not yet ready, though he did codify laws, establish schools and courts, and stop the grosser abuses of the previous regime. Later expansions included the addition of Sind (1843), Aden (1839), and the 1853 lease of the Panch Mahals from Sindhia.

The province was organized into twenty-six districts and four commissionerships: Sind, the southern (Carnatic), the central (Deccan), and the northern (Gujarat). The native states, totaling 353 units, were managed by collectors of the districts or political agents. These states were grouped into several agencies, including North Gujarat (which included Cutch and the agencies of Palanpur, Rewa Kantha, Mahi Kantha, and Kathiawar), South Gujarat, North Konkan, South Konkan and Dharwar, the Deccan Satara Jagirs, and the southern Mahratta states.

## Regional Geography and History
The presidency included several distinct regions and foreign settlements. The Portuguese settlements of Goa, Damaun, and Diu were located within its limits. Damaun, the capital of its own settlement, was divided into Damaun proper and the pargana of Nagar Havili. The Portuguese established a permanent settlement there in 1558, and the Mahrattas ceded Nagar Havili to them in 1780.

In the southern regions, the district of Belgaum (anciently known as Venugrama) had a long history of shifting rule. It was once the capital of a dynasty of nine Kadamba kings at Halsi. It was held by the Chalukyas from the middle of the 6th century until approximately 760, followed by the Rashtrakutas and later the Rattas. After the Delhi emperor overthrew the Yadavas in 1320, Belgaum was briefly under his rule before the northern part was conquered by the Bahmani dynasty in 1347. Following the overthrow of the Bijapur sultans by Aurungzeb in 1686, it passed to the Moguls, then to Hyder Ali in 1776, and finally back to the Peshwa with British help. In 1818, it was handed to the East India Company as part of the Dharwar district; in 1836, the area was split into the northern Belgaum district and the southern Dharwar district.

The district of Bijapur, formerly called Kaladgi, consisted of a barren plain. Its population in 1901 was 735,435, reflecting declines caused by severe famines in 1876-1878 and 1899-1000.

## Economy and Agriculture
Agriculture varied by region. In Khandesh and the Deccan, the staple food grains were bajra and joar. Rice was the primary product of the Konkan. Wheat, grown mainly in Gujarat and Sind, was exported in large quantities from Bombay and Karachi. Barley was grown in the north, while aboriginal tribes such as the Waralis, Bhils, and Kolis relied on inferior grains like kodra and nachani grown on hill-sides.

Important oil-seeds included mustard, linseed, safflower, castor-oil, and til. Fibres included sunn (tag), Deccan hemp, and cotton. Efforts were made to improve cotton; American varieties were introduced with advantage in the southern Mahratta country and the Dharwar collectorate. In Khandesh, the superior Hinganghat variety largely replaced the indigenous plant. Other crops included tobacco, turmeric, potatoes, red pepper, and sugar-cane.

Economic prosperity peaked under Sir Bartle Frere (1862-1867) due to the American Civil War, which created an enormous European demand for Indian cotton. This led to a period of intense speculation.

## Military Organization
Under the 1904 re-arrangement by Lord Kitchener, the Bombay command was replaced by the Western army corps, led by a lieutenant-general and divided into three divisions:
*   **4th Division (Quetta):** Managed troops in the Sind and Quetta districts.
*   **5th Division (Mhow):** Included the Bombay district north of the Tapti and the districts of Deesa, Nerbudda, Nagpur, Bundelkhand, and Mhow.
*   **6th Division (Poona):** Included the Bombay district south of the Tapti, the Aurungabad and Dharwar districts, and the Belgaum district north of the Tungabhadra. Its brigades were located in Aden, Ahmednagar, and Bombay.
