# The Ostend Manifesto

The Ostend Manifesto was a diplomatic document agreed upon on October 18, 1854, by three ministers following a series of meetings held at Aix-la-Chapelle and Ostend. The manifesto was produced in accordance with instructions from President Pierce, who directed the ministers to adopt measures for "perfect concert of action" and to "compare opinions" regarding negotiations taking place in Madrid. These negotiations concerned reparations the United States was demanding from Spain for alleged injuries caused to American commerce with Cuba.

## Core Assertions and Objectives
In the manifesto, the three ministers—which included Pierre Soule and J.Y. Mason—argued that Cuba's geographical position made the island as necessary to the North American republic as any of its current members. The document highlighted the perceived danger to the United States should an insurrection occur in Cuba. 

The ministers asserted that permitting Cuba to become "Africanized" and turn into a "second Santo Domingo" would be a betrayal of their duty and their "gallant forefathers." They claimed such a development would bring "horrors to the white race" and allow "flames to extend" to neighboring shores, which they argued would seriously endanger or actually destroy the "fair fabric" of the Union. Consequently, the manifesto recommended that the United States purchase Cuba as soon as it was practicable, arguing that such a sale would be a great advantage to Spain.

## Justification for Seizure
The most startling element of the manifesto was the declaration regarding the possibility of Spain refusing to sell the island. The ministers stated that if the United States offered a price "far beyond its present value" and Spain still refused, and if Cuba's possession by Spain seriously endangered the existence of the "cherished Union" and internal peace, then the United States would be justified in "wresting it from Spain" by "every law, human and divine," provided the power to do so existed.

## Political Impact and Aftermath
The manifesto was met with bitter attacks in the North. However, for some involved, it served a strategic political purpose. For example, by joining Pierre Soule and J.Y. Mason in issuing the manifesto, another participant was able to retain the good-will of the South. 

This alignment with Southern interests, combined with the fact that he was abroad during the Kansas-Nebraska debates—thereby avoiding the unpopularity that affected President Pierce and Stephen A. Douglas—contributed to his later political success. Upon returning from England in 1856, he was nominated by the Democrats as a compromise candidate for president and was subsequently elected, winning 174 electoral votes against 114 for Republican John C. Frémont and 8 for Millard Fillmore.
