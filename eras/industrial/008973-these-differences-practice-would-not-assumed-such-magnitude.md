# Material and Structural Magnitudes in Industrial and Civil Engineering

## Metallurgy and Boiler Construction
In the field of industrial fabrication, specifically within boiler and plating shops, differences in practice have assumed significant magnitude due to the introduction of mild steel as a replacement for malleable iron. The two materials exhibit distinct physical properties that dictate their treatment. Iron is less susceptible to irregular heating and overheating than steel. Conversely, while steel possesses higher ductility, it is more prone to developing cracks if it is subjected to improper treatment. These characteristics were evident in early steel testing and continue to influence contemporary practice.

The specific requirements of boiler and plating shops—namely the necessity to insert and create an enormous number of rivet holes—have directly influenced machine design. Because punching or drilling holes individually is considered too slow for best practice, machines are designed to produce multiple holes simultaneously. Furthermore, different types of machines are utilized for different boiler sections; some are dedicated to furnaces, some to shells, and others are specialized for specific types of shells or furnaces. Modern machines are generally capable of being adjusted to drill to an exact pitch, which removes the need to mark out holes as guides.

## Bridge Design and Load Distribution
In civil engineering, the methods for determining maximum bending moments vary based on the length of the bridge. For short bridges, the preferred method is to design the girder by drawing the curve of maximum bending moments for a typical set of assumed loads. For longer bridges, the funicular polygon provides a more convenient method for these determinations.

High precision in drawing this curve is not required because the distribution and precise magnitude of loads cannot be known due to the wide variation in railway rolling stock. Engineers instead assume a set of loads likely to produce straining that is somewhat severer than any probable actual rolling loads. Except in cases of very unequal loads or very short bridges, a parabola can be used to include the curve of maximum moments; this parabola represents the curve of maximum moments for a travelling load that is uniform per foot run. 

The uniform load per foot equivalent to any assumed set of concentrated loads is denoted as $w_e$. While Waddell has produced tables for these equivalent uniform loads, $w_e$ can be found for practical purposes through experience. It is found that a parabola agrees closely enough for practical designing if it has the same ordinate at the center of the span, or the same ordinate at one-quarter span, as the curve of maximum moments. For a girder with a span of $2c$, the bending moment at a section distant $x$ from the center due to a uniform load $w_e$ per foot run is calculated accordingly.
