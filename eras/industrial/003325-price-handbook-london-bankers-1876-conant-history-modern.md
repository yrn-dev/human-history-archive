# Banking History and Literature

## Early English Banking Proposals
The establishment of banking in England is documented in state papers included in F. G. Hilton Price's *Handbook of London Bankers* (1876). One of the earliest records is a petition from November 1581 sent to Queen Elizabeth by Christopher Hagenbuck and his partners. Written in the original Italian, the petition proposed the institution of an office capable of receiving a very large sum of money annually without expense to the Queen. Hagenbuck claimed this method would ensure the Queen, the State, and the people were provided with any notable sum of money they wished, while simultaneously keeping the country in abundance and removing "extreme usuries" that affected the people and the monarchy.

In exchange for this plan, Hagenbuck requested 6% of the total money received by the office every year for a period of twenty years. Queen Elizabeth agreed to a grant of 4% for twenty years, a grant confirmed under the great seal and signed by Francis Walsingham. However, the records indicate that nothing further resulted from this proposal. It is suggested that the idea of such a bank may have become familiar to Hagenbuck through commerce with Venice, specifically the Banco della Piazza di Rialto. Similar proposals appeared in state papers in 1621, 1622, 1662, and 1666, though none were carried into practice.

## British Banking Literature and Theory
A wide array of literature exists regarding the theory and practice of banking in Britain. Early works include *De Monetarum Augmento, variatione et diminutione, Tractatus varii* (1509) and a 1697 proposal by A. D. of Grey's Inn and others to supply the King with twelve or fourteen million (or more) of money. Other significant texts include Hayes' *Negociators' Magazine of Monies and Exchanges* (1730) and Lord King's *Thoughts on Bank Restrictions* (1804).

The 19th century saw a proliferation of banking analysis, including William Cobbett's *Paper against Gold and Glory against Prosperity* (1815), Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873), and the works of J. W. Gilbart, such as *The History, Principles and Practice of Banking* (1882). Practical guides for the industry include George Rae's *The Country Banker* (1886) and J. George Kiddy's *The Country Banker's Handbook* (1903).

Specialized studies on the Bank of England include works by T. Fortune (1802), John Francis (1847), J. E. Thorold Rogers (1887), and Sir F. Schuster (1906). Additionally, regional banking histories have been documented for Manchester (L. H. Grindon), Bristol (C. H. Cave), and various areas of Northumberland, Durham, and North Yorkshire (M. Phillips).

## American Banking and Finance
The history of banking in the United States is covered by several authorities. William M. Gouge wrote *A History of Paper Money and Banking in the United States* (1833), and Albert S. Bolles produced a three-volume *Financial History of the United States* (1884-1886). Other key texts include William G. Sumner's *A History of American Currency* (1884) and *A History of Banking in the United States* (1896), as well as John Jay Knox's *History of Banking in the United States* (1900).

Specific American financial institutions and events are addressed in works such as R. C. H. Catterall's *The Second Bank of the United States* (1903), David Kinley's *The Independent Treasury of the United States* (1893), and J. S. Gibbons' study on the Banks of New York and the Panic of 1857.

## Charles Arthur Conant
Charles Arthur Conant was a notable figure in banking and finance. He served as the Treasurer of the Morton Trust Co. in New York from 1902 to 1906 and was a member of the Commission on International Exchange of the U.S. in 1903. His written contributions to the field include *A History of Modern Banks of Issue* (1896) and the two-volume *The Principles of Money and Banking* (1905).
