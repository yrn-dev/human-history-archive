# Educational Developments and Figures of the 19th Century

The 19th century was marked by significant shifts in the organization of public schooling, the professionalization of specialized medical fields, and the rise of national educational movements across various continents. These changes were often driven by individual reformers, the establishment of new academic institutions, and the transition from private or apprenticeship-based learning to systematic state or collegiate instruction.

## Educational Reform in the United States

In the United States, Henry Barnard (1811–1900) emerged as a prominent educationalist and reformer. After graduating from Yale in 1830 and entering the Connecticut bar in 1835, Barnard served in the Connecticut legislature from 1837 to 1839. During this tenure, he framed and introduced a bill in 1838 to establish a board of "commissioners of common schools" for better supervision of the state's common schools. Barnard served as the secretary of this board until 1842, working to reform and reorganize the system.

Barnard's influence extended to Rhode Island, where the governor appointed him in 1843 to examine public schools and recommend improvements, leading to a reorganization of the system two years later. He served as the state's first commissioner of public schools from 1845 to 1849. Later, between 1851 and 1855, he returned to Connecticut as the principal of the State Normal School at New Britain and the "superintendent of common schools." His career further included serving as the chancellor of the University of Wisconsin (1859–1860), president of St John's College in Annapolis, Maryland (1866), and the first United States commissioner of education (1867–1870), where he established the foundation for the Bureau of Education. Barnard also contributed extensively to educational literature, editing the *Connecticut Common School Journal* (1838–1842, 1851–1854), the *Journal of the Rhode Island Institute of Instruction* (1846–1849), and the *American Journal of Education* (1855–1881).

Other American figures focused on specific demographics. Catherine Esther (1800–1878) conducted schools for girls in Hartford, Connecticut (1822–1832) and Cincinnati (1832–1834). While she advocated for better primary schools and founded the National Board of Popular Education to send female teachers to the West and South, she radically opposed college education for women and woman suffrage, maintaining that the domestic sphere was the proper place for women.

## Professionalization of Dentistry

Until the 19th century, dentistry was learned primarily through apprenticeship, a system that often fostered quackery and secrecy. A shift toward professionalization began in 1837 with the formation of a local association of dentists in New York, followed by the creation of a national association, The American Society of Dental Surgeons, in 1840. This society aimed to advance the science through the interchange of sentiments and free communication.

The year 1839–1840 is regarded as the birth of professional growth in the field. In June 1839, the world's first dental periodical, *The American Journal of Dental Science*, was issued. This was followed in November 1840 by the establishment of the Baltimore College of Dental Surgery, the first college in the world dedicated to the systematic education of dentists. Because medical schools refused to provide dental instruction, the field developed separately from general medicine for a time. Over time, the curriculum increased in length and content to become practically equal to that of medical specialists.

## International Educational Movements

Across Europe and Asia, the 19th century saw the rise of national and state-led education.

### Europe
In France, Lazare Hippolyte Carnot (1801–1888) served as the minister of education in the provisional government following his election as deputy for Paris in March 1839. Carnot worked to organize primary school systems, proposing laws for free and obligatory primary instruction and secondary education for girls. He opposed purely secular schools, arguing that the schoolmaster and the minister were the two columns supporting the republic.

In Bulgaria, the first Bulgarian school was opened in 1835 at Gabrovo by Neophyt Rilski and Aprilov. Prior to 1878, many rural districts lacked educational systems, and town schools were supervised by the Greek clergy with Greek as the language of instruction. After liberation in 1878, primary education was declared obligatory, though a lack of qualified teachers hindered progress. By 1891, a system of "national" (*narodni*) primary schools was established, maintained by communes with state subventions.

In Albania, the development of a national educational movement was delayed by Turkish prohibitions on Albanian schools. It was not until the Young Turk revolution in July 1908 that numerous schools were founded and a national alphabet using the Latin character was adopted.

In Serbia, Belgrade became a center for high education, hosting the *Velika Shkola* (a small university with three faculties), a military academy, a theological seminary, and several secondary schools based on German models.

### Asia
In Japan, Meiji statesmen implemented speedy reforms, organizing an extensive system of compulsory public schools, colleges, and universities modeled on foreign lines. This system ensured that over ninety per cent of school-age children received an elementary education.

In India, Calcutta University was constituted in 1857 as an examining body based on the model of the University of London. The city's educational landscape included the Government Presidency College, government medical and engineering colleges, the Sanskrit College, the Mahommedan Madrasah, and various missionary and native colleges.

## Classical Scholarship and Schooling in England

Samuel Butler (1774–1839), an English classical scholar and the bishop of Lichfield, left a significant mark on English education through his headmastership of Shrewsbury school. Under his leadership, the school's reputation and scholarship rose to the level of any other public school in England. Butler, who was educated at Rugby and St John's College, Cambridge, authored a *Sketch of Modern and Ancient Geography* (1813) for use in schools and produced various atlases.
