# Gravimetric Density (G.D.) of Cordite Charges

## Definition and Concept
Gravimetric density, referred to by artillerists as G.D., is a term used to distinguish between the specific gravity (S.G.) of a powder when it is filling a given volume in a state of gas and the specific gravity of the separate solid cord or grain of powder. 

The concept is illustrated by comparing a solid mass to a packed charge. For example, a lump of solid lead has a specific gravity, but when that lead is formed into a charge of closely packed equal spherules (lead shot), the G.D. of the charge is 0.7403 of the S.G. of the solid lead. In the case of a bundle of cylindrical sticks of cordite, the G.D. of the charge is 0.9067 of the S.G. of a single stick of cordite.

## Cordite Composition and Physical Properties
Cordite is a smokeless propellant used by the British army and navy. It is produced by pressing the material in a soft, pasty state through dies or perforations in a steel plate using screw or hydraulic pressure, resulting in cylindrical rods or strings. These rods range in diameter from approximately 1 mm for revolver cartridges to 5 mm or more for heavy guns. The size designation refers to the diameter of the die hole in hundredths of an inch; for instance, size 30 cordite is squeezed through a hole 0.30 inches in diameter.

Two primary varieties are noted:
*   **Cordite Mark 1:** Devised in 1891 by the Ordnance Committee under Sir Frederick Abel, consisting of 58% nitro-glycerin, 37% gun-cotton, and 5% mineral jelly.
*   **Cordite M.D.:** A modification containing 65% gun-cotton, 30% nitro-glycerin, and 5% mineral jelly. This version offers a lower temperature, more regular pressure in the gun, higher velocities, and a slightly reduced rate of burning.

Physically, cordite is a brownish, waterproof colloidal substance with a density of about 1.56 at 15 degrees C. It is a poor conductor of heat and exhibits elasticity, particularly in the M.D. variety.

## Role of G.D. in Interior Ballistics
In interior ballistics, the primary challenge is adjusting the G.D. of the charge relative to the weight of the shot. This adjustment ensures that as the shot advances during the combustion of the cordite, the maximum pressure does not exceed safe limits. 

If a shot were not free to move, the closed chamber pressure resulting from the explosion of a charge at a G.D. of 0.5 would be nearly 49 tons per sq. in., which is considered unsafe. By adjusting the G.D., the pressure can be managed. For example, a limit of 16 tons per sq. in. corresponds to a G.D. of 0.2.

## Application in Range Tables
The G.D. is a critical component of charge descriptions in range tables. For a 6-inch gun, a charge may be described with a weight of 13 lb 4 oz, a nature of cordite size 30, and a gravimetric density expressed as 55.01/0.504. In this instance:
*   The G.D. is 0.504.
*   The figure 55.01 represents the specific volume of the charge in cubic inches per lb when filling the chamber in a state of gas.
*   The product of these two numbers (55.01 and 0.504) is 27.73.

The chamber capacity (C) is calculated by multiplying the weight (13.25 lb) by the specific volume (55.01), resulting in 730 cubic inches. This is equivalent to an equivalent length of the chamber (E.L.C.) of 2.15 ft or 25.8 inches.

## Pressure and Combustion
The relationship between G.D. and pressure is documented in ballistic tables. As G.D. increases, the pressure in tons per sq. in. also increases. For example:
*   At a G.D. of 0.05, the pressure is 20.00.
*   At a G.D. of 0.10, the pressure is 10.00.
*   At a G.D. of 0.20, the pressure is 8.00.
*   At a G.D. of 0.50, the pressure is 2.00.

During combustion, the powder-gas expands. For cordite, it is experimentally found that an average value of $m = 1.2$ is used when assuming the gas obeys an adiabatic law, where pressure varies inversely as the $m^{th}$ power of the volume.
