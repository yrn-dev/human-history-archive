# Railway Communications and the Great Central System

The railway infrastructure of England involves a complex network of main lines and branches operated by various companies, including the Great Central, the London & North-Western, and the Great Western railways. These systems facilitate the transport of passengers, industrial goods, and agricultural products between market towns and the metropolis of London.

## The Great Central Railway and Associated Lines
The Great Central railway operates a main line that continues north-westward from Quainton Road. In certain regions, it operates joint ventures; for instance, the Great Central and Great Western companies jointly own a line that passes through Prince's Risborough, High Wycombe, and Beaconsfield. This line was connected northward with the Great Central system upon its opening in 1906. Prior to this date, the only line serving Prince's Risborough and High Wycombe was the Great Western branch from Maidenhead to Oxford.

The Great Central system also utilizes the Metropolitan-Great Central joint line, which serves Aylesbury, Chesham (via a branch), and Amersham. This joint line connects with the North-Western Oxford branch at Verney Junction. Additionally, a light railway connects Quainton Road station with the large village of Brill to the south-west. In other regions, the Great Central system touches the north and north-east of certain counties, and in different areas, it provides connections such as the line from Wrexham to Connah's Quay, as well as services to Glyn Ceiriog.

## Banbury and Regional Connections
Banbury, a municipal borough and market town in Oxfordshire located on the river Cherwell and the Oxford canal, serves as a significant point of connectivity. The town is served by the northern line of the Great Western railway, situated 86 miles north-west of London. Banbury's railway connectivity is further enhanced by branch lines of the Great Central railway, which connects to the main line at Woodford, and the London & North-Western railway, which connects to Bletchley.

## The London & North-Western Railway
The London & North-Western railway operates a main line that crosses the north-east part of the county. A primary hub on this system is Bletchley, an important junction where branches diverge west to Banbury and Oxford, and east to Cambridge, Bedford, and Fenny Stratford. The western branch provides service to Buckingham. Other connections include a branch from Cheddington to Aylesbury. In other regions, the London & North-Western (Holyhead line) operates with branches in the Clwyd and Conway valleys, and lines connecting Ruthin and Corwen with Denbigh and Ruabon.

## Other Railway Systems and Transport
Various other companies maintain critical infrastructure:
*   **Great Western Railway:** Its main line crosses the extreme south of the county via Taplow and Slough. From Prince's Risborough, there are branches to Aylesbury and Watlington.
*   **Great Eastern Railway:** This system serves residential districts and villages on the outskirts of Epping Forest, including Loughton, Chingford, and Woodford.
*   **London & South Western Railway:** This line serves Sherborne and Gillingham in the north of the county, with branches extending to Portland, Weymouth, Dorchester, Swanage, Poole, and Wimborne.
*   **Midland Railway:** This is the chief railway serving its respective county, with its main line and branches serving the north, east, and south.

## Impact on Trade and Urban Supply
The railway network has significantly influenced the economy and the supply of goods to London. For example, the transport of milk to the five million residents of London has been transformed by railway facilities and favorable rates. This has allowed milk produced in more favorable conditions at considerable distances to be delivered to retailers at a lower price than milk produced within the metropolis. This shift contributed to a decrease in the number of town "cowkeepers" and a reduction in the number of milk cows in the county of London from 10,000 in 1889 to 5,144 in 1900.

The volume of milk delivered to London via the railways is substantial: the Great Western line delivers approximately 11,000,000 gallons annually, the London & North-Western railway delivers 9,000,000 gallons, and the Midland railway delivers 5,000,000 gallons at St Pancras.
