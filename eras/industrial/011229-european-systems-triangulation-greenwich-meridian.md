# European Systems of Triangulation and the Greenwich Meridian

## The Greenwich Meridian
The meridian of Greenwich has been universally accepted as the initial meridian. Located in the south-eastern metropolitan borough of London, England, the Royal Observatory was built in 1675 to advance nautical astronomy and navigation. The observatory is situated within Greenwich Park, an 185 acres area enclosed by Humphrey, duke of Gloucester, and laid out by Charles II. 

From the Royal Observatory, the exact time is transmitted daily at one o'clock via electric signal to the primary towns of the country. The majority of foreign geographers, as well as British geographers, reckon longitude from this meridian. While it serves as the universal initial meridian, many topographical maps of foreign countries continue to use local meridians. Notable examples include:
* **Paris (Obs. nationale):** 2° 20´ 14´´ E. of Greenwich
* **Pulkova (St Petersburg):** 30° 19´ 39´´ E. of Greenwich
* **Stockholm:** 18° 3´ 30´´ E. of Greenwich
* **Rome (Collegio Romano):** 12° 28´ 40´´ E. of Greenwich
* **Brussels (Old town):** 4° 22´ 11´´ E. of Greenwich
* **Madrid:** 3° 41´ 16´´ W. of Greenwich
* **Ferro (assumed):** 20° 0´ 0´´ W. of Paris

## Russian Geodetic Surveys
Russia has implemented a direct system of triangulation carried out with high scientific precision across the European portion of its empire. This system connects St Petersburg with Tiflis on the southern slopes of the Caucasus. Furthermore, St Petersburg is connected to Greenwich through European systems of triangulation, and Russia adopts the Greenwich meridian as the zero value for all its longitude calculations.

The extension of these rigorous geodetic measurements encountered obstacles beyond the eastern shores of the Caspian Sea. The arid nature of the trans-Caspian deserts proved to be an insuperable obstacle to the first-class triangulation methods used in Europe. Consequently, Russian geography in central Asia relies on means other than direct measurement for coordinate values in longitude and latitude. For the trans-Caspian triangulation, the astronomical observatory at Tashkent serves as the initial starting point; however, this triangulation is ranked as second-class and extends to the Pamir frontier beyond Osh.

To determine the longitude of the Tashkent observatory, the telegraphic method was used differentially with Pulkova. Three independent values were determined:
* 1875 via Ekaterinburg and Omsk: 2 35 52.151"
* 1891 via Saratov and Orenburg: 2 35 52.228"
* 1895 via Kiev and Baku: 2 35 51.997"

These values fall within a range of 0^S.25, making it improbable that the mean value has an error exceeding 0^S.10. Exact surveys based on triangulation in Russia extend as far east as Chinese Turkestan, approximately 75 deg. E. of Greenwich.

## Global Geodetic Connections
In India, geodetic triangulation provides the basis for exact surveys extending as far east as the eastern boundaries of Burma (approximately 100 deg. E.). By the close of the 19th century, the final links of the great geodetic triangulation of the Indian peninsula were forged, though a geodetic connection with European systems has yet to be accomplished.

Since 1890, the telegraphic method of determining longitudes differentially with Greenwich has been applied more rigorously. This resulted in a slight correction of about 2" of arc to previous determinations made through Suez. This determination was achieved through four specific arcs:
1. Greenwich to Potsdam
2. Potsdam to Teheran
3. Teheran to Bushire
4. Bushire to Karachi

## Cartographic Projections
The application of geodetic data often involves various map projections. For wide areas, Lambert's equal area projection is used, while conical projections meet ordinary cartographer needs. The use of Mercator's projection for world maps is deprecated due to the inordinate exaggeration of areas in high latitudes.

Other specific systems include:
* **Projection de Bonne (Modified Conical Projection):** Adopted in 1803 by the "Dépôt de la Guerre" for the map of France and used for the ordnance survey map of Scotland (1 in. to a mile). It is considered ill-adapted for countries with great longitudinal extent, as intersections of meridians and parallels become very oblique.
* **Simple Polyconic Projection:** Used by the topographical section of the general staff, the U.S. geological survey, and the United States coast and geodetic survey. It is accurate near the central meridian but becomes less so as that meridian is departed. It is particularly useful for single sheets on large scales.
