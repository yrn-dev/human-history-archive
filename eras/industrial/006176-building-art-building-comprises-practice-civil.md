# BUILDING

## Definition and Relation to Architecture
The art of building consists of the practice of civil architecture and the mechanical operations required to execute the designs created by an architect. While it is sometimes referred to as "practical architecture," this term is avoided to maintain a clear distinction between architecture as a mechanical art and architecture as a liberal or fine art. 

The relationship between the two is such that the execution of architectural works necessarily involves building. However, building is often employed in instances where the result is not architectural. Consequently, a person may be a competent builder without being an architect. Conversely, an accomplished architect must be competent in specifying and directing all building operations. Building also encompasses "construction," which is the branch of architectural science concerned with the practical execution of the works needed to produce any structure.

## Requirements for the Architect
An architect must possess a diverse range of scientific and practical knowledge to successfully oversee building operations. This includes:

*   **Soil Science:** Knowledge of various soils—including rock, clay, silt, gravel, chalk, and earth—to determine the appropriate depth and type of foundation after trial holes are dug on a site.
*   **Chemistry:** An understanding of the gases and acids found in materials and the objections to their presence.
*   **Technical Proficiency:** Familiarity with plumbing, heating, ventilation, electric and gas lighting, drainage, and water-supply. They must also be acquainted with carpentry, joinery, smiths' work, stonework, brickwork, fireproof construction, and the principles of shoring and timbering in excavations and trenches.
*   **Finishing Works:** Knowledge of painting, decoration, glazing, slating of roofs, and the tiling of roofs or internal walls and pavings.
*   **Engineering and Trade:** The ability to calculate strains and strengths placed on any part of a structure and a general knowledge of the building trade to resolve defects or difficulties.

Furthermore, an architect must be able to minutely estimate the absolute cost of a proposed structure beforehand. This requires the ability to ascertain quantities and measure work, a task usually performed by a quantity surveyor in advanced stages. In ordinary practice, an architect may "cube" a building at a price per foot cube.

## Planning and Construction Standards
A successful building is planned based on several necessary conditions: ease of access, good service, good light, a pleasing environment and approaches, and true economy with minimum cost. In the specific case of office buildings, the plan should allow for ease of rearrangement to accommodate tenants.

Building regulations vary by region. In London and large British cities, the use of wood frame buildings for dwellings is prohibited. In contrast, New York allows frame or wood structures, though it defines specific areas where new ones cannot be erected. Regarding domestic buildings, New York requires a rear space at least 10 feet deep, which increases if the building exceeds 60 feet in height. London utilizes a similar 10-foot depth but requires the height of the building to remain within an angle of 63½ degrees, inclining from the rear boundary toward the building from the pavement level. Smaller English towns follow model by-laws that increase yard or garden depth based on the building's height.

## Carpentry and Joinery
Carpentry is divided into three principal branches:
1.  **Descriptive:** Using the rules of geometry to show the method or lines for forming work.
2.  **Constructive:** The practice of reducing timber into specific forms and joining them to create a whole according to a design.
3.  **Mechanical:** The study of the strains timbers are subjected to and their relative strength based on disposition.

A distinction is made between the carpenter and the joiner. The carpenter's work is directed toward the "carcass" of the building and concerns structure, stability, and efficiency. The joiner begins work only after the carcass is complete; joiners' work can be removed from a building without undermining its most important qualities or stability.

In frame buildings, the sides are covered with siding fastened to a sheathing of rough boards nailed to studs. Siding may consist of shingles, wooden tiles, matched boards placed diagonally, or weather boards (clapboards). Weather boards are thin boards, thicker at one edge, arranged horizontally so the thick edge overlaps the thin edge of the board below.
