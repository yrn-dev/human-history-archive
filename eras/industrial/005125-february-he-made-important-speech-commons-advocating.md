# Speeches and Legislative Actions in the House of Commons

The House of Commons has served as a primary venue for significant political oratory and legislative debate, where members have advocated for religious reform, constitutional changes, and the rights of various populations.

## Religious and Ecclesiastical Debates
In the 17th century, the House of Commons was a site of contention regarding the structure of the church and the legal treatment of political prisoners. George Digby, the 2nd Earl of Bristol, who was elected as a member for Dorsetshire in both the Long and Short parliaments of 1640, was a prominent figure in these debates. On February 8, Digby delivered an important speech in the Commons where he advocated for reformation while opposing the abolition of episcopacy. 

Digby's relationship with the parliamentary party was complex. While he initially showed great zeal for the impeachment of Strafford, he later opposed the attainder. On April 21, 1641, he gave an eloquent speech highlighting the injustice of *ex post facto* legislation and the weakness of the evidence provided by Vane. This led to hostility from the parliamentary party; consequently, the House of Commons ordered that his speech be burnt by the hangman on July 15.

## Reform and Civil Rights
The 19th century saw a surge in speeches focused on expanding civil rights and reforming the parliamentary system. In February 1830, Lord Lansdowne offered a "pocket borough" seat at Calne to Macaulay, who entered parliament during a period of tension between a reactionary administration and a growing party of reform. Macaulay delivered his maiden speech on April 5, 1830, regarding the second reading of the Bill for the Removal of Jewish Disabilities. He later became a key voice for reform, delivering a successful speech on the second night of the debate for the Reform Bill introduced on March 1, 1831.

Similarly, John Bright became a protagonist of Reform over a nine-year period. After returning to parliament in 1858, Bright seconded a motion in February that resulted in the removal of Lord Palmerston's government. Following this, Bright assisted in passing measures he had long advocated, specifically the transfer of the government of India from the East India Company to the crown and the admission of Jews to parliament. Between 1859 and 1867, Bright was the most influential voice in the debates on the Reform Bills submitted to the House of Commons, utilizing popular phrases such as "a free breakfast table" and "you cannot get twenty wagons at once through Temple Bar" to characterize the political struggle.

## Philanthropy and Abolition
The House of Commons also hosted advocates for humanitarian causes. Sir Thomas Fowell Buxton, an English philanthropist and M.P. for Weymouth from 1818 to 1837, was highly regarded as a straightforward and able speaker. Buxton devoted his life and his time in the House of Commons to the abolition of slavery in the British colonies. Despite facing opposition and the remonstrances of faint-hearted friends, he achieved partial success in 1833, although he was forced to accept certain clauses in the bill that contradicted his better judgment.

## Constitutional Landmarks
Beyond individual oratory, the House of Commons played a critical role in establishing fundamental English constitutional principles. On February 13, 1689, a committee of the commons drew up the Declaration of Right. This document, which embodied fundamental constitutional principles, was delivered by the lords and commons to the prince and princess of Orange (William III and Mary). These claims were later enacted in December 1689 as the Bill of Rights, which is considered one of the greatest landmarks in English constitutional history, second only to Magna Carta.
