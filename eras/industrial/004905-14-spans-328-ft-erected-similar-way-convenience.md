# Bridge Engineering and Construction

Bridge design is primarily governed by the need for adequate strength and the pursuit of the greatest economy. Historically, bridge building was an empirical art that resulted in unavoidable waste of material; however, the development of the theory of structures has allowed engineers to determine the most economical arrangements of material, particularly within the superstructure.

## Economic and Limiting Spans
In bridges consisting of many spans, there is a specific span length that minimizes total cost. While the cost of bridge flooring and abutments remains largely independent of the span length, the cost of the main girders for one span varies nearly as the square of the span for any given intensity of live load and girder type. The cost of a pier depends mainly on the height of the bridge and the character of the foundations.

Broadly, the least costly arrangement occurs when the cost of the superstructure of a span is equal to the cost of a pier and its foundation. Sir Guilford Molesworth provided a less exact but convenient formula for the economic span: $l = 100\sqrt{P}/\sqrt{G}$, where $P$ is the cost of one pier with its protection and $G$ is the cost of the superstructure of a 100-ft. span erected.

## Bridge Types and Materials
Different materials and designs are employed based on the required span and the era of construction.

### Girder and Truss Bridges
Early braced bridges in America and England utilized cast iron (often in circular or octagonal tubes) for compression members and wrought iron for tension members. Examples include:
* **Newark Dyke bridge (1851-1853):** A pin-jointed Warren girder bridge with a span between supports of 259 ft. and a clear span of 240½ ft.
* **Fink truss:** An early American deck bridge type that has since disappeared because the use of long ties to transfer loads to the ends of the bridge was uneconomical.
* **Warren type:** This system, which may include intermediate verticals or two sets of bracing bars, provides a convenient way to support floor girders. A 390 ft. span bridge was built using this system at Louisville in 1869.
* **Ohio bridge (Cincinnati & Covington railway):** Constructed in 1888 by the Phoenix Bridge Company, this is likely the largest girder span constructed. It features independent polygonal girders with a centre span of 550 ft. and side spans of 490 ft.

### Arch Bridges
Cast iron arches were common in the late 18th and early 19th centuries. The Wearmouth bridge (1793-1796) had a span of 235 ft., while the Southwark bridge (1814-1819) had a centre span of 240 ft. and a rise of 24 ft. Other examples include the Austerlitz and Carrousel bridges in Paris. In 1858, M.C. Meigs erected an aqueduct bridge in Washington with a 200 ft. span, utilizing two arched ribs formed by cast iron pipes.

Other notable arch bridges include the Douro viaduct, with a central opening span of 525 ft. and a crescent-shaped principal rib 32.8 ft. deep at the crown, and the Luiz I. bridge, which has a span of 566 ft. and features both an upper and lower roadway.

### Cantilever Bridges
The cantilever system is often the simplest and most economical method for spans greater than those practicable with independent girders. This system was adopted for the Forth bridge due to exceptional local conditions. Cantilevers can be built member by member from the piers without temporary scaffolding below, which prevents the interruption of navigation and avoids the difficulties of building in deep water.

## Erection Methods
The choice of bridge type is often influenced by the facility of erection, especially for large spans.

* **Staging and Floating:** The Britannia and Conway bridges were built on shore staging, lifted by pontoons, floated into position, and finally lifted by hydraulic presses. The Moerdyk bridge in Holland, featuring 14 spans of 328 ft., was erected similarly. While erecting girders on shore is convenient, it requires significant hauling plant and carries risks during floating operations.
* **Building Out:** Some bridges are built out from abutments or piers. In the case of the St Louis bridge, half arches were built from either side of each pier to balance the load. The Douro bridge used a similar method where side span girders were rolled out to overhang the great span by 105 ft., creating a platform for suspending arch parts.
* **Cantilever Erection:** Cantilever bridges are specifically adapted for building out. J.A.L. Waddell demonstrated that independent spans could be erected by building them as cantilevers and converting them into independent girders after completion.

## Regional Practices and Substructure
In the United States, railway companies typically provide general specifications to expert bridge-building companies rather than designing bridges themselves. This leads to the use of well-settled types and uniform designs. Standard practices include using riveted plate girders for spans up to 50 ft., riveted braced girders for spans of 50 to 75 ft., and pin-connected girders for longer spans.

The substructure—comprising foundations, abutments, and piers—usually consists of concrete, brickwork, or stone masonry, though woodwork or metal work is occasionally used for intermediate piers.
