# The Municipal Corporation of Bristol

## Municipal Reform and Governance
The city of Bristol underwent significant administrative changes during the 19th century to address a period of diminishing prosperity. This decline was attributed to several factors: the rise of Liverpool, which possessed more natural port facilities; the abolition of the slave trade, which caused the ruin of the West Indian sugar trade; and the extortionate rates imposed by the Bristol Dock Company (incorporated in 1803). Additionally, tolls were levied by the corporation and the Merchant Venturers, making competition with London and Liverpool impossible.

The decline of the city was checked through the efforts of the Bristol chamber of commerce, founded in 1823, and the implementation of the Municipal Reform Act of 1835. This act established a new corporation consisting of 16 aldermen and 48 councillors. These members were chosen by the burgesses of each ward, and they, in turn, elected the mayor.

## Infrastructure and Economic Recovery
Following the reorganization of the corporation, the city took active steps to improve its maritime infrastructure. In 1848, the corporation purchased the docks and reduced the associated fees. Further expansion occurred between 1877 and 1880 with the construction of docks at Portishead and the mouth of the river at Avonmouth; these were subsequently purchased by the corporation in 1884. These developments led to a rapid increase in population, an enlargement of the city's boundaries, and a revival of trade.

## Civic Status
As the city recovered and grew, its civic leadership received higher titles. In 1899, the chief magistrate of Bristol became a lord mayor.

## Historical Context
Prior to its 19th-century revival, Bristol was characterized as a small, dirty, and densely populated city with narrow, dark streets. The city's population gained a reputation for violence, specifically during riots that occurred in 1708, 1753, 1767, and 1831. Earlier in its history, during the reign of Anne, the city's hot wells became fashionable, leading to the granting of a charter in 1710. Bristol also served as the center for the Methodist revival led by Wesley and Whitefield.
