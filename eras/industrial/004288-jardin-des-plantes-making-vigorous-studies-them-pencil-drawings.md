# Jardin des Plantes

The Jardin des Plantes in Paris serves as a significant center for scientific research, botanical study, and artistic inspiration. It houses a museum that has historically acted as a primary repository for global biological specimens and provides a living environment for the study of diverse animal and plant species.

## Scientific Research and Collections
The museum of the Jardin des Plantes was once the central location for the deposit of ichthyological treasures from across the globe. G. Cuvier (1769-1832) utilized these collections to conduct extensive investigations into the anatomy and skeletons of fishes. His work allowed him to define the genera, families, orders, and divisions of the class of fishes, as detailed in the various editions of the *Règne Animal*. These materials also formed the basis for the *Historie naturelle des Poissons*, a work commenced by Cuvier and his pupil A. Valenciennes.

The institution has also been a site for significant botanical scholarship. René Louiche Desfontaines (1750-1833), a French botanist who collected approximately 1,600 species of plants in North Africa, was nominated as a professor at the Jardin des Plantes in 1786. He authored the *Catalogus plantarum horti regii Parisiensis* (1831), which was a third edition of his earlier *Tableau de l'école botanique du muséum d'histoire naturelle de Paris* (1804).

## Zoological Studies and the Axolotl
The Jardin des Plantes has been instrumental in the study of Batrachians, specifically the axolotl. In 1864, six specimens (one female and five males) from the vicinity of Mexico City were transferred to the institution from the Jardin d'Acclimatation. These specimens were placed under the care of Professor A. Dumeril, the administrator of the reptile collection. 

While the axolotls at the Jardin d'Acclimatation saw their eggs devoured by other aquarium occupants, the single female at the Jardin des Plantes spawned twice, successfully rearing a large number of young. Between 1865 and 1868, the original six specimens bred eleven times, producing between 9,000 and 10,000 larvae. This success allowed the Paris Museum to distribute over a thousand examples to private individuals, dealers, and other institutions throughout Europe. 

Research at the Jardin des Plantes also observed the transformation of some second-generation axolotls into the *Amblystoma tigrinum* state, characterized by the loss of dorsal crests and gills, the development of movable eyelids, and the appearance of yellow spots. Although these transformed salamanders did not initially breed, Professor E. Blanchard reported that an axolotl in its *Amblystoma* state finally spawned in the reptile house in 1876.

## Artistic Influence and Animal Studies
The wild beasts housed at the Jardin des Plantes have served as essential subjects for sculptors. Antoine Louis Barye (1796-1875), regarded as the greatest artist of animal life of the French school, discovered his artistic vocation in 1823 while watching the animals at the Jardin des Plantes. He produced vigorous pencil drawings of the beasts, which he subsequently used as the basis for sculptures on both large and small scales. Barye's work, which included pieces such as "Lion and Snake" (1832) and "Tiger devouring a Crocodile" (1831), created a new class of art that influenced followers such as Gardet, Cain, Peter, and Fremiet.

The influence of the gardens continued into the late 19th century. American sculptor Paul Wayland Bartlett (1865- ), who studied under Fremiet in Paris at the age of fifteen, modeled from the animals at the Jardin des Plantes.

## Other Notable Specimens and Expeditions
The gardens have hosted a variety of rare animals, including a male tame Yak brought from Shanghai, which was noted as the finest specimen of its kind. Additionally, the institution has sponsored scientific exploration; in 1847, the Swiss-American geologist Jules Marcou traveled to North America as a travelling geologist for the Jardin des Plantes.
