# The Influence of Exchanges on Brokerage Relations

The institution of "Exchanges" emerged in important commercial centres during the middle of the 19th century. These are associations where brokers and merchants interested in a particular trade meet to conduct business. The establishment of these exchanges has modified the traditional legal and practical relations between brokers, their principals, and third parties.

## Organization and Governance of Exchanges
Control of an exchange is vested in an association. Upon becoming a member, an individual agrees to be bound by the association's rules and to conduct market contracts in accordance with them. A committee or governing body, elected by the members, is responsible for enforcing these rules. Members who fail to conform to the rules or meet their market engagements are subject to expulsion or suspension by the committee.

To maintain order and standardization, exchanges provide a printed book of rules and specified printed contract forms for members to use. Disputes between members regarding their contracts are resolved by an arbitration tribunal composed of members, who are guided by the exchange rules in their decisions.

## Settlement and "Futures"
Exchanges utilize a clearing-house to simplify the settlement of accounts, particularly for "futures," which are contracts for future delivery. This involves a periodical or weekly settlement where members pay or receive the balance of their "differences." These differences are calculated as the gap between the contract price and the market price fixed for settlement, or the difference between the present and previous settlement prices.

## Modification of Broker-Principal Relations
Traditionally, a broker's duty is to bring a principal and a third party into a contract of privity; the broker does not possess the goods and typically drops out of the arrangement once the contract is made. A broker is expected to act with skill, diligence, and perfect good faith, ensuring the bargain matches the principal's orders regarding price, quantity, and quality.

However, membership in an exchange alters these rights and liabilities. Because all members are treated as principals among themselves, no agents are recognized on the market. Consequently, a broker employed by a non-member is treated by the rules as buying for himself and is personally liable for the contract. In futures contracts, the broker must follow weekly settlement rules; if the principal fails to take delivery, the broker must pay the seller any difference between the contract and market price.

Exchanges often provide printed contract forms for dealings with non-members. These forms may include a "principal clause," which suggests the contract is made with the broker as principal. This is intended to allow the broker to buy from or sell to their own principal on their own account, rather than acting as an agent to find a third party. While some brokers erroneously believe they are entitled to act as principals in this manner, courts may reject such provisions if they are seen as radically inconsistent with the primary object of a brokerage contract.

## Stockbrokers and Specialized Trading
A stockbroker is a specific type of broker who contracts for the sale of shares and stocks. Unlike brokers of "goods," stockbrokers are not subject to the requirement of a written memorandum under the Sale of Goods Act 1893. This allows principals to bring actions even without a written memorandum. For example, a jobber may sue a buyer whose name was passed by a broker if that broker has failed and been declared a defaulter.

Stockbrokers are subject to the rules and customs of the Stock Exchange. However, illegal customs—such as disregarding Leeman's Act (1867) regarding the necessity of stating registered numbers for joint-stock bank shares—do not make a contract of sale valid. Nevertheless, if a client instructs a broker to follow such a practice, the broker may still be indemnified by the client for money paid on their behalf.

## Documentation and Legal Standing
In the early 19th century, there was judicial disagreement over whether a broker's book or "bought and sold notes" constituted a statutory memorandum. In modern practice, brokers no longer keep formal books regulated by statute. Instead, written memoranda are usually found in "contract notes." Whether these notes are sufficient depends on various factors, including whether one or two brokers were involved and how the notes were rendered or exchanged.

Regarding third parties, a broker signing as a broker for a principal is not personally liable. However, if the broker makes themselves a party to the contract, the third party may sue either the broker or the principal. In certain trades where it is customary for brokers to receive payment, a buyer may use payment to the broker as a defense if the broker becomes insolvent or absconds.

## The "Bucketshop"
The term "bucketshop" is an American slang term originating in Chicago. It refers to the business of an inferior class of stockbroker who is not a member of an official exchange. These brokers conduct speculative operations that are essentially wagers or bets between the broker and the client, with no actual purchase or sale of stocks. The term is sometimes incorrectly applied to any stockbroker who is not a member of a recognized local exchange.
