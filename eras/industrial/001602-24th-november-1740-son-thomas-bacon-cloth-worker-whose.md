# John Bacon (1740-1799)

John Bacon was a British sculptor born in Southwark on the 24th of November 1740. He was the son of Thomas Bacon, who worked as a cloth-worker and whose ancestors had owned a considerable estate in Somersetshire.

## Early Training and Apprenticeship
At fourteen years of age, Bacon began an apprenticeship at the porcelain manufactory of Mr. Crispe in Lambeth. His initial duties involved painting small ornamental pieces of china. However, he demonstrated significant skill in moulding, which led to his promotion to the position of modeller for the works. 

During his time at the porcelain works, Bacon observed models created by eminent sculptors that had been sent to an adjoining pottery to be burned. This experience influenced his professional direction, and he began imitating these works. His success in this pursuit was recognized in 1758 when he received a prize from the Society for the Encouragement of Arts for a small figure of Peace. Between 1763 and 1776, he was awarded the highest premiums from the same society nine times. Additionally, during his apprenticeship, Bacon improved the technique of working statues in artificial stone, a skill he later perfected.

## Technical Innovations and Marble Work
Bacon began working in marble around 1763. During the early stages of his work with this medium, he invented a more perfect instrument to improve the process of transferring the form of a model to the marble, a technical process known as "getting out the points." This new instrument offered several advantages over previous tools: it was more exact, provided correct measurements in every direction, was compact in size, and was versatile enough to be used on either the marble or the model.

## Professional Recognition and Royal Patronage
In 1769, the Royal Academy awarded Bacon its first gold medal for sculpture for a bas-relief that depicted the escape of Aeneas from Troy. He continued to achieve critical success in 1770 when he exhibited a figure of Mars; this work earned him the gold medal of the Society of Arts and led to his election as an A.R.A.

Following these achievements, Bacon was commissioned to create a bust of George III for Christ Church, Oxford. Through this work, he secured the favor of the king, a relationship that lasted throughout his life. Despite his success and royal patronage, Bacon faced considerable jealousy from other sculptors.
