# Live Load on Railway Bridges

In the context of bridge engineering, the live load (also referred to as the temporary load) on a railway bridge is defined as the weight of the heaviest train likely to traverse the structure. Because the weights of locomotives and wagons have increased over time, many earlier bridges have required strengthening to accommodate modern traffic.

## Evolution of Load Calculations
In earlier girder bridges, the live load was calculated as a uniform load of 1 ton per foot run for each line of way. This standard was based on locomotives with a gauge of 4 ft. 8½ in. that weighed between 35 and 45 tons, where the average load did not exceed 1 ton per foot run. During that period, heavily loaded trains of wagons did not exceed three-quarters of a ton per foot run.

Modern engineering recognizes that the concentration of loading at the axles creates a greater straining action—particularly in short bridges—than a load that is uniformly distributed along the span. Consequently, some designers suggest that it would be safer and more convenient to design short bridges based on a typical heavy locomotive that produces the greatest possible stresses for a specific railway class, rather than assuming a uniform rolling load.

## Regional Standards and Examples
Different regions employ various regulations for determining live loads per foot run and per track:

*   **Austria:** Official regulations specify live loads based on span. For a span of 3.3 feet (1 metre), the load is 6.1 tons per foot run. This decreases as the span increases: 4.6 tons for 6.6 feet, 3.1 tons for 16.4 feet, 1.5 tons for 65.6 feet, and 1.2 tons for 98.4 feet.
*   **United States:** Some American locomotives are notably heavy. A consolidation engine may weigh 126 tons with a length of 57 feet over buffers, resulting in an average load of 2.55 tons per foot run. Long ore wagons may weigh two tons per foot run when loaded.
*   **Waddell's Classification:** J.A.L. Waddell proposed seven classes of railways based on expected traffic. For the lightest class, he assumes a locomotive and tender of 93.5 tons (average load 1.8 tons per foot run) and wagons at 1.3 tons per foot run. For the heaviest class, he assumes a locomotive and tender of 144.5 tons (average load 2.77 tons per foot run) and wagons at 1.9 tons per foot run. In his model, the live load consists of two such engines followed by a train of wagons covering the span. Waddell uses short tons of 2000 lb.

## Impact Stresses
The stresses caused by a live load are greater than those produced by the same load resting quietly on a bridge. This increment is caused by several factors:
*   Vertical imbalance in the moving parts of locomotives.
*   Irregularities in the permanent way and inequalities of level at rail ends, which cause shocks.
*   Centrifugal and lurching actions resulting from rails that are not perfectly smooth or straight.
*   The yielding of the structure.

These impact stresses are more significant on flooring girders than on main girders, and more pronounced on short main girders than on long ones. One proposed empirical allowance for impact stresses adds the following percentages to live load stresses: 20% for floor stringers, 15% for floor cross girders, 10% for main girders with 40-ft. spans, and 5% for main girders with 100-ft. spans.

## Related Structural Loads
While the live load is a primary concern, it is calculated alongside other forces:
*   **Dead Load:** The weight of the superstructure, including main girders, wind-bracing, and flooring. In railway bridges, rails and sleepers weigh 0.2 to 0.25 tons per foot run per line of way, while cross and rail girders weigh 0.15 to 0.2 tons. A footway adds approximately 0.4 ton per foot run.
*   **Longitudinal Drag:** This is the friction caused by a braking train, estimated at about one-seventh of the train's weight.
*   **Centrifugal Load:** On curved bridges, the horizontal force is calculated as $wv^2/gr$ tons, where $w$ is the locomotive weight, $r$ is the radius of curvature, and $v$ is the velocity in feet per second.
*   **Horizontal Pressure:** Transverse wind pressure, which is particularly important for high and long bridges.
