# Herald and Genealogist, vol. iii.

The *Herald and Genealogist*, specifically volume iii, is a publication containing scholarly works on heraldry and the study of lineage. One notable contribution found in this volume is an article by Nichols titled "The Dignity of Baronet."

## The Dignity of the Baronet
The study of the baronetage involves the specific rules of heraldic display and the determination of precedence. Baronets are entitled to include the red hand of Ulster in their coat of arms, positioned either on an inescutcheon or a canton. An exception exists for baronets of Nova Scotia, who instead display the saltire of that province.

The chronological order of precedence between the baronets of Nova Scotia, Ireland, and England remained undetermined by the Acts of Union and has continued to be a point of contention among heralds. In England, the premier baronet is Sir Hickman Bacon, whose ancestor was the first individual to receive the honor in 1611.

## The Role of Heralds and Genealogists
Heralds and genealogists serve as the primary investigators of family descent and nobility. Their work is often essential when royal or noble pedigrees become obsolete or ambiguous over centuries. For example, the descendants of Hugh Capet in the branches of Tanlay and Champignelles saw their royal descent fade over a four-hundred-year period, during which they descended from princes to barons and eventually to the simple gentry or the condition of peasants. In such cases, their pedigree was not recorded in the annals of the kingdom and had to be recovered through the "minute diligence of heralds and genealogists."

In other historical contexts, the work of genealogists has been met with skepticism. The emperor Vespasian is noted for having laughed at genealogists who attempted to deduce his family line from Flavius, the founder of Reate and a companion of Hercules.

## Historical Context of Heralds
The role of the herald has evolved across different civilizations. In ancient Sparta, the Talthybiadæ, or heralds, constituted a distinct caste or family. In ancient Athens, the treatment of heralds could lead to significant political and divine consequences. According to Plutarch, Themistoklês proposed the death of a Persian herald for using the Greek language as a medium for "barbaric dictation." Pausanias attributes the proposal to put heralds to death in Athens to Miltiadês, suggesting that a divine judgment fell upon Miltiadês' family as a result. However, Herodotus notes that while the city and country were later laid waste by Xerxês, he did not believe this was a direct result of the outrage against the heralds.

Additionally, the death of the herald Anthemokritus created a popular belief in Athens that the Megarians were responsible, leading to a public burial near the Thriasian gate. While the public believed this, evidence suggests that neither Periklês nor Thucydidês believed the Megarians had actually caused the death.

## Professional Genealogy in Britain
The field of genealogy in Britain has been shaped by prominent figures and publications. Sir John Bernard Burke (1814–1892), a British genealogist born in London and educated in London and France, was a central figure. He assisted his father, John Burke, who issued the *Genealogical and Heraldic Dictionary of the Peerage and Baronetage of the United Kingdom* in 1826. This work, commonly known as *Burke's Peerage*, has been issued annually since 1847.

Sir Bernard Burke held several significant offices, including:
* Keeper of the state papers in Ireland (appointed 1855).
* Ulster king-at-arms (appointed 1853).

Throughout his career, Burke edited *Burke's Peerage* and *Burke's Landed Gentry* (first published between 1833 and 1838). He also published enlarged editions of his father's *Encyclopaedia of Heraldry, or General Armoury of England, Scotland and Ireland*. His own original works include *The Rise of Great Families* (1882), *Vicissitudes of Families* (1883), and *The Romance of the Aristocracy* (1855). He was succeeded as editor by his fourth son, Ashworth Peter Burke.
