# Clearing-Houses

Clearing-houses are institutions used for the settlement of banking accounts and the clearing of cheques. These systems have seen significant development in Great Britain and the United States.

## Clearing-Houses in the United States
The system of clearing cheques has reached a higher level of development in the United States than in almost any other country, with the possible exception of Great Britain. As of the period leading up to 1907, clearing-houses existed in approximately 112 leading cities. For the year ending September 30, 1907, the aggregate clearings reached $154,662,515,258.

A substantial portion of this business is handled by the New York Clearing-House. In 1906, the clearings at this specific institution constituted 67.2% of the total clearings across 55 of the larger cities. The volume of these clearings is subject to great fluctuation, driven by the business prosperity of the country and the volume of transactions on the stock exchange.

## The London Clearing House
In the United Kingdom, nearly every large transaction is settled via cheque through a series of ledger transfers, with specie and notes used only for fractional amounts. A large proportion of these transactions are arranged through the London Clearing House, a process facilitated by the fact that every bank in the United Kingdom maintains an agent in London. The annual circulation handled by the London Clearing House exceeds £12,000,000,000. This system operates on a well-founded trust, where the reserves or gold stocks of the banks on which cheques are drawn are not questioned.

### History and Evolution
The association of purely city banks into a "Clearing House" was established by 1776, although evidence from the books of the Grasshopper suggests a less organized system of settlement existed as early as 1773, noting a quarterly charge for the use of a "Clearing-room." In 1810, a dedicated house was taken for this purpose; at that time, forty-six banking houses settled their accounts there.

Membership has evolved over time:
* **1844:** Of the forty-nine banks in London, twenty-six were connected with the Clearing House. At this time, membership was restricted to private banks.
* **1854:** Joint-stock banks were admitted to the body.
* **1906:** The membership consisted of nineteen banks in total: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank.

Certain institutions remained outside the membership. The Bank of England has never been a member, although it "clears on one side," meaning its claims on clearing bankers are made through the Clearing House, while the claims of clearing bankers on the Bank are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times daily. Additionally, banks located at Charing Cross or Fleet Street did not belong to the Clearing House.

### Country Clearing
In 1858, arrangements made by Sir John Lubbock (Lord Avebury) added the clearing of country cheques to the system. This "country clearing" provides significant assistance to business by allowing a cheque drawn on a distant village in England to be processed as conveniently as one drawn on London.
