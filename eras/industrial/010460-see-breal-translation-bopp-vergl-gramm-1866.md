# Vergleichende Grammatik (Comparative Grammar)

The *Vergleichende Grammatik des Sanskrit, Zend, Griechischen, Lateinischen, Litthauischen, Altslavischen, Gothischen, und Deutschen* (Comparative Grammar of Sanskrit, Zend, Greek, Latin, Lithuanian, Old-Slavonic, Gothic, and German) is a seminal work by the linguist Franz Bopp. The science of comparative grammar is considered to date from Bopp's earliest publications.

## Development and Publication
The *Comparative Grammar* was the center of Bopp's chief activity and was published in six parts at considerable intervals in Berlin: 1833, 1835, 1842, 1847, 1849, and 1852. The work was preceded by a series of monographs printed in the *Transactions of the Berlin Academy* between 1824 and 1831, titled *Vergleichende Zergliederung des Sanskrits und der mit ihm verwandten Sprachen*. Following the publication of the first part of the *Comparative Grammar*, Bopp published two additional essays on "Numerals" in 1835.

The scope of the work evolved over time; for instance, Old-Slavonian was included among the compared languages starting from the second part. A second German edition, which was thoroughly revised and published between 1856 and 1861, expanded the scope of the research to include Old-Armenian.

## Objectives and Methodology
Bopp intended for the *Comparative Grammar* to achieve a threefold task:
1. To provide a description of the original grammatical structure of the languages, as deduced through their intercomparison.
2. To trace the phonetic laws of these languages.
3. To investigate the origin of their grammatical forms.

The first two objectives—the description of structure and the tracing of phonetic laws—were designed to be subservient to the third goal of investigating the origin of grammatical forms. Bopp's research was based on the best available sources and incorporated new findings as they became available. His approach involved penetration into minute, almost microscopical details of linguistic phenomena.

## Impact and Influence
Bopp's researches provided a wide and distant view into the tenets, domestic usages, practices, and original seats of ancient Indo-European nations, as well as their closer or more distant affinities. His work had a significant influence on other scholars; for example, the English philologist John William Donaldson based his *New Cratylus* (1839) mainly on Bopp's comparative grammar, although the *Cratylus* was published ten years before Bopp's grammar was completed.

By the end of his life, Bopp saw the results of his labors accepted everywhere. In recognition of his contributions, a fund called *Die Bopp-Stiftung* was established in Berlin on May 16, 1866—the fiftieth anniversary of Windischmann's preface to Bopp's work—to promote the study of comparative grammar and Sanskrit.

## Translations and Related Works
The *Comparative Grammar* was translated into several languages:
* **English:** Translated by E.B. Eastwick in 1845.
* **French:** An excellent translation was produced by Professor Michel Breal in 1866, based on the revised second German edition.

Prior to focusing exclusively on grammatical investigations, Bopp produced other linguistic and literary works. In 1820, he published an essay in the *Annals of Oriental Literature* titled "Analytical Comparison of the Sanskrit, Greek, Latin and Teutonic Languages." He also edited several Sanskrit texts, including a critical edition of the story of *Nala and Damayanti* (1819), an episode of the Mahabharata, as well as other episodes such as *Indralokagamanam* (1824) and *Diluvium* (1829). He also authored the *Ausfuhrliches Lehrgebaude der Sanskrita-Sprache* (1827), which was later released in a Latin edition (1832) and a shorter grammar (1834), alongside a Sanskrit and Latin glossary (1830).
