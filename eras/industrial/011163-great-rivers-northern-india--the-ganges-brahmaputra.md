# The Great Rivers of Northern India

The great rivers of northern India—the Ganges, the Brahmaputra, and the Indus—are the primary waterways of the region. These three rivers all derive their waters from the Tibetan mountain mass, with the northern water-parting of India situated to the north of the Himalaya in the regions of central Tibet.

## The Great Plain of Northern India
The area between the delta of the Indus in the west and the deltas of the Ganges and Brahmaputra in the east forms the great plain of northern India. This plain, which includes a prolongation up the valley of Assam, covers approximately 500,000 square miles. It is characterized as one of the most populous, civilized, and richest districts of India. 

The surface of this plain is remarkably uniform and composed of finely comminuted deposits. It is noted that a traveler could potentially journey from the Bay of Bengal, up the Ganges, through the Punjab, and down the Indus to the sea—a distance exceeding 2,000 meters—without encountering a single pebble. The plain rises to a maximum height of about 1,000 feet.

## The Ganges
The Ganges is a central feature of northern Indian geography and economy. Its valley is described as one of the most fertile and densely populated tracts in the world, teeming with a vast array of natural products. These include tea, indigo, turmeric, lac, wheat, opium-poppy, various grains and pulses, pepper, ginger, betel-nut, quinine, cotton, silk mulberry, and jute. The region also produces various timber, such as the bamboo, the palm, and the iron-hearted sal tree.

The river serves as a significant geographical and linguistic boundary. For example, the river Ganges forms the northern boundary of the Magahi dialect and faces the Maithili dialect. In the west, the Bhojpuri dialect is spoken on both sides of the Gangetic valley from Benares to near Patna.

The Ganges flows through several regions, including Behar—which comprises the higher valley where the river issues from the United Provinces—and the Bengal proper region. The southern portion of Bengal is formed by the Ganges delta. The river's influence is so great that the region it serves is considered more fertile than the areas served by the port of Bombay.

## The Brahmaputra
The Brahmaputra, meaning the "Son of Brahma," rises in the Tibetan plateau far north of the Himalayas. It skirts the eastern passes of the mountains before entering Assam through a series of rapids and waterfalls. In the Lakhimpur district, the southernmost branch of the river enters through a gorge known as the Parasuramkunda or Brahmakunda, a site held in reverence by Hindus and visited annually by thousands of devotees.

In Assam, the river flows through a series of fertile valleys, where it can spread into a sheet of water several miles wide during the rainy season, creating various islands in its bed. Its course includes several tributaries:
* **The Kundil and Digaru:** Flowing from the Mishmi hills to the north.
* **The Tengapani and Dihing:** Originating from the Singpho hills to the south-east.
* **The Dihang:** Flowing from the north-east.
* **The Dihong:** Its principal confluent, which originates as the Tsangpo in Tibet near the source of the Sutlej, traversing the Tibetan table-land for nearly 1,000 meters before joining the Brahmaputra.

After flowing west through the Assam valley, the Brahmaputra turns sharply south, passing through the alluvial districts of the Bengal delta before ending its 1,800-meter course in the Bay of Bengal.

## Regional Impact and Infrastructure
The rivers are integral to the transport and commerce of the region. In Bengal, the railway system is centered around the seaport of Calcutta. The East Indian railway follows the Ganges south from the North-Western Provinces to its terminus at Howrah. To the north of the Ganges, the Eastern Bengal railway runs toward Darjeeling and maintains a service of river steamers on the Brahmaputra.

The mouths of the Ganges and Brahmaputra empty into the Bay of Bengal, a triangular portion of the Indian Ocean. The deltaic influence of the Ganges is particularly noted for extending the intervals between the 100-fathom and 500-fathom sea bottom lines opposite the river's mouth.
