# Government and National Bank Note Issues

The issuance of paper currency by governments and authorized banking institutions has historically served as a tool for managing national finances, funding wars, and stabilizing commercial exchange. These systems vary significantly in their security, denominations, and convertibility.

## United States National Banking and Government Notes
In the United States, the national banking system was developed primarily due to the Federal government's financial needs during the Civil War. Secretary Chase devised a scheme to create a compulsory market for government bonds by granting special privileges to banks organized under Federal charters. These national banks were permitted to issue circulating notes only when secured by the deposit of government bonds with the United States treasurer.

Under this system, a bank could receive circulation equal to 90% of the par-value of the deposited bonds, provided the amount did not exceed the bank's capital stock. Initially, banks were required to invest one-third of their capital in bonds, though this was reduced in 1874 to 25%, with a maximum mandatory requirement of $50,000. An act in 1900 further expanded the system by allowing national banks in areas with populations of 3,000 or fewer to incorporate with capital as low as $25,000, down from a previous minimum of $50,000. By October 31, 1907, there were 2,389 national banks with capitals under $50,000.

Regarding denominations, the law authorized the lowest national bank-note denomination at $5, with a restriction that no more than one-third of any bank's issues could be of this value. The government itself issued notes for $1 and $2, as well as higher denominations. As of October 31, 1907, the largest volume of bank-notes of a single denomination consisted of $10 bills, totaling $249,946,530 out of total outstanding issues of $609,905,441. Of this total, $562,727,614 was secured by bonds, while $47,252,852 was covered by lawful money in the government treasury for redemption and retirement.

## Greenbacks and Inconvertible Currency
During the Civil War, the U.S. government issued "greenbacks"—treasury notes named for the green ink on their backs. First issued in 1862 as a "question of hard necessity" to fund war expenses, these notes were a form of fiat money and inconvertible paper currency because the government had suspended specie payment. The first act on February 25, 1862, authorized $150,000,000 in notes, followed by two more acts in July 1862 and March 1863, each authorizing another $150,000,000. These notes experienced depreciation, reaching a low of 35 cents on the dollar. Specie payment was eventually resumed on January 1, 1879, at which time the nominal amount of outstanding notes was $346,681,000.

## International Note Issuance
Various other nations have employed government and bank-issued notes with differing results:

*   **China:** Paper money issuance dates back to at least the 9th century. By 1160, the system had reached an excess where government paper equivalent to 43,600,000 ounces of silver was issued in six years, leading to rapid depreciation. The Mongols began issuing paper money in 1236. Under Kúblái, who issued notes copiously starting in 1260, a new currency was introduced in 1287 where one new note exchanged for five of the previous series of equal nominal value. This paper money, called *Chao*, was officially valued at only half its nominal value in silver.
*   **Austria-Hungary:** The currency consisted of silver (gulden) and notes issued by the Austro-Hungarian Bank. In 1866, the government violated the bank's charter by issuing its own notes (*Staatsnoten*) worth 312 million gulden to raise money. A reform operation beginning in 1892 sought to redeem these *Staatsnoten*, substitute a gold standard for silver, and resume cash payments.
*   **Japan:** During the Meiji era, the government issued notes that initially fell to a 50% discount. While the government used "despotic" devices to compel circulation at par and credit improved—resulting in 60 million yen in inconvertible notes passing freely after the first five years—the need for funds for reforms led to a total of 180 million yen in circulation by 1881, which then stood at a heavy discount.
*   **United Kingdom:** Discussions occurred regarding whether the Bank of England should have an automatic power to augment note issues on securities, similar to the Bank of Germany (*Reichsbank*). The *Reichsbank* generally charged a rate of 5% (briefly 7% in 1899) to prevent panic. In contrast, the Bank of England's issue on securities under the act of 1844 was originally £14,000,000, increased to £18,450,000 by March 29, 1901.

## Security and Counterfeiting
To maintain public confidence and prevent fraud, issuing governments and banks must implement security measures. Essential features for a secure note include absolute identity in appearance across the issue, the use of properly selected colors to prevent photographic reproduction, and high-class engraving featuring vignettes and geometric lathe work. Additionally, the design should be striking and the inscription legible.
