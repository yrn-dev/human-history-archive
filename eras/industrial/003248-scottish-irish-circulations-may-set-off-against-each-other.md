# Note Circulation and Banking in Scotland and Ireland

## Regulatory Framework and Note Issue
The banking systems of Scotland and Ireland operated under a different policy than that established in England by Sir Robert Peel. Under the acts of 1844-1845, banks in Scotland and Ireland were permitted to exceed their authorized note issues, provided they held specie to the amount of the excess. Additionally, no restrictions were placed on amalgamations among banks in these two countries, and notes for amounts less than £5 remained permitted.

As a result of these policies, the power to issue notes was held by ten large banks in Scotland and six of the nine banks in Ireland. This capability greatly assisted the maintenance of a large proportion of local branches in both countries. While the acts of 1844-1845 required the holding of specie against notes issued beyond the legal limit, this coin was not considered a security for the issues. No security was ever required for local issues in the United Kingdom until the legislation of 1879, which established note issues as a first charge with unlimited liability on the total assets of joint-stock banks that accepted limited liability for other business.

## Scottish Banking Structure and Development
In Scotland, note circulation was historically a central component of banking. For example, the Bank of Dundee operated from 1764 to 1792 solely through the issue of notes, which ranged from £23,000 to £56,000, before it began receiving money from the public. By the time the Bank of Dundee amalgamated with the Royal Bank of Scotland in 1864, its note circulation was £41,000 and its deposits were approximately £700,000.

Private banking had little influence in Scotland; eight private banks existed in 1819, but all had disappeared by 1844. By 1906, the Scottish banking business was practically conducted by ten banks of issue, which operated 1,180 branches. This extensive network of branches is credited with contributing to the stability and prosperity of the banks and the development of the country, functioning similarly in importance to the railway network. This system encouraged an economy of capital, as people of even moderate means commonly lodged their money with banks.

## Economic Considerations of Note Circulation
While the issue of notes provided a source of profit, Scottish banks faced significant expenses. These included the costs of printing, stamp duty, and charges for importing gold from London in instances where circulation exceeded the limits fixed by the act of 1845. Furthermore, banks operating in thinly peopled districts of Scotland faced disadvantages compared to those in more active centers of commerce.

On a broader scale, the increase in Scottish and Irish circulations may be set off against the decrease of the country circulation in England. Since 1844, there has been an apparent increase of more than £6,000,000 in circulation, primarily in Bank of England notes. This is attributed in part to the growth of banking offices in England and Wales, which rose from 976 in 1844 to more than 5,880 in 1906. Because these offices must hold till-money, often consisting of Bank of England notes, the active note circulation of the United Kingdom is likely only slightly larger than it was in 1844.

## Historical Statistics and Trends
Reliable statistics for United Kingdom banking are generally unavailable for more than forty or fifty years. Scottish banks did not generally publish accounts until 1865. In Ireland, figures for cash balances and total deposits in joint-stock banks were published collectively starting in 1840 by Dr. Neilson Hancock, though many banks remained reluctant to publish balance sheets.

A 1851 estimate by W. Newmarch suggested that total deposits in England and Wales, Scotland, and Ireland ranged from £250,000,000 to £360,000,000. By 1872, Palgrave's *Notes on Banking* estimated this figure between £430,000,000 and £450,000,000 (excluding capitals of banks and deposits in discount houses). By the close of 1906, total deposits reached approximately £997,000,000, and total resources—including reserves, capitals, and a note circulation of roughly £177,500,000—were recorded.
