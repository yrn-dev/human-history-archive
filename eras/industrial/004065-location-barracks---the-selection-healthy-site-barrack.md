# Location of Barracks

The selection of a healthy site for a military station or barrack building is considered a matter of great importance. Historically, the location of these facilities was often dictated by political necessity rather than health or efficiency, but modern standards have shifted toward prioritizing sanitary conditions and military readiness.

## Historical Trends in Site Selection
In earlier periods of barrack construction, facilities were typically built within large towns. This placement ensured that troops were immediately available to suppress disturbances. Consequently, these buildings were often cramped, multi-storey structures erected on small plots of land, frequently surrounded by the city's worst slums. Examples of such sites include the cavalry barracks at Hulme in Manchester and the Ship Street barracks in Dublin. In some instances, existing buildings were purchased and converted into barracks—such as the Linen Hall in Dublin—with little regard for the convenience of the occupants or the essential requirement for the free circulation of pure air.

Before the widespread use of barracks, the British military often relied on billeting, which involved quartering soldiers among the town populations. This practice was found to be burdensome to civilians, detrimental to military efficiency, and subversive of discipline, as it exposed soldiers to temptations of disloyalty.

## Transition to Open Country
Following the Crimean War, barrack policy shifted to prioritize the training of the army for war during peacetime. It was recognized that this required quartering troops in large bodies, including all branches of service, in positions providing sufficient space for manoeuvring, rifle practice, and gun practice. Between 1856 and 1860, camps were established at Curragh, Shorncliffe, Colchester, and Aldershot, a policy later extended by the acquisition of Strensall Common near York.

Modern trends favor moving troops away from crowded towns to barracks or hutments in the open country. This transition allows for better sanitary location and more effective military training. Improvements in telegraph, road, and rail communications have made it possible to support civil power during disturbances in a timely manner without the need to permanently station troops within the immediate locality.

## Sanitary Standards and Regulation
In 1857, a commission was appointed to inspect barracks and hospitals in the United Kingdom to improve sanitary conditions, reporting its findings in 1861. Similar commissions later examined stations in India and the Mediterranean. Prominent contributors to this work included John Sutherland, M.D., Captain (later Sir Douglas) Gallon, R.E., and Sidney Herbert. In 1862, this body became the Army Sanitary Committee, which advises the secretary of state for war on type plans, sanitary construction, and the selection of new sites.

A critical focus of these commissions was the amount of air per man. While a 1845 general order directed 450 to 500 cubic feet per man for new home stations, inspections revealed that some men in existing buildings had less than 250 cubic feet. The 1857 royal commission established a minimum scale of 600 cubic feet per man. Implementing this standard required reducing the total accommodation of some barracks by nearly one-third.

The impact of these sanitary improvements is reflected in mortality rates. In 1857, the annual mortality rate for the army at home was 17.5 per 1,000 men (compared to 9.2 for the civil male population); by 1897, this rate had dropped to 3.42 per 1,000.

## International Comparisons
Different nations employ varying architectural and site strategies:

*   **Germany and France:** Many barracks are built as large three- or four-storey blocks containing all accessories and accommodation for a complete regiment or battalion. While some modern sites use a pavilion system with separate blocks, the single block is often preferred for its architectural appeal, compactness, and ease of supervision.
*   **United States:** American barracks generally utilize a separate block system. These are often centered around a soldiers' club or post-exchange, which serves as a gymnasium, sergeants' mess, and recreation establishment with an attached bath-house.
*   **Naval Barracks:** Facilities at Devonport, Chatham, and Portsmouth are designed to maintain "board-ship life." They feature large rooms with caulked floors and hammocks, which are stowed during the day to allow the rooms to function as mess-rooms.

## Modern British Barrack Layout
A modern infantry battalion barrack is designed for maximum hygiene and organization. Unmarried soldiers are placed in rooms of twelve men to balance privacy with comradeship. These rooms provide 600 cubic feet of air per man, featuring windows on both sides, inlet ventilators, and extract shafts warmed by chimney flues to maintain air circulation.

To keep the air pure, food is no longer served in the barrack-rooms; instead, men assemble in lofty, well-ventilated dining rooms warmed by hot water. Other site features include:
*   **Ablution and Sanitary Facilities:** Basins and foot-baths are located near the barrack-room door, while water-closets and urinals are separated by a lobby.
*   **Support Buildings:** Sites include cook-houses, central bath-houses with hot water, and army schools located between the barracks and married quarters.
*   **Administrative and Technical Facilities:** Large stations may require supply depots with bakeries and abattoirs, engineer offices with workshops, and specialized works for sewage disposal, electricity, or water supply.
