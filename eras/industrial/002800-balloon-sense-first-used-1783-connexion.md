# Balloon

## Definition and Etymology
A balloon is a globular bag constructed from varnished silk or other materials that are impermeable to air. When these bags are inflated with a gas that is lighter than common air, they can be utilized in aeronautics or for any other purpose where the ability to float and rise in the atmosphere is required. 

The term "balloon" in this specific aeronautical sense was first used in 1783, associated with the invention of the brothers Montgolfier. However, the word existed prior to this usage, derived from the Italian word *ballone*, meaning a large ball. In earlier contexts, the term referred to an actual ball or ball-game, a chemical receiver or retort, a primitive firework or explosive bomb, and an ornamental globe used in architecture. Due to the shape and appearance of the air balloon, the term has been applied by analogy to other items, including "balloon training" in the field of horticulture and "balloon skirts" in dress.

## Atmospheric Flight and Altitude
Balloons are used to ascend through the atmosphere, a thin covering of air surrounding the sphere. As a balloon rises from sea-shore level, the air becomes continually less dense. At a height of twenty miles, the air is so thin—less than one hundredth part of the density at the sea surface—that it cannot support life.

The altitude reached by balloons varies based on their purpose:
* **Manned Balloons:** Balloons carrying humans have reached heights of nearly seven miles, though this is achieved at the cost of considerable physical suffering.
* **Experimental Balloons:** Small balloons containing recording instruments rather than people have reached altitudes as high as twenty-two miles.

For comparison, most small insects and birds carried up by balloons or aeroplanes drop off insensible at much lower levels, and the highest point a bird (specifically the condor) is said to be able to struggle up to is about four miles.

## Military and Specialized Applications
Balloons have been employed in military operations, including the use of "kite-balloon stations." In certain naval air service operations, such as those coordinated by district commanders in cooperation with French naval air services, specific stations are designated for balloons, dirigibles, and seaplanes. Experimental balloon work has also been conducted at Brest.

In combat environments, "captive balloons" have been used for observation. These balloons may be stationed at heights such as 150 metres, with an officer situated in the car using a telephone to speak incessantly. While the officer in such a balloon cannot be reached by enemy fire, the balloon itself remains in danger; records indicate that French aviators have used bombs dropped from great heights to hit these balloons.

## Other Uses of the Term
Beyond aeronautics and military use, the term "balloon" appears in other distinct contexts:
* **Architecture and Construction:** The "Balloon frame" is a method of house construction that is less expensive and more rapidly erected than other methods. It utilizes smaller scantlings and is held together entirely by spikes and nails. The process involves laying a sill (generally 4 in. x 6 in.), erecting corner posts, and setting up common studs that run the full height from the sill to the roof plate. These studs are spiked to the sill, and floor joists are supported by a "ribbon" or "false girt" (a 1-1/4 in. x 7 in. board) notched into the inside edge of the studs.
* **Social Dance:** In the *Cotillon*, a modern development of a French dance, air-balloons are among the various toys, accessories, and presents used during the elaborate figures of the dance.
