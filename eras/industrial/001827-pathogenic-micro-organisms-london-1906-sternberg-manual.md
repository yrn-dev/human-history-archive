# Bacteriology and Pathogenic Micro-organisms

Bacteriology is the study of minute organisms commonly referred to as "bacteria," though they are also known by terms such as "microbes," "micro-organisms," "microphytes," "bacilli," and "micrococci." While these terms are frequently used, they are often considered scientifically vague or technically narrow. The designation "schizomycetes," proposed by Naegeli in 1857, is regarded as the most satisfactory term and is the one typically employed by botanists.

## Nature of Pathogenic Organisms
The distinction between pathogenic and non-pathogenic schizomycetes is not a sharp one. Modern knowledge indicates that the virulence or pathogenicity of these organisms can be modified—either increased or diminished—through definite treatment or by alterations in the environment during the natural course of epidemics. Similarly, a sharp division between parasites and saprophytes is impossible because certain species, known as facultative parasites, can function as either depending on the circumstances.

From an evolutionary perspective, it is supposed that certain races of bacteria gradually acquired the ability to produce disease and invade body tissues. While some original characters may have changed during the acquisition of pathogenic properties, these changes are often slight and not always permanent. Consequently, pathogenic bacteria are not organisms with unique features but are members of groups possessing closely allied characters. In some instances, the microscopical and cultural characters of a pathogenic organism are so similar to non-pathogenic members of the same group that differentiating between them is extremely difficult.

Furthermore, some bacteria that are never observed as pathogenic forms or parasites can still produce poisons or toxins as a result of their fermentations and decompositions of organic substances.

## Laboratory Identification and Culture
A full description of a bacterium requires an account of its biological properties, its microscopical characters, its growth characters in various culture media, and the effects produced by inoculation in animals. Because a species may not be readily recognized by one or two characters, a series of tests is often required. Specialized media are used to demonstrate specific actions:
* **Sugars (glucose, lactose, saccharose, etc.):** Added to test fermentative action.
* **Litrous:** Used to show changes in reaction.
* **Peptone solution:** Employed to test for the formation of indol.
* **Sterilized milk:** Used to determine if the growth curdles the medium.

In bacteriological laboratories, two temperature standards are maintained for incubators. Non-pathogenic organisms are generally cultured between 18° and 20° C, while pathogenic forms are cultured between 35° and 38° C. In temperate regions during summer or in hot countries, cold water is sometimes run through the incubator to prevent cultures, particularly gelatine ones, from being spoiled by high external temperatures.

## Medical and Biological Applications
The application of bacteriology to medicine includes the study of immunity. To achieve a high degree of specific immunity—which applies only to the toxin or bacterium used—injections must utilize a large dose of toxin or a bacterium in a very virulent state. Non-specific immunity or increased tissue resistance can be produced locally (such as in the peritoneum) using nucleic acid, peptone, non-pathogenic organisms, and other substances; however, this type of immunity cannot be transferred to another animal. Additionally, some organisms exhibit antagonistic actions; for example, the products of *B. pyocyaneus* provide a certain protective action against *B. anthracis*.

In clinical pathology, the term "specific bronchitis" refers to the inflammation of the bronchial tubes' mucous membrane caused by micro-organisms associated with infectious disorders such as pneumonia, measles, influenza, and diphtheria. This is distinguished from "non-specific bronchitis," which results from mechanical or chemical irritation or extension from above.

## General Utility of Schizomycetes
The vast majority of schizomycetes are not pathogenic; most are harmless, and many are indispensable to natural operations. They play a supreme role in daily life and agriculture through various fermentations, including the preparation of tobacco, indigo, the soaking of skins for tanning, ensilage, and hay. Some species are capable of carbon-assimilation, while others can fix free nitrogen.

Useful fermentations are also employed in the production of beverages. The Japanese beer *saké* is produced using the mould *Aspergillus oryzae*, which performs both saccharification (converting starch to sugar) and fermentation. *Weissbier*, produced largely in Berlin, involves a symbiotic fermentation between a fission fungus (the lactic acid bacillus) and a yeast.
