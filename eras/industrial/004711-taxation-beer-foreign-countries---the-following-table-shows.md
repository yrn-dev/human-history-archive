# Taxation of Beer in Foreign Countries

The taxation of beer varies significantly across different nations, with governments employing diverse methods of levy, including taxes on the finished product, the raw materials used in production, or the specific volume and quality of the brew.

## International Taxation Rates
Taxation levels are often calculated based on the English barrel to allow for comparison between countries. In the United States, a beer tax is applied at a rate of 5s. 9d. per English barrel. In the Austro-Hungarian Empire, the tax is levied on wort at a rate of 6s. 8d. Similarly, France applies a tax on wort amounting to 4s. 1d.

Several countries utilize a malt tax rather than a tax on the finished beer. Russia employs a malt tax ranging from 5s. to 6s. 8d. Belgium's malt tax is 2s. 9d. In Germany, the North German Customs apply a malt tax of 1s. 6d., while in Bavaria, the malt tax ranges from 3s. 5d. to 4s. 8d., depending on the quantity produced. Holland utilizes a different system, taxing the cubic contents of the mash-tun or the quality of the malt, with amounts ranging from approximately 1s. 9d. to 3s. 3d.

## The Austro-Hungarian System
The Austro-Hungarian monarchy operates as a single identical customs and commercial territory, which includes the principality of Liechtenstein as well as Bosnia and Herzegovina. This arrangement is based on a customs and commercial agreement concluded in 1867 and renewed in 1899. Under this compact, the two states follow the same principles regarding commercial affairs and customs legislation. This includes specific legislation on duties closely connected to industrial production, such as those applied to mineral oils, sugar, brandy, and beer.

## Historical Taxation in the United Kingdom
The development of the brewing industry in England is closely linked to the history of its taxation and the regulations created to protect government revenue. The first beer duty in the United Kingdom was introduced during the reign of Charles II in 1660, with rates of 6d. per barrel for weak beer and 2s. 6d. for strong beer. By the end of the 17th century, these rates rose to 1s. 3d. for weak beer and 4s. 9d. for strong beer, eventually reaching 8s. to 10s. by the year 1800. This duty remained until it was repealed in 1830.

In addition to beer duties, a malt tax was introduced in 1697 during the reign of William III. Initially under 7d. per bushel, this tax increased to 2s. 7d. before the 1830 repeal of the beer duty, and later rose to 4s. 6d. Between 1856 and its eventual abolition, the malt tax remained constant at slightly under 2s. 8½d. In 1829, the combined cost of malt and beer taxes was 13s. 8d. per barrel (or 4½d. per gallon).

The United Kingdom also implemented a hop duty between 1711 and 1862, which ranged from 1d. to 2½d. per pound. This duty was abolished primarily because crop uncertainty caused wide fluctuations in revenue; for example, revenue dropped from £728,183 in 1855 to only £149,700 in 1861.

## Production and Consumption Context
Taxation and industry growth are often viewed alongside production efficiency and consumption habits. While Germany produces roughly 25% more beer by liquid measurement than the United Kingdom, the United Kingdom uses approximately 50% more malt. According to a Viennese technical journal, the amount of malt required to produce one hectolitre (22 gallons) of beer is 0.81 cwt. in the United Kingdom, 0.72 cwt. in the United States, and 0.40 cwt. in the German empire.

Consumption patterns also vary by region. Bavaria has the highest per capita consumption at over 50 gallons, with the city of Munich exceeding 70 gallons (roughly 1-1/2 pints daily per person). Belgium follows with 47.7 gallons. In the British colonies, consumption is lower: Western Australia consumes about 25 gallons per head, while general Australian consumption is 12 gallons, New Zealand is 10 gallons, and Canada is 5 gallons.
