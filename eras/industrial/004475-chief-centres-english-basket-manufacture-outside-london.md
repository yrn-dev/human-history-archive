# English Basket Manufacture

## Primary Manufacturing Centres
Outside of London, the chief centres for the manufacture of baskets in England are Grantham, Basford (located near Nottingham), and Thurmaston (located near Leicester). Additionally, baskets are manufactured in Castle Donington, a town in the Loughborough parliamentary division of Leicestershire.

## Evolution of the Industry
Since approximately the middle of the 19th century, the nature of English basket-work has undergone significant modification. Traditional domestic items, including the old English reticule and cradle, as well as other small wares, were driven out of the market by cheap goods produced on the European continent. Similarly, rough casks and cases made from cheap imported timber largely superseded the use of hampers and coarse brown osier packing.

However, these losses were more than counterbalanced by a shift toward higher-class production. This includes the manufacture of travel requisites, such as tea-baskets and luncheon baskets, as well as furniture items including lounges, tables, and finely made chairs. Other primary categories of English manufacture include:
* Laundry and linen baskets
* Vegetable and fruit baskets
* Partition baskets for wine
* Transit and travelling hampers
* Protective wicker cases for fragile items, such as stone and other bottles or glass carboys

Furthermore, the English military authorities introduced wicker cases or shields made from cane pith for the protection of shells. The expansion of these developments is evidenced by the wages lists of the London Union of Journeymen Basketmakers; the list issued in 1865 spanned 87 printed pages, whereas the 1896 list grew to 144 more closely set pages.

## Craftsmanship and Labour
Basket-making is a process that utilizes no machinery. The quality of the texture and the perfection of the shape depend entirely on the craftsman's natural aptitude, training, and their ability to impress a conception of form onto a recalcitrant material. In England, craftsmen rarely utilize a mould; because every stroke has a permanent effect on the symmetry of the work, no subsequent pressure can alter it.

In London, wages for these workers vary between 25s. and 50s. per week, depending on their aptitude. The city of London is also home to the Basketmakers' Company, which remains one of the oldest craft gilds. 

Social welfare organizations also participate in the trade. The London Association for the Welfare of the Blind employs partially or wholly blind workpeople to create coarser baskets. Because this specific work is not commercially remunerative, the association relies on contributions from the charitable and the sale of fancy or fine work, much of which is imported, to provide partial support. Similar associations are found in Edinburgh, Dublin, Belfast, some English provincial towns, and various European cities.

## International Trade and Materials
The United Kingdom's Board of Trade has enumerated the import and export values of rods and willows for basket-making, as well as basket-ware, since 1900. In 1900, foreign basket-ware imports were valued at £239,402, increasing to £264,183 in 1901, before declining to £227,070 by 1905.

A comparison of supply sources between 1900 and 1905 shows the following values:
* **Belgium**: Increased from £72,031 to £77,766.
* **Holland**: Decreased from £58,214 to £54,407.
* **France**: Decreased significantly from £55,870 to £27,910.
* **Germany**: Decreased from £33,155 to £22,892.
* **Japan**: Increased remarkably from £8,140 to £25,536 (reaching £52,377 in 1904).
* **Portugal**: Decreased from £5,066 to £3,971.

Beyond the UK, various materials are used globally. The Chinese and Japanese use bamboo splits for furniture and for encasing egg-shell porcelain. In India, "Cajan" baskets are made from the fronds of the Palmyra palm (*Borassus flabelliformis*), a method also established as a staple in Germany's Black Forest. Other materials include the leaves of screw pine and the odorous roots of khus-khus grass (*Anatherum muricatum*) in the East and India. In the Seychelles, the palm *Lodoicea sechellarum* is used for delicate work, while New Zealand utilizes the flax plant (*Phormium tenax*). In Algeria and Spain, esparto fibre is used for rude fruit baskets. Other materials include various sedges (*Cyperus*), straw, wood splints, and shavings. In South America and the West Indies, species of *Maranta* and *Tirite* (*Calathea*) are employed.
