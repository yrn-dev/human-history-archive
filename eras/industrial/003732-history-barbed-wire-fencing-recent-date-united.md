# Barbed Wire Fencing

Barbed wire is a protective variety of fencing typically consisting of several strands of wire twisted together, with sharp points or spikes fastened into the strands. While used globally, the industry's real home is the United States, where it was developed to meet the specific needs of the cattle-raising industry in western states where timber was scarce.

## Origins and Development
Prior to the invention of barbed wire, the frontier of the United States and South America utilized No. 9 round or oval iron wire. However, iron was unsuitable due to a lack of homogeneity and insufficient tensile strength. Single strands of iron wire frequently broke because they stretched in warm weather and contracted in cold weather. Furthermore, cattle would rub against smooth fences, creating constant pressure that broke the wire and loosened the posts.

To solve these defects, farmers and other ingenious individuals sought a way to confine animals without the fence being destroyed. This led to the invention of barbed wire. Early patents were taken out in 1867 by Lucien B. Smith of Kent, Ohio, and William B. Hunt of Scott, N.Y., followed a year later by Michael Kelly of New York. The industry's practical beginning occurred in 1874 with patents issued to Joseph F. Glidden of De Kalb, Ill., for barbed fence wire, and a subsequent patent for a manufacturing machine issued to Glidden and Phineas W. Vaughan.

The development of the industry was made possible by the use of steel, specifically Bessemer and open-hearth steels, which possess the necessary tensile strength and homogeneity that iron lacked. The subsequent invention of automatic machinery allowed barbs to be placed on smooth wire rapidly and cheaply, making barbed wire less expensive than the smooth wire previously in general use.

## Manufacturing Process
Barbed wire is manufactured using soft Siemens-Martin or Bessemer steel. The wire is drawn in a mill and then undergoes a continuous galvanizing process to protect it from atmospheric corrosion and provide a bright finish. In this process, 30 to 60 wires pass simultaneously through an annealing medium (heated fire-brick tubes or red-hot lead) to soften the wire, then through a bath of muriatic acid to remove scale, and finally through a bath of spelter heated slightly above its melting point. Alternatively, some fencing is painted; in these cases, galvanizing is skipped, and the finished wire is submerged in paint and dried.

The barbing and twisting of the two longitudinal strand wires are performed by automatic machinery. For 2-point or 4-point Glidden wire, two main strand coils are fed into the machine, while one or two additional coils are fed at right angles. The machine cuts the cross wire diagonally to form a sharp point, wraps it firmly around a strand wire, and cuts it again to leave a second sharp point. A single machine can produce between 2,000 and 3,000 lb of wire during a ten-hour day.

## Applications and Advantages
Barbed wire is shipped on wooden spools holding approximately 100 lb or 80 to 100 rods. It is attached to posts—which may be wooden, steel, or reinforced concrete—using galvanized steel wire staples made from No. 9 wire.

The use of two twisted strands allows the fence to automatically adjust to temperature changes; the twist loosens during heat expansion and tightens during cold contraction without causing sag or altering the relative lengths of the wires. Other advantages include:
* **Durability:** Galvanized wire is nearly imperishable and does not rack posts by opposing the wind, which prevents water from settling and rotting the posts.
* **Effectiveness:** It serves as a barrier against dogs, wild beasts, and all kinds of stock. Cattle generally avoid pressing against it once they discover its nature.
* **Visibility and Environment:** It does not obstruct the view across a farm and does not contribute to the formation of snow drifts.
* **Economy:** It is cheap to construct and covers large amounts of land economically.

Beyond farming, barbed wire is used in field fortifications as "high wire entanglements," where 4 ft. high pointed stakes (pickets) are planted in rows and connected by lengths of barbed wire at the top and bottom.

## Production and Market Trends
Production in the United States grew rapidly following Glidden's 1874 patent. Production rose from 5 tons in 1874 to 40,000 tons by 1880, reaching 200,000 tons by 1900 and 250,000 tons by 1907.

While popular, the material faced some early opposition in America on humanitarian grounds, though this was silenced following extended tests of the material's humanity and economy. Globally, its use has been less extensive than in the western United States. While used in Australia and Argentina, these countries utilize more plain wire; Argentina specifically consumes a high-carbon oval fence wire of great strength.

Since 1899, there has been an increasing demand for meshed wire field fencing, particularly along railway lines and in thickly settled areas. This shift is attributed to the high cost and scarcity of labor and the decline of rail fence construction. Meshed wire is often preferred for smaller animals like hogs and sheep. By 1907, the production of meshed field fencing (425,000 tons) had surpassed that of barbed wire (250,000 tons).

## Legal Regulations
The use of barbed wire is regulated by law in some American states, primarily regarding its placement on highways or its use as a property line marker between owners (which sometimes requires mutual agreement). Some states require a top rail of lumber.

In England, the Barbed Wire Act 1893 allows local authorities to require landowners to remove barbed wire fences adjoining highways if the wire is likely to be injurious to persons or animals. If the occupier fails to abate the "nuisance" after written notice, a court of summary jurisdiction may order its removal, and the local authority may execute the order and recover the expenses from the occupier.
