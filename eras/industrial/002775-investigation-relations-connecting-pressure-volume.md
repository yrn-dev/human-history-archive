# The Investigation of the Relations Connecting Pressure, Volume, and Temperature

The study of the relationships between pressure, volume, and temperature manifests across several scientific disciplines, ranging from the study of gases and thermodynamics to the specialized field of interior ballistics. These relations are fundamental to determining the density of substances, the behavior of materials during phase changes, and the dynamics of explosive forces within a contained bore.

## Interior Ballistics
Interior ballistics is the specific branch of science dedicated to investigating the relations connecting the temperature, volume, and pressure of powder-gas inside the bore of a gun. This field further examines the dynamics of the shot's movement up the bore, the stress placed upon the gun's material, and the work realized through the expansion of the powder. In this context, a gun is viewed as a simple heat-engine or thermo-dynamic machine that performs its work in a single stroke, rather than through the periodic cycles characteristic of gas or steam engines.

Historical experimentation in this field includes the work of Chevalier d'Arcy in 1760, who measured bullet velocity using a ballistic pendulum while successively shortening the musket barrel. More modern results are derived from the experiments of Abel and Noble. Their methodology involves firing a charge of explosive of varying weight (P lb) within an explosion-chamber of a known volume (C cub. in.). A crusher-gauge is used to record the pressure (p). These results are plotted to show the relation between pressure and either the gravimetric volume (v)—the ratio of the volume of the gas to the volume of an equal weight of water—or the gravimetric density (D), which is the specific gravity of the powder when filling the known volume in a state of gas.

## Gas Density and Volume
The determination of absolute densities of gases requires precise measurement of pressure and volume. A method originated by Regnault involves filling a large glass globe with pure, dry gas using an air-pump. The flask is cooled to 0° by immersion in melting ice, the pressure is recorded, and the flask is then weighed at room temperature. By partially exhausting the flask and repeating the cooling and weighing process, the difference in weights corresponds to the volume of gas at a pressure equal to the difference in recorded pressures.

This method was further refined by Lord Rayleigh and Morley. Morley utilized high-efficiency air-pumps to exhaust the flask to an almost complete vacuum during his investigation into the composition of water. Lord Rayleigh, in his work on artificial and atmospheric nitrogen (which led to the discovery of argon), identified a critical correction to Regnault's method: the experimental globe's volume changes due to shrinkage under diminished pressure, an effect ranging between 0.16% and 0.04% of the globe's volume.

Generally, when expressing the density of a gas, it is necessary to specify the pressure and temperature. If a mass (M) of gas occupies a volume (V) at temperature (T) and pressure (P), its absolute density is $\Delta = M/V$. If conditions change to $P_1$ and $T_1$, the new volume $V_1$ is $VPT/P_1T_1$. To standardize these measurements, gases are often reduced to "normal temperature and pressure" (N.T.P.), defined as 760 mm and 0°C.

## Pressure and Phase Changes
Pressure exerts a significant influence on the fusing-point (F.P.) of substances, depending on whether the substance expands or contracts during the process. Substances that expand upon freezing, such as ice, experience a lowering of their freezing points when pressure increases. Conversely, substances that expand upon fusing, such as wax, see their melting points raised by pressure. In both instances, the effect of pressure is to retard the increase of volume.

This phenomenon was predicted by James Thomson and numerically verified by Bunsen (for spermaceti and paraffin) and Lord Kelvin (for ice). The change in the fusing-point can be calculated using a simple application of the Carnot cycle. The relationship is expressed as $d[\theta]/dp = [\theta](v'' - v')/L$, where $L$ is the latent heat of fusion in mechanical units, $v'$ is the volume of unit mass of the solid, and $v''$ is that of the liquid.

Furthermore, the latent heat of fusion is affected by temperature; it generally increases as temperature rises and diminishes as it falls. It is theoretically possible for pressure to lower the F.P. to a point where latent heat vanishes, potentially resulting in a continuous passage from a liquid to a solid state, similar to amorphous substances.

## Osmotic Pressure and Solubility
The relationship between pressure and volume also applies to the solubility of substances. The lowering of the fusing-point in a solution as concentration increases can be explained by the osmotic pressure of the dissolved substance. In salt solutions, separating 1 gramme of salt using a semi-permeable membrane requires work ($PV$) against the osmotic pressure ($P$), where $V$ is the diminution in the volume of the solution. In dilute solutions, the osmotic pressure is equal to that of an equal number of gas molecules in the same space, deduced from the gas equation $P = R[\theta]C/M$, where $C$ is the concentration of the solution.
