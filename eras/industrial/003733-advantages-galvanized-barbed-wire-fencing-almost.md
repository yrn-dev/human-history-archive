# Barbed Wire Fencing

Barbed wire is a protective variety of fencing typically consisting of several strands of wire twisted together, with sharp spikes or points fastened into the strands. The industry developed primarily in the United States to address the needs of the cattle-raising industry in western states, where timber was scarce.

## History and Development
Prior to the introduction of barbed wire, No. 9 round or oval iron wire was popular in South America and on the United States frontier. However, iron lacked homogeneity and sufficient tensile strength. Single strands of iron wire frequently broke because they stretched in warm weather and contracted in cold weather. Additionally, cattle would rub against smooth fences, which broke the wire and loosened the posts.

The development of the industry was made possible by the use of Bessemer and open-hearth steels, which possess high tensile strength and homogeneity. Early patents were taken out in 1867 by Lucien B. Smith and William B. Hunt, and in 1868 by Michael Kelly. The practical foundation of the industry began in 1874 with patents issued to Joseph F. Glidden for barbed fence wire, and to Glidden and Phineas W. Vaughan for a manufacturing machine.

## Manufacturing Process
Barbed wire is manufactured from soft Siemens-Martin or Bessemer steel. The wire is drawn in a mill and then undergoes a continuous galvanizing process to protect it from atmospheric corrosion and provide a bright finish. This process involves:
1. Passing the wire through an annealing medium (heated fire-brick tubes or red-hot lead) to soften it.
2. Feeding the wire through a bath of muriatic acid to remove scale.
3. Washing the wire in water.
4. Passing the wire through a bath of spelter heated slightly above its melting point.
5. Cooling the wire with water before winding it into coils.

Between 30 and 60 wires pass through this apparatus simultaneously. For those desiring painted fencing, the galvanizing step is omitted, and the finished wire is submerged in paint and dried. The twisting of the two longitudinal strands and the addition of barbs are performed by automatic machinery. For example, in 2 and 4 point Glidden wire, cross wires are fed at right angles to the strand wire, cut diagonally to form sharp points, and wrapped firmly around the strand.

## Advantages and Characteristics
Galvanized barbed wire is described as almost imperishable and economical in both its initial cost and the amount of land it covers. Because it does not present a large surface to the wind, it does not rack the posts, which prevents water from settling around them and causing rot. It is quickly constructed and does not harbor weeds.

The fencing is effective as a barrier against wild beasts, dogs, and all kinds of stock. Once cattle discover the nature of the wire, they will not go near it or press against it, allowing farmers to divide ranches into desired fields. Furthermore, the fence does not impede the view, allowing a man to see across his farm as if no fences were present. Because it does not contribute to the formation of snow drifts, it is frequently used along highways and railroads.

A key technical advantage is the use of two wires twisted together. This design allows the fence to adapt to varying temperatures; heat loosens the twist without causing a sag, and cold tightens it without altering the relative lengths of the wires. This ensures the fence remains taut through many seasons without repair.

## Installation and Usage
Barbed wire is usually shipped on wooden spools holding approximately 80 to 100 rods or 100 lb. It must be stretched with great care using a suitable barbed wire stretcher. The wire is attached to posts using galvanized steel wire staples, typically made from No. 9 wire with long, diagonal points. While wooden posts were originally used, steel and reinforced concrete posts have become popular to increase durability.

Beyond farming, barbed wire is used in field fortifications as "high wire entanglements." In this application, 4 ft. high pointed stakes (pickets) are planted in rows and secured to the ground with pegs, with lengths of barbed wire connecting each picket at the top and bottom.

## Production and Regulation
Production in the United States grew rapidly from 5 tons in 1874 to 250,000 tons by 1907. During this time, meshed wire field fencing also grew in popularity, particularly after 1899, as a better enclosure for hogs and sheep.

The use of barbed wire is subject to various laws. In some American states, regulations apply to its placement on highways, and some prohibit its use as a property line marker unless both owners agree. Some states require a top rail of lumber. In England, the Barbed Wire Act 1893 allows local authorities to require the removal of barbed wire if it is deemed a nuisance or injurious to persons and animals lawfully using a highway.
