## The Bank of England and the Act of 1844

The early history of the Bank of England is detailed in Thorold Rogers' *History of the First Nine Years of the Bank of England*. A pivotal development in its operation was the act of 1844, the details of which were described by Sir Robert Peel in parliament on May 6, 1844. 

Under this act, the bank was divided into two distinct departments:
1. **The Issue Department**: This department was responsible for the issue of notes. Bullion held by the bank was transferred here. The issue of notes was restricted to £14,000,000 based on securities, with any additional issues based on bullion and governed by fluctuations in bullion stocks.
2. **The Banking Department**: This department handled the ordinary business of banking.

The act also changed the bank's reporting requirements. Previously, the bank only needed to publish a balance-sheet for the previous quarter every month. Following the act, the bank was required to issue weekly returns. The first of these new returns was issued on September 7, 1844. Unlike the old form, which provided a general statement of assets and liabilities, the new form showed the balance-sheets of the Issue and Banking Departments separately.

## Stockbroking and Legal Requirements

A stockbroker is defined as a broker who contracts for the sale of shares and stocks. They differ from "brokers proper" primarily because stocks and shares are not classified as "goods." Consequently, the requirement for a memorandum in writing established by the Sale of Goods Act 1893 does not apply to stockbrokers. This allows principals to bring actions for the sale of stocks and shares even in the absence of a written memorandum. For example, if a broker fails and is declared a defaulter, a jobber who cannot recover the price of shares from the buyer's broker may sue the buyer whose name was passed.

The employment of stockbrokers is governed by the rules and customs of the Stock Exchange. However, illegal customs are not binding on clients. An example is the Stock Exchange practice of disregarding Leeman's Act (1867), which mandates that contracts for the sale of joint-stock bank shares are void unless the registered numbers of the shares are stated. While such a contract may be unenforceable, a broker is still entitled to be indemnified by a client for money paid on the client's behalf if the client specifically instructed the broker to buy shares according to that practice.
