# Austria in the Mid-19th Century

## Political State in 1851
By 1851, Austria appeared to have overcome its primary difficulties. The revolutionary movements of the era had been suppressed, and the old Federal Diet of 1815 was restored. Additionally, Austria had defeated the efforts of Prussia to assume leadership within Germany. This period saw Vienna return as the center of a despotic government. The primary objectives of this regime were to suppress any attempts to secure a free press, check all agitation for a constitution, and Germanize the Slavs and Magyars. For approximately ten years, the Austrian dominion existed under one of the worst possible forms of autocratic government.

## Relations with Prussia and Germany
The restoration of the *status quo ante* and the recovery of Austrian influence in Germany were facilitated by the capitulation of Vilagos, which ended the Hungarian insurrection and gave Schwarzenberg a free hand. A significant milestone in this process was the Convention of Olmuetz on September 29, 1850. While the convention seemed to be a complete triumph for Austria over Prussia at the time, Count Beust later characterized it not as a Prussian humiliation, but as an "Austrian weakness." Rather than using its power to crush Prussia and end the unworkable dual influence in the Confederation, Austria chose to re-establish a discredited system. This decision provided Prussia with the time and opportunity to gather strength for an inevitable future conflict.

## The Crimean War and International Diplomacy
The despotic regime of the Habsburg emperor eventually failed due to three main factors: the successful assertion of Prussian leadership in Germany, the establishment of Italian unity, and the Crimean War. 

The Crimean War highlighted that international affairs are rarely governed by "gratitude." Although Emperor Nicholas of Russia relied on a sense of obligation, Austrian statesmen prioritized the free navigation of the Danube, which was threatened by Russian occupation of the Principalities. Austria initially avoided active participation in the war due to the fear of a Sardinian attack on its Italian provinces and the refusal of Prussia to join her. 

However, after Russian forces withdrew from the Principalities, Austrian troops occupied them. On December 2, 1854, Austria signed a treaty of alliance in Vienna with France and Great Britain. Under this agreement, Austria undertook to defend the frontier of the Principalities against returning Russian forces and occupy Moldavia and Walachia. Article III of the treaty stipulated that the alliance would become effective, both offensively and defensively, should war break out between Austria and Russia. As Russian arms suffered progressive disasters, Austria became bolder, delivering an ultimatum to Emperor Alexander II in December 1855 that forced Russia to come to terms.

## Diplomatic Involvement in Denmark
In 1851 and 1852, Austria and Prussia were involved in the foreign relations of Denmark. The continental powers viewed the new Danish constitution as too democratic, and German sentiment opposed the Liberal Eiderdansk party's plan to divide Schleswig into three administrative belts based on nationality. 

Through the Conventions of 1851 and 1852, an arrangement was made where each part of the monarchy maintained local autonomy under a common constitution for common affairs. Austria and Prussia consented to participate in the London conference, which upheld the integrity of Denmark and settled the succession on Prince Christian (the husband of Louise of Hesse and son of Duke William of Schleswig-Holstein-Sonderburg-Glücksburg). Duke Christian of Sonderburg-Augustenburg, the heir under Salic law, accepted this decision after the Danish government purchased his estates in Schleswig.
