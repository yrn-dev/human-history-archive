# Board of Trade

The Board of Trade is a public department of the state in Great Britain that is directly responsible to parliament. Historically, certain functions now discharged by the Board of Trade were previously the responsibility of a committee of the lords of the council.

## Role in Bankruptcy Administration
Following the act of 1883, a separation was established between judicial and administrative functions. The administrative functions, which had previously been exercised by the court, were transferred to the Board of Trade. The primary objective of this commercial legislation was to promote and enforce principles of commercial morality for the benefit of the trading community while providing regulations for the equitable distribution of a debtor's assets.

Under the powers granted by the act, the bankruptcy department of the Board of Trade was created. This department is a subordinate division of the board and is headed by an officer known as the inspector-general in bankruptcy. Under the direction of the Board of Trade, this department provides general supervision over all administrative work arising under the act. Its responsibilities include:
*   Conducting audits of trustees' accounts.
*   Exercising extensive control over the appointment of trustees.
*   Removing trustees from office for unfitness, neglect, or misconduct, a decision that may be appealed to the court.

The Board of Trade presents an annual report on the proceedings under the act to parliament. Although the department is practically self-supporting, a nominal vote is placed on the public estimates each year to ensure the administration remains under direct parliamentary control and criticism.

## Official Receivers
The Board of Trade is responsible for the appointment and removal of officers called official receivers. These officers operate within their respective districts under the general authority and directions of the Board of Trade. They are clothed with the status of officers of the courts to which they are attached. 

The duties of official receivers relate to the administration of estates and the conduct of bankrupts. This includes the interim management of estates until creditors can be consulted. By operation of law, an official receiver becomes a trustee upon the making of an order of adjudication. They hold this office until a trustee is appointed by the creditors and certified by the Board of Trade. However, the official receiver resumes the role of trustee if the creditors' trustee dies, resigns, is removed from office, or is released.

## Supervision of Trustees
While the ultimate distribution and realization of assets are the responsibility of a trustee appointed by creditors, the Board of Trade maintains significant oversight. A trustee may be appointed by a committee of inspection or by a majority in value of voting creditors, but this appointment requires confirmation by the Board of Trade.

The Board of Trade may object to an appointment on several grounds:
*   The person appointed is not fit to act.
*   The creditors did not act in good faith in the interests of the general body.
*   The appointee occupies a position relative to the estate, any creditor, or the debtor that makes impartiality difficult.
*   The appointee was previously removed from office for failure to render accounts for audit without good cause or for misconduct.

If the Board of Trade objects, the appointment is fatal unless a majority in value of the creditors appeals to the High Court. Additionally, the trustee-elect must provide security to the satisfaction of the Board of Trade. This security must be maintained at the originally fixed amount, or a lesser amount if required by the department; failure to do so makes the trustee liable for removal. In instances where creditors fail to appoint a trustee, the Board of Trade may make the appointment, though creditors may supersede this at any time.

## Other References
In the United States, the term "Board of Trade" is associated with commodity exchanges. In Chicago, the Board of Trade forbade dealings in "options" in grain of less than 5,000 bushels. This regulation led to the opening of an "Open Board of Trade" (an unauthorized exchange) in a neighboring street to accommodate small gamblers.
