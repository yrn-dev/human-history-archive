# The Comparative Grammar of Franz Bopp

Franz Bopp (1791–1867) was a German philologist whose work is credited with the beginning of the science of comparative grammar. His academic career was marked by a deep interest in the languages and literature of the East, a passion stimulated during his youth at the Lyceum in Aschaffenburg by the lectures of Karl J. Windischmann and the influence of Fr. Schlegel's 1808 work, *Uber die Sprache und Weisheit der Indier*.

## Academic Foundation and Early Research
Bopp's formal pursuit of Sanskrit began in 1812 in Paris, where he studied for four years with the support of the Bavarian government. During this time, he utilized the Imperial library's collection of Sanskrit manuscripts and books from the Serampore and Calcutta presses, and associated with scholars such as Alexander Hamilton, L.M. Langles, S. de Sacy, and A.L. Chezy.

His initial research focused on the common origin of grammatical structures. In 1816, he published *Uber das Conjugationssystem der Sanskritsprache in Vergleichung mit jenem der griechischen, lateinischen, persischen und germanischen Sprache*. In this work, Bopp did not seek to prove the common parentage of these languages—as that had already been established—but rather to trace the origin of their grammatical forms. He later expanded this research in an 1820 essay titled "Analytical Comparison of the Sanskrit, Greek, Latin and Teutonic Languages," which extended his analysis from the verb alone to all parts of grammar.

## The Comparative Grammar
Bopp's primary scholarly achievement was the elaboration of his *Comparative Grammar* (*Vergleichende Grammatik des Sanskrit, Zend, Griechischen, Lateinischen, Litthauischen, Altslavischen, Gothischen, und Deutschen*). This work was published in six parts between 1833 and 1852. The development of the text was preceded by a series of monographs published in the *Transactions of the Berlin Academy* from 1824 to 1831, titled *Vergleichende Zergliederung des Sanskrits und der mit ihm verwandten Sprachen*.

The scope of the work evolved over time; for instance, Old-Slavonian was included from the second part onwards, and a revised German edition published between 1856 and 1861 incorporated Old-Armenian. The work reached a wider international audience through an English translation by E.B. Eastwick in 1845 and a French translation by Professor Michel Breal in 1866.

## Objectives and Methodology
The task Bopp endeavoured to carry out in his *Comparative Grammar* was threefold:
1. To provide a description of the original grammatical structure of the languages, as deduced through their intercomparison.
2. To trace the phonetic laws of these languages.
3. To investigate the origin of their grammatical forms.

Bopp viewed the first two objectives as subservient to the third. His methodology involved a meticulous analysis of linguistic phenomena, characterized by "wonderful penetration" into minute and almost microscopical details. By basing his researches on the best available sources and incorporating new findings, Bopp provided a wide view into the original seats, the affinity, and the domestic usages, practices, and tenets of the ancient Indo-European nations.

## Other Contributions and Legacy
Beyond his comparative work, Bopp held the chair of Sanskrit and comparative grammar at Berlin starting in 1821 and was a member of the Royal Prussian Academy. He authored the *Ausfuhrliches Lehrgebaude der Sanskrita-Sprache* (1827), a shorter grammar (1834), and a Sanskrit and Latin glossary (1830), the latter of which accounted for cognate languages in its 1847 and 1867 editions. He also edited episodes of the Mahabharata, including *Nala and Damayanti*, before focusing exclusively on grammatical investigations.

The influence of Bopp's work extended to other philologists; for example, John William Donaldson's *The New Cratylus* (1839) was founded mainly on Bopp's comparative grammar.

Bopp died on October 23, 1867. In recognition of his contributions, *Die Bopp-Stiftung* was established in Berlin on May 16, 1866, to promote the study of comparative grammar and Sanskrit.
