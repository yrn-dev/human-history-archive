# French Livres-à-Vignettes and 18th-Century English Book Illustration

## Development of 18th-Century Book Production
The 18th century was characterized by a notable improvement in presswork, type, and paper within England and France, with similar advancements appearing in Italy and Germany toward the end of the century. During this period, books generally became more elegant and neat. This era saw a revival in book illustration, specifically through the French *livres-à-vignettes*.

## Illustration in England and France
English books of the 18th century were illustrated by French artists, including Gravelot. Later in the century, specifically during the last quarter, the work of Bewick led to a significant revival in woodcut illustrations. Due to the use of the graver, these were then referred to as wood engravings. 

While the French books of the 18th century are well-chronicled by H. Cohen in the *Guide de l'amateur de livre a gravures du XVIII^e siecle* (also referred to as *Guide de l'amateur de livres a figures et a vignettes du XVIII^e siecle*), other periods of illustration have been less appreciated. For example, the copper-engraved book illustrations of later England and the Netherlands, as well as the "pretty French books" of the middle of the 16th century, have been imperfectly appreciated. In contrast, a sudden fashion has arisen for English books featuring coloured illustrations.

## Binding and Material Trends
In the 18th century, the technique of average bookbinding improved, although the best binders of the era remained inferior to those of the 17th century until the arrival of Roger Payne. There was a shift in trade bindings away from the use of calf and sheepskin, with books mostly being cased in paper boards.

In France, a strong attraction to fine bindings persisted; the high prices of French first editions and Elzevirs were often attributed more to their 18th and 17th-century jackets than to the content of the books. In England, the appreciation for old bindings increased following an 1891 exhibition at the Burlington Fine Arts Club. This included a heightened interest in embroidered bindings, English blind stamped bindings, and those attributable to Samuel Mearne from the time of Charles II.

## Commercial Aspects of 18th-Century Publishing
The publishing industry utilized various pricing models and formats. Poetry was sometimes published by subscription at very high prices, a lucrative practice followed by Pope and Prior. However, Pope also sold single poems for 1s. and 1s. 6d. 

Novels were typically published in several volumes. At the start of the century, the price was generally 1s. 6d. per volume, remaining steady for many years before rising again toward the end of the century. Examples of this price increase include Miss Burney's works: *Evelina* (3 vols., 1778) sold for 7s. 5d., *Cecilia* (5 vols., 1882) for 12s. 6d., and *Camilla* (5 vols., 1796) for L1:1s. Additionally, Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755), consisting of two folio volumes, cost L4:4s. in sheets and L4:15s. in boards.
