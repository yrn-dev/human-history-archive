# Sand-lime Bricks

Sand-lime bricks are a type of building block produced through a chemical hardening process involving a mixture of sand and lime. Patented in the early 1880s by Dr. Michaelis of Berlin, the process utilizes high-pressure steam to harden the blocks, leading to the extensive production of these bricks in numerous countries.

## Manufacturing Process
The general method for producing sand-lime bricks involves mixing dry sand with powdered slaked lime. While proportions may vary based on the purity of the sand and the nature of the lime, a common average is one part slaked lime to ten parts sand. This mixture is slightly moistened with water and then formed into bricks using powerful presses capable of exerting pressure of approximately 60 tons per square inch.

Once removed from the press, the bricks are placed into large steel cylinders, typically 7 feet in diameter and 60 to 80 feet in length. They are then subjected to high-pressure steam, ranging from 120 to 150 lb per square inch, for a period of ten to fifteen hours.

## Chemical Composition and Properties
A typical German sand-lime brick consists of the following analysis:
* Silica ($\text{SiO}_2$): 84%
* Lime ($\text{CaO}$): 7%
* Alumina and oxide of iron: 2%
* Water, magnesia, and alkalis: 7%

The high-pressure steam causes the lime to attack the sand particles, creating a chemical compound of silica, lime, and water. This resulting bond of hydrated calcium silicate is of a better type and stronger than the calcium carbonate filling found in ordinary mortar-bricks. 

In terms of appearance, sand-lime bricks are usually light-grey. However, they can be stained using pigments or colouring oxides that are unaffected by the manufacturing conditions and the lime. Because the process is simple, the bricks maintain a constant quality if reasonable care is taken during production.

## Comparison with Mortar Bricks
Sand-lime bricks differ significantly from mortar bricks, which are unburnt blocks of artificial stone. Mortar bricks are made by moulding sand and slaked lime and allowing them to harden naturally in the air. This natural hardening occurs through the evaporation of water and the conversion of slaked lime (calcium hydrate) into calcium carbonate via atmospheric carbonic acid. 

The natural hardening process for mortar bricks is very slow, taking between six and eighteen months. While various improvements were introduced in the latter half of the 19th century—such as the use of hydraulic presses, the addition of cement or ground blast-furnace slags, and treatment with pressurized carbon dioxide—the newer sand-lime brick has practically superseded the old mortar brick.

## Related Materials and Mortars
The production of various masonry elements relies on different ratios of lime, sand, and cement:
* **Grey lime mortar:** Typically one part lime to two or three parts sand.
* **Lias lime mortar:** Similar proportions, though equal quantities of lime and sand are used for work below ground.
* **Portland cement mortar:** Usually one part cement to three or five parts sand.
* **Roman cement mortar:** One or one and a half parts cement to one part sand.
* **Selenitic lime mortar:** One part lime to four or five parts sand.

For specialized applications, other mixtures are used. Pargetting for inside chimney flues utilizes one part lime and three parts cow dung (free from litter or straw). Mortar for tall chimney shafts may consist of one part blue lias lime mixed with one part sand and one part foundry ashes.

## Materials for Construction
The quality of the sand used in these mixtures is critical. Pit sand is considered the best because it is sharp and angular, though it must be screened for gravel and washed to remove clay or earth. River sand is less desirable as attrition rubs the particles smooth. Sea sand is avoided because it is rounded and contains a saline taint that attracts moisture, keeping brickwork damp. Substitutes for ordinary sand include fine stone grit, clinkers, cinders, or thoroughly burnt clay ground to a uniform size.

In the context of burnt bricks, the source materials are divided into clays containing small percentages of carbonate of lime (which burn to red, salmon, or buff colours) and "marls," which contain considerable carbonate of lime (up to 40% chalk) and burn to a distinctive sulphur-yellow colour.
