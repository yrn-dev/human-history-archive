# Port Florence and the Uganda Railway

## Overview of the Uganda Railway
The construction of the railway from Mombasa to Victoria Nyanza represented the most significant engineering project undertaken in the protectorate. The project began with a preliminary survey executed in 1892, and physical construction commenced in 1896. The railway serves as a mountain line, characterized by gradients of one in fifty and one in sixty.

The line originates at Mombasa, crossing to the mainland via a bridge measuring half a mile in length. It ascends the plateau to the Kikuyu Escarpment, reaching an altitude of 7,600 feet above sea level at a distance of 346 miles from its starting point. From there, the line descends across ravines using viaducts to reach the valley floor at 6,011 feet, before ascending the Mau escarpment to its highest point of 8,321 feet above sea level.

## Route and Terminus at Port Florence
The railway's path roughly follows the route of a trunk road from Mombasa, passing through Kibwezi in the upper Sabaki basin and Lake Naivasha. Upon reaching the equator, the railway deviates from the road to take a more direct route across the Mau plateau. The line terminates at Port Florence, located on the Kavirondo Gulf of Victoria Nyanza. The station at Port Florence sits at an altitude of 3,738 feet.

## Technical Specifications and Construction
The railway spans a total length of 584 miles and utilizes a metre gauge (3.28 ft.), which distinguishes it from the South and Central African and Sudan lines, which use a 3 ft. 6 in. gauge. 

The project was built entirely at the cost of the British government, totaling £5,331,000, which averages to approximately £9,500 per mile. Sir George Whitehouse served as the engineer in charge of construction from the project's inception until his resignation in March 1903, by which time the permanent way was practically complete. The first locomotive arrived at Victoria Nyanza on December 26, 1901.

## Historical and Economic Impact
The establishment of the railway had profound effects on the region:
* **Suppression of Slave Trade:** By eliminating the need for the carriage of goods by men, the railway dealt a final death-blow to the slave trade in that portion of East Africa.
* **Development of Uganda:** The railway facilitated the continued occupation and development of Uganda. Prior to the railway, such development was nearly impossible due to the prohibitive cost of transporting goods from the coast, which cost £60 per ton.

## Political Context
The drive to open the country via the railway was linked to broader geopolitical efforts. In July 1890, representatives of various powers met in Brussels to agree on common efforts to suppress the slave trade. While a company initially sought to establish authority in Uganda—sending Captain F.D. Lugard to the capital, Mengo, in December 1890—the company lacked the resources to build the railway. Although the company sought imperial aid and a preliminary grant for the survey was nearly agreed upon, the initial scheme was wrecked in parliament before the British government eventually funded the construction in full.
