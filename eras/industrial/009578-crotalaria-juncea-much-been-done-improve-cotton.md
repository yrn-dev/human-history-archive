# Crotalaria juncea

*Crotalaria juncea*, known as sunn or tag, is a plant utilized for its fibers. It is categorized as one of the most important fiber crops in certain agricultural regions, alongside cotton and Deccan hemp (*Hibiscus cannabinus*).

## Agricultural Context
In the presidency where *Crotalaria juncea* is grown, it exists as part of a diverse agricultural system. This system includes various staple food grains such as rice, which is the chief product of the Konkan, and joar (*Sorghum vulgare*) and bajra (*Holcus spicatus*), which serve as staple food grains in Khandesh and the Deccan. Other grains grown in the region include barley, primarily in the northern parts of the presidency, and wheat, which is grown generally in the northern part—specifically in Gujarat and Sind—and exported in large quantities from Bombay and Karachi.

The region also produces inferior grains on hill-sides, such as kodra (*Paspalum serobiculatum*) and nachani (*Eleusine coracana*), which provide food for aboriginal tribes including the Waralis, Bhils, and Kolis.

## Related Crops and Industry
The cultivation of *Crotalaria juncea* occurs alongside other significant agricultural products:

*   **Pulses:** The most important pulses include mug (*Phaseolus Mungo*), kulti (*Dolichos biflorus*), tur (*Cajanus indicus*), and gram (*Cicer arietinum*).
*   **Oil-seeds:** Principal seeds include linseed, safflower, castor-oil, mustard, and til (*Sesamum orientale*).
*   **Miscellaneous:** Other crops grown in favored localities with perennial water-supply and rich soil include sugar-cane, as well as tobacco, turmeric, potatoes, and red pepper.

The fiber industry in this region is heavily influenced by cotton. Efforts have been made to improve cotton production, including the introduction of American varieties in other parts of the southern Mahratta country and the Dharwar collectorate. In Khandesh, the superior Hinganghat variety has almost entirely superseded the indigenous plant that gave its name to one of the lowest classes of cotton in the Bombay market.

## Commercial and Industrial Application
The production of fibers like *Crotalaria juncea* supports a broader commercial infrastructure. In Bombay, the milling industry is a primary feature of commercial success, second only to the docks. The staple manufacture within this industry is cotton-spinning. 

The scale of the cotton industry has fluctuated over time. Between 1881 and 1890, the number of factories grew from fifty-three to eighty-three, accompanied by an influx of industrial population from surrounding districts. However, the period between 1891 and 1901 saw a temporary setback due to over-production and the ravages of plague. The plague caused an exodus from the island and disorganized labor in the mills and docks, while requiring the municipality to spend large sums on sanitary improvements and plague operations. By 1901, the cotton industry employed 131,796 persons, and both trade and population began to revive after that year.
