# Main Styles in England at the Beginning of the 19th Century

At the beginning of the 19th century, English styles across various disciplines—ranging from bookbinding and musical instrumentation to chronological systems—were characterized by a mixture of imitation, mechanical innovation, and the lingering effects of previous eras.

## Bookbinding Styles
In the field of bookbinding, the primary styles utilized in England during the early 19th century were largely distant imitations of the work of Roger Payne. Several practitioners, including Hering, Walther, Staggemeier, and Kalthoeber, were disciples of Payne and followed his influence. 

However, Charles Lewis distinguished himself by working on original lines. His specific contributions to the style of the period included the development of arabesques and a focus on gold-tooled doublures that were richly detailed. Lewis also employed gold end papers. A characteristic feature of his bindings is the use of bands at the back that were always gold-tooled, broad, and flat, and were frequently double. His high quality of workmanship led him to work for great collectors, most notably Thomas Grenville.

## Musical Instrumentation
The beginning of the 19th century marked a transition in the style and construction of the kettledrum. Traditionally, tuning these instruments was a time-consuming process, even with the use of T-shaped screw-heads. To increase efficiency, various countries sought to create mechanisms that allowed a performer to tune the drum to a fixed note using a single movement.

These first mechanical kettledrums appeared at the start of the 19th century. While such systems were developed in Holland by J. C. N. Stumpff, in France by Labbaye (1827), in Germany by Einbigler (1836), and in Italy by C. A. Boracchi (1839), the English contribution to this mechanical style was developed by Cornelius Ward in 1837.

## Chronological Styles
The term "style" also applied to the calendar systems used during this period. The difference between the "old style" (the Julian calendar) and the "new style" (the Gregorian calendar) shifted over time due to differing rules regarding leap years. 

Because the year 1800 was a common year in the new calendar but a leap year in the Julian calendar, the difference between the two styles during the 19th century was twelve days. This difference later increased to thirteen days from 1900 to 2100.
