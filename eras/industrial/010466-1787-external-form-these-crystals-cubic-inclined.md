# Boracite

Boracite is a mineral noted for its optical anomalies. It was first observed in 1787 in the salt deposits at Luneburg in Hanover, where small crystals with sharply defined faces are found embedded in anhydrite and gypsum.

## Physical and External Characteristics
In its external form, boracite crystals are cubic with inclined hemihedrism, possessing the same symmetry as tetrahedrite and blende. The habit of the crystals varies depending on whether the cube, the tetrahedron, or the rhombic dodecahedron predominates. In some instances, penetration twins are observed with a tetrahedron face serving as the twin-plane.

The crystals possess a vitreous lustre and range in appearance from translucent to transparent. While they are typically white or colourless, they are often tinged with green, yellow, or grey. Boracite has a specific gravity of 3.0 and a hardness of 7 on the Mohs' scale.

## Pyroelectric Properties
As first observed by R.J. Hauy in 1791, boracite crystals are markedly pyroelectric. When a cube of the mineral is heated, it becomes negatively electrified on four corners and positively electrified on the four opposite corners. In crystals where the rhombic dodecahedron predominates, the larger and bright tetrahedral faces (s') are located at the antilogous poles, while the smaller and dull tetrahedral faces (s) are situated at the analogous poles; the latter become positively electrified upon heating.

## Optical Anomalies and Internal Structure
Although the external characters of boracite are in accordance with cubic symmetry, Sir David Brewster observed in 1821 that the mineral is doubly refracting when examined in polarized light. Thin sections reveal a division into definite areas that are optically biaxial and the presence of twin-lamellae.

By cutting sections in suitable directions, it can be demonstrated that a rhombic dodecahedral crystal is actually a compound (pseudo-cubic) crystal composed of twelve orthorhombic pyramids. The bases of these pyramids coincide with the dodecahedral faces, and their apices meet at the center. Other crystal forms exhibit different types of internal structure.

## Thermal Modifications
The optical characters of boracite change when the mineral is heated. At a temperature of 265 degrees, the crystals suddenly become optically isotropic. Upon cooling, the complex internal structure reappears.

Various explanations have been proposed for these anomalies. Some observers suggest they are caused by internal strains or alteration. However, evidence suggests there are two crystalline modifications of the boracite substance:
*   A cubic modification that is stable at temperatures above 265 degrees.
*   An orthorhombic (or monoclinic) modification that is stable at lower temperatures.

This is considered strictly analogous to silver iodide (iodyrite), which has rhombohedral and cubic modifications at different temperatures. However, while both forms of silver iodide are known in nature, only the pseudo-cubic crystals of boracite have been encountered.
