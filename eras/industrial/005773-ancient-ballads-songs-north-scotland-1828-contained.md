# Ancient Ballads and Songs of the North of Scotland

*Ancient Ballads and Songs of the North of Scotland* is a collection of Scottish ballads published in 1828. The work was compiled and edited by Peter Buchan (1790–1854), a Scottish editor born in Peterhead, Aberdeenshire. Buchan began his professional life as a printer in Peterhead in 1816; after achieving sufficient success in business to retire, he devoted his time to the editing and collection of Scottish ballads.

## Content and Contribution
The 1828 publication is noted for containing a large number of ballads that had not been previously published, as well as versions of existing ballads that were newly discovered. Buchan's efforts in this field extended beyond this single volume; he also produced a collection titled *Scottish Traditional Versions of Ancient Ballads*, which was published by the Percy Society in 1845. Additionally, the British Museum holds two volumes of Buchan's ballad collections that remain unpublished.

## Nature of Scottish Ballads
Scottish ballads are often characterized by a universal sameness of tone, legend, incident, and primitive poetical formulae. These characteristics are shared with the ballad traditions of other nations, including Italy, France, Greece, Portugal, Denmark, and Provence. This commonality suggests that some ballads are part of a stock of primitive folk-lore with an immemorial antiquity, similar to the "story-roots" of *Maerchen* (nursery tales).

Common plots found in Scottish and international ballads include:
* **The Faithful Leman:** A story where a lord brings home a "braw new bride," but the original lover recovers the partner's affection at the eleventh hour. In Scotland, this is represented by the ballad of Lord Thomas and Fair Annie.
* **The Feigned Death:** A plot involving a girl who pretends to be dead to be carried on a bier to meet her lover.
* **The Grave Flora:** The motif of two rose trees or a bush and briar that meet and plait over the graves of unhappy lovers.

## Stylistic Features
Scottish ballads frequently employ "primitive luxury" in their descriptions, specifically a lavish use of gold and silver. An example is found in the ballad *Willie's Lady*, where the steed is described as being "Silver shod before, And gowden shod behind." Other common features include the use of talking birds, such as the omniscient popinjay found in Border minstrelsy or the bird in *Water o' Wearies Well*.

Stylistically, these ballads share a directness and simplicity with early epic poetry, such as the works of Homer. This includes the use of recurring epithets—such as "salt sea," "green grass," and "shadowy hills"—and the repetition of speeches.

## Origins and Evolution
There has been historical controversy regarding the origin of Scottish folk-songs. One theory suggested they were the production of professional minstrels, such as the court-bards kept by Scottish kings or the wandering *jongleurs* (strollers) who sang in towns and at farm-houses. However, the similarity between Scottish ballads and those of other European cultures suggests a more primitive, popular origin.

Evidence suggests that many ballads sprang from the institution of communal dances. In Scotland, "ring-sangs" served as dancing songs; for example, *Young Tamlane* possessed an appropriate air for dancing. Historical accounts, such as the *Complaynt of Scotland*, describe shepherds and their wives singing melody songs before dancing in a ring. Similarly, the chronicler Fabyan noted that the song of triumph over Edward II was sung in dances by the minstrels and maidens of Scotland.

While the present forms of these ballads are relatively recent due to centuries of oral recitation and automatic language alteration, the stock ideas and situations are considered to be of dateless age. By the 15th century, communal processes of composition had largely ceased, and traditional formulae became conventional aids for individual singers.
