# Great Britain and North America: Commercial, Political, and Natural Relations

## Accounting Practices
In modern business, the growth of transactions made the use of a single book of first-entry impractical. This led to two different recording methods. Continental European countries generally treat the journal as an intermediate book where transactions are focused before being recorded in a ledger. In contrast, Great Britain and North America more generally employ a system of splitting the journal into numerous books of first-entry, each dedicated to a specific class of transaction.

Under this method, several specialized journals are used:
*   **Sales journal (sales book or day book):** For recording particulars of goods sold.
*   **Bought journal (invoice book or purchases book):** For recording particulars of goods purchased.
*   **Returns inwards book:** For goods sold that were subsequently returned by customers.
*   **Returns outwards book:** For goods purchased that were subsequently returned.
*   **Bills receivable book:** For recording bills of exchange received from debtors.
*   **Bills payable book:** For recording bills of exchange given to creditors.

## Political and Social Dynamics
During the eighteenth century, the governing class in Great Britain acquired land and destroyed the liberty of common people, while the industrial revolution was brought about blindly and greedily. The British Parliament, including both the upper and lower houses, became an instrument of government for big landowners due to the decay of representative methods in the House of Commons.

Both the crown and big property-holders held deep interests in America. The landowners acted as private adventurers, while the crown represented the state's search for funds for foreign policy and the speculative exploitations of the Stuart kings. Neither group showed more consideration for the traders, planters, and common people in the colonies than they did for small cultivators and yeomen at home. While the oppressor and oppressed were closely tangled in an intimate social system in Britain, the distance between the crown/exploiters and the people in America allowed the latter to develop a sense of community against a common enemy.

## Legal Institutions and Civil Order in America
In the 18th and 19th centuries, the American population expanded westward faster than civil institutions could be developed. This led to the use of lynch law to suppress desperadoes on the western frontier. While less common before settlements crossed the Alleghany Mountains, extra-legal violence occurred in the East in several forms:
*   The mistreatment of Indians in the Middle Colonies and New England, ignoring laws that protected them.
*   Summary justice administered to idlers, wife-beaters, and other obnoxious persons.
*   The acts of the Regulators of North Carolina between 1767 and 1771.
*   Popular tribunals during the Revolutionary period, where hostilities between "Tories" and "Patriots" and disaffection toward Great Britain weakened civil government authority.
*   In the South, lynching was used against anti-slavery agitators and those charged with exciting slaves to crime or insurrection against masters.

## Governance and Franchise
The creators of political America sought a community of will through a wide franchise. While conditions varied by state—Pennsylvania, for example, allowed every adult male taxpayer to vote—the United States were closer to manhood suffrage by the end of the eighteenth century than Great Britain was. Efforts were made to secure a widely diffused common education, though the information of citizens regarding domestic and foreign affairs was left to privately owned printing presses and public meetings.

## Colonial History and Global Commerce
Nieuw Amsterdam became British by treaty in 1674 and was renamed New York. Between 1755 and 1763, Britain and France struggled for India and America during the Seven Years' War. In 1759, General Wolfe took Quebec, and by the 1763 Peace of Paris, Canada was ceded to Britain. The United States of America issued a Declaration of Independence in 1776, and a Treaty of Peace between Britain and the new United States was established in 1783.

By the latter quarter of the nineteenth century, Great Britain, Portugal, and Holland gained commercial advantages through their control of sub-tropical and tropical products. Canada was the first British possession to respond richly to new economic possibilities created by new methods of transport.

## Natural History
### Flora and Fauna
The common maple (*A. campestre*) is the only species indigenous to Great Britain. The genus *Acer* includes about sixty species native to Asia, North America, and Europe.

Regarding the common fox, the animal is regarded only for sport in Great Britain. In contrast, skins from the European continent are sold annually, chiefly to the United States. However, these do not compare in quality to species found in the Arctic circle and North America. The best and darkest cross foxes come from Hudson Bay and Labrador, while ordinary sorts come from the north-west of the United States.

### Marine Life
Two species of mackerel occur in British seas. *Scomber scomber* is the most common in the North Atlantic and crosses the ocean to abound in America. The Spanish mackerel (*S. colias*) is native to southern Europe, but small schools or individuals frequently reach the shores of the United States and Great Britain.
