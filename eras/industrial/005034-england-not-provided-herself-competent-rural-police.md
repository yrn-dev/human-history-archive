# Rural Policing and Brigandage in England

## Factors Contributing to Brigandage
The development of brigandage is primarily fostered by bad administration and, to a lesser extent, the availability of convenient hiding places. Natural landscapes consisting of forests and mountains are particularly favorable to brigands. In Scotland, the highlands provided a safe refuge for "gentlemen reavers" who stole cattle from Sassenach landlords. Similarly, the "banditos" of Italy utilized the Apennines and the mountains of Calabria, while the "bandoleros" (banished men) and "salteadores" (raiders) of Spain utilized the Sierras. Other examples include the "maquis" (bush) and hills of Corsica for Corsican brigands and the Australian bush for bushrangers.

In England, the forests provided cover for outlaws, a reality reflected in the ballads of Robin Hood. While forests and thickets offer temporary cover, they do not provide lasting protection against a government that employs an intelligent police force supported by the law-abiding community.

## Government Strength and Lawlessness
The presence of brigands does not always indicate a fundamentally feeble government. For instance, England was ruled vigorously during the reign of William III, yet a "fraternity of plunderers" numbering at least thirty people established huts in Epping Forest near Waltham Cross. From these bases, they used swords and pistols to stop passengers. Similarly, the "Gubbings"—so named in contempt due to the refuse and trimmings of fish—infested Devonshire for a generation, operating from headquarters near Brent Tor on the edge of Dartmoor. In these instances, the persistence of such groups was not due to a weak state, but rather because England had not provided herself with a competent rural police.

In contrast, when brigands multiply through success and impunity to the point where they publicly defy the justice of their country rather than eluding it, it indicates that the lowest ranks of the community are abusing an excessive weakness in the government. This observation was made by Gibbon regarding the disorders in Sicily during the reign of emperor Gallienus. In Europe, the most significant haunts of brigands were found in southern and central Italy and the worst-administered regions of Spain (excluding those held by the Turks).

## Legal and Administrative Contexts in England
Beyond the issue of rural policing, English law and administration have addressed various social and civil matters:

**Ecclesiastical and Criminal Law:** Under the ecclesiastical law of England, bigamous marriages are considered simply void. A statute from 1604 classified this offense as a felony. Although repealed in 1828, it was re-enacted in the Offences against the Person Act 1861. The law applies regardless of whether the second marriage occurred in Ireland, England, or elsewhere, provided the defendant is a British subject. Exceptions exist for those whose spouse has been continuously absent and unknown for seven years, or for those who have been divorced or had a marriage declared void by a competent court. Punishments include imprisonment (up to two years, with or without hard labour) or penal servitude between five and seven years.

**Billeting of Troops:** The practice of providing board and lodgings (billeting) for soldiers was historically managed by civil magistrates. Due to abuses where magistrates spared friends at the expense of opponents, billeting was declared illegal by the Petition of Right 1628 and an act of 1679. However, the practice continued under James II, leading to the Bill of Rights citing the illegal quartering of soldiers as a grievance. Following the revolution, the Mutiny Act 1689 authorized billeting among victuallers and innkeepers due to a lack of barrack space (which only housed 5,000 men). This was renewed annually until the Army Discipline Act 1879 and the Army Act 1881. By 1909, the Army (Annual) Act provided for the billeting of Territorial forces during national emergencies at the discretion of the chief officer of police.

**Public Health and Burial:** To address overcrowded churchyards, the Burial Acts (1852–1900) established a system to close dangerous burial grounds and open new ones. The Local Government Board (formerly the home office) superintended these grounds to maintain public decency and health. The Local Government Act 1894 transferred the duties of burial boards in rural parishes to parish meetings and parish councils.
