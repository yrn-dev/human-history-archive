# Bookbinding in 18th Century England

## General Trends and Production
During the 18th century, England experienced a notable improvement in presswork, type, and paper. Books became generally elegant and neat. In terms of binding, the technique of average work improved, although the best binders of the century remained inferior to those of the 17th century until the arrival of Roger Payne. 

Trade bindings saw a shift in materials; the use of calf and sheepskin became much less common, with books mostly being cased in paper boards. The cost of books varied by format and period. For example, Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755), a two-volume folio, cost L4:4s. in sheets and L4:15s. in boards. Novels were typically published in several volumes; Miss Burney's *Evelina* (3 vols., 1778) sold for 7s. 5d., while her *Camilla* (5 vols., 1796) sold for L1:1s.

## The Influence of Roger Payne
Towards the end of the 18th century, English binding was at a decorative low level. This changed when Roger Payne, a native of Windsor, established himself as a bookbinder in London. A splendid workman, Payne introduced several innovations to the art:
*   **Ornamentation:** He introduced ornamental "doublures" (inside linings) and richly gold-tooled corner-pieces.
*   **Morocco Leather:** He invented the "straight grain" method of graining morocco, which involved graining the leather in only one direction.
*   **Tooling:** Payne is said to have cut his own iron binding tools, which are described as exquisitely made. In many of his bindings, he included written descriptions of the loving work he performed upon them.

Despite being a drunkard, Payne is credited with rendering an immortal service to the art of bookbinding in England. His influence extended into the beginning of the 19th century, where the main styles used in England were distant imitations of his work. His disciples included Hering, Walther, Staggemeier, and Kalthoeber.

## Specialized Techniques and Styles
Other binders of the era developed distinct methods of ornamentation. John Edwards of Halifax patented a method in 1785 to make vellum transparent, which he used as a covering for delicate paintings. Following a 17th-century method used by Samuel Mearne, Edwards also painted pictures on the fore-edges of books that remained hidden until the book was opened.

John Whitaker utilized calf for his bindings, ornamenting the material using prints from engraved metal plates and strong acids. Both Whitaker and Edwards favored classical ornaments and borders, leading their work to be known as "Etruscan."

Other notable 18th-century English binding styles include:
*   **Harleian Style:** Used by Eliot and Chapman for the library of Robert Harley, earl of Oxford, around the middle of the century. These bindings featured morocco with diamond-shaped centre-pieces and broad, richly gold-tooled borders.
*   **Hollis Bindings:** Thomas Hollis had books bound in fine red morocco. These were ornamented with small, well-cut stamps engraved by the medallist Thomas Pingo, featuring figures such as Britannia, a figure of liberty, and a cap of liberty.

## Comparative and Later Perspectives
The attraction of fine bindings was a strong factor in book collecting. In France, high prices for 18th and 17th-century jackets often exceeded the value of the books themselves. In England, the appreciation for old bindings increased significantly following an 1891 exhibition at the Burlington Fine Arts Club. This led to a higher demand for embroidered bindings, English blind stamped bindings, and those attributable to Samuel Mearne.

By the beginning of the 19th century, some binders moved beyond the imitation of Roger Payne. Charles Lewis, for instance, worked on original lines, developing arabesques and focusing on gold end papers and richly gold-tooled doublures. His bindings are characterized by broad, flat, gold-tooled double bands at the back.
