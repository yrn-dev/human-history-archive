# The Valve and Pitch Modification in Brass Instruments

The valve is a mechanical device used in brass wind instruments to provide a chromatic compass by instantaneously altering the length of the tubing. This system allows a performer to connect additional lengths of tubing to the main tube of the instrument, thereby deepening the pitch and transposing the instrument into different keys at will.

## Principles of Operation
The fundamental principle of the valve is to provide additional tubing to lower the pitch. When a piston or valve is depressed, it either admits the wind into additional lengths of tubing to lower the pitch or cuts off a length to raise it. This is an instantaneous application of the general principle used by crooks—interchangeable spiral tubes that lower the pitch and change the key of an instrument—but valves are superior because they allow for the rapid transition between notes.

In instruments like the French horn, the first valve is attached to tubing that lowers the pitch by a tone (for example, moving a horn in F to E-flat). The second valve opens a passage into a shorter length of tubing that lowers the pitch by a semitone (moving the instrument to E). A third valve further lowers the pitch by a tone and a half. Similarly, the cornet uses three pistons to lower the pitch by one tone, half a tone, and one tone and a half, respectively.

## The Bombardon and Regional Variations
The bombardon, also known as the bass tuba, was the first bass wind instrument to be fitted with valves. These instruments typically possess a chromatic compass of 3-1/2 to 4 octaves, with a harmonic series consisting of the 1st through the 8th harmonics. 

The number of pistons and the specific intervals they lower vary by model and region. Bombardons generally utilize three or four pistons to lower the pitch. In most regions, these pistons lower the pitch by 1, 1/2, 1-1/2, and 2-1/2 tones. However, in Belgium, the standard intervals are 1, 1/2, 2, and 3 tones. 

In Germany and Austria, a "cylinder action" is frequently employed. In this system, a four-way brass cock operated by a series of cranks and a key is used instead of a piston or pump.

## Intonation and Technical Challenges
A significant inherent defect in the valve system is the issue of intonation when pistons are used in combination. To lower the pitch by one tone, a length of tubing proportional to the main tube is added. If a third valve (which lowers the pitch by 1-1/2 tones) is depressed while the first valve is already active, the additional tubing provided by the third valve is too short relative to the now-lengthened main tube. Consequently, notes played with this combination are too sharp.

To obtain a complete chromatic scale, seven different lengths of tubing are required. On the bombardon, these are achieved through combinations:
* Pistons 2 and 3 combined lower the pitch two tones.
* Pistons 1, 2, and 3 combined lower the pitch three tones.
* Pistons 1, 2, 3, and 4 combined lower the pitch five and a half tones.

Because these combinations often fail to provide acoustically correct intervals, performers must sometimes correct the intonation by varying lip tension.

## Historical Improvements and Systems
Various makers have developed "ingenious contrivances" to remedy intonation errors. These include:
* **U-shaped slides:** Used to compensate for slight errors in intonation.
* **Adolphe Sax's six-valve independent system:** Designed around 1850, this used a separate valve for each position to avoid combinations. However, it was abandoned because the valves were "ascending" (cutting off tubing) rather than "descending" (adding tubing).
* **Besson systems:** Including the *Registre* (providing eight independent positions) and the compensating system *Transpositeur*.
* **Other innovations:** The Boosey automatic compensating piston (invented by D.J. Blaikley), V. Mahillon's automatic regulating pistons, and the Besson enharmonic valve system, which utilizes three pistons and six independent tuning slides.
* **Rudall, Carte & Company:** Developed a new bore (Klussmann's patent) that is conical throughout the open tube and additional lengths.
