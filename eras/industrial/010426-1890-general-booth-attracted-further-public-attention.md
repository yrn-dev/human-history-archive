# William Booth and the Salvation Army

## Early Life and Ministry
William Booth (1829– ), the founder and "general" of the Salvation Army, was born in Nottingham on April 10, 1829. At fifteen years of age, Booth experienced a strong religious turn under the influence of the Wesleyan Methodists, eventually becoming a local preacher within that body. In 1849, he moved to London, where his passion for open-air preaching led to his severance from the Wesleyans. He subsequently joined the Methodist New Connexion and was ordained as a minister; however, he left this organization in 1861 because he was not employed in the active "travelling evangelization" he desired.

During this period, Booth married Miss Catherine Mumford in 1855, with whom he had four children. Both William and Catherine engaged in preaching, working first in Cornwall and later in Walsall and Cardiff. In Walsall, Booth organized a "Hallelujah band" consisting of others and converted criminals who provided public testimony of their conversion.

## Foundation of the Salvation Army
In 1864, Booth returned to London to continue his services in the open air and in tents. He founded an organization that underwent several name changes, known successively as the East London Revival Society, the East London Christian Mission, and the Christian Mission, before becoming the Salvation Army in 1878. 

The Salvation Army is a quasi-military organization, and Booth based its "Orders and Regulations" on those of the British army. The organization operates through several methods:
* Conducting processions and outdoor meetings.
* Visiting private houses, prisons, and public-houses.
* Holding meetings in factories, theatres, and other unusual buildings.
* Utilizing the language of everyday life and popular song-tunes.
* Encouraging every convert to act as a daily witness for Christ in both private and public settings.

The early campaigns of the Army faced violent opposition. A "Skeleton Army" was formed specifically to break up meetings, and for many years, the followers of Booth were subjected to imprisonment and fines as breakers of the peace. These disorders had largely subsided by 1889.

## Global Expansion and Public Recognition
Under the leadership of "General" Booth—described as an indefatigable speaker, organizer, and traveller—the operations of the Army expanded internationally. This growth included the United States in 1880, Australia in 1881, and further spread to India, Ceylon, the European continent, and other regions. Authorities in the United States and elsewhere cordially encouraged his work.

While Booth's work was met with ridicule and opposition for many years, widespread sympathy grew toward the end of the 19th century as the results of his genius were realized. This shift in public perception was highlighted by the active encouragement of King Edward VII. At the King's instance, Booth was officially invited to attend the coronation ceremony in 1902. By 1905, when the "general" toured England, he was received in state by the corporations and mayors of numerous towns.

## "In Darkest England, and the Way Out"
In 1890, General Booth attracted further public attention through the publication of his work, *In Darkest England, and the Way Out*. In this text, he proposed ten expedients to remedy vice and pauperism:
1. The city colony
2. The farm colony
3. The over-sea colony
4. The household salvage brigade
5. The rescue homes for fallen women
6. Deliverance for the drunkard
7. The prison-gate brigade
8. The poor man's bank
9. The poor man's lawyer
10. Whitechapel-by-the-Sea

A large portion of this scheme was carried out thanks to liberal subscriptions of money.
