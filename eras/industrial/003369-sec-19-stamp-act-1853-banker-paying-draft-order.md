# Banker Liability and the Stamp Act 1853

## Relief from Liability for Forged Endorsements
Under section 19 of the Stamp Act 1853, a banker who pays a draft or order that is payable to order on demand and drawn upon him is relieved from liability if the endorsement of the instrument was unauthorized or forged. This specific enactment was not repealed by the Bills of Exchange Act 1882. Furthermore, the legal precedent established in *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* (1903) confirmed that this protection extends to cases where drafts are drawn by a bank branch on its own head office.

## Extensions and Limitations of Protection
The Bills of Exchange Act 1882 provides similar protections to bankers regarding cheques under section 60. In this act, cheques are defined as "bills drawn on a banker payable on demand." Because of this specific definition, drafts of the description mentioned in the Stamp Act 1853 are excluded from the protections of section 60.

Additionally, the requirement for the instrument to be unconditional means that certain documents issued by others or corporations are also excluded from the protection of section 60. These documents typically direct bankers to make payments only upon the presentation of a duly signed, specific attached receipt (*London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon*). 

However, section 17 of the Revenue Act 1883 applies the crossed cheques sections of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 to these specific types of documents (as seen in *Bavius, Jr., & Sims v. London & South-Western Bank* [1900]). While the Revenue Act denies these documents the status of negotiable instruments, a banker who pays one of them crossed—provided it is done in accordance with the crossing and there is no indication of transfer—could likely claim immunity under section 80.

## Regulations on Crossed Cheques
The Bills of Exchange Act 1882 does not explicitly prohibit a banker from paying a crossed cheque in a manner that contradicts the crossing. However, if a banker does so, they are liable to the true owner for any resulting loss (section 79) and are likely unable to charge the customer for that amount. Conversely, a banker who pays a crossed cheque according to its ostensible tenor is granted protection under section 80 and the proviso to section 79.

## Related Financial Instruments
The legal framework for these protections exists alongside other instruments such as promissory notes. Under section 83 of the act, a promissory note is an unconditional written promise signed by a maker to pay a certain sum of money to a specified person or bearer, either on demand or at a determinable future time. While most rules for bills of exchange apply to promissory notes with adaptation, a note is a direct promise to pay rather than an order to pay. 

Under the Stamp Act 1891, all promissory notes are subject to an *ad valorem* stamp duty. Inland notes must use impressed stamp paper, while foreign notes use adhesive stamps. Bank notes are generally regarded as promissory notes made by a banker payable to bearer on demand, though they are subject to special stamp regulations and statutory restrictions regarding their issue.
