# Paris in 1871 and the War Indemnity

## The Fall of Paris and the Franco-Prussian War
The year 1871 marked a pivotal conclusion to the struggle for European predominance between France and Prussia, a conflict that began in 1870. France, described as being rotten with financial corruption, suffered a swift and dramatic defeat. Following the capitulation of a great French army at Sedan in September 1870 and another surrender at Metz in October, the city of Paris underwent a period of siege and bombardment. In January 1871, Paris fell into German hands. The resulting peace was signed at Frankfort, which saw the surrender of the provinces of Lorraine and Alsace to the Germans and the unification of Germany (excluding Austria) as an empire under the King of Prussia.

## The Paris Commune and Civil Unrest
Following the fall of the city, Paris experienced significant internal turmoil during the reign of the Commune in 1871. This period was characterized by extreme instability and violence. Georges Darboy, the Archbishop of Paris, remained at his post during the siege and refused to flee during the brief triumph of the Commune. On April 4, 1871, he was arrested by the communists as a hostage and imprisoned first at Mazas and later at La Roquette as the army of Versailles advanced. On May 27, 1871, Darboy was shot within the prison alongside other distinguished hostages. He was buried at public expense on June 7. Darboy was the third archbishop of Paris to perish by violence between 1848 and 1871.

## The War Indemnity and Financial Recovery
The aftermath of the war left France with significant financial obligations, specifically a war indemnity. The payment of this indemnity was a prolonged process that was not completed until 1873. 

The Bank of France played a central role in navigating the financial and political crises of this era. The institution successfully surmounted the difficulties associated with the 1870 war with Germany, the subsequent reign of the Commune in Paris in 1871, and the burden of the war indemnity payments. In the years following these crises, the Bank of France expanded its reach to support agricultural industry and businesses of various sizes. By 1877, the bank had 78 offices, a number that grew to 447 by 1906. This expansion included "connected" towns, which allowed locations to receive the advantages of a branch without the bank incurring the full expense of opening one.
