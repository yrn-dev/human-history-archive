# Dwelling-Houses in London

The construction and planning of dwelling-houses in London are subject to a complex system of regulations and historical transitions in material usage. These structures must be erected in accordance with numerous building acts that dictate specific technical requirements and safety standards.

## Building Regulations and Requirements
In London, the erection of dwelling-houses is governed by various building acts. These laws specify the materials to be used and the methods of their employment. Key regulated areas include:
*   **Structural Specifications:** The acts govern the thickness of walls and the rates of inclination for roofs.
*   **Safety and Sanitation:** Regulations dictate the means of escape from fire, drainage systems, and the required space at the rear of the property.
*   **Rear Space Requirements:** The depth of the space at the rear of domestic buildings in London is 10 feet. The height of the building in relation to this rear space must be constructed to remain within an angle of 63½ degrees, inclining from the rear boundary toward the building from the level of the pavement in front. This position may vary under special circumstances.

## Prohibited Materials and Construction Methods
A primary mandate of London's building laws is the prohibition of timber-framed buildings for use as dwellings. While New York allows frame or wood structures (except within a specifically defined portion of the city), such constructions are forbidden in London and other large cities in Great Britain.

Certain modern patents exist that allow for the creation of fire-proof dwellings with walls only 3 to 5 inches thick—utilizing steel dovetailed sheets fixed between small steel stanchions and plastered in cement. While this method was used for the Newgate prison cells in London, such convenient forms of construction are generally prohibited for ordinary London buildings because they fail to comply with specific clauses regarding materials or wall thickness.

## Specialized Residential Structures
Different rules apply to specific types of residential or combined-use buildings:
*   **Combined Shop and Dwelling-Houses:** If such a building in London exceeds 10 squares or 1,000 square feet in area, a large portion of the building and the stairs must be constructed from fire-resisting materials.
*   **Flats:** Under certain conditions, the corridors and stairs in London flats must be made of fire-resisting materials.

## Historical Evolution of Materials
Prior to the Great Fire of London in 1666, smaller buildings, including dwelling-houses and shops, were typically constructed using timber frameworks filled with plaster and lath. Following the fire, bricks were used extensively for rebuilding. From the end of the 17th century onward, bricks became the almost exclusive material for ordinary buildings across the country, except in regions where good brick-clay was unavailable and building stone was plentiful.

The standardization of bricks occurred in 1625, when statute regulated the size to the current standard of 9 x 4½ x 3 inches. Between 1784 and 1850, a tax was levied on bricks, averaging approximately 4s. 7d. per thousand for ordinary bricks, with special bricks taxed more heavily.

## Financial and Legal Contexts
The acquisition of dwelling-houses has been facilitated by various societies. In London, early organizations used titles such as "Mutual Associations," "Societies of Equality," or "Investment Association." By 1849, the term "Benefit Building Society" became the sole distinctive name for these entities in England. According to the act of 1836, these societies enable members to receive the value of shares to purchase or erect a dwelling-house.

Legally, a "place" is defined by Sir E. Coke as a mansion-house, meaning a man's private residence or dwelling-house. In the context of burglary, other buildings within the same curtilage are not considered part of the dwelling-house unless there is an immediate communication or a covered and enclosed passage. Furthermore, rooms in a private house are considered the dwelling-house of the owner if the owner lives elsewhere or if the lodger enters through a different outer door; otherwise, the lodger is viewed as an inmate and the apartment as part of a single dwelling-house.
