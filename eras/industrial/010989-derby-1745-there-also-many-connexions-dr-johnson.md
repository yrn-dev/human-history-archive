# Derby in 1745

## Political Events and the Jacobite Rising
In 1745, the town of Derby entertained the young Pretender during his advance. This event occurred within a broader history of political volatility for the town; Derby had previously been royalist in sympathy during the 17th-century Civil War, refusing to surrender until 1646, and had rebelled against Richard Cromwell in 1659. 

The military movements of 1745 are further linked to the nearby market-town of Ashbourne, located 13 miles west-north-west of Derby. Ashbourne Hall, an ancient mansion, was occupied by "Prince Charlie" both before and after his advance on Derby during this period.

## Connections with Dr. Johnson
The region maintains several associations with Dr. Johnson. He was a frequent visitor to the town of Ashbourne, where he visited his friend Dr. Taylor. Dr. Taylor's residence was located opposite the grammar school. Additionally, Dr. Johnson was warmly attached to the English physician Richard Brocklesby (1722–1797). Around 1784, Brocklesby offered Dr. Johnson a life annuity of £100 and attended to him on his death-bed.

## Industrial and Economic Context
By the mid-18th century, Derby was becoming a center for various manufactures. The 18th century generally saw a rise in numerous industries in the area. Specifically, the Derby porcelain or china manufactory was started around 1750, producing porcelain that rivalled that of France and Saxony. 

The town's industrial foundation was heavily influenced by the silk industry. In 1718, John Lombe and Sir Thomas Lombe established an improved silk-throwing machine in Derby. John Lombe had discovered the secrets of this craft while visiting Piedmont in 1717. Silk-throwing eventually became the principal industry of the town. Other significant developments followed shortly after the 1745 period, such as Jedediah Strutt's introduction of a machine for making ribbed stockings in 1758, which became known as the "Derby rib."

## Historical Administration and Development
The town of Derby, known as Northworthig during the heptarchy, became one of the five boroughs of the Danes following the treaty of Wedmore. It was later won back in 917 by Æthelflæd, lady of the Mercians. By the time of Edward the Confessor, the town had 243 burgesses, though this number fell to 100 by 1086.

The town's legal and administrative framework evolved through various charters:
* **1206:** A charter granted privileges similar to those of Nottingham, including a gild merchant, freedom from toll, and the power to elect a provost. It also mandated that cloth could not be dyed within ten leagues of Derby except within the borough.
* **1225:** A charter confirmed privileges and established that the *comitatus* of Derby be held in the borough on Thursdays.
* **1229:** A charter limited the election of the provost, stating he must be removed if he displeased the king.
* **1260:** A charter granted by Henry III stipulated that no Jew should be allowed to live in the town.
* **1337:** Edward III granted the burgesses the right to have two bailiffs instead of one.

The town was incorporated by James I in 1611 as the bailiffs and burgesses of Derby. In 1637, Charles I appointed a government consisting of a mayor, nine aldermen, fourteen brethren, and fourteen capital burgesses. While the burgesses were forced to resign their charters in 1680, the new charter they received did not alter the government of the town.
