# Essential Chemical Constituents of Brewing Waters

In the context of brewing, the water supply is referred to as "liquor." The quality and chemical composition of this liquor are of great importance to the brewer, as the presence or absence of specific mineral substances significantly influences the brewing process. While these minerals do not apparently affect the appearance or flavor of the finished beer, they play a critical role in the solvent action of the water on the constituents of the hops and the malt.

## Requirements for Different Beer Types

Different styles of beer require different water characteristics to be produced successfully. Water contaminated with organic matter is entirely unsuitable for brewing, as it leads to abnormal flavor, cloudiness, and unsatisfactory fermentation.

For the production of black beers, a soft water is desired. In contrast, hard water is a necessity for ales of the Burton type. Mild ales require water that contains a specific proportion of chlorides. For stout, waters should contain very little mineral matter, with the possible exception of the carbonates of the alkaline earths, which are precipitated during the boiling process.

## Chemical Analysis of Typical Brewing Waters

Analyses provided by W.J. Sykes in *The Principles and Practice of Brewing* illustrate the chemical constituents of various brewing waters, measured in grains per gallon:

*   **Burton Water (Pale Ale):** This water contains a considerable quantity of calcium sulphate (gypsum) and other magnesium and calcium salts. Its composition includes Calcium Sulphate (77.87), Magnesium Carbonate (21.31), Calcium Carbonate (7.62), Sodium Nitrate (1.97), Potassium Sulphate (1.59), Sodium Chloride (3.90), and Silica and Alumina (0.98).
*   **Dublin Water (Stout):** This water contains Calcium Carbonate (14.21), Calcium Sulphate (4.45), Sodium Chloride (1.83), Magnesium Carbonate (0.90), Iron Oxide and Alumina (0.24), and Silica (0.26).
*   **Mild Ale Water:** This water is characterized by a higher salt content, including Sodium Chloride (35.14), Calcium Sulphate (6.23), Calcium Carbonate (4.01), Calcium Chloride (3.88), and Iron Oxide and Alumina (0.24) and Silica (0.22).

## Artificial Treatment of Water

Knowledge of these essential chemical constituents allows brewers to artificially treat unsatisfactory water supplies to modify their character. 

If only soft water is available and the brewer intends to produce a bitter ale, it is necessary to add sufficient quantities of calcium chloride, magnesium sulphate, and gypsum. To convert soft water that lacks chlorides into a liquor suitable for mild ale, the addition of 30-40 grains of sodium chloride is required. 

Additionally, brewers may use kainite, which is a naturally deposited composite salt containing magnesium chloride as well as magnesium and potassium sulphates. However, while soft water can be hardened, converting hard water into a soft supply is considered scarcely feasible for brewing purposes.
