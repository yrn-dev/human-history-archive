# John Bright

John Bright (1811–1889) was a prominent British statesman and orator known for his advocacy of free trade, religious freedom, and electoral reform. Born on November 16, 1811, in Rochdale, he was the eldest son of Jacob Bright and Martha Wood. His father was a respected Quaker who established a cotton mill in Rochdale in 1809. Bright's mother, educated at Ackworth school, was noted for her refined taste and strength of character.

## Early Life and Education
Bright's education was conducted across several institutions. As a delicate child, he began as a day-scholar at a boarding school run by Mr. William Littlewood. He subsequently spent one year at Ackworth school, two years at a school in York, and a year and a half at Newton, near Clitheroe. While he stated that he learned very little Greek and Latin, he developed a strong love for outdoor pursuits and English literature, the latter of which was encouraged by his mother. At the age of sixteen, Bright entered his father's mill and eventually became a partner in the business.

## Early Political Activity
Bright's entry into public life began in Rochdale, where he participated in two primary agitations: one for parliamentary reform—which successfully claimed a member for Rochdale under the Reform Bill—and another opposing a local church-rate, a movement led by his father. An ardent Nonconformist, Bright took pride in his ancestry, specifically John Gratton, a friend of George Fox and a persecuted preacher of the Society of Friends.

Bright described his twenty-first year as providing his "first lesson in public speaking," though he did not immediately seek a public career. He was a prosperous businessman and a co-founder of the Rochdale Literary and Philosophical Society, where he engaged in debates and delivered a lecture on his travels to the East. 

In 1836 or 1837, Bright met Richard Cobden, an alderman of the Manchester corporation. After Bright delivered a short speech at an education meeting in Rochdale, Cobden urged him to speak against the Corn Laws. Bright gave his first speech on this topic in 1838 and joined the Manchester provisional committee that founded the Anti-Corn Law League in 1839. In November 1839, Bright and Cobden appeared together on a Free Trade platform for the first time at a dinner in Bolton for Abraham Paulton. In 1840, Bright continued his opposition to the Rochdale church-rate, notably speaking from a tombstone in the churchyard.

## Parliamentary Career
In July 1847, Bright was elected to parliament for Manchester without a contest, alongside Mr. Milner Gibson. During his tenure, he opposed legislation that restricted hours of labour and, reflecting his Nonconformist beliefs, spoke against the clerical control of national education. He introduced a bill to repeal the Game Laws in 1848 and voted for Hume's household suffrage motion. He was also a critic of Lord John Russell's Ecclesiastical Titles Bill, which he described as a "little, paltry, miserable measure."

Bright became an effective debater in the House, speaking against the Irish Established Church, church-rates, capital punishment, and flogging in the army. He supported Cobden's motion to reduce public expenditure and advocated for peace. In the 1852 election, he was returned for Manchester again on a platform of religious freedom, electoral reform, and free trade.

One of the most significant periods of his career was his opposition to the Crimean War. He delivered his most memorable and greatest speech on February 23, 1855, which moved the House deeply with an appeal to the prime minister regarding the "angel of death" abroad in the land.

## Later Years and Recognition
In his later years, Bright received significant social and literary recognition. He was elected lord rector of the university of Glasgow in 1882, though he admitted to Dr. Robert William Dale that he had grown weary of public speaking and felt his mind was "almost a blank." He received an honorary degree from the university of Oxford in 1886, and a statue was erected in his honor at Birmingham in 1888.

The 3rd marquess of Salisbury characterized Bright as the greatest master of English oratory of several generations, praising his robust and vigorous style that gave expression to "burning and noble thoughts."

## Personal Life
Bright married Margaret Elizabeth Leatham of Wakefield in June 1847. The couple had seven children, the eldest of whom was John Albert Bright. In 1839, Bright had built a house which he named "One Ash."
