# Carriage and Transport History

## Carriage Types and Origins
Various forms of horse-drawn carriages have been utilized across different regions and eras. The Britzska (or Britska), derived from the Polish word *bryczka* (a diminutive of *bryka*, meaning a goods-wagon), is a carriage form copied in England from Austria during the early 19th century. In Russia and Poland, this vehicle featured four wheels and a long wicker-work body designed for reclining, topped with a calash (hooded) top. Another variety is the Barouche (from the Latin *bi-rotus*, meaning double-wheeled), which consists of a four-wheeled carriage with a hood, designed to allow two couples to sit inside facing each other.

The "cab" is a term shortened around 1825 from the French *cabriolet* (derived from *cabriole*, implying a bounding motion). Introduced to London from Paris in 1820 as the *cabriolet de place*, cabs are horsed vehicles for passengers that may have either four wheels (known as a "growler" or "four-wheeler") or two wheels (known as a "hansom"). In a different context, the term "cab" also refers to the shelter for a stoker or driver on a locomotive engine.

## Hackney Carriages and Regulation
Public carriages for hire, known as hackney coaches, were first established in London in 1625. Despite opposition from the king and court, who feared the vehicles would damage the roads, their numbers grew from a restriction of fifty in 1635 to 300 by 1650, and eventually to 700 by 1694. Many of these early hackney carriages were former private coaches belonging to the gentry and nobility; specialized smaller coaches for hackney use were not built until 1790. In Paris, these vehicles were introduced by Nicholas Sauvage during the minority of Louis XIV; because Sauvage lived at the sign of St Fiacre on the rue St Martin, Parisian hackney carriages became known as *fiacres*.

In the United Kingdom, hackney carriages are regulated by various statutes. In London, these include the Hackney Carriage Acts of 1831-1853, the Metropolitan Public Carriages Act 1869, the London Cab Act 1896, and the London Cab and Stage Carriage Act 1907. The 1869 act defined a hackney carriage as a passenger conveyance plying for hire within the metropolitan police district that is not a stage coach (where passengers pay separate fares for seats). 

Licensing for London cabs is managed by the home secretary and issued by the commissioner of police, with licenses costing £2 and requiring annual renewal. Before licensing, a cab must be inspected and certified as fit for public use by the inspector of public carriages. Requirements include painting the licensed passenger capacity on the outside of the vehicle and carrying a lighted lamp from one hour after sunset until one hour before sunrise. Drivers must also hold a license from the home secretary, costing five shillings per annum, which requires passing an examination on their driving ability and knowledge of London's topography. In other large British towns, regulation is typically handled via private acts incorporating the Town Police Clauses Act 1847.

## Stage Coaches and Travel Times
Stage coaches provided long-distance travel between major cities. By 1673, coaches ran from London to Exeter, Chester, and York, with each route employing forty horses and carrying six inside-passengers; the journey to Exeter took eight days. By 1706, the trip from London to York was reduced to four days, with departures every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. In the same year, a coach ran from London to Birmingham, departing Monday and arriving Wednesday. Later, in 1754, a "flying coach" from Manchester advertised a travel time to London of four and a half days. By 1784, coaches had become universal, traveling at a speed of 8 miles per hour.

## Regional Industry and Infrastructure
Carriage-related industry is noted in various English locales. The market town of Horsham in Sussex lists carriage-building as one of its industries, alongside flour-milling, founding, and tanning. In Buckinghamshire, the town of Wolverton houses the carriage works of the London & North-Western railway.
