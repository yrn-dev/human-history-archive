# The German Banking Law Amendment of 1899

The amendment of the banking law of Germany, passed in 1899, was a legislative action that significantly altered the operational and financial structure of the Reichsbank (the Imperial Bank of Germany) and the four other private note-issuing banks within the empire. This amending act, alongside the original law of 1875, governs the note circulation of the Reichsbank.

## Impact on the Reichsbank
The 1899 bill resulted in several financial adjustments for the Reichsbank. The bank's capital was raised to £9,000,000. Additionally, the reserve fund was increased using surplus profits to reach £3,240,000, an amount that exceeded the £3,000,000 requirement established by the act of 1899. The amending act also modified the distribution of profits by increasing the share obtained by the government and diminishing the dividends receivable by the stockholders of the Reichsbank.

## Regulation of Note Circulation
The note circulation of the Reichsbank is governed by the laws of 1875 and 1899. These regulations establish an uncovered limit for notes not represented by specie. Originally set at £12,500,000, this limit was increased to £14,811,450 due to the lapse of issues from other banks, and was further extended by the 1899 and 1902 acts to £23,641,450.

To cover notes issued without specie, the bank must hold discounted bills or notes from issuing banks (which are reckoned as specie) and treasury notes (*Reichskassenscheine*). These bills must generally have three good indorsements—never fewer than two—and must mature no later than three months after being taken. Furthermore, the law requires that at least one-third of the notes in circulation be covered by gold in bullion, foreign coin (reckoned at £69, 12s. per pound fine), money, current German notes, or notes of the imperial treasury.

The Reichsbank is legally bound to redeem its notes in current German money, which may include silver thalers, gold coin, or bar-gold at a rate of 1392 marks (or £69, 12s.) per pound fine of gold.

## Coordination with Private Note-Issuing Banks
The 1899 amendment established a framework to ensure that the four private note-issuing banks work in harmony with the Reichsbank, particularly when the Reichsbank must raise its bank-rate to protect its gold reserves. 

Under *Artikel 7* of the amending act, effective after January 1, 1901, the private banks are subject to the following discount rate restrictions:
*   If the official published rate of discount of the Reichsbank reaches or exceeds 4%, that rate becomes binding on the private note-issuing banks.
*   At other times, private banks may not discount at more than 1/4% below the official Reichsbank rate.
*   If the Reichsbank itself discounts at a rate lower than its official rate (a fact it must announce in the *Gazette*), private banks may not discount at more than 1/8% below that lower rate.

## Statutory Power of Over-Issue
A distinct feature of the German banking system is the automatic statutory power to exceed the allowed limits of note circulation if business conditions require it. The Reichsbank is permitted to overpass these limits upon the payment of 5% on the surplus. This arrangement is intended to provide necessary banking accommodation and prevent panic. While the rate was 7% for a short time in 1899, it has generally remained at 5%. This power has been utilized annually since 1895; between 1881 and the end of 1906, the duty on extra issues amounted to £839,052, providing funds for the relief of taxation.
