# Ireland in 1835

The year 1835 in Ireland was characterized by developments in the financial sector, the ongoing production of literature reflecting Irish life, and the maintenance of traditional cottage industries.

## Financial Institutions and Banking
By 1835, the Irish banking landscape was evolving under a restrictive legislative framework. For a significant period, the establishment of banks other than the Bank of Ireland (founded in 1783) had been hindered by legislation. A specific act had previously mandated that no company or society exceeding six in number, with the exception of the Bank of Ireland, could borrow or take up money on their bills or notes payable on demand. 

Although this act was modified in 1821 to allow banking companies with more than six members to be established if they were located at least 50 miles from Dublin, ambiguity regarding the law required a further explanatory act in 1824. The 50-mile restriction remained in place until 1845. Within this environment, the National Bank was established in 1835, entering a market that included other institutions such as the Hibernian Bank (1824), the Provincial Bank (1825), the Northern Banking Co. (1825), and the Belfast Banking Co. (1827).

## Literature and the "Scott of Ireland"
The 1830s saw the influence of the Banim brothers, who sought to create a series of tales illustrative of Irish life that would serve as a counterpart to the Waverley Novels of Scotland. John Banim (1798–1842), often referred to as the "Scott of Ireland," began his literary career in Dublin in 1820. He achieved significant success starting in April 1825 with the first series of *Tales of the O'Hara Family*.

Following this success, a second series was published in 1826, which included the novel *The Nowlans*. Due to John Banim's failing health, much of the subsequent work for the "O'Hara family" series was produced by his elder brother, Michael Banim (1796–1874). Works published in quick succession following the earlier tales included *The Croppy, a Tale of 1798* (1828), *The Denounced*, *The Ghost Hunter* (written by Michael), *The Smuggler*, and *The Mayor of Windgap*.

## Industry and Trade
In the 19th century, various regions of Ireland maintained specialized trades. In County Antrim, the town of Ballymena owed its prosperity primarily to the linen trade, which had been introduced in 1733 and employed the majority of its inhabitants, with brown linen being a particular specialty. Similarly, the market town of Ballymeney engaged in linen manufacture, alongside tanning, distilling, and brewing.

Additionally, traditional crafts were fostered as cottage industries in parts of Ireland. This included the hand-weaving of pile carpets and tapestry, a revival of hand-made crafts that persisted even as steam-powered carpet production became predominant in Europe and the United States.
