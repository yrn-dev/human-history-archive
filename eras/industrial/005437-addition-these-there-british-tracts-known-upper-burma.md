# British Burma and the Upper Burma Frontier

The province of British Burma, as a division of the Indian empire, is characterized by a complex administrative structure and a diverse geography. In addition to the established government territories, there are British tracts specifically known as the Upper Burma frontier and the Burma frontier.

## Government and Administration
The province is administered by a lieutenant-governor, a position first appointed on May 1, 1897. This official is supported by a legislative council of nine members, five of whom are officials. The administrative hierarchy includes a chief secretary, a revenue secretary, a secretary, two under-secretaries, and a public works department secretary with two assistants. 

Revenue administration is overseen by a financial commissioner, assisted by two secretaries and a director of land records and agriculture. Judicial matters are handled by a chief court established in May 1900, consisting of a chief justice and three justices. Other judicial officers include the civil judges of Moulmein and Mandalay, as well as the judicial commissioner for Upper Burma. The province is further divided into districts; Upper Burma has four commissioners and seventeen deputy commissioners, while Lower Burma has four commissioners of revenue and circuit and nineteen deputy commissioners. Specialized administration is provided for the Shan States via two superintendents (one for the northern and one for the southern states), and a superintendent oversees the Chin hills and Arakan hill tracts.

## Law and Legal Systems
The British government applies principles of law in Burma identical to those used in other British dominions in India, generally utilizing Anglo-Indian law. The Burma Laws Act of 1898 declared that acts of the governor-general in council and English law introduced into India without legislation are applicable to Upper Burma, as they had been to Lower Burma.

However, the British government maintains native laws regarding marriage, caste, inheritance, succession, and religious usage. Under the 1898 act, the law administered depends on the parties involved:
* **Buddhist law** is applied to Buddhists, with the law contained in sacred books called *Dhammathats*.
* **Mahommedan law** is applied to Mahommedans.
* **Hindu law** is applied to Hindus.

## Natural Divisions and Climate
The province is divided into three natural divisions: the Irrawaddy basin, Arakan (including the Chin hills), and the old province of Tenasserim (including parts of the Karen-ni and Shan states in the Salween basin and part of Kengtung in the Mekong basin). 

The Irrawaddy basin is further subdivided. The highland tract includes the Kachin hills, the Northern Shan states, and districts such as Bhamo, Myitkyina, Ruby Mines, Katha, and the upper waters of the Irrawaddy and Chindwin. This region is largely rugged hills and narrow gorges, though it contains fertile plateaus in the Shan States and the Pyin-u-lwin plateau, where Maymyo serves as a hill-station for the government during hot weather. The "dry zone" extends from the north of Mandalay to Thayetmyo, encompassing districts such as Sagaing, Shwebo, and Minbu.

Climate varies significantly by region. The delta is cooler and more temperate than Upper Burma. Rainfall is highest in the Tenasserim and Arakan divisions (about 200 inches) and averages 90 inches in Rangoon. In the dry zone, annual rainfall drops to between 20 and 30 inches.

## Agriculture and Forestry
Rice is the staple crop of both Lower and Upper Burma. In Lower Burma, it is the overwhelmingly largest crop, while in Upper Burma, it is grown wherever practicable. The agricultural season typically follows the south-west monsoon from mid-May until November. In dry districts of Upper Burma and parts of Lower Burma, irrigation allows for a hot season crop during spring. Other crops grown, particularly in Upper Burma, include cotton, tobacco, wheat, maize, sugar-cane, tea, and various oil-seeds.

Irrigation systems in the Minbu and Kyauksè districts, which were in disrepair at the time of British annexation, have been renewed. New projects include the Mandalay Canal (opened 1902), the Shwebo canal (opened 1906), and the Môn canal (begun 1904), bringing over 300,000 acres under irrigation.

The forests of Burma are considered the finest in British India, primarily valued for teak. The demand for teak, which preserves iron and steel in shipbuilding, was a primary driver for the annexation of Burma. Tenasserim was annexed in 1826 for its timber, and disputes with a British forest company led to the Third Burmese War of 1885. The government now strictly preserves these forests through a forest department.

## Defense and Security
Burma is garrisoned by a division of the Indian army under a lieutenant-general. This includes seven Burma regiments consisting of Pathans, Sikhs, and Gurkhas. Security is further maintained by 15,000 military police—recruited from northern India—and 13,500 civil police. Volunteer forces include the Upper Burma rifles, as well as the Railway, Rangoon, and Moulmein rifles.

## History of Annexation
The annexation of Upper Burma followed a period of strained relations under King Thibaw. After the withdrawal of the British resident in 1879, the Burmese court created monopolies and sought alliances with France and Italy. The crisis peaked in 1885 when the Burmese government imposed a £230,000 fine on the Bombay-Burma Trading Corporation and refused impartial arbitration. Following an ultimatum and the king's proclamation to drive the British into the sea, British forces crossed the frontier on November 14, 1885. They occupied Mandalay on November 28, and King Thibaw was transferred to Ratnagiri on the Bombay coast.
