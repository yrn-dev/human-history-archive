# The Suffolk System of Redemption

The Suffolk system was a method of banking redemption that came into operation in 1824. It emerged from a broader movement in New England toward the prompt redemption of bank notes at par, a trend pioneered by the New England Bank in 1814. This approach contrasted with the banking laws of other states, some of which permitted the issuance of notes based on state bonds—many of which became valueless during the outbreak of the Civil War.

## Operational Mechanism
Under the Suffolk system, a bank would deposit a redemption fund within the Suffolk Bank located in Boston. When notes required redemption, they were redeemed from this fund and subsequently sent home by the Suffolk Bank for collection. To expand the reach of this mechanism, a branch redemption agency was established in Providence.

The primary merit attributed to the Suffolk system was its ability to keep the volume of circulation constantly adjusted to meet the requirements of business. The system remained in successful operation until 1858, though it underwent slight modifications during that period.

## Statistical Impact and Legal Status
By 1858, the circulation of New England banks was less than $40,000,000. However, the volume of redemptions processed through the Suffolk Bank during that same year reached $400,000,000.

The system also received legal recognition in Vermont through an act passed in 1842. This legislation imposed a 1% tax on bank capital; however, the tax was remitted to any bank that uniformly caused its bills to be redeemed at par in the city of Boston and maintained a sufficient deposit of funds there.
