# Classes of Boilers

Boilers are generally categorized into two distinct classes: "tank" boilers and "water-tube" boilers. While some types combine properties of both, each class is represented in both marine and land-based applications.

## Tank Boilers
Tank boilers are characterized by containing relatively large quantities of water. Their outer shells are either partially or wholly cylindrical, a design that provides necessary strength without requiring a large number of stays. These boilers are generally internally fired, with the furnace plates surrounded by water, which constitutes the most efficient part of the heating surfaces. 

After leaving the furnace, the products of combustion are directed into a chamber and then passed through flues or numerous small tubes to transmit heat to the water. In tank boilers, maintaining close contact between the heating surfaces and the water is generally not a difficulty.

Specific variations of tank boilers include:
*   **Cornish Boiler:** A type containing only one furnace, used when boilers less than approximately 6-1/2 ft. in diameter are required.
*   **Galloway Boiler:** This type features two furnaces that extend roughly 9 or 10 ft. into the boiler, leading into a large flue or chamber containing "Galloway" tubes that extend to the end of the boiler.
*   **Cylindrical Multitubular Return Tube Boiler:** Almost universally used in merchant steamers, these can reach 17 ft. in diameter. They typically operate at pressures between 160 and 200 lb per sq. in., though some reach 265 lb per sq. in. They are available in double-ended (furnaces at each end) or single-ended (furnaces at one end) types. Single-ended boilers usually have three furnaces, though they can range from one to four.

## Water-Tube Boilers
In water-tube boilers, the water is primarily contained within numerous comparatively small tubes, which form the major portion of the heating surface. The fire is usually positioned beneath these tubes, and both the fire and tubes are enclosed in a casing made of brickwork or other fire-resisting material. In certain designs, the fire is entirely surrounded by water-tubes, ensuring the casing is not exposed to direct fire. Unlike tank boilers, water-tube boilers require special design provisions to maintain the circulation of water through the tubes.

Notable types of water-tube boilers include:
*   **Belleville Boiler:** Used in the British navy, it consists of an "economizer" and the boiler proper. Both contain sets of elements made of seamless steel tubes connected by malleable cast iron junction-boxes. The boiler elements sit directly over the fire, while the economizer elements are in the uptake above the boiler.
*   **Express Boilers:** Used in cruisers and torpedo-boat destroyers to maximize power relative to weight. These utilize smaller diameter tubes and a high rate of fuel combustion achieved through "forced draught." Examples include the Normand and the Yarrow (the latter having smaller, more closely arranged tubes than the large tube Yarrow boiler).

## Locomotive Boilers
The locomotive boiler consists of a cylindrical barrel attached to a nearly rectangular fire-box. The fire-box sides are secured to the shell by stays approximately 1 in. in diameter, pitched 4 in. apart. Depending on the boiler size, the top of the fire-box is supported by either longitudinal girder stays or vertical stays connecting the fire-box crown to the shell crown. 

These boilers typically operate at pressures of 175 to 200 lb per sq. in. They utilize a copper tube plate and ordinary tubes usually 1-3/4 in. in diameter. Some locomotive boilers also incorporate water-tubes made of seamless steel that extend across the fire-box.

## Materials and Construction
The materials used in boiler construction vary by type:
*   **Land and Marine Boilers:** Plates and stays are almost always mild steel. Shell plates and long stays typically have a tensile strength of 28 to 32 tons per sq. in., while furnaces, flues, and short-screwed stays use a softer steel (26 to 30 tons per sq. in.). Tubes are usually lap-welded wrought iron, though naval water-tube boilers invariably use seamless steel.
*   **Locomotive Boilers:** Shells are generally mild steel. Fire-box plates are copper (though steel is used in America), and side stays are copper or special bronze. Tubes are typically brass (composed of copper and zinc), though steel or copper are occasionally used. Water-tubes are made of seamless steel.

Modern manufacturing has shifted toward specialization. The use of machine tools and templeting allows for repetitive operations, ensuring that every plate, hole, and flange in a set of similar boilers is identical.

## Accessories and Maintenance
All boilers require specific mountings to ensure the water level remains above the highest heating surfaces. Water levels are monitored via a glass water-gauge (consisting of a glass tube and three cocks) and three try-cocks. While some land and shipboard water-tube boilers use automatic float-actuated feeding, most rely on hand regulation. Water is supplied via a pump (driven by the main engine or an independent steam-pump) or an injector, and is admitted through a check-valve.
