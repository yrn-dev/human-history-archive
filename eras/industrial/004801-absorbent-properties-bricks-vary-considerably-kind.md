# Bricks and Brickmaking

Bricks are a type of artificial stone, generally manufactured from burnt clay, and are extensively utilized as a building material. The production and application of bricks have evolved through various historical periods and geographical regions, influenced by the availability of raw materials and the development of architectural arts.

## Material Composition and Properties
The primary material for brickmaking is clay. Ordinary brick clays are widely distributed, though they possess a more extensive geological range than marls, which are only found in connection with limestone or chalk formations. These clays vary in composition; many are unsuitable for use in their natural state and require the addition of sand or other types of clay.

The strength of brick clays—specifically their tensile strength and plasticity—is usually highest in those containing the highest percentage of hydrated aluminium silicates. All such clays contain some amount of undecomposed felspar and free silica in the form of sand. A critical ingredient is oxide of iron, which typically ranges from 2% to 10%. This oxide determines the hardness, durability, and color of the burnt brick:
* **Light buff to chocolate:** The general range of colors based on the percentage of oxide of iron.
* **Red:** Produced by clays containing 5% to 8% oxide of iron under ordinary firing conditions.
* **Darker/Purple:** Produced if the clay contains 3% to 4% alkalis or is fired too hard, bringing the clay closer to its fusion point.
* **Lighter and brighter:** Resulting from an excess of alumina.

The absorbent properties of bricks vary by type. A good quality ordinary London stock brick should not absorb more than one-fifth of its bulk after soaking for twenty-four hours, whereas inferior bricks may absorb as much as one-third.

## Historical Development
The Romans were prolific users of both sun-dried and burnt bricks. They are credited with introducing brickmaking to England, where they used large, thin bricks primarily as a bond for rubble masonry. Following the decline of the Roman Empire, the art of brickmaking fell into disuse for several centuries before being revived.

In the Low Countries, the scarcity of stone led to the development of a distinct brick architecture. In England, the art reached a high level of perfection during the reigns of the early Tudor kings, who produced some of the finest known ornamental brickwork. Later, the Great Fire of 1666 provided a significant impetus for the industry during the rebuilding of London. This period saw the passage of a statute regulating the thickness of walls in different rates of dwelling-houses by specifying the number of bricks required.

## Construction and Measurement
In the surrounding counties and city of London, brickwork is measured by a "rod," defined as a 16½ ft. square and 1½ bricks thick. For a rod gauged with joints ¼ in. thick and bricks measuring 8¾ in. long, 4¼ in. wide, and 2¾ in. thick (with four courses to a foot), 4,356 bricks are required. If bricks are formed with hollows or frogs, an additional allowance of approximately 11 cub. ft. of mortar is needed.

Standard bricks weigh about 7 lb each, and are traded by the thousand (approximately 62 cwt). A rod of brickwork weighs between 13½ and 15 tons, with cement mortar resulting in a heavier weight than lime mortar. In terms of labor, a workman performing straight walling can lay an average of 500 bricks in an eight-hour day.

## Structural Strength and Application
The strength of brickwork depends on the quality of workmanship, the position of use, the quality of the mortar (cement or lime), and the type of brick. Experiments conducted by the Royal Institute of British Architects in 1896-1897 measured crushing loads per sq. ft. as follows:
* **Staffordshire blue:** 114.34 tons (lime) / 135.43 tons (cement)
* **Leicester red:** 45.36 tons (lime) / 83.36 tons (cement)
* **Fletton:** 30.68 tons (lime) / 56.25 tons (cement)
* **Gault:** 31.14 tons (lime) / 51.34 tons (cement)
* **Stock:** 18.63 tons (lime) / 39.29 tons (cement)

Regarding structural limits, a brick pier's height should not exceed twelve times its least width. The London Building Act stipulates that for non-public or non-warehouse buildings, external or party walls must not exceed a height sixteen times their thickness; for warehouse buildings, this limit is fourteen times the thickness.

Beyond general architecture, specially moulded bricks have superseded sandstone blocks in the construction of modern round-section blast furnaces. Additionally, because larger parrots can destroy woodwork, brick is cited as one of the only suitable materials for constructing an aviary.
