# Gauged Brickwork

Gauged brickwork is a specialized method of masonry characterized by the use of bricks that are cut and rubbed to specific gauges or templates. This process, known as gauging, ensures that every brick is brought exactly to a certain form, allowing for the creation of perfectly fitting voussoirs. While gauging is applicable to general walling, it is frequently employed in the construction of gauged arches.

## Materials and Composition
Unlike ordinary brickwork, gauged brickwork is set in lime putty rather than common mortar. Lime putty consists of pure slaked lime that is "run" in a bin or wooden tub. To ensure proper quality, at least three weeks should pass between the preparation of the lime putty and its use in construction.

The finished joints in gauged brickwork are designed to be extremely fine, with a width not exceeding 1/32 in. To provide necessary stability, the sides of the voussoirs are gauged out hollow and then grouted with Portland cement. This technique creates a joggle joint, which connects each brick to the next.

## Gauged Arches
Gauged arches are composed of specially made soft bricks. Because these arches are typically only a half-brick thick on the soffit and are not tied by a bond to the structure behind them, they must be executed with great nicety and care. The structure behind a gauged arch generally consists of a lintel with a rough discharging arch over it, which supports the remaining width of the wall.

A common error among workmen is rubbing the bricks thinner at the back than at the front to reduce the effort required to achieve a fine face joint. This practice can cause the work to bulge outwards. The highest quality work maintains an exact consistency of thickness from the front to the back of the brick. Similar bulging issues may occur when a gauged arch is inserted into an existing old wall due to the difficulty of filling the space behind the bricks with cement.

## Comparison with Other Arch Types
Brickwork arches are generally classified into three categories: plain, rough-cut, and gauged. 

*   **Plain Arches:** These are built from uncut bricks. Because ordinary bricks are not wedge-formed, the necessary wedge shape is created using mortar, resulting in wedge-shaped joints. To maximize strength, these are often built in independent rings of half-brick thickness, with joints that thicken in each succeeding ring to prevent the lowest ring from settling and creating fissures.
*   **Rough-cut Arches:** In these arches, bricks are roughly cut with an axe into a wedge form. They are typically used over openings like windows and doors where a strong arch with a neat appearance is required. Unlike gauged work, the joints in rough-cut arches are usually the same width as those found in ordinary brickwork.

## Related Materials: Grout and Mortar
Grout is a thin liquid mortar used in gauged arches and other specialized work where fine joints are desired. While grout is legitimate for this purpose, its use in ordinary brickwork—where it is sometimes used every four or five courses to fill inadvertent spaces—is considered a sign of inefficient workmanship. In standard work, such spaces should be filled with mortar during the laying process, as grout is prone to oozing out and staining the face of the brickwork.

The strength of any brickwork, including gauged work, is influenced by the quality of the workmanship, the position of the bricks, and the quality of the cement or lime mortar used. For example, experiments conducted by the Royal Institute of British Architects between 1896 and 1897 showed that the crushing pressure of brickwork varies significantly by material; for instance, Staffordshire blue brickwork in cement mortar can withstand 135.43 tons per sq. ft., whereas stock brickwork in lime mortar crushed under 18.63 tons per sq. ft.
