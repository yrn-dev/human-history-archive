# Simon Bolivar

Simon Bolivar (1783–1830) was a central figure in the independence movements of South America. Born in Caracas, Venezuela, on July 24, 1783, to noble parents Juan Vicente Bolivar y Ponte and Maria Concepcion Palacios y Sojo, Bolivar was educated in Europe and resided in Madrid for several years. During his travels, he visited Paris and witnessed the final stages of the French Revolution. After returning to Venezuela in 1801 and marrying the daughter of Don N. Toro, the premature death of his wife from yellow fever led him back to Europe. He returned home in 1809 via the United States, where he observed the operation of free institutions.

## Early Revolutionary Activities
Bolivar became involved in the cause of independence shortly after his arrival in Venezuela, helping to promote the April 1810 insurrection in Caracas. The revolutionary junta commissioned him as a colonel and sent him on a mission to the court of Great Britain alongside Louis Lopez Mendez. Following Venezuela's declaration of independence on July 5, 1811, Bolivar was given command of Puerto Cabello, though he was eventually forced to evacuate the post.

After fleeing to Curacao, Bolivar traveled to Cartagena in September 1812. He led a successful expedition against Spanish troops on the Magdalena river, eventually entering Venezuela with a force of 500 men. He secured support in Truxillo and Merida and issued a decree of "war to the death." After a crushing victory over General Monteverde at Lastoguanes, Bolivar entered Caracas in triumph on August 4, 1813. While the patriots held the country by January 1814, royalist forces under Boves defeated Bolivar near Cura in the plains of La Puerta. By the end of 1814, the Spaniards had retaken Caracas and regained control of Venezuela.

## The Republic of Colombia and the Liberation of Venezuela
Bolivar sought refuge in Cartagena and Tunja, where the revolutionary congress of New Granada appointed him to lead an expedition against Santa Fe de Bogota. Following a return to Angostura, where he was hailed as the father and deliverer of his country, Bolivar oversaw the enactment of the fundamental law of December 17, 1819. This law united the republics of New Granada and Venezuela into a single state known as the Republic of Colombia, with Bolivar serving as president. The seat of government was provisionally moved to Rosario de Cucuta.

Bolivar led a well-appointed republican army against the Spaniards under Morillo, concluding a six-month armistice at Truxillo on November 25, 1820. When the armistice expired and General Torre assumed command, Bolivar sought a decisive engagement. At the battle of Carabobo, Bolivar routed the Spanish forces, effectively ending the war in Venezuela. He entered Caracas on June 29, 1821, and by the end of that year, the Spaniards were driven from the province, except for Puerto Cabello (where they eventually surrendered to Paez two years later). On August 30, 1821, the constitution of Colombia was adopted, confirming Bolivar as president and Santander as vice-president.

## Campaigns in Ecuador, Peru, and Bolivia
Bolivar expanded his efforts to liberate Ecuador and Peru. In June 1822, after a victory at Pichincha led by Sucre, republicans entered Quito. Bolivar then marched on Lima; the royalists evacuated the city, and Bolivar was invested as dictator with absolute power. Despite temporary setbacks and a withdrawal to Truxillo due to republican intrigues in Peru, Bolivar reorganized his army. He defeated Canterac on the plains of Junin on August 6, 1824, while Sucre achieved a decisive victory at Ayacucho, dispersing the Spanish forces.

In June 1825, Bolivar visited Upper Peru. This region detached from the government of Buenos Aires to form a separate state called Bolivia in Bolivar's honor. In August 1825, the first congress of Bolivia declared Bolivar its perpetual protector and tasked him with preparing a constitution.

## Later Political Struggles and Legacy
Internal instability in Colombia eventually required Bolivar's return. While Bolivar was away, Paez—the commander in Venezuela—rebelled against the government and the senate, encouraged by those seeking separation from the republic. Bolivar left Lima in September 1826, arriving in Bogota on November 14 to exercise extraordinary presidential powers to quell the rebellion. He reached Puerto Cabello on December 31 and issued a general amnesty before entering Caracas to establish his headquarters.

Although re-elected president, Bolivar formally resigned in February 1827 to retire to his private estate and refute claims of ambition. However, urged by Santander and a resolution of congress, he resumed his functions. He issued decrees for a national convention at Ocana and the establishment of constitutional order throughout Colombia.

Bolivar spent the majority of his personal fortune in service to his country and died without public money in his possession. He is credited with achieving the independence of three states, fostering national interests, and encouraging the arts and sciences. His remains were moved to Caracas in 1842. He is commemorated by statues in Bogota (1846), Lima (1858), and New York's Central Park (1884).
