# Brewing and Malting

Brewing, in its modern definition, consists of a series of operations designed to produce an alcoholic beverage known as beer. This process primarily utilizes water, hops, and cereals, with malted barley serving as the chief cereal component. While the art of preparing ale or beer is ancient, literature regarding the methods and apparatus used in early times is scarce; however, it is considered likely that these methods remained primitive until the 18th century.

## Historical Materials and Substitutes
Historically, before the general introduction of hops as an astringent and preservative, various other aromatic and bitter plants were utilized. According to J.L. Baker in *The Brewing Industry*, these included the use of yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*) by Icelanders, the fruit and twigs of the chaste tree (*Vitex agrius castus*) by the Cauchi, the fruit of the sweet gale (*Myrica gale*) by Scandinavians, and *Tamarix germanica* by the Cimbri.

In the United Kingdom, sugar was the only permitted substitute for malt prior to the repeal of the Malt Acts. The use of sugar increased significantly over twenty-five years, rising from 295,865 cwt. in 1870 to 2,746,615 cwt. in 1905, while the amount of malt used did not increase materially. Other substitutes that gained favor include preparations of maize and rice, as well as unmalted corn. In 1905, 1,348,558 cwt. of rice, maize, and similar substances were used, alongside 125,671 bushels of unmalted corn.

## Brewing Chemistry
The technology of brewing is largely based on physiological chemistry, a branch of knowledge that also exclusively governs the cognate industry of malting. Physiological chemistry is required for the harvesting, maturing, and conversion of barley into malt, as well as for the conditioning of beer, the fermentation of wort, and the mashing of malt. Pure chemistry is applied to the analysis of brewing products and materials, the composition of beer and wort extracts, and the study of saline matter in waters.

Scientific focus has been placed on the transformation products of starch. It was previously believed that diastase converted starch into a gummy substance called dextrin, which then became glucose. However, F.A. Musculus demonstrated in 1860 that dextrin and sugar are produced simultaneously. Between 1872 and 1876, Cornelius O'Sullivan proved that the resulting sugar was maltose. O'Sullivan also identified how the temperature of the mash influences the character of the products, work which was later extended by J. Heron and Horace T. Brown.

The degradation of starch can be monitored using iodine solution; starch-paste treated with iodine produces a deep blue color. As degradation proceeds via diastase or acids, the reaction shifts from blue to purple, then to reddish, and finally ceases. This process is also marked by an increase in cupric reducing power (towards Fehling's solution) and a decrease in optical rotating power.

## Regulation and Taxation
In the United Kingdom, strict regulations govern mashing operations. Brewers must use a brewing book provided by the Inland Revenue to record the day and hour of mashing or sugar dissolution at least twenty-four hours in advance. Two hours before mashing, the brewer must record the quantity of sugar, corn, and malt to be used, as well as the time worts will be drawn off the grains. Worts must be collected within twelve hours of the start of collection and remain undisturbed in the vessel for twelve hours unless an officer has taken account of them.

Taxation standards have evolved; for example, rice and flaked maize were classified as malt or corn regarding wort-producing powers until 1896, after which they were deemed sugar. In 1896, these were classified at 28 lb, but the standard shifted to 32 lb to the bushel in 1897. Under the act of 1880, 28 lb of sugar or 42 lb of corn were considered equivalent to one bushel of malt.

International taxation varies by country:
*   **United States:** Beer tax of 5s. 9d. per English barrel.
*   **France:** Tax on wort of 4s. 1d.
*   **Austro-Hungarian Empire:** Tax on wort of 6s. 8d.
*   **Russia:** Malt tax ranging from 5s. to 6s. 8d.
*   **Belgium:** Malt tax of 2s. 9d.
*   **Germany:** Varies by region, with Bavaria charging a malt tax between 3s. 5d. and 4s. 8d. and North German Customs charging 1s. 6d.
*   **Holland:** Tax on the quality or cubic contents of the mash-tun, ranging from approximately 1s. 9d. to 3s. 3d.
