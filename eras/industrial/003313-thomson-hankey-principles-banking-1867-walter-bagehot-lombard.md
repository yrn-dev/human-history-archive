# Thomson Hankey and Walter Bagehot: Contributions to Banking Literature

The study of banking and the money market in the 19th century was shaped by several key texts and practitioners. Among these were Thomson Hankey, who authored *Principles of Banking* (1867), and Walter Bagehot, whose work *Lombard Street* (1873) is regarded as a classic description of the actual working of the money market.

## Walter Bagehot and Lombard Street

Walter Bagehot (1826–1877) was an English economist and publicist who served as the editor of the *Economist* newspaper from 1860 until his death. Born in Langport, Somerset, to a father who was a banker, Bagehot was called to the bar in 1852. However, he spent a significant portion of his life engaged in the banking business of Stuckey & Co. in the west of England. While editing the *Economist*, he managed the bank's London agency, where he attended to its London affairs and lent surplus money in "Lombard Street." He also served as an underwriter at Lloyd's, though his detailed business was handled by proxy.

Bagehot's writing is characterized by a conversational, epigrammatic style and a scientific, dispassionate approach to subjects that were not previously treated as scientific studies. His work *Lombard Street*, published in 1873, provides a brilliant picture of the city at that time. In this text, Bagehot observes that in the early days of English banking, the power to issue notes served as the introduction to the system of deposit-banking; he notes that banks lacking this power could scarcely exist outside of London.

## Evolution of the Money Market Since Bagehot

While much of Bagehot's 1873 analysis remains true, certain developments have occurred in the money market. One significant change is the increased extent to which money is "used up" every night. In Bagehot's era, discount houses quoted "allowance" rates for loans at call and short notice based on the rate banks allowed for loans at seven days' notice. By 1888 or 1889, it became usual for bill-brokers to "allow" a rate for "money for the night" and occasionally fix terms independently of the banks.

This "fugitive" money often derives from balances left temporarily with banks or finance houses by foreign governments or parties with payments to make in London, or from subscriptions to loans offered to the public. While this money is often cheap, it is not considered "good" in City slang because it is uncertain how long such loans will remain undisturbed.

Additionally, the landscape of banking has shifted toward the growth of large joint-stock banks, often through the absorption of smaller institutions. This growth has facilitated a closer combination between these banks and the Bank of England to maintain the latter's reserve—the sole reserve for all banks—thereby stabilizing the money market.

## Thomson Hankey and Banking Administration

Thomson Hankey is noted for his 1867 work *Principles of Banking*. Beyond his literary contributions, Hankey provided data regarding the growth of banking personnel and expenditures. He stated that by 1867, upwards of one thousand persons were employed by the bank, with salaries and wages totaling nearly £260,000, in addition to approximately £20,000 paid in pensions to superannuated clerks. This represented a significant increase from 1847, when the bank employed upwards of nine hundred people with salaries exceeding £210,000, and the earliest stages of business when the bank commenced with fifty-four assistants earning a total of £4,350.

## Bagehot's Intellectual and Political Context

Bagehot's insights into the city and politics were informed by his extensive social and professional network. Through his marriage to the daughter of James Wilson (the first editor of the *Economist* and a former secretary of the treasury), Bagehot gained access to the inner circles of political life. He maintained constant communication with political chiefs such as Gladstone, Robert Lowe, and Grant Duff, as well as the permanent heads of great state departments. In the financial sector, he was intimate with the directors and governor of the Bank of England and other leading commercial magnates. His membership in the Political Economy Club further bridged the gap between city affairs and politics.
