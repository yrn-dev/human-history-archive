# Brickwork Bonding and Wall Construction

In the construction of brick walls, the arrangement of bricks is known as the bond. The primary purpose of bonding is to ensure that the bricks of every course cover the joints of the course below. This arrangement allows the entire mass of bricks to act together as dependently as possible. When weight is placed on a top brick, a proper bond distributes that weight over an area that increases with every subsequent course.

## Types of Bonding
Different bonding methods are employed depending on the thickness and purpose of the wall:

*   **English Bond:** This is considered the most perfect bond in use. It consists of alternate courses of headers and stretchers. In walls that are a multiple of a whole brick, the front and back faces appear similar; however, in walls requiring an odd half-brick for thickness, the opposite sides of a course are inverted.
*   **Flemish Bond:** A variation known as "single Flemish bond" serves as a compromise for appearance. In this method, the face of the wall is laid in Flemish bond while the backing is English bond. This improves upon the standard Flemish bond by removing the need for bats in the interior of the wall.
*   **Stretching Bond:** This method is reserved exclusively for walls that are half a brick in thickness, with all bricks laid as stretchers and a half-brick used in alternate courses to start the bond.
*   **Heading Bond:** This is used for footings, projecting mouldings, corbels, and work that is curved too sharply on a plan to allow for stretchers. In this bond, all bricks are laid as headers, meaning their ends face the front and their length runs across the thickness of the wall.

## Construction of Thick Walls
For walls that are three bricks thick and upwards, a saving of labour can be achieved without sacrificing strength by adopting a "diagonal bond" or "herring bone" pattern in the interior of the wall. In these instances, the outer faces are still built using English and Flemish bonds. This technique should not be used for walls thinner than 27 inches, as walls of a lesser thickness provide almost no advantage from this method.

To maintain both transverse and longitudinal bonds in walls above 9 inches thick (excluding English bond), half-bricks are frequently necessary. General practice dictates that a brick should not be cut if it can be worked in whole to avoid creating new joints, which can cause debility in the construction.

## Materials and Measurement
In the surrounding counties of London, brickwork is measured by a "rod," which is 16½ feet square and 1½ bricks thick. A rod of brickwork using bricks that are 8¾ inches long, 4¼ inches wide, and 2¾ inches thick (with ¼-inch joints) requires 4,356 bricks. The weight of a rod of brickwork ranges between 13½ and 15 tons, with cement mortar resulting in a heavier weight than lime mortar.

Individual bricks weigh approximately 7 lb and are sold by the thousand (weighing about 62 cwt). Mortar requirements in London are generally reckoned at 50 cubic feet for a ¼-inch joint and 72 cubic feet for a 3/8-inch joint, with an additional allowance of 11 cubic feet if the bricks have hollows or frogs.

## Technical Execution and Stability
Proper execution requires strict supervision to avoid failures caused by worker ignorance or carelessness. Bricks should be placed frog upwards and pressed firmly to ensure adhesion. They must be well-wetted before laying—especially in hot, dry weather—to remove dust and prevent the brick from absorbing water from the mortar.

For walls thicker than one brick (9 inches), two men should be employed simultaneously—one on the inside and one on the outside—as a single man cannot properly manage a wall even 14 inches thick from one side. When a wall can only be accessed from one side, it is termed "overhand" work.

To increase longitudinal tie, hoop-iron (1½ inches wide and 1/16 inch thick) may be used, typically with one strip for every half-brick of wall thickness. Stability can also be compromised if facing bricks differ in size from backing bricks, as this requires varying the thickness of mortar joints, which can lead to cracking and unequal settlement.

## Specialized Brickwork and Foundations
*   **Arches:** Rough-cut arches use bricks cut with an axe into wedge forms. Gauged arches use soft bricks cut to templates (voussoirs) and are set in lime putty with joints no wider than 1/32 inch.
*   **Footings:** These are wide courses at the base of a wall used to spread pressure. Offsets of 2¼ inches are made on each side until the width is double the wall's thickness. For example, a 13½-inch thick wall would have a bottom course 2 feet 3 inches wide.
*   **Foundations:** Concrete foundations are often used on soils like clay that expand and contract. The London Building Act requires concrete walls used as substitutes for brick to have an extra thickness of one-third.
