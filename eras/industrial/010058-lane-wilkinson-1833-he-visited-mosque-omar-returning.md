# Lane and Wilkinson

The figures of Lane and Wilkinson are associated with the study and documentation of Egypt and its history, appearing in the records of the Human History Archive primarily through their contributions to Egyptological publications and their association with other explorers of the 19th century.

## Egyptological Contributions
Lane and Wilkinson were part of a group of individuals involved in the study of Egypt during the early 19th century. Between 1824 and 1832, they were in Egypt in the company of Burton and the sculptor Giuseppe Bonomi. During this period, Bonomi worked as an illustrator for the leading Egyptological publications of the era, producing drawings of various monuments.

The archive references specific scholarly works authored by these individuals. Wilkinson is credited with the work *Ancient Egyptians*, while Lane is the author of *Mod. Egyptians* (specifically the 1860 edition). These texts have been utilized as sources for information regarding various subjects, including the history and practices of the Egyptians.

## Exploration and the Vocal Memnon
In addition to the work of Lane, the archive records a specific discovery attributed to an English traveller named Mr. Wilkinson. Wilkinson is credited with discovering the secret behind the "vocal Memnon." According to the records, the phenomenon was not miraculous but resulted from a cavity where a person was concealed. This person would strike a stone to produce a ringing sound similar to brass. This discovery was performed in the presence of Arabs, who described the sound in a manner consistent with the event.

## The Mosque of Omar
While Lane and Wilkinson are linked to Egyptological study, the archive also contains records regarding the Mosque of Omar in Jerusalem. In 1833, Giuseppe Bonomi visited this site and returned with detailed drawings. 

The mosque was built by Omar, the second Khalif, who died in A.D. 644. The structure is described as covering the entire consecrated ground of the Jewish temple and forms nearly a square with a circumference of one Roman mile (or 760 *toises*).
