# Metal Arch Bridges

Metal arch bridges are a category of bridge construction where the principal members are in compression. While classical and medieval bridges were primarily constructed from masonry, timber, brick, or concrete, the introduction of metal allowed for the achievement of great spans ranging from 500 to 1800 feet. In urban settings, metal arch bridges are often considered less objectionable in appearance than most forms of girder bridges.

## Evolution of Materials
The use of wrought iron began in the late 18th century, initially appearing in combination with cast iron or timber. Cast iron was utilized for arches during this same period, and early railway bridges employed cast iron girders. In modern applications, cast iron is reserved for arched bridges of moderate span.

Wrought iron was used extensively for suspension road bridges in the early 19th century and for large girder bridges in the middle of the 19th century, such as those at Saltash near Plymouth and over the Menai Strait. However, from 1880 onwards, mild steel—which possesses greater toughness and tenacity than wrought iron—wholly superseded wrought iron for girders with spans exceeding 100 feet. More recently, the introduction of ferro-concrete, armoured concrete, or concrete strengthened with steel bars has been used for arched bridges.

## Early Cast Iron Arch Bridges
The first iron bridge was erected between 1773 and 1779 at Coalbrookdale over the Severn, constructed by Abraham Darby (1750-1791) and John Wilkinson (1728-1808). This bridge featured five cast iron arched ribs and a center span of 100 feet. Although still in use, the bridge required patching for ninety years because the high side arches and the main arch did not work together, leading to fractures caused by expansion and contraction. Sir B. Baker noted that while the bridge remained perfectly strong with moderate stress in vital parts, the fractured ribs required fishing with oval holes and bolts that were not screwed too tight.

Due to the difficulty of casting heavy arch ribs, some late 18th and early 19th-century bridges utilized cast voussoirs, similar to masonry construction. Examples include:
* **Wearmouth Bridge:** Designed by Rowland Burdon and erected between 1793 and 1796 with a span of 235 feet.
* **Southwark Bridge:** Designed by John Rennie and erected between 1814 and 1819 over the Thames, featuring a center span of 240 feet and a rise of 24 feet.
* **Parisian Bridges:** The Carrousel (1834-1836) and Austerlitz (1800-1806) bridges both utilized cast iron arches.

In 1858, M.C. Meigs erected an aqueduct bridge in Washington with a 200-foot span. This structure used two arched ribs formed by cast iron pipes (4 feet internal diameter and 1½ inches thick) lined with 3-inch thick pine staves to prevent freezing.

## Wrought Iron and Steel Arch Bridges
Wrought iron was used for bridges such as the one over the Rhine at Coblenz, which consists of three spans of approximately 315 feet each. 

Later developments led to the combination of different bridge types. The Victoria Falls bridge over the Zambezi, completed in 1905 and designed by Sir Douglas Fox, combines girder and arch designs. It has a total length of 650 feet, with a parabolic center arch spanning 500 feet. The crown of this arch has a rise of 90 feet and a depth of 15 feet. The width between the ribs of the main arch is 53 feet 9 inches at the springings and 27½ feet at the crown.

## Construction and Engineering
The erection of large metal arches often involves building out from the abutments, where the completed section serves as a stage for lifting appliances. In the case of the St Louis bridge, half arches were built from either side of each pier to balance the load, supported by chains attached to arched ribs and skeleton towers on the piers. However, temperature-induced expansion created difficulties when connecting the arches at the crown.

For the Victoria Falls bridge, each half arch was supported by cables until they were joined at the center, utilizing an electric cableway with a 900-foot span capable of carrying 10 tons.

Unlike girder bridges, which impose only a vertical load on abutments and piers, arch bridges create a horizontal thrust. While they require more stiffening than suspension bridges, they are often preferred for their aesthetic qualities in town environments.
