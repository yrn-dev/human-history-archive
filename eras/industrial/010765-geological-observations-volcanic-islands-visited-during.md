# Geological Observations on the Volcanic Islands visited during the Voyage of H.M.S. "Beagle"

The publication *Geological Observations on the Volcanic Islands visited during the Voyage of H.M.S. "Beagle"*, authored by C. Darwin and published in London in 1844, serves as a primary record of geological study during the expedition.

## Related Volcanic Research and Figures
The study of volcanic formations and the nature of volcanic islands has been a central focus for various geologists. Baron Christian Leopold von Buch, a German geologist, conducted extensive research on volcanoes to refute erroneous ideas of the time. In 1799 and 1805, von Buch visited Vesuvius, the latter trip accompanied by Gay Lussac and Alexander von Humboldt, where they witnessed a remarkable eruption. Von Buch also explored the extinct volcanoes of Auvergne in 1802. His observations of the Puy de Dôme, specifically its strata of basaltic lava and cone of trachyte, led him to abandon the Neptunian theory of Werner, which advocated for the aqueous origin of basaltic formations.

Other significant contributors to volcanic geology include Lyell, who visited Teneriffe and Madeira with G. Hartung. Through these visits, Lyell gathered evidence regarding the formation of volcanic cones and the deposition and age of lava-beds. In 1858, Lyell revisited Sicily to study the structure of Etna, resulting in observations that refuted the "craters of elevation" theory supported by Élie de Beaumont and von Buch.

## Global Distribution of Volcanic Rocks
Basalts are distributed worldwide and often appear in immense masses. In the Sandwich Islands and Iceland, they are the prevalent lavas. This Icelandic volcanic province extends southward through the Faeroe Islands to include the columnar jointed basalts found in Antrim (Giant's Causeway), Staffa, and Skye. Other significant basaltic lava fields are located in the Deccan in India, as well as across thousands of square miles in Idaho, Oregon, and Washington. Additionally, volcanoes such as Vesuvius and Etna emit basaltic rocks.

## Regional Volcanic Observations
Volcanic activity is noted in various diverse geographical regions:

*   **Central Africa:** In the Albertine (or Central African) rift-valley, Lake Kivu is separated from the Nile basin by a line of volcanic peaks. The shores of the lake feature high, bold precipitous cliffs of volcanic rock. This region, often called Mfumbiro, saw the elevation of volcanic peaks in recent geological times, which dammed back water and caused the lake to overflow to the south.
*   **British East Africa:** The volcanic plateau of Central East Africa was formed by immense quantities of volcanic rocks accumulating on ancient crystalline rocks from the Post-Jurassic period to the Recent era. This includes the volcanoes of the rift-valley and Kibo, as well as plateau eruptions and Kimawenzi.
*   **Russia:** Around the shores of Lake Baikal, volcanic activity occurred during the Quaternary Age or at the end of the Tertiary, leaving great streams of lava covering the Khamar-daban and Sayan mountains and the valley of Irkut.
*   **Scotland:** In the island of Bute, Lower Carboniferous age contemporaneous lavas and cement-stones surmount Upper Old Red Sandstone strata. The bedded volcanic rocks there include andesite, porphyritic basalts, and brownish trachyte near Port Luchdach. Intrusive igneous rocks from the Carboniferous age, such as the olivine-basalt mass on Suidhe Hill, also appear as bosses and sills.

## Scientific Methodology and Theory
The study of these formations has involved analyzing the differentiation of rock-types during solidification as volcanic or plutonic rocks from magma, a subject explored by Waldemar Christofer Brögger.
