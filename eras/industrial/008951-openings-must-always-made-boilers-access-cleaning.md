# Boiler Access and Cleaning

Boilers must be constructed with openings to allow for examination and cleaning. These openings are essential because impurities in feed-water, such as lime or magnesia salts, do not evaporate with the steam. Instead, they concentrate in the boiler water and precipitate as mud or scale on heating surfaces. While mud and soluble impurities can be removed via blow-off cocks, scale requires periodical cleaning. If left unaddressed, incrustations reduce boiler efficiency by obstructing heat transmission and can create danger by causing plates to become weakened and overheated.

## Man-holes and Hand-holes
When access openings are large enough to permit a man to enter the boiler, they are termed man-holes. These are typically oval in shape, a design that allows the closing doors to be placed on the inside. This configuration ensures that steam pressure tends to keep the doors shut. These doors are secured to the outside of the boiler using one or two bolts attached to cross-bars or "dogs." It is critical that these doors fit the holes accurately to prevent jointing material from being forced out of position. In rare instances where doors are fitted on the outside—meaning steam pressure tends to push them open—they are secured by several bolts so that the failure of a single bolt will not result in the door being forced off.

In other installations, smaller openings known as hand-holes are used. For example, in simple vertical boilers, a hand-hole with a suitable door is provided in the outer shell opposite each cross-tube for cleaning purposes.

## Access in Specific Boiler Types
Different boiler designs employ various methods to ensure internal components remain accessible for maintenance:

*   **Yarrow Boilers:** Access to the interior is generally obtained through man-holes located in the water-chamber ends and the steam-chest. In smaller versions used in torpedo boats, where water-chambers are too small for man-holes, the chambers are divided into two parts connected by a bolted joint to make all tube ends accessible. Additionally, the tubes are spaced to allow for external cleaning from the front and cross-ways in two directions.
*   **Babcock & Wilcox Boilers:** These utilize headers with holes on two opposite sides. One side connects the headers to the tubes, while the other provides access for cleaning and fixing the tubes. These outer holes are oval and closed by special fittings; the oval shape allows the inside fitting to be placed from the outside. These fittings are designed to cover the opening and prevent a significant outrush of water or steam if a bolt breaks.
*   **Reed Boilers:** Access to the top ends of the tubes is obtained via the steam-chest, while the lower tube ends are accessed through numerous doors located in the water-chambers.
*   **Cochran Boilers:** These feature a combustion chamber with a "dry" back, where firebrick-lined doors are fitted to provide easy access to the tube ends.

## Maintenance and Cleaning Accessories
Beyond physical access openings, boilers are equipped with several accessories to assist in maintenance and the removal of impurities:

*   **Blow-out Cocks:** These are fitted to empty the boiler and remove soluble impurities and mud.
*   **Water-Gauges:** A glass water-gauge is used to ascertain the actual water level. This assembly includes three cocks; one acts specifically as a blow-out for testing the gauge's operation and cleaning it.
*   **Softeners:** To reduce the need for frequent cleaning, feed-water may be passed through a "softener." This process involves mixing the water with chemical reagents and raising the temperature to precipitate impurities before the water is filtered and pumped into the boiler.

Because of the difficulty of thorough cleaning in multitubular or water-tube boilers, types such as the Cornish, Galloway, and Lancashire are preferred in environments where feed-water is very impure.
