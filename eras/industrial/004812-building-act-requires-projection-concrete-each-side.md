# Footings and Concrete Foundations

## Overview of Footings
Footings are wide courses of brickwork located at the base or foot of a wall. Their primary purpose is to spread the pressure of the structure over a larger area of ground. To achieve this, offsets measuring 2¼ inches wide are made on each side of the wall. This process continues until the footing reaches a width equal to double the thickness of the wall. For example, in a wall that is 13½ inches thick (equivalent to 1½ bricks), the bottom course would be 2 feet 3 inches wide (equivalent to 3 bricks). To increase strength, it is preferable to double the lowest course.

## Concrete Bedding and Regulations
A foundation bed of concrete is often used to further distribute weight. While a projection of 6 inches on each side is generally made to facilitate easy working—bringing the total width of the surface bearing on the ground to 3 feet 3 inches for a 13½ inch wall—the London Building Act has a different requirement. The Act requires the projection of concrete on each side of the brickwork to be only 4 inches.

Footings should be constructed using hard bricks, laid primarily as headers. If stretchers are necessary, they should be positioned in the middle of the wall.

## Concrete in Variable Soils
Concrete foundations are commonly used on soils liable to change form due to meteorological influences, such as clays that shrink when dried and swell when wetted. In these instances, concrete is interposed to protect the building from derangement. Alternatively, concrete walls may be used as a cheaper substitute for stone or brick structures, extending from a level sufficiently below the ordinary surface of the ground.

When concrete is employed to prevent soil from yielding to pressure, the base must have sufficient expanse or extent. The thickness or depth of the concrete layer in these cases is determined by its own ability to transmit the weight of the wall to the ground without being crushed or breaking across.

## Concrete as a Wall Substitute
When concrete is used as a substitute for a wall to carry a structure down to a low level, it functions as a wall itself. Because it lacks manipulated bond in its construction, it is relatively weak and must be wide in proportion to that weakness, encased by the surrounding soil. Under the London Building Act, when a concrete wall is used instead of brick, an extra thickness of one-third is required. Currently, no regulations regarding thickness have been established for reinforced concrete.

## Specialized Foundation Methods
In specific soil conditions, alternative foundation methods are utilized:
*   **Disk Piles:** Used in sand, these piles feature a flat flange at the bottom. Water is pumped in at the top while the pile is weighted to prevent it from rising, allowing the pile to be lowered into ground where driving by blows is ineffective.
*   **Wells:** In running sands, a masonry casing similar to a well is built, and the soil inside is excavated. As the casing sinks, the masonry is continued at the surface. Once the required depth is reached, the interior is typically filled with brick or concrete.
*   **Piers and Abutments:** These may be constructed from brickwork, masonry, or wrought or cast iron. Cast iron piers may consist of hollow cylindrical pillars—either raking or vertical—united by flanges, bolts, and sockets. These are strengthened by diagonal and horizontal bracing. In some instances, cast iron cylinders with diameters of 10 feet or more are filled with concrete.

## Caissons and Water-Bearing Strata
In civil engineering, caissons (enclosures of iron or wood) are used to keep out water during the execution of foundations in water-bearing strata, such as the sea or rivers. 
*   **Open-top Caissons:** These may have a water-tight bottom or be carried down into a water-tight stratum using a cutting edge and weights, often aided by internal excavation.
*   **Bottomless Caissons:** These act as diving-bells where compressed air keeps out water. Men excavate the bottom while a quay-wall or pier is built on the roof as the caisson descends.

Examples of these applications include the quays at Rouen, where open-timber caissons were sunk to 9¾ feet below low-water to allow brick and concrete lower portions of the quay-wall to be built. At Zeebrugge, Scheveningen, and Bilbao, large metal caissons were ballasted with concrete, floated into position, and then filled with concrete to form foundation blocks for breakwaters.
