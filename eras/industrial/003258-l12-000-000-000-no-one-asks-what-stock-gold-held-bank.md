# Banking Systems and Currency Circulation

## United Kingdom Banking and the London Clearing House
In the United Kingdom, the majority of large transactions are settled via cheque through a system of ledger transfers. This process is facilitated by the London Clearing House, as every bank in the United Kingdom maintains an agent in London. The annual circulation managed by the London Clearing House exceeds L12,000,000,000. This paper circulation is characterized by a high degree of trust; participants generally do not inquire about the specific reserves or the stock of gold held by the banks on which cheques are drawn. This medium is described as highly flexible, expanding to meet demand and contracting once strain has passed.

Actual note circulation in the United Kingdom for 1906 totaled L43,372,000. This was comprised of L29,443,000 in England (of which the Bank of England accounted for L28,890,000), L7,477,000 in Scotland, and L6,452,000 in Ireland.

## The Bank of England and Reserve Fluctuations
The Bank of England's capital is L18,000,000. Its reserves have historically fluctuated, showing decennial averages such as L8,500,000 in 1845, L8,000,000 in 1865, L15,100,000 in 1885, and L29,900,000 in 1895, reaching L23,500,000 in 1906. These figures have been viewed as a narrow and sometimes inadequate basis for the country's business requirements. For example, the L15,100,000 reserve of 1885 dropped to L12,700,000 by 1888, and the L29,900,000 of 1895 rose to L34,600,000 in 1896 before falling to L21,200,000 in 1899.

Beyond the Bank of England, other banks maintain their own reserves, some as balances at the Bank of England and others as bank notes and specie in their own tills.

## European National Banks
### The Bank of France
Business transactions in France are primarily liquidated using notes from the Bank of France rather than cheques. These notes are preferred over specie, which accumulates in the bank's vaults. In 1906, the Bank of France held an average of L115,000,000 in gold and L42,000,000 in silver. This large specie reserve has provided stability to French trade and prevented the frequent fluctuations in discount rates seen in England. While this imposes a heavy burden on shareholders, it results in a low rate of interest for borrowed capital, benefiting French industry. The Bank of France's capital is L9,000,000, and its note circulation in 1906 was L186,300,000.

### The Imperial Bank of Germany (Reichsbank)
The Bank of Germany has a capital of L12,200,000 and a note circulation of L69,000,000 in 1906. Its note circulation is governed by laws from 1875 and 1899. The bank is permitted an uncovered limit, which was extended to L23,641,450 by the act of June 5, 1902. Notes not represented by specie must be held against treasury notes, notes of issuing banks, or discounted bills with at least two or three good indorsements. 

By law, at least one-third of notes in circulation must be covered by gold bullion, foreign coin, money, or imperial treasury notes. The bank must redeem notes in gold coin, silver thalers, or bar-gold. In the 1906 balance sheet, the bank held L24,069,000 in gold, L67,000,000 in bills, and L14,200,000 in loans and advances. Unlike the English system, the German bank can exceed its legal issue limits by paying 5% on the surplus, a power used annually since 1895.

### The Austro-Hungarian Bank
Founded in 1816 as the Austrian National Bank and renamed in 1878, this joint-stock company has a stock of L8,780,000. Its notes must be covered by legal specie (gold and silver) to the extent of two-fifths. At the end of 1900, the bank had a note circulation of L62,251,000 and cash of L50,754,000. By 1907, these figures rose to a note circulation of L84,501,000 and cash of L60,036,625.

## Regional and Colonial Banking
### Ireland
Ireland maintains the authorized circulation limits established by Peel's Act of 1845. In 1906, several joint-stock banks operated there, including the Bank of Ireland (capital L2,769,230), the National Bank (L1,500,000), and others such as the Provincial, Hibernian, Northern Banking Co., Belfast Banking Co., Ulster Banking Co., and Royal Bank. Some, like the Munster Bank, Ltd., are not banks of issue.
