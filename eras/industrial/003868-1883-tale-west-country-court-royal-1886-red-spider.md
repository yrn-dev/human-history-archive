## Sabine Baring-Gould
Sabine Baring-Gould (1834– ) was an English novelist born in Exeter on January 28, 1834. He was educated at Clare College, Cambridge. Following his graduation, he spent several years traveling before entering the clergy. His ecclesiastical career included serving as the curate of Horbury, Yorkshire, in 1864, the perpetual curate of Dalton in the same county in 1867, and the rector of East Mersea, Essex, in 1871. 

In 1872, following the death of his father, Baring-Gould inherited the estate of Lew Trenchard in North Devon, a location where his family had been established for nearly three centuries. In 1881, he exchanged his living in Essex to become the rector of Lew Trenchard.

Beyond fiction, Baring-Gould conducted serious research into antiquities, topography, and folk-lore. Notable titles in these fields include *Book of Were-wolves* (1865), *Curious Myths of the Middle Ages* (1866), and *Curious Survivals* (1892). He also wrote numerous volumes of popular theology and sermons. From 1871 to 1873, he served as the editor of *The Sacristy*, a quarterly review focused on ecclesiastical literature and art.

## The Red Spider (Tetranychus telarius)
Separate from the literary works of Baring-Gould, the archive contains technical data on the red spider (*Tetranychus telarius*), which is classified as one of the "spinning mites." This pest is particularly destructive during very hot summers, specifically affecting hop crops.

The red spider congregates on the under surfaces of leaves, where it exhausts the sap. This process causes the leaves to fall, a phenomenon referred to in Germany as "fire-blast." While a wash of soft soap and quassia is effective against aphis blight, it is of little use against the red spider. Instead, some success has been found using a solution of 100 gallons of water mixed with 8 lb. to 10 lb. of soft soap and three pints of paraffin. Because the spiders protect themselves with webs, these washes must be applied with great force. This is typically achieved using garden engines worked by hand or horse, and occasionally steam engines that force the solution through pipes located between the rows of hops.
