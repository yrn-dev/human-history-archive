# Slovene National Movement and Political History

The Slovenes, who constituted a large majority of the population in Carniola and a considerable minority in the south of Styria and the adjoining territory of Carinthia, engaged in a sustained political movement to secure the recognition of their language and national identity within the Austro-Hungarian framework.

## Political Ideals and Language
The political objective of the Slovenes was the establishment of an "Illyrian" kingdom, which would encompass Croatia and all southern Slavs in the coast district. As part of this vision, a movement was initiated to create a so-called "Illyrian" language intended for use by both Slovenes and Croats, though this effort met with limited success. The primary demands of the Slovene movement centered on the use of their language for education and government purposes.

In the field of linguistics, some scholars, including Miklosich and Kopitar, have identified the liturgical language of the Orthodox Slavs (ecclesiastical Slavonic) as "Old Slovene," although a more generally accepted theory describes it as "Old Bulgarian."

## Legislative and Administrative Gains
Slovenes did not receive government support until 1879. Following this, they achieved significant political milestones in Carniola, winning a majority in the diet in 1882. While the diet of Styria remained a center for German interests, the diet of Carniola became the primary support for Slovene agitation. In the same year, Slovenes won the majority in the town council of Laibach, which had previously been German.

These victories allowed the Slovenes to introduce Illyrian as the official language and mandate that street names be written in Illyrian. Similar efforts to introduce Illyrian alongside Italian in some towns of Istria resulted in serious riots. 

Further linguistic recognition was achieved through a resolution of the diet of Carniola and a motion by Slovene members of the Reichsrath. The government subsequently recognized Slovenian as an official language for:
* The entirety of Carniola.
* The district of Cilli in Styria.
* The mixed and Slovene districts in southern Carinthia.

Additionally, the government determined that a Slovene gymnasium would be maintained in Laibach alongside the existing German one. In 1880, the government declared Slovenian a customary language in Klagenfurt, Carinthia; despite a population of 16,491 German-speakers and only 568 Slovenian-speakers (including hospital and gaol inhabitants), the ruling required public offices and law courts to conduct business in Slovenian.

## Parliamentary Representation and Conflict
Slovene representation in the Reichsrath varied over time. In one recorded period, there were 30 Slovenes, including 17 Clericals, while others were part of the Southern Slav Club. Later records show 16 Slovenes (11 Clerical and 5 Radical). In 1900, Carniola—which had a population of 508,348 (95% Slovene and 5% German)—sent eleven deputies to the Reichsrat.

The Slovene movement eventually became a catalyst for government instability. In 1894, a coalition ministry was formed consisting of the Left, the Poles, and the Clericals, presided over by Prince Alfred Windisch-Graetz. The Slovenes, as members of the Hohenwart Club, requested that the gymnasium at Cilli establish Slovenian-language classes parallel to German classes. 

This request was supported by Hohenwart and adopted by the ministry, but it sparked intense opposition from the Styrian diet and the German Left, who were influenced by popular indignation in German districts. On June 12, 1895, the vote in favor of the Slovenian classes passed against the German Left. Consequently, the German Left withdrew their support from the government, leading to the ministry's immediate resignation.

## Regional Demographics and Influence
In Carniola, the population was overwhelmingly Roman Catholic (99%). The province was administered through eleven districts and one autonomous municipality, Laibach. Other significant locations included Oberlaibach, Idria, Gurkfeld, Zirknitz, Adelsberg, Neumarktl, Krainburg, and Gottschee.

While the Slovenes sought their own national goals, other elements in the southern districts, specifically the Serbs, maintained different priorities. Being members of the Orthodox Church, the Serbs were attracted to Russia and hoped for the creation of a great Servian kingdom. In recent years, attempts were made to shift the Slovenian national movement in this direction to align Slovenes with the non-Austrian Orthodox Slavs.
