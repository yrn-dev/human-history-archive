# The Stealthy School of Criticism

The "Stealthy School of Criticism" refers to a specific literary response published in the *Athenaeum* on 16th December 1871. This response was written by D.G. Rossetti as a reply to a controversial attack on poetry led by Robert Williams Buchanan.

## Origins of the Conflict
The dispute began with an article published in the October 1871 issue of the *Contemporary Review*. The article was written by Robert Williams Buchanan, a British poet, novelist, and dramatist born in 1841. Writing under the *nom de plume* of Thomas Maitland, Buchanan titled the piece "The Fleshly School of Poetry." This article sought to criticize certain poetic trends and was later expanded into a pamphlet in 1872.

Buchanan, who had previously published works such as *Undertones* (1863), *Idyls and Legends of Inverburn* (1865), *London Poems* (1866), and *North Coast and other Poems* (1868), had also attempted a study in mysticism titled *The Book of Orm: A Prelude to the Epic* in 1870. However, it was his attack as "Thomas Maitland" that brought him notoriety.

## The Response in the Athenaeum
D.G. Rossetti responded to Buchanan's accusations in a letter to the *Athenaeum* dated 16th December 1871. Rossetti titled his reply "The Stealthy School of Criticism," effectively mirroring and mocking the phrasing of Buchanan's "Fleshly School of Poetry." 

The conflict did not end with Rossetti's letter; another poet, Mr. Swinburne, also replied to Buchanan's criticisms in a work titled *Under the Microscope*, published in 1872.

## Aftermath and Legacy
In later years, Robert Williams Buchanan expressed regret regarding the violence of his attack. The depth of the animosity is evidenced by the fact that Buchanan dedicated his work *God and the Man* to Rossetti, whom he referred to as the "old enemy."

## The Athenaeum Periodical
The *Athenaeum*, in which the 1871 exchange took place, is a London literary weekly founded in 1828. The name "Athenaeum" has a long history, originally referring to buildings dedicated to Athena in ancient Greece, specifically a temple in Athens where men of learning and poets met to read their productions. The name was later used for an academy built by Emperor Hadrian in Rome around A.D. 135, which provided instruction in philosophy, grammar, jurisprudence, and rhetoric. In modern times, the term has been used for various literary clubs, Dutch high schools, and academies in Marseilles and Lyons.
