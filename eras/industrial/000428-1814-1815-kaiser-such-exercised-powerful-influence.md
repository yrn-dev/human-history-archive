# The Influence of the "Kaiser" and the Habsburg Monarchy (1814-1815)

## Origins of the Austrian Empire
The official designation of the territories ruled by the Habsburg monarchy as the Empire of Austria began in 1804. This occurred when Francis II, the final emperor of the Holy Roman Empire, proclaimed himself Francis I, emperor of Austria. This action was motivated by a desire to prevent the House of Habsburg from falling into a position inferior to the Bonapartes should the Holy Roman Empire collapse or if Napoleon were elected as the successor to the throne of Charlemagne. Following the end of the Holy Empire in 1806, the title of emperor of Austria replaced "Imperator Romanorum semper Augustus."

From its inception, the title was viewed as a poor representation of the actual relationship between the Habsburg sovereigns and their various states. The Germans viewed it as an inadequate substitute for the title that had previously symbolized the political unity of the German race. Simultaneously, the Slavs and Magyars did not willingly recognize a style that implied the superiority of the monarchy's German elements and ignored their own national rights.

## The Vienna Congress and European Power
In September 1814, Vienna was selected as the site for a great international congress intended to re-establish the balance of power in Europe, which had been disrupted by the conquests of Napoleon. This choice recognized the decisive role Austria played in these events. The congress resulted in a triumphant vindication of the diplomacy of Metternich.

Through a secret treaty on January 3, 1815, and with the assistance of France and Great Britain, Metternich frustrated Prussian attempts to absorb all of Saxony. Additionally, Bavaria was forced to return territories to Austria that had been gained through Napoleon. Austria regained Dalmatia and Illyria, and the kingdom under the Habsburg crown was expanded by adding Lombardy to Venetia. These shifts ensured that Austrian influence replaced French influence across the Italian peninsula.

## The "Kaiser" and the German People
Following the Vienna Congress of 1814-1815, the "Kaiser" continued to exercise a powerful influence over the imaginations of the German people living outside the Habsburg dominions. This influence persisted because the title remained surrounded by an "ancient halo," and the fundamental changes regarding the nature of the title were not immediately recognized.

In Germany, the settlement reached during this period was particularly fateful. Despite protests from the Holy See, the Holy Empire was not restored. Austria instead preferred a presidency over a loose confederation of sovereign states, known as a *Staatenbund*. Metternich believed this body would be "powerless for offence" but "powerful for defence," thereby guaranteeing the peace of central Europe and maintaining Austrian preponderance. He argued that Austrian diplomacy, supported by Habsburg power beyond German borders, would provide more influence than a venerable title that had become synonymous with practical impotence and unlimited pretensions.

## Decline of the Imperial Title
The influence of the title eventually waned. The spell was finally broken in 1866 following a long struggle with Prussia, and the subsequent proclamation of the German empire in 1871 stripped the title of emperor of Austria of everything except a purely territorial significance. 

Furthermore, a 1867 compact with Hungary meant the title no longer fully represented the emperor's relation to all his dominions. The title, originally designed to cover the entire Habsburg monarchy, became the official style for only half of the sovereign's territories. Even within the proper Austrian empire, the title was resented by peoples such as the Bohemians, who sought the same recognition of national independence that had been granted to Hungary.
