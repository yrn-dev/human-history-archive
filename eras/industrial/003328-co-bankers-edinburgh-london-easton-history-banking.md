# Banking Institutions and Legal Frameworks in Britain

The history of banking in Britain is characterized by the evolution of specialized banking houses, the central role of the Bank of England, and the development of a complex legal framework governing the movement of funds and the liabilities of bankers.

## The Bank of England
Founded to provide the government with a loan of £1,200,000, the Bank of England has served as the banker to the government since its inception. Its primary responsibilities include the management of the British national debt, the registration of stock transfers, and the payment of interest on debt stock, which is currently paid quarterly. 

The Bank of England maintained a position of early pre-eminence due to several exclusive privileges. Until 1826, it was the only joint-stock bank permitted to exist in England and Wales. Furthermore, it was the only English bank with limited liability for its shareholders until 1862. Since 1833, the Bank has held the privilege of issuing notes that serve as legal tender throughout England and Wales, except at the Bank itself. While it acts as the banker for other banks in the country, its historical dominance has shifted; currently, some competitors hold larger deposits, and the collective deposits of other banks with London offices significantly exceed those of the Bank of England. Despite this, its strength remains evident, with stocks inscribed in its books exceeding 1,250 million sterling.

## Banking Houses and the Clearing House
Beyond the central bank, various banking houses operated across British cities. Historical records identify several specialized works on the subject, including Richardson's study of Coutts & Co., Bankers, Edinburgh and London, and H. T. Easton's 1903 *History of a Banking House* regarding Smith, Payne & Smiths.

In London, "purely city banks" established a "Clearing House" by 1776, though records from a house known as the "Grasshopper" suggest a less organized settlement system existed as early as 1773. By 1810, forty-six banking houses used a dedicated house for settling accounts. The Bank of England is not a member of the Clearing House, though it "clears on one side," meaning its claims on clearing bankers are processed through the house, while claims against the Bank are sent directly to Threadneedle Street multiple times daily. 

The membership of the Clearing House evolved over time. In 1844, twenty-six of the forty-nine London banks were members, but at that time, membership was restricted to private banks. Joint-stock banks were admitted in 1854. By 1906, the body consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank. A significant advancement occurred in 1858 when Sir John Lubbock (Lord Avebury) arranged for the clearing of country cheques, allowing cheques from distant English villages to be processed as conveniently as those from London.

## Legal Obligations and Liabilities
The legal responsibilities of bankers are defined by various acts and judicial precedents. Under the Stamp Act 1853 (Section 19), bankers paying a draft or order payable to order on demand were relieved of liability if the endorsement was unauthorized or forged. This was upheld in the 1903 case *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon*. Similarly, Section 60 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 provides protection for bankers regarding cheques.

Regarding crossed cheques, Section 82 provides qualified immunity to bankers collecting such cheques for customers, provided specific conditions are met:
* The banker must receive the cheque and money from a customer with whom they have existing business relations.
* The cheque must already be crossed generally or specially to the banker.
* The banker must act in good faith and without negligence.

Negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care for the true owner, such as failing to verify that endorsements are ostensibly correct or accepting an "account payee" cheque for a different account. The *Gordon* case established that a banker must act strictly as an agent or "conduit pipe"; crediting cheques as cash before receiving the funds is considered taking them as an owner, which removes the bank's protection under Section 82.

## Fraud, Forgery, and Safe Custody
When statutory protection does not apply, a banker's right to relief from losses due to fraud or forgery depends on general principles. A banker generally cannot charge a customer for payments made on a forged signature. However, if the customer accredits the forgery or fails to warn the banker after becoming aware of it, the customer is estopped from disputing the debit.

Bankers also provide safe custody for valuables. Following a 1896 case involving Mrs. Langtry and the Union Bank of London, the Central Association of Bankers issued a memorandum clarifying liability. It distinguished between the wrongful delivery of goods to the wrong person (where the bank is liable regardless of negligence) and the loss, theft, or destruction of goods. In the latter case, the banker is viewed as a gratuitous bailee and is only liable if they failed to exercise the care an ordinary prudent man would take of his own valuables, taking into account the nature of the goods and the use of strong-rooms.
