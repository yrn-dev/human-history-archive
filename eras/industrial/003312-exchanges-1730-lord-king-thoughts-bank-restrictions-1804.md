# Banking and Financial Instruments in British History

The history of modern banking in Europe from the 16th century onward is characterized by a division between two primary types of institutions: exchange banks and banks of deposits. Exchange banks, such as the Bank of Amsterdam and the Bank of Hamburg, were established specifically to facilitate trade with other countries and deal with foreign exchange. While these institutions were important in their era, their period of activity has largely passed, and they are now primarily of historical interest. In contrast, banks of deposits—including the Bank of England, the Bank of France, the Bank of Germany, the Bank of Sweden, and the Bank of Venice—received deposits and provided loans, thereby associating themselves with a country's general industries and trade.

## Bills of Exchange and Negotiable Instruments

The bill of exchange is a form of negotiable instrument with an obscure history, though it is suggested they may have been invented by Florentine Jews. These instruments were well known in England during the middle ages, although no reported legal decisions regarding them exist prior to 1603. Initially, their use was limited to foreign bills between English and foreign merchants. Over time, this use expanded to include domestic bills between traders, and eventually to bills used by all persons regardless of whether they were traders.

For a period, bills were alleged in legal proceedings to be drawn *secundum usum et consuetudinem mercatorum*. The foundations of modern English law regarding these instruments were established by Lord Mansfield, who utilized juries of London merchants. Later, the common law of estoppel continued to apply to bills, notes, and cheques, unless specifically annulled by the Bills of Exchange Act. This principle ensures that a person providing inchoate negotiable instruments to an agent to raise money remains responsible to any party taking them bona fide and for value, even if the agent fraudulently abused their authority.

Other financial instruments developed at different stages. Promissory notes are of a more modern origin than bills of exchange; their validity as negotiable instruments was confirmed by a statute of Anne in 1704. Cheques are described as a creation of the modern banking system.

## Banking Development in Ireland

The early development of banking in Ireland closely mirrored the process seen in England, where circulation preceded and fed deposits. The system grew gradually as banks obtained credit through the ready acceptance of their notes, which attracted customers. However, the early history of Irish banking was marked by significant instability. 

Sir Robert Peel, in a speech on June 9, 1845, cited a 1804 report from the committee of Irish exchanges stating that there were fifty registered banks at that time, all of which failed. These failures resulted in "the most fearful distress." The instability was compounded by the views of the common people; in one instance, a Dublin banker named Beresford became so unpopular that a group of "ignorant country people" collected his notes and publicly burned them, believing the act would break his bank. Following the legislation of 1845, however, Irish banking achieved a level of steadiness and success equal to any other part of the United Kingdom.

## Evolution of the British Banking System

The structure of banking in England and Wales underwent significant changes through various acts passed in 1826–1833, 1844–1845, 1862, and 1879. Until 1862, partners in private banks were restricted to a maximum of six. An act in 1862 increased this limit to ten, though this did not apply to issuing private banks, which remained restricted to six partners.

The Bank Act of 1844 had an unexpected impact on the system. While it had favored small banks for many years, it eventually led to the formation of large, powerful banks when they became necessary. Under this act, no new banks of issue were permitted. Private issuing banks with no more than six partners could remain or amalgamate with other private issuing banks to retain joint issues. Joint-stock banks with issues could also continue, but if two joint-stock banks amalgamated, only the continuing bank retained its issue. Furthermore, if a private issuing bank joined or was formed into a joint-stock bank, its issue lapsed.
