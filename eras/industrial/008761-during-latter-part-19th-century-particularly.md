# Developments in the Latter Part of the 19th Century

The latter part of the 19th century was characterized by significant shifts in global economics, national political stability, cultural production, and industrial engineering. These changes ranged from the expansion of European raw-material acquisition to the evolution of linguistic and literary identities in Central Europe.

## Global Economics and Imperial Expansion
During the latter quarter of the 19th century, European governments were forced to look abroad for staple foods due to increasing populations. Simultaneously, the growth of scientific industrialism created a demand for new raw materials, including rubber, fats, greases, and other previously disregarded substances. While Great Britain, Portugal, and Holland maintained considerable control over semi-tropical and tropical products, other nations began seeking new opportunities. After 1871, Germany, followed by France and later Italy, began searching for Oriental countries capable of profitable modernization or unannexed areas for raw materials.

In the Americas, Canada was the first British possession to respond richly to new transport methods and economic possibilities. Similarly, South American republics, particularly the Argentine Republic, experienced increased nearness to European markets through their coffee growing and cattle trade.

## Political Crisis in the Dual Monarchy
At the close of the 19th century, both states of the Dual Monarchy faced severe political crises. In Austria, parliamentary life became paralyzed by a feud between Czechs and Germans. This conflict resulted directly from the Badeni language ordinances of 1897 and indirectly from the growth of Slav influence—specifically that of Poles and Czechs—during the Taaffe era (1879-1893). 

During this period, the Austrian constitution was observed in letter rather than spirit, and political life reached a low ebb. Government was conducted by cabinets of officials utilizing the emergency clause (paragraph 14) of the constitution. While ministers were nominally responsible to parliament, they were in practice responsible only to the emperor.

## Bohemian Literature and Culture
Bohemian literature developed to an unforeseen extent during the latter part of the 19th century, a growth spurred particularly by the 1882 foundation of the national university. Notable writers of this era included:
* **John Neruda (1834-1891):** A talented writer of prose and poetry, known for his philosophical epic *Kosmicke basne*.
* **Julius Zeyer (1841-1901):** Author of the successful novel *Jan Maria Plojhar* and the epic poem *Vysehrad*, which celebrates the Prague acropolis.
* **Svatopluk Cech:** A poet inspired by national enthusiasm, noted for *Basne otroka* and *Vaclav z Michalovic, Lesetinsky Kovar*.
* **Jaroslav Vrchlicky (b. 1853):** A brilliant dramatist and poet whose works, such as *Rok na jihu* and *Poute k Eldoradu*, show patriotic feelings but a more catholic choice of subjects.
* **Other contributors:** Bozena Nemceva (author of *Babicka*), Benes Trebizky, Alois Jirasek, Jacob Arbes (author of *Romanetta*), and Vaclav Hladik, whose *Evzen Voldan* represents modern Prague life.

## Industrial and Technical Advancements
The late 19th century saw critical transitions in materials science and engineering:

**Bridge Construction:** From 1880 onwards, the introduction of mild steel—which possessed greater toughness and tenacity than wrought iron—led to the wholesale supersession of wrought iron for girders with spans exceeding 100 feet. This transition, along with the later introduction of ferro-concrete (concrete strengthened with steel bars), enabled the construction of great spans ranging from 500 to 1800 feet.

**Billiard Technology:** In the late 19th century, pneumatic cushions (tubes into which air could be pumped) were trialed, though they did not achieve popularity. This followed earlier 19th-century experiments with india-rubber (introduced around 1835) and Vulcanite.

## Regional Demographics: Biscay
In the Basque Provinces, the inhabitants of Biscay experienced rapid population growth during the latter part of the 19th century. This increase was driven by a uniformly high birth-rate, a low death-rate, and very slight loss through emigration. By 1900, the population density of Biscay reached 372.4 per sq. m., the highest of any Spanish province.
