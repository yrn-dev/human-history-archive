# Bromine and its Medicinal Applications

Bromine is a chemical element of the halogen group, symbolized as Br with an atomic weight of 79.96. Its name is derived from the Greek word *brômos*, meaning a stench, referring to its pungent and unpleasant smell. First isolated in 1826 by A.J. Balard from salts found in Mediterranean waters, bromine does not occur in nature in an uncombined state. Instead, it is widely but sparingly distributed in combination with various metals.

## Natural Occurrence and Commercial Sources
Bromine is found in river water, sea-water, mineral waters, and occasionally within marine animals and plants, typically as potassium, sodium, and magnesium bromides. The primary commercial sources of the element are the brines of West Virginia, Pennsylvania, Ohio, and Michigan in the U.S.A., as well as salt deposits at Stassfurt in Prussian Saxony, where magnesium bromide is associated with various chlorides. Additionally, small quantities are derived from kelp and the mother liquors of Chile saltpetre. In mineral form, it is found as bromargyrite (bromite) when combined with silver.

## Manufacture and Purification
The bromine industry is centered primarily in the central district of Michigan and Stassfurt. It is manufactured from magnesium bromide found in "bittern" (the mother liquor of the salt industry) using two primary methods:

*   **The Continuous Process:** This method utilizes the decomposition of bromide by chlorine. A current of steam mixed with chlorine is passed through a tower of broken bricks containing hot bittern. The liberated bromine is swept out by steam and condensed in a worm. Any remaining vapour is absorbed by moist iron borings, creating iron bromide, which is then used to produce potassium bromide.
*   **The Periodic Process:** This involves the interaction of a bromide, sulphuric acid, and manganese dioxide (pyrolusite) in sandstone stills heated to 60° C. If calcium chloride is present in the bittern, potassium chlorate is recommended as a substitute for pyrolusite.

Commercial bromine often contains impurities such as bromoform, hydrobromic acid, and chlorine. Purification is achieved through repeated shaking with ferrous, sodium, or potassium bromide followed by redistillation. Other methods include shaking with water to remove chlorine and distillation with mercuric oxide or pure manganese dioxide to remove hydrobromic acid.

## Chemical and Industrial Applications
Bromine's chemical properties are generally intermediate between those of iodine and chlorine. It reacts energetically with potassium and, when heated to 200° C, with sodium. In organic chemistry, it is used as an oxidizing and substituting agent. In analytical chemistry, bromine water is utilized for its oxidizing action, and both bromine and bromine water are used to bleach organic colouring matters.

Beyond medicine, bromine salts are used in photography, particularly bromide of silver. For antiseptic use, "bromum solidificatum" is prepared by impregnating a substance like kieselguhr with bromine (approximately 75% of its weight).

## Medicinal Use of Bromides
In medicine, bromine is largely employed as sodium, potassium, and ammonium bromides. These are used as sedatives for the nervous system, hypnotics, and standard anaphrodisiacs. They are applied in cases of:
*   Mania and morbid mental excitement.
*   Hyperaesthesia, neuralgia, and hysteria.
*   Insomnia, delirium tremens, and asthma.
*   Whooping-cough (specifically ammonium bromide, which provides sedation while a stimulant is also indicated).
*   Migraine, laryngismus stridulus, and certain nervous disorders of the heart.
*   Strychnine poisoning, nymphomania, and spermatorrhoea.
*   Symptoms attendant upon the climacteric in women.

Hydrobromic acid is specifically used to prevent or relieve the singing in the ears and headaches that can follow the administration of salicylates or quinine.

## Treatment of Epilepsy
Bromides are considered the specific remedy for epilepsy. Epilepsy, known in ancient times by various names such as *morbus sacer*, *morbus divus*, and *morbus comitialis*, manifests in three well-marked varieties: Jacksonian epilepsy, *le petit mal*, and *le grand mal*. The latter is generally known as the "epileptic fit."

Bromide of potassium is described as the most rapid and certain in its action among the three common bromides, though it may depress the heart in morbid states. In such instances, the sodium salt may be used as the base is inert. For grave cases of epilepsy (*grand mal*), doses of ten to fifty grains or more may be administered and continued for long periods without ill effect.
