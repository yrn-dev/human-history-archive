# Mechanical Stoking

Mechanical stoking refers to the systems used to feed fuel into boilers, serving as an alternative to hand-firing. While most boilers are hand-fired, that system is characterized by frequent openings of the furnace doors and a requirement for significant labor, both of which allow large quantities of cold air to enter above the fires. Although various mechanical stoking systems have been tested, none have been found entirely free from objections.

## The Chain-Grate Stoker
The most commonly employed mechanical stoking system is the "chain-grate" stoker. In this arrangement, the grate is composed of a wide, endless chain made of short cast-iron bars. This chain passes over drums located at the front and back of the boiler. Through the slow rotation of these drums, the grate travels very slowly from the front to the back.

The fuel used in this system is coal that has been broken small. It is fed from a hopper across the entire width of the grate. The thickness of the fire is managed by a door that can be lowered or raised as needed. This process allows the volatile portions of the coal to be distilled at the front of the fire before passing over the incandescent fuel at the back. The speed of the grate's travel is regulated so that combustion is nearly complete by the time the fuel reaches the back end. Because the fire becomes thinner toward the rear, vanes actuated from the front of the boiler are used to prevent too much air from entering the thin portion of the fire.

## Boiler Fuel and Combustion
While the chain-grate is used for coal, other fuel types require different furnace arrangements. For liquid fuel, it has been found that removing the fire-bars typically used for coal produces a better result than keeping them in place and protecting them with a layer of broken fire-brick. Boilers designed for liquid fuel benefit from longer and narrower boiler tubes and larger combustion chambers than those intended for coal alone.

The combustion process is further influenced by the type of draught used:
*   **Natural Draught:** Produced by a chimney, this allows for the combustion of approximately 15 to 20 lb. of coal per square foot of grate area per hour.
*   **Forced Draught:** Common in marine installations, this uses fans to drive air. In "closed stokehold" systems, fans maintain air pressure in the stokehold above that of the outside atmosphere. In the "closed ashpit" or Howden system, fans drive air through tubes in the uptake, heating the air before delivering it into the ashpit and a casing around the furnace front. Forced draught allows for much higher combustion rates, ranging from 20 to 35 lb. per square foot in large vessels, and up to 70 or 80 lb. per square foot in express boilers used in craft such as torpedo boats.

## Boiler Construction and Specialization
The manufacture of boilers has moved toward specialization, with firms restricting their product ranges to increase volume. This shift is driven by the growth of machine tools and the diminution of hand work. Repetitive operations are now handled by specialized machines and a system of templeting. For example, if twenty or fifty similar boilers are produced annually, every stay, flange, hole, and plate is made identical. Drilling machines with self-acting mechanisms can pitch and drill plates without the need for manual marking.

Another area of mechanical advancement is flanging—the bending of plate edges to create unions. This has replaced the older use of angle iron rings, which were prone to explosions and grooving due to a lack of elasticity. Modern shops use hydraulic presses with massive dies to bend entire flanges in a single squeeze for plates up to 8 feet in diameter. For marine boiler ends that are too large for this method, a special press bends edges in successive heats, while Adamson seams are rolled over in a special machine using revolving rollers.

## Boiler Accessories and Maintenance
To function correctly, boilers require specific mountings to ensure the water level remains above the highest heating surfaces. While some water-tube and land boilers use automatic regulation via a float mechanism, most rely on hand regulation. The water level is monitored using a glass water-gauge—consisting of a glass tube and three cocks—and three small try-cocks positioned above, below, and at the proper water level.

Feeding the boiler is achieved through an injector, an independent steam-pump, or a pump driven by the main engine. Feed-water enters through a check-valve, which is kept on its seating by boiler pressure when the pump is inactive and is regulated by a hand-wheel and screw.
