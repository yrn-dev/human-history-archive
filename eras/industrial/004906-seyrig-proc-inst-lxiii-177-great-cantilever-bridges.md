# The Great Cantilever Bridges

Cantilever bridges represent a specialized system of engineering that allows for spans greater than those achievable with independent girders. By utilizing the cantilever principle, large spans are effectively reduced to smaller spans in terms of the stresses involved. This system is often the simplest and most economical method for bridging in specific locations.

## Construction Methods and Advantages
A primary advantage of the cantilever system is the method of erection. These bridges can be built out from the piers, member by member, which eliminates the need for temporary scaffolding below the structure. This process provides several logistical benefits: it prevents the interruption of navigation, reduces the costs associated with scaffolding, and overcomes the difficulties inherent in building in deep water.

The construction process typically involves building out from the abutments, where the completed sections serve as an erecting stage for lifting appliances. In some instances, wire cables are stretched across the span to suspend lifting tackle. Because of the straining action that occurs during this process, engineers must study these stresses and add material to resist them. Once the cantilevers are in place, the center girder may be lifted from the water level or rolled into position upon the cantilevers.

In certain designs, such as those proposed by J.A.L. Waddell, simple independent spans are created by building them out as cantilevers and then converting them into independent girders after the erection is complete. This involves erecting side spans on staging and anchoring them to the piers, then using temporary members to build out the center span from both sides.

## Structural Principles
The cantilever system addresses the risks associated with continuous girder bridges. In continuous girders, the settlement of supports can seriously alter the points of contrary flexure—where the bending moment changes sign—thereby altering the distribution and magnitude of stresses. By introducing hinges or joints at these points of contrary flexure, the bending moment becomes zero, removing ambiguity regarding the stresses.

## Notable Examples

### The Forth Bridge
Begun in 1882 and completed in 1889, the Forth bridge was constructed at a narrowing of the Forth where the channel is divided by the island of Inchgarvie. The original plan for a stiffened suspension bridge was abandoned following the fall of the Tay bridge in 1879. 

The bridge has a total length of 5,330 ft. and utilizes 38,000 tons of steel (excluding approach viaducts). Its structure consists of:
* A central tower on Inchgarvie (260 ft.)
* Fife and Queensferry piers (145 ft. each)
* Six cantilevers (680 ft. each)
* Two central girders between cantilevers (350 ft. each)

The two main spans each measure 1,710 ft., with towers reaching a maximum height of 361 ft. above high water and a clear headway of 157 ft. To balance the rolling load, the weight of the central girder, and an additional 200 tons, the outer ends of the shore cantilevers are loaded. The bridge features an internal viaduct of lattice girders for a double line of rails and includes provisions for wind pressure, distortion caused by the sun, and longitudinal expansion due to temperature changes.

### The Lansdowne Bridge
Completed in 1889 over the Indus at Sukkur, the Lansdowne bridge was designed by Sir A. Rendel for the Indian government. It supports a single railway line with a 5 ft. 6 in. gauge. The bridge has a clear span of 790 ft. and a suspended girder 200 ft. in length. The distance between the centers of the end uprights is 820 ft.

The most heavily strained components are the back guys, which are designed for a stress of 1,200 tons. This stress results from the wind pressure, the rolling load on half the bridge, the weight of the cantilever, and half the weight of the center girder. The anchors, consisting of angle bars and steel plates, are buried in concrete; each anchor plate measures 32 ft. by 12 ft.

### Other Cantilever Structures
Other significant examples include:
* **The Niagara Bridge:** Completed in 1883 for two railway lines, featuring a total length of 910 ft. and a clear span of 495 ft. between towers.
* **The Red Rock Cantilever Bridge:** Located over the Colorado river with a center span of 660 ft.
