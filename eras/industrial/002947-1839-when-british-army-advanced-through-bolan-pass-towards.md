# The Bolan Pass and the British Advance of 1839

The Bolan Pass is a significant route on the Baluch frontier that connects Sibi and Jacobabad with Quetta. Due to its location, it has historically held an important position in British military campaigns directed toward Afghanistan. The geography of the pass is characterized by extreme conditions; in summer, temperatures are very high, while winter brings extreme cold and destructive ice-cold winds near the head of the pass. Notable landmarks include the Sir-i-Bolan, where limestone rocks narrow the passage to a width that barely allows three persons to ride abreast, and the Khundilani gorge, where conglomerate cliffs rise to 800 feet.

## The British Advance and the Conflict at Kalat

In March 1839, a British force under the command of Sir Willoughby Cotton advanced through the Bolan Pass. This movement was part of a larger campaign against Dost Mahommed Khan, the founder of the Barakzai dynasty in Afghanistan. Dost Mahommed had previously sought an alliance with England, but after Lord Auckland, the governor-general, demanded that the amir abandon attempts to recover Peshawar and place his foreign policy under British guidance, Dost Mahommed renewed relations with Russia. Consequently, British troops were set in motion against him. By April 26, 1839, the British force reached Kandahar.

During this advance, the conduct of Mehrab Khan, the ruler of Baluchistan, was perceived by the British as treacherous and dangerous. This perception led to the decision that retribution was required from the chieftain to establish future security in the region. General Willshire was detached from the army of the Indus with 1,050 men to assault Kalat. The British used field-pieces to knock in a gate, storming the citadel and town within a few minutes. The assault resulted in the deaths of over 400 Baluches, including Mehrab Khan, and the capture of 2,000 prisoners.

## Political Intrigue and Aftermath

Subsequent inquiries revealed that the treachery attributed to Mehrab Khan was actually the work of his vizier, Mahommed Hussein, and certain allied chiefs. These individuals had instigated the plundering of British convoys as they passed through the Bolan Pass and Kach Gandava. The vizier manipulated "too credulous" British political officers into blaming Mehrab Khan in an attempt to ruin his master and seize power for himself, knowing that Mehrab's successor was a child.

The political situation in Kalat remained unstable following the assault. In the year following the death of Mehrab Khan, the British-established governor and a feeble garrison were overpowered, and Kalat changed hands. By the end of that same year, General Nott reoccupied the area for the British. In 1841, the British recognized Nasir Khan II, the youthful son of Mehrab Khan, before eventually evacuating the country.

## Relations with the Marri Tribe

The Marri, a Baluch tribe located on the Dera Ghazi Khan border of Baluchistan, also clashed with the British. Their hostilities began in 1840 when they attacked the communications of Sir John Keane's army after it had traversed the Bolan. A British attempt to punish the tribe failed disastrously; Major Clibborn was repulsed while attempting to storm the Naffusak Pass, resulting in 179 killed and 92 wounded out of a force of 650, with many others dying from thirst and heat. Additionally, the fort of Kahan was forced to capitulate.

The relationship between the Marris and the British fluctuated over the following decades. The tribe joined the British against the Bugtis in 1845 and was later pacified through the policies of Sir Robert Sandeman and General John Jacob. However, during the second Afghan War in 1880, the Marris again raided British communications and plundered a treasure convoy. This led to a march by 3,070 British troops under Brigadier-General Macgregor, after which the tribe submitted, provided hostages, and paid 1¼ lakh (£12,500) from a fine of 2 lakhs (£20,000).

## Administrative and Infrastructure Development

The Bolan route was brought directly under British control following the treaty of Gandamak in 1879. The area became part of British Baluchistan, a province leased from Kalat and administered as part of British India. The Bolan Pass is one of the directly administered districts, totaling 36,401 square miles.

The British selected the Bolan route for the first alignment of the Sind-Pishin railway. Originally, the line ran from Sibi south-west to Rindli and followed the Bolan stream. However, due to the destructive nature of floods, this alignment was abandoned. The railway was rerouted through the Mashkaf valley, eventually joining the Bolan at Mach. While an alternative route through the Harnai valley was later adopted for ordinary traffic, the Bolan loop remains reserved for emergencies. Since the founding of the Quetta agency in 1877, the pass has been secured from Baluch marauders, specifically the Marris.
