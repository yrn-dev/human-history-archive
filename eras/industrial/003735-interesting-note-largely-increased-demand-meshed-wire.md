# Meshed Wire and Fencing Trends

The demand for meshed wire field fencing has seen a significant increase in the United States, particularly along railway lines and in more thickly settled regions. This growth in demand began in 1899 and has continued annually. Several factors contributed to this shift, including the high cost and scarcity of labor, as well as a decline in the construction of rail fences.

## Comparison with Barbed Wire
For a period, barbed wire was the dominant fencing material, especially among American farmers in the west and for railroad use. Barbed wire was originally developed to address the needs of the cattle-raising industry in western states where timber was scarce. Before its invention, No. 9 round or oval iron wire was used, but it was prone to breaking due to temperature-induced stretching and contracting, and cattle often loosened posts by rubbing against the smooth wire.

While barbed wire remained popular, meshed wire has been substituted with advantage in several areas. Many consider meshed wire to be a superior enclosure for small animals, such as hogs and sheep. To ensure greater stability, the fabric of meshed wire has been manufactured using wires of a larger diameter than in previous versions.

Production statistics for the United States illustrate the rise of these materials. Barbed wire production grew from 5 tons in 1874 to 200,000 tons by 1900, reaching 250,000 tons by 1907. In contrast, meshed field fencing production was recorded at 50,000 tons in 1900, before increasing sharply to 425,000 tons by 1907.

## Specialized Applications of Wire Netting
Beyond field fencing, wire netting is utilized in various specialized constructions:

*   **Aviaries:** For the construction of bird enclosures, wire-netting is stretched over a framework of iron (such as stout gas-piping) or creosoted/painted wood. To prevent the entry of vermin like weasels, stoats, and rats, the netting is sunk 2 feet into the ground and turned outwards for an additional foot. While interlinked wire is available and looks neater, the old mesh type is considered more durable. Mesh sizes of half or five-eighths of an inch are generally recommended as they are practically vermin-proof.
*   **Naval Warfare:** In maritime contexts, loose wire nets with very large meshes that are not fastened at the ends are used to entangle and clog submarines.
*   **Agriculture:** In the training of hops, wires are stretched between permanent upright poles at heights of 1/2 foot and 6 feet, with a third wire along the tops of the posts. This system, which uses hooks and coco-nut-fibre strings, is considered cheaper in the long run than traditional poling.

## Manufacturing and Industrial Context
The production of wire products has seen substantial economic growth. In Illinois, for example, the value of wire products increased from $2,879,188 in 1900 to $14,099,566 in 1905, an increase of nearly 390%.

The manufacturing process for barbed wire involves using soft Siemens-Martin or Bessemer steel. The wire is drawn in a mill and undergoes a continuous galvanizing process to protect it from atmospheric corrosion. This involves passing the wire through an annealing medium (heated fire-brick tubes or red-hot lead), a muriatic acid bath to remove scale, and finally a bath of spelter. Alternatively, some fencing is painted, in which case the galvanizing process is skipped and the finished wire is submerged in paint.

## International Usage
While barbed wire is ubiquitous among American farmers, its use is less extensive in other countries. Argentina and Australia utilize barbed wire on a comparatively large scale, but both countries use a higher quantity of plain wire fence. In Argentina, there is a specific demand for high-carbon oval fence wire of great strength to meet local conditions.
