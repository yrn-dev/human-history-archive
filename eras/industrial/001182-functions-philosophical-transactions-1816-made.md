# Functions and Administrative Roles

In the context of historical governance and academic contribution, the term "functions" refers both to the specific duties discharged by officials within a political system and to mathematical concepts explored in scientific literature.

## Administrative Functions in the Tokugawa Bakufu

The organization of the Tokugawa Bakufu reached a state of competence and consistency under the third shogun, Iemitsu, though the foundations of its administrative functions were established by Ieyasu following the battle of Sekigahara. The system included a cabinet of seniors known as *roju*, who discharged the functions generally associated with ministers of State. Among these, the *dairo* (great senior) served as a counterpart to a Western prime minister.

Below the *roju* were junior ministers, followed by a hierarchy of specialized administrators, including:
*   Administrators of accounts.
*   Municipal administrators (*machi bugyo*), including those established in Kyoto.
*   Inspectors.
*   Administrators of shrines and temples (*jisha-bugyo*).

The *jisha-bugyo* were responsible for supervising all affairs involving nuns, bonzes, Shinto officials, temples, shrines, and the persons residing within those domains. Additionally, they discharged judicial functions for these specific classes. Originally numbering three, the *jisha-bugyo* were later increased to four, with each transacting business in succession for one month at a time. Furthermore, the *soshaban*, who were permitted to make direct reports to the shogun, were required to fill the office of *jisha-bugyo* in addition to their functions regarding etiquette and ceremony.

State business was conducted in the *Go-Yo-beya*, a chamber located near the shogun's sitting-room where senior and junior ministers assembled. While administrative functions were centralized, judicial business initially lacked a dedicated space until the establishment of the *Hyojo-sho* (court of justice) at Tatsunokuchi in Yedo.

## Mathematical Functions and Scientific Contributions

In the field of mathematics, the study of functions and symbolic calculus has been a recurring theme in the publications of the Royal Society and other scientific bodies.

### Charles Babbage
Charles Babbage (1792-1871), an English mechanician and mathematician, contributed three papers on the "Calculus of Functions" to the *Philosophical Transactions* between 1815 and 1817. Babbage sought to improve mathematical instruction in England, specifically working to replace Newtonian notation with Leibnitzian notation in infinitesimal calculus. His interest in the inaccuracies of computation tables led him to develop the principles of a calculating engine, a project that received government aid and private funding. Although he later desired to evolve this into an "analytical engine," the original calculating engine was never completed.

### Other Mathematical Contributors
Several other scholars utilized the *Philosophical Transactions* and similar journals to advance mathematical theory:
*   **De Morgan**: His memoirs on the "Foundation of Algebra" in the *Cambridge Philosophical Transactions* contributed to the philosophy of mathematical method and influenced Sir W. Rowan Hamilton's work on quaternions. De Morgan also wrote on symbolic calculus in *Trigonometry and Double Algebra* (1849).
*   **Boole**: A prolific author of abstruse mathematical papers, Boole published six important memoirs in the *Philosophical Transactions* of the Royal Society, as well as contributions to the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* and the *Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh*.
*   **Abraham de Moivre**: His papers were included in the *Philosophical Transactions*, and he is noted for the *Doctrine of Chances, or Method of Calculating the Probabilities of Events at Play*.
