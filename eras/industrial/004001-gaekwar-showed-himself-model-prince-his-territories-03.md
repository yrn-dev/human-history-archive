# The Gaekwars of Baroda

The Gaekwars were the rulers of the native state of Baroda and represented one of the primary branches of the Mahratta confederacy. During the 18th century, this confederacy was responsible for spreading terror and devastation across India.

## Origins and Early History
The house was established around 1721 when Pilaji Gaekwar carved out a fertile portion of territory within Gujarat. Following this acquisition, the peshwa granted him the title of "Leader of the Royal Troops." However, during the final thirty-two years of the 18th century, the house was destabilized by bitter and unappeasable family feuds, a pattern noted as the ruin of many great Indian families.

## British Relations and Administration
In 1800, the inheritance of the state passed to a prince who was described as almost idiotic in mind and feeble in body. To protect this hereditary ruler from various claimants, British troops were deployed. This led to the signing of a treaty in 1802, which secured the prince's dependence on the British government and his independence from the peshwa.

By 1805, these arrangements were consolidated into a systematic administrative plan for the Baroda territory. Under this system, the prince maintained a revenue of three-quarters of a million sterling. While he remained practically dependent on subsidiary British troops to keep him on his throne, he was granted perfect independence regarding all internal matters.

## Governance and Political Stability
The history of the Gaekwar dynasty was characterized by inconsistency in leadership. While the state occasionally benefited from an able prince or minister, it was more frequently marked by a long list of incompetent heads, venal advisers, and taskmasters who were oppressive to the population.

These internal tensions reached a climax in the late 19th century. In 1873, an English committee of inquiry was formed to investigate complaints of oppression against the reigning Gaekwar, Malhar Rao. Malhar Rao had ascended to the throne after spending a significant period in prison, held there by his brother, the previous Gaekwar.

The situation deteriorated in 1874 when an attempt was made to poison the British resident. Malhar Rao was formally accused of this crime and tried by a mixed commission. Although the 1875 trial failed to produce a unanimous verdict regarding the poisoning charge, the viceroy, Lord Northbrook, deposed Malhar Rao on the grounds of gross misgovernment. Following this deposition, the widow of the predecessor, Khande Rao, was permitted to adopt an heir from the descendants of the founder.

## Regional Context and Influence
The state of Baroda is located within the Bombay Presidency, a province of British India. The presidency consists of both British districts and native states. Baroda is noted as a native state that maintains direct relations with the government of India.

The influence of the Gaekwars extended to other nearby native states through financial tributes. For example, the native state of Idar (or Edar), which is part of the Mahi Kantha agency in the Gujarat division of Bombay, paid a tribute of £2,000 to the Gaekwar of Baroda.
