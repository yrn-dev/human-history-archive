# Boiler and Plating Shops

The boiler and plating shops, along with bridge and girder shops, share similar materials and methods of construction, primarily utilizing iron and steel. While they are often relegated to separate departments due to divergences in practice, they are closely linked by their reliance on the working of rolled sections and plates, which predominate over the bars and rods used in the smithy.

## Industrial Specialization and Mechanization
The boiler and plating shops have been affected by a general tendency toward the specialization of manufactures. Firms now frequently restrict their range of products to increase volume, ending the era when a single shop could produce several classes of boilers while also undertaking girder and bridge work. This shift is driven by the growth of machine tools and the diminution of hand work.

Modern production relies on highly specialized machines and a system of templeting for repetitive operations. For sets of twenty or fifty similar boilers, components such as flanges, holes, and plates are made identical. Dimensions are marked from sample plates, or in many instances, self-acting drilling machines pitch and drill plates without any prior marking. Similarly, bracing bars for girders and bridges are cut and punched from templet bars, allowing them to be installed without further fitting or adjustment.

## Construction Methods and Materials
The introduction of mild steel in place of malleable iron has significantly influenced construction practices. While iron is less susceptible to irregular heating or overheating, steel offers higher ductility, though it is more prone to cracking if treated improperly.

Key differences in construction include:
*   **Flanging:** This process—bending the edges of a plate to join it to another—is central to boilermaking (used for tube-plates, furnace flues, and end-plates) but is scarcely used in bridge and girder work.
*   **Bending:** Plates are bent into cylindrical shapes for furnaces and shells in boilermaking, a practice not found in girder work.
*   **Welding:** Welding is more common in boiler work, particularly for stand pipes and furnace flues. In large shops, steam-driven power hammers are used to close welds.
*   **Joining:** In boiler work, holes are generally drilled through the seams of adjacent plates, whereas in bridge work, plates or bars are usually drilled or punched individually.

## Flanging and Riveting Processes
Flanging has superseded the older use of angle iron rings, which were a source of explosions and grooving in steam boilers due to a lack of elasticity. Modern flanging uses a large radius to counteract temperature changes. While skilled angle ironsmiths still perform hand-flanging in small shops by heating and hammering edges around iron blocks, modern shops use hydraulic presses with massive dies to bend entire flanges in a single squeeze. Special machines are also used to roll over the flanges of Adamson seams.

Riveting has also transitioned from hand-work to the use of pneumatic and hydraulic machines. In boiler shops, these machines are typically fixed, while bridge and girder shops use portable machines slung from chains and powered by compressed air or pressure water.

## Boiler Design and Specifications
Boilers must be constructed to withstand high pressures and temperatures, requiring materials that combine strength with high ductility.

### Locomotive Boilers
These consist of a cylindrical barrel and a nearly rectangular fire-box. The fire-box sides are secured to the shell by stays approximately 1 inch in diameter, pitched 4 inches apart. Depending on the size, the fire-box crown is supported by either longitudinal girder stays or vertical stays. These boilers typically operate at pressures between 175 lb. and 200 lb. per sq. in.

### Marine Boilers
The cylindrical multitubular return tube boiler is widely used in merchant steamers, with diameters up to 17 feet and working pressures generally between 160 and 200 lb. per sq. in. They are categorized as:
*   **Single-ended:** Furnaces are fitted at one end only (usually three, but ranging from one to four).
*   **Double-ended:** Furnaces are fitted at both ends.

Two types of furnace connections exist: one where the furnace is flanged at the crown and riveted to the tube plate, and the "Gourlay back end," where the furnace is contracted into an oval conical form and flanged outwards. The Gourlay design allows a damaged furnace to be replaced without disturbing the end plates, though it requires more longitudinal stays for stiffness.

## Material Composition
The choice of material varies by boiler type and component:
*   **Land and Marine Boilers:** Plates and stays are almost always mild steel. Shell plates and long stays use steel with a tensile strength of 28 to 32 tons per sq. in., while flanged plates and furnaces use a softer steel (26 to 30 tons per sq. in.). Tubes are usually lap-welded wrought iron, though naval water-tube boilers use seamless steel.
*   **Locomotive Boilers:** Shells are generally mild steel. Fire-box plates are copper (or steel in America), and fire-box side stays are copper or special bronze. Tubes are typically brass (composed of copper and zinc), though steel or copper is occasionally used. Water-tubes are made of seamless steel.
