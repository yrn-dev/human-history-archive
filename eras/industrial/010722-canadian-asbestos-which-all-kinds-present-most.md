# Canadian Asbestos

Canadian asbestos is currently the most industrially important variety of asbestos. It is found within the province of Quebec in a small belt of serpentine, primarily located near Thetford and Black Lake. This resource was first identified as having commercial value around 1877.

## Extraction and Processing
The production of Canadian asbestos involves several mechanical stages. The rock is generally quarried and then cobbed by hand. If necessary, the material is dried before being crushed in rock-breakers and passed between rollers. To achieve a finer state of division, the material is processed through "fiberizers." Finally, the loosened fibres are sorted and graded using a shaking screen, though the specific process may vary between different mills.

## Mineralogical Characteristics
The Canadian variety is a fibrous form of serpentine known mineralogically as chrysotile. This differs from the amphibole-asbestos (or hornblende-asbestos), such as tremolite or anthophyllite, which was more commonly used in the arts in the past. 

Serpentine-asbestos typically occurs in narrow veins. Its fibres are shorter than those of amphibole-asbestos—reaching only 2 or 3 inches in length, compared to Italian amphibole fibres which can exceptionally reach 5 or 6 feet—but they possess great tensile strength. These fibres are characterized by a delicate silky lustre, high elasticity and flexibility, and a yellowish or greenish colour. In contrast, amphibole-asbestos is usually grey or white and is often brittle and harsh. Both types of mineral share similar properties regarding their resistance to heat.

## Industrial and Commercial Applications
The economic value of asbestos is derived from its low thermal conductivity, its ability to withstand high temperatures, and its partial resistance to acid attacks. Because of these properties, it is utilized in the following ways:

*   **Industrial Equipment:** It is used as a filtering medium for corrosive liquids and for the jacketing of steam-pipes and boilers. It also serves as an electric insulator.
*   **Machinery and Construction:** Asbestos is employed as packing for stopcocks, glands, and joints in machinery. It is manufactured into millboard, felt, and yarn. For building purposes, fire-proof felt and sheathing are used for roofing and flooring.
*   **Safety and Specialized Gear:** It is used to create fire-proof curtains for the stage and clothing for firemen. It is also used for packing safes.
*   **Materials:** Asbestos is an ingredient in fire-proof paints, plasters, and cements. When mixed with fire-clay, it is formed into balls for gas-stoves.

A specific Canadian product known as "Asbestic" is created by mixing lime with crushed serpentine rock that contains thin seams of asbestos to produce a plaster.

## Historical Context and Other Varieties
The term "asbestos" comes from the Greek word for "unquenchable" or "incombustible," referring to the mineral's resistance to fire. Historically, it was a rare curiosity spun into fabrics; for example, Charlemagne is said to have owned an asbestos tablecloth that could be purified by throwing it into a fire. In 1676, a merchant from China displayed a handkerchief of *linum asbesti* (or "salamander's wool") to the Royal Society. The Eskimos of Labrador have used the material as lamp-wicks, a practice mirrored in some ancient sacred lamps.

While Canadian serpentine is currently dominant, other varieties exist globally. Amphibole-asbestos is found in the United States (including Georgia's Sall Mountain, as well as Vermont, Virginia, Massachusetts, Arizona, and South Carolina). Other sources include Italy and Corsica, the Asbestos Mountains in Cape Colony (yielding blue crocidolite), and limited deposits in Russia, Hungary, Tirol, New Zealand, Queensland, New South Wales, Ireland, and North Wales.
