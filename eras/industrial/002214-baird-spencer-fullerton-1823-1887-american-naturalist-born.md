# BAIRD, SPENCER FULLERTON (1823-1887)

## Early Life and Education
Spencer Fullerton Baird was an American naturalist born on February 3, 1823, in Reading, Pennsylvania. He attended Dickinson College in Carlisle, Pennsylvania, where he graduated in 1840. In the year following his graduation, Baird undertook an ornithological excursion through the mountains of Pennsylvania. According to one of his biographers, this journey involved walking 60 miles on the final day and a total of 400 miles over the course of twenty-one days.

## Academic Career and Scientific Interests
Baird's scientific focus became largely ornithological after he met J. J. Audubon in 1838, an encounter that resulted in Audubon providing him with a portion of his own bird collection. Although he studied medicine for a period, Baird transitioned into academia in 1845 when he was appointed professor of natural history at Dickinson College. During his tenure there, he also took on the responsibilities of the chair of chemistry and provided instruction in mathematics and physiology. This variety of academic duties contributed to a breadth of scientific interest that defined his career and established him as perhaps the most representative general man of science in America.

## Leadership at the Smithsonian and Government Service
Baird held a significant leadership role at the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, serving as assistant-secretary for the long period between 1850 and 1878. Following the death of Joseph Henry, he ascended to the position of secretary. In addition to his work at the Smithsonian, Baird served as the U.S. Commissioner of Fish and Fisheries from 1871 until his death.

Between 1850 and 1860, Baird provided general superintendence for several government expeditions tasked with the scientific exploration of the western territories of the United States. To support these efforts, he prepared a manual titled *Instructions to Collectors*.

## Research and Publications
As an officer of the Smithsonian, Baird supervised workers across many different fields, and his own research and writings reflected this diversity. His published work covered a wide range of disciplines, including:
* Botany
* Anthropology
* Geology
* Mineralogy
* Iconography
* General zoology
* Ornithology

For several years, Baird edited an annual volume that summarized progress across all lines of scientific investigation. A bibliography compiled by G. Brown Goode covering the years 1843 to the end of 1882 lists 1,063 entries for Baird's publications. The majority of these, 775 entries, were short articles appearing in his *Annual Record*. Among his most important volumes was *Birds*, which was part of a series of reports regarding explorations and surveys for a railway route.
