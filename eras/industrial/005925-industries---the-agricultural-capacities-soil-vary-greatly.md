# Industries and Agricultural Capacities

The agricultural capacities of soil vary significantly across different geographic localities, influencing the types of crops grown, the prevalence of livestock, and the development of local industries.

## Soil Composition and Fertility
Soil quality ranges from extremely fertile to barren depending on the region. In some areas, such as the Vale of Aylesbury near the headwaters of the Thame, the soil is extremely fertile on lower lands but poor and thin on the hills. Similarly, the "rich coast" of Costa Rica possesses a wonderfully fertile soil combined with moisture and sunshine, allowing for the cultivation of almost any flower or fruit. In the San Jose plateau, the soil consists of layers of black or dark-brown volcanic ash ranging from 1 to 6 yards in depth.

Other regions exhibit more varied soil types. One county's soils are categorized into four types: calcareous in limestone districts, deep mellow loams in less elevated sandstone districts, light shallow soils, and moorland or peat soils with a coarse retentive clay substratum; approximately one-sixth of this area is quite barren. In other territories, the soil is generally a light reddish loam, though the eastern plains feature a heavy black loam. In some districts, the soil is thin and cold in granite, quartz, and mica slate areas, while it is warm and friable on carboniferous limestone. 

In the Blue Grass Region, the best soils are alluvium in bottom-lands and limestone-derived soil rich in phosphorus and organic matter, particularly within a 20-mile radius of Lexington. Conversely, the Highland Rim Plateau and lowlands west of the Tennessee river commonly feature sandy loam or clay containing carbonate of lime, while mountain and Alleghany Plateau soils are often light and thin.

## Crop Production and Land Use
Agricultural output is dictated by soil and climate. In regions where wheat and oats are the principal grains, there has been a noted decrease in their importance in some areas. In other locations, oats remain the predominant crop, with barley acreage maintained by the demands of distillers. In north Derbyshire, harsh climates and poor soil prevent grain from flourishing at higher altitudes, whereas the south produces heavy crops of wheat, turnips, and cereals. Barley is specifically cultivated near Repton and Gresley to supply the Burton breweries.

In tropical and coastal regions, production is more diverse. Maranhão's river valleys and coastal zones are devoted to tobacco, mandioca, coffee, rice, cotton, sugar-cane, and various fruits. Costa Rica produces coffee (first planted in 1838), bananas, rice, maize, barley, potatoes, and beans in the interior, while the warm coast-lands produce indigo, cotton, sugar-cane, vanilla, and cocoa. In other settler-dominated higher regions, European settlers produce large crops of potatoes, as well as coffee, wheat, barley, and oats.

## Livestock and Stock-Farming
Stock-farming is a primary industry in many regions. In the Vale of Aylesbury, cattle and sheep are reared for London markets, alongside ducks for which the district is famous. In Derbyshire, the Trent valley is largely permanent pasture for dairy-farming and cattle-feeding, with Derbyshire cheeses exported or sent to London. In other areas, the number of cattle, sheep, and poultry is rising as dairies become more numerous, with Cork butter maintaining a high standing in foreign and English markets.

Some regions face specific challenges in stock-raising. In south-east Costa Rica, farmers must occasionally retreat before thousands of migratory vampires capable of bleeding a strong animal to death in one night. Similarly, in the southern highlands of Maranhão, the industry has declined due to vampire bats, troublesome insects, and a failure to introduce new blood into degenerated herds. In other regions, livestock improvement is managed through the state importation of swine, sheep, cattle, and horses from the United States and Europe.

## Manufacturing and Mineral Industries
While many of these regions are essentially agricultural, some maintain localized manufacturing. In the Aylesbury area, there is no general manufacturing, but local lace-making and straw-plaiting exist. High Wycombe is known for a thriving trade in turnery, producing bowls and chairs from hard woods and beech. Lace-making is attributed to French and Flemish immigrants, as well as Catherine of Aragon during her residence at Ampthill around 1532; lace-makers celebrated "Cattarn's Day" on November 25th until the late 19th century.

Mineral extraction varies by location. In Costa Rica, while silver, copper, iron, zinc, lead, and marble exist, only gold, silver, and copper have been worked; silver and copper mines were abandoned by the beginning of the 20th century, leaving goldfields exploited by American capital. In other territories, copper is found in the Tsavo district, and diamonds have been discovered in the Thika river. Other industries include stone and lime quarries, cotton ginning, and bacon-curing factories.

## Forestry and Land Reclamation
Significant efforts have been made in land reclamation and forestry. In the counties of Nairn, Moray, and Banff, a Seafield planted over 31 million forest trees between 1811 and 1845, including 18,938,224 Scots firs, 11,904,798 larches, and 843,450 hardwoods across 8,223 acres. In Costa Rica, virgin forests along the Atlantic slopes provide an abundance of ebony, rubber, rosewood, mahogany, cedar, and precious dye-woods like fustic.
