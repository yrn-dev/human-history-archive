# Barthelemy and Mery

Auguste Marseille Barthelemy (1796–1867) and J. P. A. Mery (1798–1866) were French satirical poets and collaborators whose professional partnership was so intimate that their individual personalities are difficult to distinguish within their joint works. Both men were compatriots from Marseilles.

## Early Career and Political Satire
Barthelemy first established a local reputation as a poet before moving to Paris. He initially gained the favor of the court with one of his first efforts, *Le Sacre de Charles X*, published in 1825. However, he soon shifted his energies toward the opposition party. 

In 1825, he produced a clever political satire titled *Les Sidiennes*. This was followed by a series of joint productions with Mery in 1827, including *La Corbiereide*, *La Peyronneide*, and *La Villeliade ou la prise du chateau de Rivoli*. The success of these works was immediate and pronounced; *La Villeliade* alone required fifteen editions within a single year.

The duo continued to produce a rapid succession of satires and political squibs. Notable among these were *Biographie des quarante de l'academie francaise* (1826) and *Napoleon en Egypte* (1828), the latter of which passed through nearly a dozen editions in one year.

## Legal Conflict and the Revolution of 1830
The authors' political commentary led to legal repercussions for Barthelemy. In 1829, he was fined 1,000 francs and imprisoned for the publication of *Fils de l'homme*, a poem concerning Napoleon's son, the duke of Reichstadt. 

Barthelemy was liberated by the Revolution of 1830. Following this event, he and Mery celebrated the triumph of the people with one of their most brilliant works, *L'Insurrection*. Their collected works, the *Oeuvres*, were published in four volumes in 1831, featuring a notice by L. Reybaud.

## Later Works and Decline in Influence
From March 1831 to April 1832, Barthelemy and Mery issued a weekly series of verse satires called the *Nemesis*, which attacked the ministers and government of Louis Philippe. As a result of these activities, the small pension Barthelemy received was stopped. When the publication of *Nemesis* ended, suspicions arose that Barthelemy had been paid to remain silent.

In 1832, Barthelemy published an anonymous poem that supported certain government acts that were particularly obnoxious to the Liberal party. This change of political alignment destroyed his influence, and his subsequent writings passed unnoticed. For several years following this shift, he refrained from satirical writing and enjoyed a handsome government pension.

## Historical Interactions
The works of Barthelemy and Mery also intersected with other historical figures. Marshal Grouchy wrote *Fragments historiques relatifs a la campagne et a la bataille de Waterloo* between 1829 and 1830 specifically in reply to Marshal Gerard and to Barthelemy and Mery. Additionally, Jules Garsou wrote a study titled *Barthelemy et Mery etudies specialement dans leurs rapports avec la legende napoleonienne*, which was included in volume lviii of the *Memoires of the Academie Royale ... de Belgique*.
