# Hennequin Haulx

## Overview
Hennequin Haulx was an instrument-maker based in Brussels during the 15th century. He is noted in historical records for his work providing keyed wind instruments to the nobility of the period.

## Work for the Duke of Burgundy
In 1438, Haulx was commissioned by the duke of Burgundy. For this engagement, the duke paid Haulx 4 *ridres* a piece for the construction of three tenor bombards. These specific instruments were distinguished by the application of keys.

## Historical Significance of Keyed Instruments
The work of Haulx represents an early instance of the use of keys for woodwind instruments, a practice known by the early 15th century or perhaps even before. The development of keys was one of two primary methods—the other being the use of valves—adopted to increase the musical possibilities and the compass of wind instruments. 

While Haulx applied keys to the tenor bombard in the 1400s, similar mechanical applications appeared in later centuries for other instruments. By the 16th century, keys were applied to the large tenor cornetto and the bass flûte-à-bec. Much later, in 1770, a Russian imperial band horn-player named Kölbel experimented with keys on the trumpet, followed by Weidinger of Vienna in 1795, who produced a trumpet featuring five keys.

## Evolution of Related Wind Instruments
The trajectory of instrument modification seen in Haulx's work eventually led to the development of various specialized wind instruments. The application of keys to the bugle resulted in the ophicleide and the Kent bugle. The latter was patented in 1810 by Joseph Halliday, the bandmaster of the Cavan militia, as the "Royal Kent Bugle." This instrument featured five keys and a compass of twenty-five notes.

In contrast to the keyed developments, the application of valves led to the creation of the saxhorn family. Valves, consisting of pistons or cylinders, were first applied to the trombone, trumpet, and horn by Heinrich Stolzel, providing these brass instruments with a chromatic compass.
