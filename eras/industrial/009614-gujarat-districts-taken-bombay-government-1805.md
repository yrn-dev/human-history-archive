# The Acquisition of Gujarat Districts by the Bombay Government

The acquisition of the Gujarat districts in 1805 was a pivotal step in the territorial expansion and administrative shaping of the Bombay Presidency. Between 1803 and 1827, the presidency evolved from a limited holding into a vast province of British India. In 1803, the presidency's holdings were restricted to Surat, Bankot, Salsette, and the islands of the harbour. The takeover of the Gujarat districts in 1805, followed by further enlargement in 1818, formed a core part of the presidency's growth.

## Administrative Structure and Geography
The Bombay Presidency was a province consisting of both British districts and native states, managed by a governor. Geographically, it extended from 13 deg. 53' to 28 deg. 45' N. and from 66 deg. 40' to 76 deg. 30' E. It was bounded by the Arabian Sea to the west, Madras and Mysore to the south, Indore, Hyderabad, and the Central Provinces to the east, and Rajputana, the Punjab, and Baluchistan to the north.

The province was organized into four commissionerships: Sind, the southern or Carnatic, the central or Deccan, and the northern division, known as Gujarat. The Gujarat region is characterized by rich, highly cultivated alluvial plains watered by the Nerbudda and Tapti rivers, though its northern parts differ from these plains.

## Territorial Expansion and Governance
Following the 1805 takeover of Gujarat districts, the British government initiated measures for the settlement of Mahi Kantha and Kathiawar between 1807 and 1820. The presidency expanded further after the defeat and capture of Baji Rao, the last of the peshwas, in 1817–1818. This resulted in the inclusion of large portions of his dominions, including Nasik, Poona, Ahmednagar, Belgaum, Sholapur, Dharwar, and Kaladgi.

Mountstuart Elphinstone, who served as governor from 1819 to 1827, completed the settlement of these areas. Elphinstone's administrative policy focused on ruling according to native lines to avoid premature changes for the population, while simultaneously codifying laws, establishing schools and courts, and stopping the grosser abuses of the previous regime.

Later expansions included the addition of Sind in 1843, the addition of Aden in 1839, and the 1853 lease of the Panch Mahals from Sindhia. The presidency also contained the native state of Baroda, which maintained direct relations with the government of India.

## Districts and Native States
The presidency was divided into twenty-six districts. Those located in or associated with the northern and western regions included Ahmedabad, Surat, Broach, Kaira, Panch Mahals, and Thana. Broach, an ancient city on the right bank of the Nerbudda, had a complex history of ownership; it was held by the Mahrattas in subordination to the peshwa until 1772, ceded to Sindhia in 1783, stormed by Colonel Woodington in 1803, and finally ceded to the East India Company by Sindhia under the treaty of Sarji Anjangaom.

In addition to British districts, the presidency included 353 separate native state units. In the north, these were grouped into the Palanpur, Mahi Kantha, Rewa Kantha, and Kathiawar agencies, as well as Cutch and Cambay. South Gujarat included Sachin, Bansda, and Dharampur.

## Economy and Industry
The industrial life of the region was heavily defined by the growth and manufacture of cotton. By 1905, there were 432 factories in the presidency, the majority of which were cotton mills. These were concentrated in Khandesh, Ahmedabad, and Bombay City. Ahmedabad and Surat were specifically noted for their carved wood-work, and Ahmedabad was a center for silk goods and government salt works at Udu and Kharaghoda.

Agricultural prosperity peaked between 1862 and 1867 under Sir Bartle Frere, driven by the American Civil War, which created an enormous demand for Indian cotton in Europe. This period was marked by an epidemic of speculation.

## Social and Health Challenges
The region faced severe hardships toward the end of the 19th century due to famine and disease. The failure of the monsoon in 1899 caused famine in Gujarat. Additionally, bubonic plague broke out in Bombay City in September 1896 and spread to the surrounding country. While a few districts in Gujarat escaped the plague, mortality was heavy in Surat and the native states of Palanpur and Cutch. 

Government efforts to combat the plague included segregation and inoculation. Segregation was often misunderstood by the local population, leading to outbreaks of violence. In 1898, a riot by Mahommedan weavers occurred in Bombay, and the native chairman of the plague committee was murdered by a mob in the Nasik district.
