# The Great Plain and the Bengal Delta

The great plain of northern India is a vast expanse of land extending from the delta of the Indus in the west to the delta of the Ganges and Brahmaputra in the east. Situated between the Indian peninsula's table-land and the Himalayan slope of the Tibetan plateau, this plain includes the valley of Assam and covers an area of approximately 500,000 square miles. It is characterized as one of the most populous, civilized, and richest regions of India. The surface is so consistently composed of finely comminuted deposits that it is possible to travel over 2,000 miles from the Bay of Bengal, through the Punjab, and down the Indus to the sea without encountering a pebble.

## Geography and the Bengal Delta
The region of Bengal, derived from Sanskrit geography, strictly refers to the country stretching from Bhagalpur southwards to the sea. Its southern portion is formed by the delta of the Ganges, while the northern part consists of the Ganges valley. This deltaic region is characterized by alluvial formations; it is noted that no substance as coarse as gravel exists within 400 miles of the river mouths.

Specific districts within this delta illustrate its physical nature. Backergunje, located in the Dacca division of Eastern Bengal and Assam, is a flat country of 4,452 square miles intersected by a network of sluggish, dark-colored streams. The area is so saturated with water-courses and natural hollows filled with aquatic plants that travel is difficult except by boat. To the south of Backergunje, along the coast of the Bay of Bengal, lie the Sundarbans, forest tracts inhabited by leopards, tigers, and other wild beasts. Similarly, the district of Khulna, formed from Jessore in 1882, lies in the middle of the Ganges delta and includes a portion of the Sundarbans seaward fringe of swamps.

## River Systems
The region is defined by two primary river systems: the Ganges and the Brahmaputra. The Brahmaputra, meaning "Son of Brahma," rises in the Tibetan plateau north of the Himalayas. It enters Assam through a series of rapids and waterfalls, including a gorge in the Lakhimpur district known as the Parasuramkunda or Brahmakunda, which is visited annually by thousands of Hindu devotees. After flowing west through the Assam valley, the Brahmaputra turns south, spreading over the alluvial districts of the Bengal delta before ending its 1,800-mile course in the Bay of Bengal.

The valley of the Ganges, divided between Bengal and Eastern Bengal and Assam, is among the most fertile and densely populated areas in the world. It supports a vast array of agricultural products, including jute, tea, indigo, cotton, wheat, turmeric, lac, ginger, pepper, quinine, and the opium-poppy. It also produces various grains, pulses, oil-seeds, and timber, such as the iron-hearted *sal* tree, bamboo, and coroneted palm.

## The Bay of Bengal and Climate
The Bay of Bengal is a triangular portion of the Indian Ocean situated between Burma and India. It receives the waters of the Ganges and Brahmaputra to the north, the Irrawaddy to the east, and the Mahanadi, Cauvery, Kistna, and Godavari to the west. The bay contains numerous islands, including the Mergui, Nicobar, and Andaman groups. In the north-east, off the Burmese coast, the Cheduba group is known for active mud volcanoes; in December 1906, one such volcano created a new island measuring 307 by 217 yards.

The region is subject to revolving storms known as cyclones during the hot months from June to October (and occasionally November). These storms typically originate over the Nicobar and Andaman islands, moving north-west to strike the east coast of the Indian peninsula before passing with extreme violence across the delta of the Ganges.

## Administrative and Historical Terminology
The term "Bengal" has shifted in meaning over time. In ancient Sanskrit times, the Banga kingdom was roughly conterminous with the Bengal delta. During the Mahommedan period, the term "Bangala"—possibly derived from a city near Chittagong—was used by Mussulmans to refer specifically to the Gangetic delta, eventually including conquests east of the Brahmaputra. For fiscal purposes, the Delhi emperor's deputy ruled Bengal as a central province, with Orissa to the south-west and Behar to the north-west.

Under British rule, the term expanded further. The "Bengal Establishment" of the East India Company originally included factories from Patna in Behar to Balasore on the Orissa coast. Eventually, the presidency of Bengal included all British territories north of the Central Provinces, stretching from the Himalayas and the Punjab to the mouths of the Brahmaputra and Ganges.

## Infrastructure and Transport
The seaport of Calcutta serves as the center of the railway system in Bengal. South of the Ganges, the East Indian railway runs from the North-Western Provinces to its terminus at Howrah, with a chord line passing through the Raniganj coalfield to increase economic efficiency. The Bengal-Nagpur railway also terminates at Howrah. North of the Ganges, the Eastern Bengal railway runs to Darjeeling and operates river steamers on the Brahmaputra. The Bengal Central railway serves the lower Gangetic delta. Both the Eastern Bengal and Bengal Central railways have termini at Sealdah, a suburb of Calcutta. Additionally, the Bengal & North-Western railway traverses Northern Behar and extends through Tirhoot.
