# Brewing Industry

## Historical Development in England
Manufacturing-scale beer preparation in England was primarily conducted within monasteries until approximately the 12th or 13th century. The industry transitioned into a specialized trade with the formation of an association of London brewers during the reign of Henry IV, who were subsequently granted a charter in 1445. Following the Reformation, the trade was further expanded as monks from expropriated monasteries joined the ranks of trade brewers.

Until the 18th century, professional brewers—referred to as "brewers for sale"—catered mainly to the masses, while wealthier classes produced their own beer. However, due to other reasons and improved brewing methods, the wealthier classes eventually found it more economical and less troublesome to utilize regular breweries. During this period, the typical charges were approximately 8s. for small beer and 30s. per barrel for bitter ale.

## Industrial Centralization and Scale
The brewing industry in the United Kingdom has seen a marked trend toward centralization. Between 1895 and 1905, the number of private brewers fell from 17,041 to 9,930. About four-fifths of these remaining private brewers were exempted from beer duty, consisting of persons occupying houses with an annual value not exceeding £15, or farmers in houses not exceeding £10 annual value who brewed for their laborers. Private houses subject to both license and beer duty produced fewer than 20,000 barrels annually. It is estimated that over 99% of beer produced in the United Kingdom is brewed by public brewers.

The number of licenses issued to brewers for sale declined from 8,863 in 1894-1895 to 5,164 by 1904-1905, as smaller public brewers were either absorbed by larger concerns or disappeared. By the year ending September 30, 1905, the number of brewers for sale stood at 5,180. Among these, Messrs Guinness owned the largest brewery in the world, producing over two million barrels and paying roughly one million sterling to the revenue. Three other firms produced one million barrels or more.

## Production and Economics (1900–1906)
Production reached a maximum of 37,090,986 barrels in 1900. Although production declined after that peak, the 1905-1906 figure of 34,109,263 barrels remained higher than any year prior to 1897. In the year ending September 30, 1905, the exchequer received £13,156,053 in beer duty.

The materials used in production for the 1905-1906 period included:
*   **Malt:** 51,818,697 bushels
*   **Hops:** 62,360,817 lb
*   **Sugar:** 2,746,615 cwt.
*   **Rice, flaked maize, and similar materials:** 1,348,558 cwt.
*   **Unmalted corn:** 125,671 bushels
*   **Hop substitutes:** 49,202 lb

The average specific gravity of beer produced in 1905-1906 was 1053.24. Regarding trade, 520,826 barrels were exported and 57,194 barrels were imported, figures that had remained nearly stationary for thirty years. Consumption was concentrated in England (28,590,563 barrels), followed by Ireland (3,265,084 barrels) and Scotland (1,648,463 barrels).

Financial investment in the liquor trade in the United Kingdom was calculated at £117,000,000 in 1871, a figure that may have doubled by 1908. A 1906 *Brewers' Almanack* entry valued capital invested in limited liability breweries alone at £185,000,000.

## Brewing Chemistry and Technology
Brewing technology is largely based on physiological chemistry, which governs the growth, harvesting, and conversion of barley into malt, as well as mashing, fermenting, and conditioning. Pure chemistry is applied to the analysis of brewing materials, the composition of worts and beers, and the saline matter in waters.

Scientific focus has been placed on the transformation of starch. While it was once believed that diastase converted starch into dextrin and then into glucose, F.A. Musculus demonstrated in 1860 that sugar and dextrin are produced simultaneously. Between 1872 and 1876, Cornelius O'Sullivan proved the sugar produced was maltose and identified the influence of mash temperature on the resulting products. 

The degradation of starch can be monitored using iodine solution; starch-paste treated with iodine produces a deep blue color. As degradation proceeds, this changes to purple, then reddish, and finally ceases. This process is also marked by a decrease in optical rotating power and an increase in cupric reducing power toward Fehling's solution.
