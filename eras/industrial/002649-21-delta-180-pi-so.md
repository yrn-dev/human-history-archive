# Ballistic Calculations and the Degree Table

In the study of ballistics, specifically regarding the motion of a shot, various tables and functions are utilized to determine the trajectory and the change in direction of motion. One such tool is the degree table, attributed to Sir W. D. Niven, F.R.S., which is used to calculate the change in the direction of motion of a shot as its velocity changes from an initial velocity $V$ to a final velocity $v$, assuming the shot is flying nearly horizontally.

## Theory of Directional Change
The theory of the degree table begins with the assumption that the tangent at a specific point of the trajectory, where the velocity is $v$, forms an angle $i$ (measured in radians) with the horizon. By resolving normally in the trajectory and assuming that air resistance acts tangentially, the relationship is expressed as $v(di/dt) = g \cos i$, where $di$ represents the infinitesimal decrement of $i$ over an infinitesimal increment of time $dt$.

In scenarios involving direct fire, the trajectory is sufficiently flat that $\cos i$ is considered undistinguishable from unity. Under these conditions, the equation simplifies to $v(di/dt) = g$, or $di/dt = g/v$. This leads to the determination that $\Delta i/\Delta t = g/v$.

## Conversion and Calculation of Inclination
To express the change of inclination in degrees rather than radians, the archive provides the conversion formula $\delta/180 = i/\pi$. Using this relationship, the change in inclination in degrees ($\Delta\delta$) is calculated as:
$$\Delta\delta = 180/\pi \Delta i = 180g/\pi \Delta t/v$$

When these variables are applied to a standard projectile, the notation changes to $D$ and $I$. The differences are calculated as $\Delta I = g \Delta T/v = \Delta v/vp$ and $\Delta D = 180g/\pi \Delta T/v$. The total difference in these values between two velocities $V$ and $v$ is expressed as:
$$I(V) - I(v) = \int_{v}^{V} dv/vp \text{ (or the sum } \sum_{v:V} \Delta v/vp)$$
$$D(V) - D(v) = 180/\pi [I(V) - I(v)]$$

These values, $D(v)$ and $I(v)$, are obtained via summation using an arithmometer and entered into respective columns. In cases of direct fire, where $i$ is small, it is sometimes preferable to retain the circular measure $i$ radians, as it is undistinguishable from $\sin i$ and $\tan i$.

## The Ballistic Table and Associated Functions
The ballistic table uses velocity as its argument, typically proceeding by an increment of 10 f/s. Several functions are numerically represented in these tables: $T, S, D, I,$ and $A$. The function $A$ is known as the altitude function and is utilized when considering high angle fire.

The construction of the table begins with the determination of time $t$ (in seconds) for a shot of diameter $d$ inches and weight $w$ lb to fall from an initial velocity $V$ to a final velocity $v$. The ballistic coefficient $C$ (also called driving power) is defined as $w/nd^2$. The time increment $\Delta t$ is expressed as $\Delta t = C \Delta T$, where $\Delta T = \Delta v/gp$.

The function $S$ represents the advance in feet of a shot where $C=1$ while the velocity falls by $\Delta v$. The advance $s$ for a shot with a specific ballistic coefficient $C$ is calculated as $s = C[S(V) - S(v)]$.

## Application in Direct Fire
For direct fire, Siacci's altitude-function is used to immediately determine the angle of descent ($\beta$) and the angle of elevation ($\phi$) required for a specific range $X$ ft. or $R$ yds. In these instances, the real velocities $V$ and $v$ and the pseudo-velocities $U$ and $u$ are undistinguishable, and $\sec \eta$ may be replaced by unity.

The angles are calculated as follows:
*   $\tan \phi = C [I(V) - \Delta A/\Delta S]$
*   $\tan \beta = C [\Delta A/\Delta S - I(v)]$
*   $\tan \phi - \tan \beta = C [I(V) - I(v)]$

For small angles, these are expressed as $\sin 2\phi = 2C [I(V) - \Delta A/\Delta S]$ and $\sin 2\beta = 2C [\Delta A/\Delta S - I(v)]$.
