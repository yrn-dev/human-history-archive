# Bankruptcy in France

## Legal Framework and Definition
Bankruptcy in France is governed by the Commercial Code of 1807, which was subsequently supplemented and amended by the law of June 9, 1838. According to Article 437 of this code, bankruptcy is defined as the state of a trader who is unable to meet his commercial engagements. 

The French legal system distinguishes between different types of insolvency. Simple insolvency is referred to as *faillite*. When insolvency is accompanied by fraud or circumstances of misconduct, it is categorized as either *banqueroute simple* or *banqueroute frauduleuse*.

## Eligibility and Adjudication
The laws regulating bankruptcy are designed exclusively for the protection of commerce; consequently, only a trader can become bankrupt. Furthermore, the debt required for obtaining adjudication must be a commercial debt. 

A trader does not necessarily need to be insolvent to be declared bankrupt; it is sufficient that the trader has suspended payment. This liability extends to commercial companies of all kinds, which are declared bankrupt in the same manner as individual traders.

Adjudication of bankruptcy can occur through three different channels:
* Upon the petition of the trader-debtor.
* Upon the petition of a creditor.
* By the court acting *proprio motu*.

When a debtor petitions for bankruptcy, they are required to file a declaration of suspension at the office of the Tribunal of Commerce of the district within fifteen days. This filing must include a true account of the debtor's affairs, including their conduct, assets, debts, profits, losses, and personal expenses.

## Administration of the Estate
Upon the adjudication of bankruptcy, the Tribunal of Commerce appoints two specific roles to manage the process:
1. A *syndic provisoire*, who is tasked with managing the estate of the bankrupt.
2. A *juge commissaire*, who is appointed to supervise the *syndic*.

## Termination and Rehabilitation
A bankruptcy proceeding in France can terminate in one of three ways: through a sale of the debtor's assets (*union*), an ordinary composition (*concordat*), or a composition by relinquishment of assets.

A distinctive feature of the French system is the treatment of remaining debts. Even when a discharge in bankruptcy is accompanied by a *declaration d'excusabilite*, any unpaid balance remains a debt of honour. This reflects a principle established during the French Revolution by the National Convention, which resolved that any man who contracted a debt should never be free from the liability to pay it. Consequently, a trader is not rehabilitated and continues to suffer various disabilities until every penny owed has been paid.
