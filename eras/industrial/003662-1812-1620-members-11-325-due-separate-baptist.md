# Baptist Growth and the "Separate" Baptist Movement

The Baptist denomination experienced significant numerical expansion in the United States during the late 18th and early 19th centuries. By 1812, the total number of Baptist church members in the United States reached 173,972, a figure that represented more than a doubling of the denomination's strength since the start of the 19th century. Following 1812, this growth continued rapidly; by 1906, membership had increased from fewer than 200,000 to more than 5,000,000.

## The "Separate" Baptist Movement
A primary driver of this expansion, particularly in the southern colonies, was the "Separate" Baptist movement. Led by "New Light" evangelists Shubael Stearns and Daniel Marshall, this movement was instrumental in the multiplication of Baptist churches. Stearns, described as an evangelist of great power, settled at Sandy Creek, North Carolina, with Marshall and approximately twelve fellow-believers, establishing a church that grew to over six hundred members within a few years. Marshall subsequently founded a church at Abbott's Creek and later established a church at Kiokee, Georgia.

From these centers, "Separate" Baptist influence spread across North and South Carolina, into Georgia, and extended further into Tennessee and Kentucky. In Virginia and the Carolinas, the growth of the "Separate" Baptists was described as phenomenal between 1760 and 1770, aided by evangelists such as John Waller and Samuel Harris. In Virginia, the "Separate" Baptists more than doubled their membership between May 1771 and May 1773.

## Impact in South Carolina and Georgia
The influence of the "Separate" Baptists was particularly evident in South Carolina. Between 1787 and 1812, the number of Baptists in South Carolina grew from 1,620 members to 11,325. This remarkable numerical progress was attributed far more to the "Separate" Baptist movement under Stearns and Marshall than to the activities of the churches within the Charleston Association.

In Georgia, two distinct types of Baptist life existed: the influence of the Charleston Association was felt primarily in more cultivated communities and cities such as Augusta and Savannah, while the "Separate" Baptists focused on evangelizing the masses. Additionally, many negro slaves became Baptists in Georgia, the Carolinas, and Virginia. While most joined white Baptist churches, they were encouraged to establish their own churches in certain towns, including Savannah and Richmond.

## Organizational Evolution and Conflict
The "Separate" Baptists often clashed with authorities due to their enthusiasm and opposition to state control of religious matters, refusing to take licenses for their meetings. Internally, the movement faced tensions. The Sandy Creek Association, under Stearns, attempted to "unfellowship" ministers and churches that acted independently, leading to a division that created the Congaree Association of South Carolina and the General Association of Virginia.

For a brief period starting in 1774, some Virginia brethren adopted the apostolic office, ordaining Samuel Harris, John Waller, and Elijah Craig as apostles, though this arrangement was soon abandoned. To preserve harmony and align with "Regular" Baptists of the Philadelphia type, the movement adopted the Calvinistic Philadelphia Confession of Faith. By 1780, "Separate" and "Regular" Baptists cooperated in the struggle for religious equality and the separation of church and state.

## Baptist Governance and Institutional Development
Baptists maintain that each separate church is complete in itself, possessing the power to choose its own ministers and create regulations to advance the kingdom of Christ. While most are united in voluntary geographical associations, a small section known as "Scotch Baptists" in Scotland and northern England requires a "plurality of elders" for church organization.

In the United States, the denomination's infrastructure grew alongside its membership. In 1812, American Baptists possessed only one degree-conferring college with a small faculty and almost no endowment, and they had no theological seminary. By 1906, they had 11 seminaries and more than 100 universities and colleges. Church property, which had a small value in 1812, was estimated at $100,000,000 by 1906.

## Sectional Division
Tensions over slavery between Northern and Southern members occasionally disrupted the sessions of the Triennial Convention before 1844. A controversy between Richard Fuller and Francis Wayland regarding slavery convinced Southern Baptists that a separate organization for missionary work was necessary. This led to the formation of the Southern Baptist Convention in 1845, with its own Sunday-school Board and Home and Foreign Missionary Boards. Although Northern and Southern Baptists remained in fellowship, they maintained separate annual meetings and boards. In 1905, a General Baptist Convention for America was formed to promote comity and fellowship without interfering in administrative or sectional organization.
