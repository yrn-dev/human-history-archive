# Flowers, Fruit, and Honey-Producing Insects

## Honey and Its Production
Honey is a sweet, viscid liquid primarily obtained by bees from the nectaries of flowers, which are specialized parts of the flower constructed for honey elaboration. Bees transport the nectar to the hive using their crop (proventriculus), where it is discharged into prepared cells. While authors differ on whether the nectar is altered within the bee's crop, the resulting product has been used since ancient times as a primary source of sugar and for medicinal purposes. In the *British Pharmacopoeia*, honey serves as a flavoring agent and demulcent in preparations such as *mel boracis* and *oxymel*.

Beyond bees, certain wasps, including the genus *Nectarina* and *Myrapetra scutellaris*, also collect honey. Additionally, "honey-ants" or "pouched ants" (*Myrmecocystus mexicanus*) of Mexico produce a honey-like fluid consisting of a nearly pure solution of uncrystallizable sugar (formula C6H14O7). In these ants, inactive individuals serve as honey-bearers; their abdomens become globular, thin-walled sacs due to the accumulation of nectar supplied by working comrades. In Colorado, these insects have been observed hanging from the roofs of underground nest chambers in groups of about thirty, resembling "bunches of small Delaware grapes." A similar function is attributed to a bladder-like formation on the metathorax of the ant *Crematogaster inflatus*.

## Botanical Sources of Honey
The volume of blossoms required to produce honey is immense. A. S. Wilson estimated that 125 heads of common red clover yield only one gramme of sugar; since each head contains roughly 60 florets, approximately 7,500,000 flower-tubes must be exhausted to collect one kilogramme of sugar. Some researchers, such as Pettigrew, suggest that few bees travel more than 2 meters from their home in search of honey.

Rich sources of honey include a wide variety of plants:
*   **Trees and Shrubs:** Apple, cherry, pear, quince, peach, maple, silver maple, sycamore, poplar, basswood (*Tilia americana*), European lime or linden (*T. europaea*), hawthorn, and willow.
*   **Flowers and Herbs:** Aster, borage, buckwheat, catnip, clover, crocus, dandelion, golden-rod, hyacinth, mint, mustard, sage, snapdragon, thyme, and violet.
*   **Other Sources:** Asparagus, barberry, beans, bonesets, broom, cotton, currant, eucalyptus, figwort, furze, gooseberry, heather, hepatica, horehound, lucerne, mignonette, motherwort, onion, rape, raspberry, strawberry, teasel, and turnip. 

Honey can also be derived from "honey-dew" found on the leaves of beech, oak, linden, and whitethorn.

## Toxicity in Honey and Nectar
While generally useful, some honeys are poisonous or noxious. Examples include:
*   **Wasp Honey:** Reddish honey stored by the Brazilian wasp *Nectarina Lecheguana*.
*   **Floral Sources:** Honey from rhododendron flowers in East Nepaul, and honey from Trebizond derived from *Rhododendron ponticum* and *Azalea pontica*, which is described as an intoxicant narcotic and irritant. Honey from the calico bush (*Kalmia latifolia*) and sour-wood is also reputed to be deleterious or possess griping properties.
*   **Regional Varieties:** Livid-colored honey from Gaetulia and Persia, and honey from the Coorg jungle, which can cause nausea, headache, intense thirst, and extreme prostration.
*   **Other:** A South African *Euphorbia* species yields poisonous honey, and some flower nectars are asserted to cause fatal vertigo in bees.

## Fruit and Flower Anatomy
Fruits exhibit various forms and anomalies. A *siliqua* is a dry, many-seeded syncarpous fruit formed from two carpels with a false septum, common in cabbage and cress. When broad and short, it is termed a *silicula*. Multiple or confluent fruits, such as the pine-apple, jack-fruit, and bread-fruit, are known as *sorosis* and are formed by the confluence of a spike of flowers.

Anomalies can occur where carpels are changed into leaves or where leaves grow from the upper part of the fruit. In the genus *Citrus*, the separation of carpels produces fingered citrons and horned oranges. The navel orange occurs when a supernumerary row of carpels creates the appearance of small oranges inside the original fruit. Double fruits may result from the union of two flowers or the abnormal development of a second carpel.

## Specialized Plant Species
The buckthorn (*Rhamnus cathartica*) is a shrub with small green flowers and succulent black globose fruit that possesses strong purgative properties. An allied species, *Rhamnus Frangula*, has bisexual flowers and fruits that yield a green dye when unripe. 

The *Casuarina* genus consists of trees with unisexual flowers. Staminate flowers are borne in spikes, while pistillate flowers are in spherical heads. In these trees, pollen is transferred by wind, and the pollen-tube enters the ovule through the chalaza rather than the micropyle.

## The Role of the Honey-Bee
The honey-bee is regarded as the most serviceable insect to mankind. Beyond the production of honey, bees fertilize flowers and fruit. They also produce wax for store-combs and larval cells, which is a significant commercial product. The hive bee is noted as the only insect capable of domestication under the direct control of a bee-master.
