# Art and Collecting in England

The history of art and collecting in England is characterized by the growth of public galleries, the development of specialized collecting theories, and the recognition of both international and domestic artists.

## The Acquisition of Fine Art
England has played a significant role in the valuation and acquisition of international artists. For instance, England was the first country to recognize the merit of the artist De Hooch, who was not valued in Holland until the middle of the 18th century. Consequently, while examples of De Hooch's work are found in various international museums—including the Louvre, the Amsterdam Museum, and those in St Petersburg, Munich, and Darmstadt—many are held in private galleries in England. Additionally, the National Gallery in London holds one of two replicas of "The Horse Fair" (1853) by the French painter Rosa Bonheur.

The demand for certain artists in England could be intense. Following the exhibition of 1855, the works of Rosa Bonheur became highly sought after, leading to eager competition for her pieces between public galleries and private collectors.

## The Evolution of Book-Collecting
Book-collecting in England evolved from a general love of literature into a specialized modern practice. Early owners of significant libraries, such as Archbishop Cranmer, Henry, prince of Wales (1594-1612), and Lords Lumley and Arundel, focused on gathering the best books of their day across all departments of learned literature. These works were often bound in handsome leather and marked with heraldic badges or coats of arms.

By the reign of George III, some buyers began to specialize. James West and John Ratcliffe, for example, focused specifically on the products of early English presses and Caxtons, with Ratcliffe possessing 48 and West possessing 34. Other collectors, such as Garrick and Capell, maintained small, homogeneous collections.

In the 19th century, the "cabinet theory" of collecting became popular in England, France, and the United States. This approach was carried out with conspicuous skill by Henry Perkins (d. 1855). Rather than maintaining a miscellaneous library that required a dedicated gallery or room, the cabinet theory emphasized small, compact collections that epitomized the owner's tastes and could fit into a neat bookcase or cabinet. This shift was driven by the increasing cost of books and limitations regarding house-room. The significance of this method was evidenced in 1870, when Perkins' collection of 865 specimens of early printing and fine manuscripts sold for nearly £26,000.

## Galleries and Art Institutions
England has hosted various specialized galleries and art institutions. In the late 18th century, Alderman Boydell organized a celebrated Shakespeare gallery, for which the artist Fuseli painted a number of pieces. Later, in 1799, Fuseli attempted to create a corresponding Milton gallery consisting of forty-seven paintings based on the works of Milton; however, this exhibition, which closed in 1800, was a financial failure.

Publicly accessible art has also been integrated into municipal spaces. In Birmingham, the corporation acquired Aston Hall and its park in 1864. The mansion, built of red brick by Sir Thomas Holte between 1618 and 1635, was subsequently converted into an art gallery and museum. This institution houses the panelling of a room from the former house of Edmund Hector in Old Square, a location frequently visited by Dr Samuel Johnson.
