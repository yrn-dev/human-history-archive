# Building Societies

Building societies are organizations established for the purpose of raising a stock or fund through the subscriptions of their members. These funds are then used to make advances to members via mortgages on leasehold, copyhold, or freehold estates. In popular terms, these societies allow individuals to become "their own landlord" by collecting small periodical subscriptions until a member receives a sum sufficient to assist in buying a dwelling-house.

## Classification and Nomenclature
According to the Building Societies Act 1874, these organizations are classified as either "permanent" or "terminating." A terminating society is one that, by its own rules, ends at a fixed date or once a specific result is attained. A permanent society has no such fixed date or specified result for its termination.

Historically, these organizations used various titles. In London, early societies often used names such as "Investment Association," "Savings Fund," "Accumulating Fund," "Mutual Associations," "Societies of Equality," or "Societies for obtaining freehold property." In Scotland, they were commonly called "Economic" or "Property Investment" societies, and some adopted the title "menages." While the term "Benefit Building Society" appeared in the act of 1836, it did not become the sole distinctive name in England until 1849. This name is noted as being somewhat inaccurate, as these societies generally do not undertake building operations themselves, but instead provide the money for members to buy land or build houses. Additionally, the term "Building Society" overlooks their function as a means for investing small savings.

## Legal Development in the United Kingdom
The legal framework for these societies evolved through several key acts of Parliament:

*   **Friendly Societies Act 1834:** This extended benefits to associations for frugal investment, leading several building societies to be certified under it.
*   **Act of 1836:** This confirmed the privileges of the 1834 Act and added exemptions from stamp duty and usury laws, as well as simplified forms of conveyance. It defined the societies as means for members to receive the value of shares to purchase or erect a dwelling-house. Members who did not wish to purchase a home could still receive the value of their shares, improved by interest payments from those who had received advances.
*   **Building Societies Act 1874:** This act made future societies incorporated bodies and allowed existing ones to apply for incorporation, removing the need to trace titles through successive trustees. It limited member liability to arrears due at winding up or the amount secured by mortgage deeds. It also granted the power to borrow money, provided the limitation of liability was printed on the loan acknowledgment and the borrowed amount did not exceed one year's subscription income (for terminating societies) or two-thirds of the amount secured by member mortgages.
*   **Building Societies Act 1894:** Passed following the "Liberator" disaster, this act required incorporated societies to provide regular returns. These returns included schedules of mortgages exceeding £5,000, mortgages in arrears for more than twelve months, and properties the society had possessed for over twelve months due to mortgagor default.

## Financial Crisis and the Liberator Disaster
By the late 19th century, signs of instability appeared, including the rise of "mushroom societies" that primarily paid fees to promoters, the trafficking of advances awarded by ballot, and the practice of receiving deposits repayable at call despite funds being tied up in long-term loans.

These issues culminated in the insolvency of the Liberator society in October 1892. The Liberator had drawn over a million sterling from various classes of people through specious representations, using the funds to support other undertakings managed by the same individuals rather than legitimate building society business. The resulting fraud ruined many investors and weakened general confidence in the sector. The capital of incorporated building societies fell from a peak of nearly 54 million in 1887 to below 43 million by 1895. While some societies faced runs on deposits, the Birkbeck Society survived by using easily convertible securities and assistance from the Bank of England to pay depositors, which helped stabilize the wider industry.

## International Variations and Rivals
In the British colonies, legislation similar to the UK's was adopted. In Canada, these organizations are sometimes called "loan companies" and may invest in assets other than real estate, though approximately 90% of advances are secured by real estate. As of the end of 1904, Canadian societies had liabilities of £21,000,000 to the public and £13,000,000 to stockholders, with current loans totaling £28,000,000. In Australia, the movement progressed in several colonies, though a crisis in Victoria caused many societies to suffer severely.

In Belgium, the Government Savings Bank provides advances to construction or credit societies at 2% or 3% interest, which the societies then lend to borrowers at 4%. In Germany, building societies exist as a form of self-help but are fewer in number due to the influence of credit societies founded by Schulze-Delitzsch.

In the UK, building societies have faced competition from bond and investment companies, industrial and provident land and building societies, and local authorities authorized to lend to the working classes. Additionally, friendly societies have begun investing surplus funds in loans based on the building society principle.
