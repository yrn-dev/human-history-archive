# Baron

The term "baron" has varied in meaning and application across different cultures and eras, evolving from a description of a man's relationship to a lord into a formal title of nobility.

## Origins and Evolution in England
The word "baron" was introduced to England during the Conquest to describe "the man"—specifically one who had performed homage—of a great lord or the king. Those who held land directly from the king were known as *barones regis* and were theoretically members of his council, bound to perform stipulated services. While great nobles referred to their tenants as barons, lesser magnates used the term "men" (*homines*). This distinction was particularly evident in palatinates; for example, the earl of Chester had a recognized body of barons, such as the Venables family (barons of Kinderton), who existed until 1679. Similarly, the bishop of Durham had barons, including the Hiltons of Hilton Castle, whose title became extinct in 1746.

Historically, the position of a baron was tied to land tenure. However, a shift occurred when presence in the council of the realm (the House of Lords) became dependent on a writ of summons from the king rather than land ownership. While some attribute this change to Henry III, others assign it to Edward I. Initially, those summoned were not distinguished by a specific title, often using the prefix *Dominus* (lord). The formal style of "baron" was introduced by Richard II in 1387 with the creation of John de Beauchamp as baron of Kidderminster. Despite these patents, the style "Lord" remained the primary form of address. To this day, a baron is addressed in correspondence as "the Lord A.," while the practice of using "Baron A." is considered a recent and unhistorical development.

## Classification and Legal Roles
Due to differences in the amount of land held, barons were early on divided into "greater" and "lesser" barons. By 1164, a practice emerged where greater barons received special summons to the council, while the lesser barons were summoned via sheriffs, as stipulated in the Magna Carta of 1215. This eventually restricted peerage rights and privileges to the greater barons.

In the legal sphere, "Barons of the Exchequer" served as six judges (one chief baron and five puisne barons) who administered justice in revenue cases between the king and his subjects. This title became obsolete in 1875 when the court of exchequer merged into the High Court of Judicature.

## European and International Titles
In Germany and Austria, the term *barones* appeared in Latin documents during the Middle Ages, but the title "Baron" only became a common equivalent to the old German *Freiherr* (free lord of the Empire) in the 17th century, possibly influenced by the court of Versailles. Originally, *Freiherr* implied a dynastic status. In the 16th century, Charles V began conferring the title by imperial letters, a practice continued by all German sovereigns. Because the title is typically passed to all children of the father, it has become common and is no longer tied to territorial possession. Nevertheless, it still denotes significant social status and privilege in societies with a strict divide between the nobility (*Adeliger*) and commoners.

Foreign titles of baron are occasionally held by English subjects, though they confer no precedence within the United Kingdom. Examples of such titles include:
* **Russian:** Baron Dimsdale (1762)
* **German:** Baron Stockmar and Baron Halkett (Hanoverian)
* **Austrian:** Baron Rothschild (1822) and Baron de Worms
* **Italian:** Baron Heath
* **French:** Baron de Teissier
* **French-Canadian:** Baron de Longueil (1700)
* **Dutch:** Baron Mackay (Lord Reay)

Outside of Europe, Japan introduced the title baron (*Dan*) in 1885 as the lowest of five nobility titles. It was granted to the least important territorial nobles or bestowed as an honor regardless of land ownership.

## Notable Figures
Various individuals have held the title across different regions. In the German context, Baron Stockmar served as a friend and servant to King Leopold of Belgium and acted as a keeper of the international conscience for Queen Victoria and her consort. Stockmar and the Saxe-Coburg-Gotha group were known for their liberal enthusiasm for the unity and well-being of Germany.

In Hungary, Baron Joseph Eotvos was a figure of intellectual note; biographies of his life and art were published in Budapest between 1902 and 1904. In the English peerage, the Barons Grey de Wilton began with Reginald de Grey, summoned to parliament in 1295.

## Heraldry
In heraldry, the term *Baron and Feme* refers to the practice of bearing the coats-of-arms of a husband and wife "per pale" in a single escutcheon. The husband's arms are placed on the dexter side and the wife's on the sinister. This arrangement is used when the woman is not an heiress; if she were an heiress, her coat would be borne by the husband on an escutcheon of pretence.
