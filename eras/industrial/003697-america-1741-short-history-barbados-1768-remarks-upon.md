# History and Geography of Barbados and Colonial America

## The Island of Barbados
Barbados is described as one of the oldest British possessions, having been occupied by the British in 1605. The island's climate is characterized as pleasant, with seasons divided into wet and dry periods. The dry season, which also serves as the cold season, extends from December through the end of May. Temperatures typically range between 70°F and 86°F, and rarely drop below 65°F even during the coldest days. 

The island receives an average annual rainfall of approximately 60 inches, with September being the wettest month. For eight months of the year, the tropical heat is tempered by invigorating N.E. trade winds. Due to the porous nature of the soil, the extent of cultivation, and an absence of swamps, the island is free from miasma, and fever is unknown. The climate is noted for arresting the decay of vital power associated with old age and providing a beneficial effect on pulmonary diseases, particularly in their early stages. Regarding health, elephantiasis is frequent enough to be termed "Barbados leg," and leprosy occurs among the negroes.

## British and French Presence in North America
By 1750, British power was established along the east coast of North America, stretching from Savannah to the St. Lawrence River. This included Newfoundland and northern areas acquired from the French via treaty, such as the Hudson Bay Company territories. The British also acquired the Bahamas, Jamaica, and British Honduras from the Spaniards. Additionally, the Dutch and Swedish settlements succumbed to British control; specifically, Nieuw Amsterdam became British in 1674 and was renamed New York.

During this period, the British colonies were solidly settled by a population exceeding one million people. In contrast, the French population was hardly a tenth of that size. While the French had established real settlements in Montreal, Quebec, and New Orleans, and utilized missionaries and travellers to make treaties with American Indians of the great plains, they set up claims across the continent without establishing towns.

## Notable Figures and Works (1741–1768)
The mid-18th century saw various contributions to law, architecture, and literature. In 1741, Sir Thomas (1694–1753), the biographer and literary executor of Burnet, became a judge in the court of common pleas. That same year, the Dutch classical scholar Pieter Burmann "the Elder" died on March 31. Burmann, a professor of history for the United Provinces and chief librarian, was known for his immense learning and accuracy in editorial and critical works, though he was viewed more as a compiler than a critic.

In the field of architecture, George Dance the elder (1700–1768) served as the architect to the city of London. His works include the Mansion House (1739) and the church of St Botolph, Aldgate, which was designed in 1741.

In literature, 1741 marked the publication of *Gevallen van Friso*, a historical epos written by Willem van Haren (1710–1768), who, along with his brother Onno Zwier van Haren, sought success in epic writing following the French style of Voltaire.

## Historiography and Geological Study
The archive contains numerous references to the historical documentation of Barbados. Key texts include Ligon's *History of Barbados* (1657), *A Short History of Barbados* (1768), and *Remarks upon the Short History* (1768). Later accounts include works by Poyer (1808), Capt. Thom. Southey (1827), Schomburgk (1848), and J. H. Stark (1893).

The geology of Barbados has been the subject of detailed study in the *Quart. Journ. Geol. Soc. London*. Research conducted between 1891 and 1902 by A. J. Jukes-Browne, J. B. Harrison, J. W. Gregory, G. F. Franks, and J. W. Spencer explored the physical development of the island, specifically the basal reef-rocks and Globigerina-marls.
