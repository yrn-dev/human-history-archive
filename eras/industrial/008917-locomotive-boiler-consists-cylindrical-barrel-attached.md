# Locomotive Boilers

A locomotive boiler is a specialized steam vessel consisting of a cylindrical barrel attached to a fire-box. The fire-box is nearly rectangular in both its vertical and horizontal sections.

## Structural Design and Support
The construction of the locomotive boiler involves several staying mechanisms to maintain structural integrity under pressure. The sides of the fire-box are secured to the fire-box shell using numerous stays, typically 1 inch in diameter, which are generally pitched 4 inches apart both horizontally and vertically. 

The support for the top of the fire-box varies by the size of the boiler:
* **Small Boilers:** These utilize girder stays that run longitudinally. These stays are supported at the ends by the opposite fire-box plate and the tube plate. In certain instances, slings from the crown of the boiler provide partial support for these girders.
* **Larger Boilers:** The crown of the boiler above the fire-box is constructed to be flat. In this design, the fire-box crown is supported by vertical stays that connect it to the shell crown.

In express locomotive boilers, such as those used by the Great Northern Railway in England, the fire-box crown is stayed by vertical stays extending to the shell crown, with the exception of the three rows of stays closest to the tube plates. These specific rows are supported by cross girders that rest upon brackets secured to the outer shell.

## Components and Materials
The tube plate of a locomotive boiler is made of copper. To accommodate expansion, the two or three cross rows of stays nearest to the tube plate are designed with freedom of motion upwards, though they cannot move downwards. Standard tubes generally have a diameter of 1-3/4 inches.

The fire-box includes fire-bars, which are typically designed to slope downwards away from the fire door. A baffle or fire-bridge is fitted just below the lowest tubes, extending approximately half-way from the tube plate to the fire-door side of the fire-box.

Some boilers are equipped with water-tubes made of seamless steel that extend across the fire-box and are expanded into the fire-box side plates. In these configurations, the outer shell side plates are supported by stay bars that pass directly through the water-tubes. For example, a boiler used by the London & South-Western Railway Co. features 112 water-tubes, each 2-3/4 inches in diameter. These are arranged in two clusters of 56; one set is positioned above the fire-bridge, and the other is located nearer the fire-door end. This specific boiler has a total heating surface of 2727 sq. ft. and a grate area of 31.5 sq. ft. Another example of an express locomotive boiler features a grate area of 30.9 sq. ft., a heating surface of 2500 sq. ft., and a barrel that is 16 ft. long between tube plates with a diameter of 5 ft. 6 in.

## Manufacturing and Operation
The fabrication of locomotive boilers employs a process called flanging, which involves bending the edges of a plate to allow it to be joined to other plates. This process is used for the fire-boxes of locomotive boilers. While this was once done by hand by an anglesmith, modern boiler shops use hydraulic presses with massive dies to bend the entire flanges of fire-box plates in a single squeeze. This machine-flanging is faster and causes less injury to the material than hand-flanging.

Locomotive boilers typically operate at a pressure of approximately 175 lb. to 200 lb. per sq. in. To manage this pressure and ensure safety, every boiler is equipped with:
* A steam-gauge to indicate current pressure.
* A stop-valve to regulate the admission of steam into the steam-pipes.
* One or two safety-valves. In locomotive boilers, these valves are held closed by steel springs. At least one safety-valve includes easing gear, allowing it to be lifted to blow off steam.
* Blow-out cocks used for emptying the boiler.

For maintenance, boilers include openings for cleaning and examination. When these are large enough for a person to enter, they are called man-holes. These are usually oval-shaped, which allows the doors to be placed on the inside so that steam pressure helps keep them shut. These doors are secured by one or two bolts attached to "dogs" or cross-bars on the outside of the boiler.
