# Historical Documentation and Academic Studies (1877–1907)

The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw a significant increase in the systematic exploitation of archives and the publication of specialized historical, legal, and scientific bibliographies. This period was marked by the emergence of comprehensive collections of state treaties, the study of ancient astrology, and the documentation of imperial policies.

## Austrian Historical Sources and Diplomacy
During the latter half of the 19th century, public and private archives in Austria began to be systematically utilized. A primary example of this effort is the *Fontes rerum Austriacarum*, published by the Historical Commission of the Imperial Academy of Sciences at Vienna. This series, which began in 1855, is divided into *Scriptores* (with the 9th volume appearing in 1904) and *Diplomataria et Acta* (with the 58th volume appearing in 1906), covering both medieval and modern Austrian history. Other notable works include the *Quellen und Forschungen zur Geschichte, Literatur und Sprache Oesterreichs und seiner Kronlaender*, edited by J. E. Wackernagel and J. Hirn, which saw its 10th volume published in 1906.

In the realm of diplomacy, L. Neumann published a six-volume collection of treaties concluded by Austria with foreign powers since 1763 in 1855, which was later continued by A. de Plason in 18 volumes between 1877 and 1905. In 1907, the Imperial Commission for the Modern History of Austria introduced a new series titled *Oesterreichische Staatsvertraege*, compiled on the principle of devoting separate volumes to treaties with different states.

## Studies in Astrology and Ancient Science
Academic interest in astrology and its relationship to astronomy and popular belief produced several key works between 1877 and 1909. Notable contributions include A. Bouche-Leclercq's *L'Astrologie grecque* (1899) and *Histoire de la divination dans l'antiquite* (1879), as well as Franz Cumont's *Catalogus Codicum Astrologorum Graecorum* (1898–1909) and *Les Religions orientates dans le paganisme romain* (1907). 

Research into ancient Near Eastern astrology includes R.C. Thompson's *Reports of the Magicians and Astrologers of Nineveh and Babylon* (1900), F.X. Kugler's *Sternkunde und Sterndienst in Babel* (1907), and Ch. Virolleaud's *L'Astrologie chaldeenne* (1905), the latter of which provides translations and transliterations of cuneiform texts.

## British Imperialism and Legal Figures
The subject of British imperialism inspired a growing body of literature toward the end of the 19th century. Key texts include Sir J.R. Seeley's *Expansion of England* (1883) and *Growth of British Policy* (1895), Sir Charles Dilke's *Greater Britain* (1869) and *The British Empire* (1899), and H.J. Mackinder's *Britain and the British Seas* (1902). While many works focused on expansion and federation—such as those by G.R. Parkin and Sir John Colomb—J.A. Hobson provided an anti-imperialist perspective in his 1902 work *Imperialism*. Official documentation also includes the reports of colonial conferences, specifically the Imperial Conference of 1907.

In the legal sphere, Sir Edward Fry (1827– ) served as a prominent English judge. Raised to the bench and knighted in 1877, he was noted for his elucidations of the Judicature Acts. After serving as a lord justice of appeal from 1883 to 1892, he acted as a chairman for many commissions and represented Britain at the Paris North Sea Inquiry Commission in 1905.

## Religious and Legal Codes
The study of church constitutions and law saw several publications, including works by Richter on 16th-century evangelical church orders (1846) and Catholic and evangelical church law (1886). Other contributions include Emil Sehling's *Geschichte der protestantischen Kirchenverfassung* (1907) and Schaff's *History of the Creeds of Christendom* (1877).

Regarding state legal codes and executions, Sweden's Royal Ordinance of 1877 specifies that executions take place within a prison on the order of the king. In the Chinese empire, an imperial edict issued on April 24, 1905, suppressed certain "attendant barbarities" associated with decapitation, such as slicing the body and exhibiting the head to the public.
