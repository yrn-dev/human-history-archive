## Bridge Erection Methods
The erection of bridges is influenced by local conditions, which can dictate the final design. There are four primary classes of erection methods:
1. Erection on staging or falsework.
2. Floating the structure to the site and raising it.
3. Rolling the structure out from one abutment.
4. Building out member by member, where the completed section serves as the stage for further additions.

In staging erection, materials such as earth banks, timber stacks, or iron staging—which allows for rapid movement between sites—are used. A common staging type involves timber piles placed every 20 ft. to 30 ft. to support a timber platform. In cases of deep rivers or great headway, clusters of piles or timber-braced piers may be placed at distances of 50 ft. to 100 ft. to support steel or timber temporary trusses. An example of this is the Kuilenburg bridge in Holland; with a span of 492 ft. and a superstructure weighing 2150 tons, it was erected using 5 tons of bolts and 81,000 cub. ft. of timber.

## Military Logistics and Manpower
Records from the Great War detail the expansion of British land and sea forces. In August 1914, land forces consisted of 250,000 Regulars, 200,000 trained Reserves, and 250,000 partly-trained Territorials. Following a request by Lord Kitchener for 100,000 volunteers, these were enrolled in less than a fortnight, with 30,000 enlisting in a single day. By July 1915, there were 2,000,000 men in arms, increasing to over 5,000,000 voluntary enrollments by May 1916, and reaching 8,500,000 men in the armed forces of the Crown by August 1918.

The navy grew from 145,000 officers and men and 2,500,000 tons in August 1914 to 450,000 men and 8,000,000 tons four years later. In one month of 1918, auxiliary vessels travelled 6,000,000 miles, while warships travelled 1,000,000 sea miles in home waters. Throughout the war, the navy transported 2,000,000 horses and mules, 20,000,000 men (with 2,700 lost to enemy action), 25,000,000 tons of explosives, 51,000,000 tons of oil and fuel, and 130,000,000 tons of food and other materials.

## Regional Economic Data
### Mineral Resources of Louisiana
Louisiana possesses important mineral resources, specifically salt, petroleum, natural gas, and sulphur. Rock salt deposits on Petite Anse Island, worked since the Civil War, are in places 1000 ft. thick and can reach 99% purity. Deposits also exist on Belle Isle, Week's Island, and Orange Island, where they are at least 1800 ft. thick. In 1907, Louisiana's salt output was valued at $226,892, ranking it sixth in the U.S.

Sulphur deposits near Lake Charles are found 400 to 600 feet underground. These are extracted using superheated water forced down pipes and then pushed upward by hot air pressure, resulting in sulphur that is 99% pure. In 1907, the value of Louisiana's sulphur was over $5,000,000. Petroleum fields include Jennings—one of the largest in the U.S.—as well as Vinton, Caddo, Anse la Butte, and Welsh.
