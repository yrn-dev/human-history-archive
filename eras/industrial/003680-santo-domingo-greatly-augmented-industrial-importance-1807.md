# Santo Domingo

## Colonial History and Spanish Policy
Early in the 16th century, the island of Santo Domingo was nearly depopulated as a result of the oppressive colonial policies enacted by Spain. During this period, Spanish officials presided over several isolated establishments along the coast. These Spaniards were hindered by monopolies imposed by their mother country, which deprived them of a convenient market for the produce of the soil.

Because of these restrictions, the inhabitants welcomed vessels from the French, Dutch, and English, readily purchasing their cargoes. The reduction of the island's former population led to the emergence of immense herds of wild cattle. This environment attracted smugglers who used Santo Domingo for provisioning.

## The Rise of the Buccaneers
The native inhabitants who remained on the island were skilled in a method of preserving flesh at small establishments known as *boucans*. Adventurers learned this process, referred to as "boucanning," from the natives. Consequently, Hispaniola became the site of an extensive and illicit butcher trade.

The Spanish monopolies created a natural hatred of everything Spanish among the seamen sailing the Caribbean. This, combined with the appeal of a roving life and occasional skirmishes with Spanish officials, led to the rise of the buccaneer. These individuals acted alternately as hunters, sailors, and occasionally planters. They were characterized as bold, unscrupulous, and often savage, possessing an intense detestation of Spain.

Because Spain refused to recognize the right of other races to trade or establish settlements in the West Indies, the governments of Holland, England, and France did not control their subjects who invaded the islands, leaving them to settle at their own risk. While some settled on St Kitts (or St Christopher), others were encouraged to settle in Santo Domingo. For three-quarters of a century, these buccaneers became a scourge to Spanish dominions and trade in the Spanish-American region.

## Decline of the Buccaneer Community
The community of buccaneers was eventually undermined by its own nature, as it was bound primarily by a desire for gain and a shared hatred of the Spaniard. While they rarely attacked one another—including English, French, Dutch, and occasionally native Indians—their unity fractured when war broke out between England and France in 1689.

By the end of the 17th century, the buccaneers remained along the coasts of Santo Domingo and Jamaica, but their influence waned. They appeared as auxiliaries to French regular forces during the siege of Carthagena, but the community's extinction was accelerated by the resumption of hostilities in 1700 and the rise of Spain following the accession of the French claimant to the throne.

## External Influence and Trade
Throughout the 17th century, various European powers attempted to exert influence over the island. In 1641, the Governor-General for the French West India Islands took possession of Tortuga and attempted to expel the English from Santo Domingo, though this was met with less success.

In the late 18th century, during various wars, the region saw significant movement of people. Approximately 100 fugitive immigrant families from Santo Domingo migrated to Baracoa (a seaport in N.E. Cuba), which greatly increased the industrial importance of that city.

## Agricultural Contributions
Santo Domingo served as a source for agricultural development in other regions. In 1751, the Jesuits successfully introduced sugar-cane from Santo Domingo to Louisiana. While the sugar industry in Louisiana practically dates from 1796—when Étienne Boré succeeded in clarifying and crystallizing syrup—the initial introduction of the cane occurred in the middle of the 18th century from Santo Domingo.
