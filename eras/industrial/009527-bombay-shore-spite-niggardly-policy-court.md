# Bombay and the Shore

## Early Foundation and Fortification
The modern foundation of Bombay was primarily the work of Gerald Aungier, who succeeded Sir George Oxenden as president of Surat in 1669 and served until his death in 1677. During this era, the settlement faced significant threats from multiple directions: the Mahrattas attacked from the inland, while the Dutch and Malabar pirates posed threats from the sea. Additionally, the Portuguese occupied the island of Salsette and maintained a customs-barrier in the channel between the shore and Bombay, effectively cutting the city off from the mainland.

Despite a "niggardly policy" from the court of directors, who refused to fund the employment of skilled engineers, Aungier successfully fortified both the shore and the town. To ensure security, he established a force of regulars and militia; the regulars were composed mainly of Germans, who were considered more trustworthy than the individuals recruited by the Company's crimps in London. In 1670, Aungier frightened off an imposing Dutch fleet that had attempted to surprise the island, and he subsequently transferred his headquarters to Bombay in 1672 to reorganize the settlement.

## Governance and Social Reform
Gerald Aungier implemented policies based on the traditions of English liberty, contrasting sharply with the previous Portuguese rule, which had been characterized by religious intolerance, oppressive fiscal policies, and the cruel treatment of natives. With the approval of a general assembly of the people's chief representatives, Aungier replaced the burdensome land tax with a fixed money payment. He also forbade the forced carrying of burdens by natives and protected all castes in the celebration of their religious ceremonies.

These reforms led to a rapid increase in population, which grew from 10,000 to 60,000 within eight years. The city became a haven for Parsees and Armenians, who sought freedom for their religion and trade. Additionally, a specific quarter was established for the Hindu capitalist class, known as the banya. However, this rapid concentration of people in an unwholesome location resulted in the prevalence of disease due to appalling sanitary conditions.

## Political Instability and Rebellion
Following Aungier's death on June 30, 1677, the position of Bombay became increasingly precarious. The city remained isolated between the Mogul empire and the Mahrattas, and the Malabar pirates continued to menace trade. During the tenure of Aungier's weak successor, Rolt (1677–1682), the waters around Bombay became a battleground for rival navies, including the Siddi admirals of the Moguls, who used the harbor as winter quarters for their fleet.

Internal instability also plagued the Company's rule. The board of directors' focus on peaceful dividends over military governance caused resentment among soldiers of fortune, leading to a mutiny in 1674 that ended with the execution of the ringleader. In 1683, Richard Keigwin, a naval officer and former governor of St Helena, led a more formidable movement. Elected governor of Bombay by popular vote, Keigwin declared his government under the authority of the crown, citing the "exactions, oppressions and intolerable extortions" of the Company. Keigwin reformed taxation, increased port trade by admitting "interlopers," and obtained concessions from the Mahrattas. His rebellion ended when he received a royal order to surrender the fort to Sir John Child.

## Expansion of the Bombay Presidency
By 1803, the Bombay presidency consisted of Surat, Bankot, Salsette, and the islands of the harbor. Between 1803 and 1827, the presidency expanded significantly. The Gujarat districts were taken over in 1805 and enlarged in 1818, while measures for the settlement of Mahi Kantha and Kathiawar occurred between 1807 and 1820. Following the defeat and capture of Baji Rao, the last of the peshwas (1817–1818), large portions of his dominions—including Nasik, Poona, Belgaum, Sholapur, Ahmednagar, Dharwar, and Kaladgi—were added to the presidency.

Mountstuart Elphinstone, governor from 1819 to 1827, managed the settlement of these areas by ruling on native lines and avoiding premature changes, though he did codify laws, establish schools and courts, and stop gross abuses of the old regime. Later expansions included the addition of Sind (1843), Aden (1839), and the lease of the Panch Mahals from Sindhia (1853). Under Lord Elphinstone (1853–1860), the presidency remained largely stable during the Mutiny, with only local outbreaks in Kolhapur, Ahmedabad, and Karachi. Later, under Sir Bartle Frere (1862–1867), the region saw peak agricultural prosperity due to the American Civil War, which created an enormous European demand for Indian cotton.

## Administration and Education
The government of Bombay is administered by a governor in council, with the governor serving as president and two ordinary members selected from the Indian civil service. Legislative functions are handled by a legislative council consisting of the governor, the executive council, and between eight and twenty other persons, at least half of whom are non-officials. Justice is administered by a high court at Bombay, featuring a chief justice and seven puisne judges.

In education, the University of Bombay was established in 1857. The governor serves as the ex officio chancellor. By 1905, there were 10,194 educational institutions with 593,431 pupils, including ten art colleges. Despite these institutions, a 1901 census indicated that nearly 24 million of the 25.5 million population were illiterate.
