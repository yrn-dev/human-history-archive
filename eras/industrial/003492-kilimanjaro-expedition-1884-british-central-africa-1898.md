# The Kilimanjaro Expedition and British Central Africa

## Mount Kilimanjaro: Geography and Discovery
Mount Kilimanjaro is a volcanic cone located in East Africa at 3° 5´ S. and 37° 23´ E. It rises from a plateau of approximately 3,000 feet to a height of 19,321 feet, making it the highest known summit on the continent. While isolated, it is one of several summits on the eastern edge of the equatorial African plateau; it is situated some 25 miles east of Mount Meru and about 200 miles south of Mount Kenya.

Reports of snow-covered mountains reached Zanzibar around 1845 via Arab traders. In 1848, Johannes Rebmann of the Church Missionary Society traveled inland from Mombasa and discovered Kilimanjaro. Although his findings were supported by Dr. Ludwig Krapf, professional geographers initially viewed the account with incredulity. This skepticism was resolved following visits by Baron Karl von der Decken in 1861 and 1862, and by Charles New in 1867, the latter of whom reached the lower edge of the snow.

## The Kilimanjaro Expedition and Exploration
The mountain was further explored by Joseph Thomson in 1883 and Sir H. H. Johnston in 1884. Sir H. H. Johnston later published *The Kilimanjaro Expedition* (1884/1886), a work that includes illustrative vocabularies. The summit was first successfully ascended in 1889 by Dr. Hans Meyer, who conducted four expeditions to the mountain.

Local beliefs held that the summit of Kilimanjaro was composed of silver. This has led to conjectures that Aristotle's mention of a "Silver Mountain" as the source of the Nile was based on reports of Kilimanjaro, though it is also possible he referred to the Ruwenzori, where several headstreams of the Nile descend from snow-clad heights.

## Political History and Strategic Importance
In 1884, Sir H. H. Johnston concluded the first treaties with native chiefs in the Kilimanjaro region on behalf of a British company. However, during the partition of Africa by western European powers, Germany secured Kilimanjaro in 1886, establishing a government station at Moshi on the southern side of the mountain.

During later conflicts, the northern frontier near Kilimanjaro proved strategically difficult. The area was protected by an almost impassable mountain system. A gap of four to five miles between the Pare Mountains and Kilimanjaro became a site where enemy forces established extensive fortifications surrounded by impassable swamps and dense forests. These forces threatened British East Africa for eighteen months until they were driven out in March 1916, leading to the conquest of the Kilimanjaro-Meru areas by the end of that month.

## British Central Africa and Linguistic Studies
The region of British Central Africa has been the subject of extensive documentation and linguistic research. Sir H. H. Johnston published a work titled *British Central Africa* in 1898, which, like his Kilimanjaro writings, contains illustrative vocabularies. Other significant contributions to the study of the region include the *Encyclopaedic Dictionary of the Mang'anja Language* (1891) by Rev. D.C. Scott and the works of Miss A. Werner, including *The Natives of British Central Africa* (1906).

Linguistic classifications in the region identify several Bantu groups. The Kilimanjaro (Chaga-Siha) group encompasses the peculiar dialects of Mount Meru, Mount Kilimanjaro, and Ugweno. Other groups in British East Africa include the Kikuyu-Kamba group east of the Rift valley and the Pokomo-Nyika-Giriama-Taveita group, which spans the coast province from the Tana river to the frontier of German East Africa.
