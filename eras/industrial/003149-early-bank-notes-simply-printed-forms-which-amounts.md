# Early Bank-Notes and Their Evolution

## Early Forms and Production
In their earliest iterations, bank-notes were simple printed forms where the specific amounts were written by hand. These notes were typically issued for large sums, specifically £40 and above, and were printed on water-marked paper. During this early period, forgeries were relatively rare, despite the fact that no specific precautions were taken in the engraving process to prevent fraudulent imitation.

The production of these notes relied on engraving, which utilized two primary systems: relief-engraving and line-engraving. In relief-engraving, similar to wood-engraving, the lines stand above the plate and ink is spread upon the face of those lines for surface-printing. Line-engraving involves cutting lines into copper or steel plates for plate-printing, where ink is delivered from the lines to the pressed paper. While plate-printing produces a sharper and finer impression, it leaves a perceptible body of ink on the paper. Conversely, surface-printing is less sharp, which occasionally allowed imperfect counterfeit notes to escape detection.

Early notes were often engraved on copper plates, which wore out quickly and required frequent replacement. This led to significant variations in appearance; notes from new plates were clear and sharp, while those from old plates appeared blurred and pale. Because the public became accustomed to these variations in genuine notes, forgers found it easier to introduce counterfeits.

## The Rise of Counterfeiting and Security Measures
The nature of bank-note forgery changed at the end of the 18th century when small notes for £1 and £2 were put into circulation. This led to a surge in crime; in a single year in England, 352 people were convicted of forgery. This initiated a continuous struggle between counterfeiters and note makers. To combat this, engravers introduced emblematical figures, views, and engine-turned ornaments, combined with special water-marks.

The discovery of photography provided counterfeiters with a dangerous tool, allowing them to perfectly reproduce vignettes and complicated patterns. In 1856, Henry Bradbury introduced anti-photographic printing to counter this. This method involved printing essential portions of the note in one color and overlaying it with a protective second color, which created a confused mingling of colors when photographed. As photographic technology advanced and allowed for the separation of these colors, a third color was introduced with a specific photographic relation to the other two to prevent separation.

## Legal Framework and Issuance in England
The Bank of England was incorporated by the act of 1694. Subsequent acts in 1697 and 1708 restricted the establishment of joint-stock banks in England and limited the ability of partnerships exceeding six persons to borrow or take up money on notes payable on demand or in less than six months. These restrictions were confirmed by the act of 1800.

Significant changes occurred with the act of 1826, which prohibited bank notes under £5. A second act in 1826 allowed joint-stock companies (co-partnerships of more than six persons) to be established beyond 65 miles from London. The act of 1833 further allowed joint-stock banks within the 65-mile limit and removed restrictions on notes for less than £50, though joint-stock banks within that 65-mile radius were still prohibited from issuing notes. In the early days of English banking, the power to issue notes was considered the introduction to the system of deposit-banking; banks without this power could scarcely exist outside of London.

## The Bank Charter Act of 1844
A major shift occurred with the act of 1844, as described by Sir Robert Peel. The Bank of England was divided into two departments: one for the transaction of ordinary banking business and one for the issue of notes. Bullion was transferred to the issue department. Note issuance was restricted to £14,000,000 upon securities, with the remainder issued upon bullion and subject to fluctuations in bullion stocks.

The act also changed reporting requirements. Previously, the bank published a quarterly balance-sheet every month; after the act, it was required to issue weekly returns. The new returns separated the balance-sheets of the Banking Department and the Issue Department.

## Regional Variations and International Examples
Different policies were applied in Scotland and Ireland compared to England. Under the acts of 1844-1845, Scottish and Irish banks were permitted to exceed their authorized issues if they held specie (gold) equal to the excess. Notes for less than £5 continued to be allowed in these regions. Consequently, six of the nine banks in Ireland and ten large banks in Scotland maintained the power to issue notes, which assisted their local branches. In Scotland, the power to issue notes was so essential to ordinary business that no important bank has been established since 1845 that lacked this power.

In Germany, the Reichsbank's average note circulation was between £60,000,000 and £70,000,000. Following a 1906 law, the Imperial Bank issued *Reichsbanknoten* in values of 20 marks (£1) and 50 marks (£2, 10s.), replacing previous treasury notes (*Reichskassenscheine*). These were added to existing notes of 5, 10, 100, and 1000 marks.

## Historical Precedents in Asia
The bank-note system was also utilized in Asia. The Mongols borrowed the system from the Kin Dynasty. Evidence of this includes a printing block in Si-ngan-fu used for Kin bank-notes, which were similar in shape and size to those of the Ming. Kin notes featured the characters *pao küan* (precious charter) and dated to the reign of *Chêng Yew* (1213–1216).

The Mongols first attempted to introduce bank-notes during the time of Ogodai Khan (1229–1242). At that time, taxes were collected in silk by weight on the project of the minister *Ye lü ch’u ts’ai*. It is assumed that the term *sze ch’ao* (bank-notes referring to the weight of silk) originates from this period. Later, under Kubilai, bank-note issuance was decreed, and silk became the standard for expressing the value of silver, with 1000 *liang* of silk estimated at 50 *liang* (or 1 *ting*) of silver. This led to the gradual transfer of rents and taxes from silk into silver.
