# The Ashanti

The Ashanti are a powerful African nation and former empire whose history is characterized by a strong military tradition and a complex relationship with European powers on the Gold Coast. Traditionally believed to have originated from fugitives driven south by Moslem tribes from the Niger and Senegal regions in the 16th or 17th century, the Ashanti established themselves in a region of impenetrable forest. This environment fostered a national character of valor, allowing them to rise as a conquering nation.

## Social and Political Structure
The Ashanti are composed of numerous tribes, including the Kumasi, Mampon, Adansi, Bekwai, Juabin, Kokofu, Nsuta, Nkwanta, Dadiassi, Daniassi, Ofinsu, and Adjisu. While each tribe has its own king, the king of Kumasi has been recognized as the paramount king of Ashanti since the beginning of the 18th century. The paramount king succeeds to the "golden stool," which serves as the symbol of authority.

The government was historically a blend of military aristocracy and monarchy. The state's social fabric was based on six great divisions or clans organized for war. The King's Council, consisting of clan chiefs and certain hereditary sub-chiefs, often held more power than the king himself, unless the monarch possessed an exceptionally strong character. Land is held communally by the tribes, with unallotted "stool lands" attached to the king or head chief. Socially, the Ashanti practice polygamy among those who can afford it.

## Early Expansion and European Contact
Osai Tutu is regarded as the real founder of Ashanti power. He extended the empire through conquests to the east and west, subduing Banna, the Mahommedan countries of Gaman (Jaman), and the state of Denkera in 1719. He also built or expanded the capital, Kumasi. Following his death in 1731, his successor, Osai Apoko, continued acquisitions toward the coast.

In 1800, King Osai Tutu Quamina sought to open communications with white nations. In 1807, after the Fanti refused to surrender two defeated Assin chiefs, Osai Tutu invaded Fantiland. This campaign resulted in the destruction of a town near Anamabo and the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants. Although the Ashanti failed to storm the English fort, a truce was reached. Colonel G. Torrane, the governor, acknowledged the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland and agreed to pay rent for the Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort.

## Conflicts with the British
The 19th century was marked by fluctuating relations with the British. In 1820, a treaty was drawn up by consul Joseph Dupuis acknowledging Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory, though this was disowned by Governor J. Hope Smith and the government in London. In 1824, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles M'Carthy at Essamako; the governor and eight Europeans were killed, and M'Carthy's skull was used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi.

Following a signal defeat at Dodowah on August 7, 1826, Ashanti power over coast tribes declined. By 1831, the king purchased peace from administrator George Maclean for 600 oz. of gold and provided his son as a hostage.

Under King Kwaka Dua I (who succeeded Osai Okoto in 1838), the nation experienced a period of peace and trade. However, tensions returned in 1852 when the Ashanti attempted to reassert authority over the Fanti. In 1863, a war broke out after the British refused to surrender a runaway slave-boy and a fugitive chief. The Ashanti won two battles and retired unmolested. King Kwaka Dua I noted that while "white men" brought cannons to the bush, "the bush is stronger than the cannon."

## The War of 1873-1874
Following the death of Kwaka Dua I in 1867 and a period of civil war, Kofi Karikari was enstooled, swearing that his business would be war. Two primary events triggered the subsequent conflict: the British occupation of Elmina fort on April 2, 1872—which the Ashanti considered their territory—and the captivity of four Europeans (Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, his wife, their infant son, Mr. J. Kuhne, and French trader M.J. Bonnat) in Kumasi.

On January 22, 1873, Ashanti forces invaded the British protectorate, defeating the Fanti and encamping at Mampon. The British responded by sending Sir Garnet Wolseley with a budget of £800,000. By the time Wolseley landed on October 2, the Ashanti forces had been decimated by fever and smallpox and were preparing to return home.

## Economy and Culture
The Ashanti are primarily spirit-worshippers and generally show a repugnance to Islam, though some traders are Mahommedans and some have converted to Christianity via Basel and Wesleyan missionaries. They are skilled in pottery, gold work, and weaving cotton. In 1874, masks of beaten gold and elaborate goldsmith work were discovered in the palace at Kumasi.

Economically, the Ashanti rely on the rubber tree, kola nuts, and gum copal. Since 1905, cocoa plantations have been established in the eastern districts, and tobacco is grown in the north. Gold mining is a major industry, particularly in the western districts and the Obuassi mines, which produced 68,259 oz. of gold in 1905. Kumasi serves as the primary distributing center for the region.
