# Battle of Essamako

The Battle of Essamako took place on January 21, 1824, at a location beyond the Prah called Essamako. The engagement was a significant military clash between the forces of the Ashanti and the British.

## Background and Political Context
The conflict arose from escalating tensions between the Ashanti and the coast natives, who received support from the British. In an attempt to cultivate relations with the powerful African kingdom, the British government appointed Mr. Joseph Dupuis as consul to Kumasi. Dupuis arrived at Cape Coast in January 1819 and reached Kumasi on February 28, 1820. Following meetings with the king, a treaty was established that acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over the territory of the Fanti and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of their enemies.

This treaty was disowned by Mr. J. Hope Smith, the governor of Cape Coast, who viewed the agreement as a betrayal of the natives under British protection. The government in London supported Mr. Hope Smith, and in 1821, the crown assumed direct control of the British settlements. Sir Charles M'Carthy was appointed as the first governor by the crown. Sir Charles espoused the cause of the Fanti, a position that led to the eventual military confrontation with the Ashanti.

## The Battle of January 21, 1824
The battle resulted in a defeat for the British forces. There was a significant disparity in manpower, with the Ashanti deploying 10,000 men against Sir Charles M'Carthy's force of 500. 

Sir Charles M'Carthy and eight other Europeans were killed during the engagement. Following the battle, the governor's skull was taken to Kumasi and used as a royal drinking-cup. There were assertions that the defeat was caused by an ordnance-keeper who brought up kegs filled with vermicelli instead of ammunition; however, records indicate that this mistake, if it occurred, merely hastened an inevitable catastrophe.

## Immediate Aftermath and Succession
The day of the defeat coincided with a change in Ashanti leadership; Osai Tutu Quamina died on January 21, 1824, and was succeeded by Osai Okoto. Following the battle, a period of chronic warfare ensued.

## Subsequent Conflicts and Resolution
The tide of the conflict shifted on August 7, 1826, when the Ashanti suffered a signal defeat at Dodowah. This event marked the beginning of the decline of Ashanti power over the coast tribes. The British also ceased the payment of ground rent for the forts they held after the battle of Dodowah.

By 1831, the king of Ashanti was forced to purchase peace from Mr. George Maclean, the administrator of the Gold Coast. The terms of this peace included a payment of 600 oz. of gold and the delivery of the king's son as a hostage to Cape Coast.
