# Clearing House

The Clearing House is a system used by banks to settle accounts with one another. This mechanism has been developed in various forms in both Great Britain and the United States to facilitate the processing of cheques and the settlement of financial claims.

## Development in Great Britain

By 1776, purely city banks had associated themselves into a "Clearing House." Evidence of an earlier, though perhaps less formally organized, system of settlement is found in the books of the Grasshopper, which record a quarterly charge of 19/6d. for the use of a "Clearing-room" in 1773. In 1810, a dedicated house was acquired for this purpose; during that year, forty-six banking houses settled their accounts with each other at the Clearing House.

The membership of the Clearing House has evolved over time. In 1844, of the forty-nine banks located in London, twenty-six were connected with the Clearing House. At that time, membership was restricted to private banks. Joint-stock banks were permitted to join the body in 1854. By 1906, the membership consisted of nineteen banks in total: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank.

Certain institutions remained outside the Clearing House. The Bank of England has never been a member, although it "clears on one side." This means that while its claims on clearing bankers are processed through the Clearing House, the claims that clearing bankers have against the Bank of England are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times a day. Additionally, banks located at Charing Cross or in Fleet Street did not belong to the Clearing House.

## Country Clearing and Business Impact

In 1858, arrangements made by Sir John Lubbock (then Lord Avebury) expanded the system to include the clearing of country cheques. This "country clearing" provided significant assistance to business operations by allowing a cheque drawn on a distant village in England to be handled as conveniently as one drawn on a London bank.

## The Clearing House System in the United States

The United States has achieved a higher development of the cheque clearing system than any other country, with the possible exception of Great Britain. Clearing houses are established in approximately 112 leading cities. For the year ending September 30, 1907, the aggregate clearings reached $154,662,515,258.

The New York Clearing-House handles a substantial portion of this activity. In 1906, its clearings accounted for 67.2% of the total clearings across 55 of the larger cities. The volume of these clearings is subject to great fluctuation, driven by the prosperity of business in the country and the volume of transactions on the stock exchange.
