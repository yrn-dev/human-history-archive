# Austria and Belgium: Political and Economic Relations

The historical relationship between Austria and Belgium has been characterized by periods of direct imperial rule, diplomatic negotiations among European powers, and later economic tensions regarding trade and tariffs.

## Austrian Rule and the Belgian Republic
During the late 18th century, Austria maintained control over the "Austrian Netherlands." This rule was interrupted by the emergence of the “République des États Belgiques unis.” The young republic was plagued by internal conflict between the conservative partisans of Van der Noot and the partisans of Vonck, the latter of whom were influenced by French revolutionary clubs and advocated for popular sovereignty and national assemblies.

External support for the Belgian Patriots had been provided by Prussia, Holland, and England. However, this support was motivated by the desire to maintain the European balance of power and to weaken Austria's ability to extend its policy toward Constantinople in eastern Europe, rather than a commitment to Belgian independence. Consequently, these powers betrayed the republic. Following a conference at Reichenbach involving Austria, Prussia, Holland, and England, it was decided to restore Austrian rule. This was formalized in the Treaty of the Hague (1790), which provided amnesty for the past and guaranteed the maintenance of ancient institutions. Emperor Leopold II subsequently re-occupied the territory, though this restored rule was short-lived as the French Revolution eventually drove the Austrians out of Belgium.

## Diplomatic Settlements and the 1831 Treaty
In the 19th century, the status of Belgium remained a point of contention among the great powers. Following the accession of King Leopold to the Belgian throne, negotiations were held at the Conference of London. While Leopold attempted to settle disputes through a declaration of eighteen articles, the Dutch King refused to recognize them and invaded Belgium on August 2. After the Belgian army suffered defeats at Hasselt and Louvain, a French army sent by Louis-Philippe intervened to repulse the Dutch.

The intervention of France alarmed England and other powers, who feared a resurgence of French influence. This led to the drafting of the "Twenty-four Articles," which stripped Belgium of most disputed areas, except for the district of Arlon in Luxemburg. Although the Belgians initially refused these terms, the threat of a German army invasion forced their accession. On November 15, 1831, the Treaty of the Twenty-four Articles was signed by England, France, and Belgium, and was subsequently assented to by Austria, Prussia, and Russia.

## Economic Relations and Protective Tariffs
By the late 19th century, the relationship between Austria and Belgium shifted toward commercial competition. In 1867, Austria-Hungary established a Customs Union. Initially, its commercial policy was based on treaties with nations including Great Britain, France, Italy, and Germany, which utilized "most favoured nation" clauses and resulted in a system that nearly lacked customs duties.

However, after 1873, Austrian manufacturers felt the pressure of competition from English, German, and Belgian goods. This led to a strong movement in favor of protective duties, influenced by a general shift in economic thought reacting against free trade doctrines. While Hungary initially favored free trade to secure markets for its agricultural produce, the commercial treaties expired after 1875. By 1877, a new treaty was established where raw materials remained duty-free, but low duties were placed on corn and textile goods, and excises on brandy and sugar were increased. This period marked the end of the system of commercial treaties, allowing Austria-Hungary to introduce tariffs based on legislation.

## Cultural and Individual Connections
Beyond politics and trade, there are recorded instances of cultural exchange. The Belgian painter and etcher Fernand Edmond Jean Marie Khnopff (born 1858) produced a work titled "The Empress" (1899), which entered the collection of the emperor of Austria. Additionally, the Italian general Giacomo Durando, who served in the Belgian army during the war of 1832, later commanded Lombard volunteers during the war with Austria in 1848.
