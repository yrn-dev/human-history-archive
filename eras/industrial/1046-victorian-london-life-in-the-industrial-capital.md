# Victorian London: Life in the Industrial Capital

## Overview
During the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), London evolved into the undisputed center of the global economy, serving as the heart of the British Empire and the vanguard of the Industrial Revolution. This era was defined by a jarring dichotomy: unprecedented wealth and imperial grandeur existing alongside systemic squalor and urban decay.

The city’s population exploded from roughly 2 million in 1840 to over 6 million by the century's end. This rapid urbanization outpaced infrastructure, leading to the infamous "Great Stink" of 1858, where the polluted Thames became an open sewer. The industrialization of the city brought the rise of massive docklands and factories, but it also created the "East End" slums, characterized by overcrowding and disease. While the Victorian era is often remembered for its strict moral codes and rigid class hierarchies, it was also a period of profound social reform, witnessing the birth of modern policing, public health acts, and the first underground railway.

## Key Facts

*   **The Class Divide:** Society was strictly stratified into the upper class (landed gentry and industrial magnates), the burgeoning middle class (clerks, managers, shopkeepers), and the working class. Historians often debate the "permeability" of these classes; while some argue the middle class provided a ladder for social mobility, others suggest that economic barriers remained largely insurmountable for the urban poor.
*   **Public Health:** Cholera outbreaks plagued the city until the mid-century. Dr. John Snow’s 1854 discovery that cholera was waterborne—rather than caused by "miasma" (bad air)—revolutionized urban planning and medicine.
*   **The Industrial Engine:** The Port of London was the busiest in the world, processing goods from every corner of the empire, which fueled the city's growth but also created a precarious economy dependent on global trade.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Fog was Chemical:** The famous "pea-soupers" were not natural fog but smog—a mixture of water vapor and thick soot from millions of coal-burning fireplaces and factories.
2. **The Mudlarks:** Impoverished children and adults, known as "mudlarks," scavenged the shores of the Thames at low tide for scrap metal, coal, or jewelry to sell for food.
3. **The First Tube:** The first underground trains were steam-powered, meaning tunnels had to be vented to prevent passengers from suffocating on smoke.
