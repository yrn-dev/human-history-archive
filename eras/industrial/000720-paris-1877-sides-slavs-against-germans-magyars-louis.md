# Slav and Magyar Relations in the Austrian Empire

## Ethnic Tensions and National Movements
Within the Austrian Empire, the Slav populations experienced growing restlessness under the domination of Germans and Magyars. In Bohemia, the Czech literary movement evolved into an organized resistance against the established order, utilizing the watchword "Repeal," a term borrowed from the criticism of English administration in Ireland. 

Among the southern Slavs, the "Illyrian" movement emerged around 1836, promoted by Ljudevit Gaj through the *Illyrian National Gazette*. Initially, this movement sought a shadowy Pan-Slav union. However, following interference from the Austrian government in 1844, the objective shifted toward the revival of the "Triune Kingdom"—comprising Dalmatia, Slavonia, and Croatia—independent of the Hungarian crown. In Hungary, these growing Slav separatist movements, particularly in the south, provoked a revival of the spirit of Magyar ascendancy.

## Conflict and the 1848 Revolutions
During the mid-19th century, political instability led to direct conflict between various ethnic and political factions. In Vienna, the radical populace clashed with both the government and the Slav majority of the Reichsrath. While German democrats sought assistance from the Hungarian government, the Magyars' commitment to constitutional legality caused delays that rendered the help ineffective.

During this period, Emperor Ferdinand fled to Olmuetz, a Slav district, from which he called upon those who loved "Austria and freedom" to support the throne. Subsequently, the members of the Right and the Slav majority of the Reichsrath abandoned Vienna for a meeting of the diet at Bruenn.

## The Role of Jellachich and the Croatian Alliance
Jellachich, the ban of Croatia, became a powerful representative for an alliance between conservative elements and Slav nationalities. Jellachich identified with the "Illyrian" party and broke communications with the Hungarian government. On June 9, he summoned a diet to Agram, which decreed that the "Triune Kingdom" separate from Hungary. 

Although the imperial government initially repudiated Jellachich's actions—accusing him of treason and stripping him of his military rank in hopes of maintaining Magyar aid against Viennese revolutionists—Jellachich's strategy was to use Illyrian nationalism to counter the radicalism of Germans and Magyars to preserve his province for the monarchy. This alliance was cemented on June 10, when Jellachich proclaimed that Croatian regiments in Italy should remain and fight for the emperor and the common Fatherland.

## Dualism and Political Inferiority
Later settlements in Austria established a principle of dualism that divided the empire into two portions: one ruled by Germans and the other by Magyars. This arrangement left the Slav races—including the Slovenes, Czechs, Poles, Serbs, and Croatians—in a position of political inferiority. While the settlement satisfied Hungarian demands, it removed any hope for Bohemians or Galicians to achieve similar independence.

## Late 19th Century Balkan Influence
By the late 19th century, disturbances in the Balkans, specifically an insurrection in Bosnia and Herzegovina, further complicated Austrian policy. The government of Vienna faced a dilemma: the Slav inhabitants of the empire sympathized with the suffering of Christians in Turkey, but the Hungarians felt sympathy for the Turks. 

Austria maintained a cardinal principle against the creation of new Slav states on its southern frontier, fearing the influence of Russian agents and the Pan-Slav party. In early 1877, a secret understanding was reached between Austria and Russia, where Russia agreed not to annex territory or take steps injurious to Austria. However, the Russian army's advance on Constantinople remained a menace to Austrian influence, leading Andrassy to demand a European conference in Vienna to determine the terms of peace.

## Early Historical Context
In earlier European history, the figure of Charlemagne is noted for having made war against various groups, including the Magyars and the Slavs, as well as the Saxons, Spanish Saracens, and other heathen German tribes. In the history of Budapest, the area was invaded by Slavs, Avars, Ostrogoths, and Huns after the Roman colony of Aquincum fell in A.D. 376. When the Magyars arrived at the end of the 10th century, they preserved the names of Buda and Pest; the name "Pest" is believed to derive from the old Slavonic *pestj*, meaning a stove, suggesting an early Slavonic settlement.
