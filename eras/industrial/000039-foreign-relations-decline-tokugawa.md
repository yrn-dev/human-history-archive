# Foreign Relations and the Decline of the Tokugawa

The decline of the Tokugawa Bakufu was a complex process driven by a combination of financial instability, internal political friction, and the challenges posed by foreign intercourse. While the Yedo Court maintained a strict policy of conservatism regarding external relations, the resulting tensions contributed to the eventual erosion of the shogunate's power.

## Policy of Seclusion and Foreign Intercourse
For a significant period, the Yedo Court adhered to a policy of hereditary conservatism. In 1825, the Bakufu issued a general order mandating that any foreign vessel coming within range of coast batteries be fired upon immediately. This harsh stance remained in effect until 1842, when the command was modified to allow ships driven into Japanese ports by stress of weather to receive provisions, such as food and water, provided they were warned to resume their voyage immediately.

Despite this policy of seclusion, the threat of foreign presence prompted the Bakufu to strengthen littoral defenses. This period saw an active revival in the study of military art across the empire. Simultaneously, a growing number of patriots began to openly condemn the policy of seclusion.

## Financial Decline and Internal Instability
The decline of the Tokugawa was attributed as much to an empty treasury as to foreign complications. During the first half of the nineteenth century, the Bakufu faced a financial crisis where expenditures constantly exceeded income. To manage this, the government repeatedly resorted to the debasing of currency, an expedient that undermined public respect for the administration.

This economic instability had several social and political consequences:
* **Alienation of the People:** The appreciation of commodities made the burden of living more severe for the general population.
* **Rise of the Tozama Daimyo:** Feudatories in the west of the empire, known as *tozama daimyo*, possessed weak loyalty to the Bakufu and used the administration's impecuniosity as an opportunity to assert themselves.
* **Failed Reforms:** The "Tempo Reformation" attempted to curb extravagance through sumptuary laws. These measures included sealing residences and storehouses, banishing inmates, and removing 570 restaurants from the city's most populous area, with the maidservants of those establishments being degraded to the class of "licensed prostitutes." The reformation ended in the resignation of its author and failed to achieve its purpose.

## Political Conflict and the Imperial Court
As the strength of the Bakufu waned, discussions regarding the restoration of administrative power to the sovereign increased in freedom. Historically, the functions of the Tokugawa were viewed by some as usurpations that deprived the Imperial Court of its proper place in the state polity.

Internal divisions grew within the leadership regarding how to handle foreign powers. Ii Naosuke (Kamon no Kami), a prime minister at Yedo, advocated for foreign intercourse and was responsible for putting through the Harris commercial treaty. This conciliatory foreign policy was strongly opposed by Tokugawa Nariaki, the daimyo of Mito. Nariaki’s court became a center for loyal soldiers and scholars, including Toda Tadanori and Fujita Toko. Ii Naosuke was eventually assassinated.

## The Shift in Authority
The relationship between the Yedo Bakufu and the Imperial Court shifted significantly during the reign of Emperor Komei. Traditionally, policies decided in Yedo were presented to the Imperial capital as accomplished facts. However, Emperor Komei departed from this sequence by formally instructing the Bakufu that the traditional policy of the empire in foreign affairs must be strictly maintained. 

The Emperor demonstrated his concern for the country's safety through fasting and praying at the shrines of national deities, offering his own life as a substitute for the safety of the nation. Unlike early Tokugawa shoguns who would have resented such interference, the twelfth shogun, Ieyoshi, acquiesced. Consequently, the Bakufu began submitting all questions of foreign policy to the Imperial Court before making final decisions.

The power of the Bakufu continued to diminish as the Crown and Court party interfered further. By 1861, the Bakufu pledged to drive out foreigners within ten years, but the overthrow of the Yedo administration had become an absorbing objective for many high officials in Kyoto.
