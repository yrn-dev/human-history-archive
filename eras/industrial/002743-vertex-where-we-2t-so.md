# Vertex

In various scientific and mathematical contexts, the vertex refers to a specific point of maximum, minimum, or central significance within a geometric curve, a physical trajectory, or a mechanical component.

## Ballistics and Trajectories
In the study of ballistics, the vertex (denoted as point A) is the highest point of a shot's trajectory. In direct fire—defined as fire from guns with full charge at an elevation not exceeding 15 degrees—the vertical component of air resistance is considered insensible. In such cases, the time to reach the vertex is $1/2T$, where $T$ represents the total time of flight from the origin (O) to the point (B) where the trajectory intersects the line of sight. At this vertex, the shot flies parallel to the line of sight.

The height of the vertex ($H$) can be calculated using the formula $H = 1/8gT^2$. For practical purposes, where $g$ is taken as 32, this is replaced by $H = 4T^2$ or $(2T)^2$. For example, if a shell has a time of flight of 5 seconds, the height of the vertex is approximately 100 feet.

In high-angle fire, the vertex reaches such heights that the tenuity of the air becomes a significant factor. Captain James M. Ingalls proposed a rule for approximating these trajectories in a single arc, based on the fact that in an unresisted parabolic trajectory, the average height of the shot is two-thirds the height of the vertex. In extreme cases, such as those involving 42-cm giant mortars, the vertex of the trajectory is located several kilometres above the ground.

For range table calculations, the vertex is utilized as the point of "half-time." Because velocity continually diminishes, the vertex is located beyond the half-range.

## Structural Engineering and Suspension Bridges
In the context of suspension bridges, a chain under a weight uniformly distributed relative to a horizontal line forms a parabola. The vertex of this curve is the point from which the distance $x$ to any other point $F$ is measured. The horizontal tension at the vertex, denoted as $H$, is expressed as $H = wL^2/8y$ for a span $L$, or $H = wx^2/2y$, where $y$ is the dip of the bridge.

The value of $H$ is equal to the compression on the top flange or the maximum tension on the bottom flange of a girder with an equal span and similar loading, provided the girder's depth equals the dip of the suspension bridge.

## Mechanical and Geometric Applications
The term vertex is also applied to specific mechanical components and geometric projections:

*   **Planimeters:** In early planimeters, such as those invented by Hermann in 1814, a cone (VCC') is used where the vertex $V$ is positioned opposite a point $Q'$. The rotation of the cone and the movement of a wheel resting on its edge allow for the measurement of an area.
*   **Cartography:** In the orthomorphic conical projection (Gauss's projection), the vertex of the cone is a theoretical point. When the value $h$ (as suggested by Sir John Herschel) reaches a limit of zero, the meridians become parallel, indicating that the vertex of the cone has been removed to infinity. This limit results in the characteristic equation of Mercator's projection.
*   **Girder Shear:** When a uniform train weighing $w$ per foot advances over a girder of span $2c$, the shear at the head of the train is represented by the ordinates of a parabola. This parabola has its vertex at point A.
