# Bacillus anthracis and the Work of Louis Pasteur

## Attenuation and Immunity
Around 1880, Louis Pasteur conducted research demonstrating that *Bacillus anthracis* could lose its virulence under specific cultivation conditions. He showed that when the bacillus was cultivated in chicken broth at a temperature of 42-43°C with an abundance of oxygen, it ceased to kill mice after a few "generations." These observations were later confirmed by Chauveau and Toussaint and further extended by other researchers.

Pasteur discovered that animals inoculated with these "attenuated" bacilli developed a curious resistance to the deadly effects of subsequent inoculations using the non-attenuated, wild form of the bacterium. This process established that animals vaccinated with the cultivated bacillus showed immunity from the disease. Along with Toussaint and Chauveau in France, and W. S. Greenfield, J. S. Burdon-Sanderson, and Duguid in Great Britain, Pasteur was a pioneer in producing active immunity through the use of attenuated viruses.

## Bacteriological Context and Discoveries
Pasteur's demonstration that bacteria could produce definite diseases served as a significant stimulus for research into the etiology of infective conditions. This period of rapid advancement in human knowledge was further aided by Robert Koch, who published an account of the "plate-method" and the introduction of solid culture media in 1881, which simplified the separation and cultivation of bacteria. 

By 1876, Koch had obtained *Bacillus anthracis* in pure culture. The decade between 1880 and 1890 became a critical era for the field, during which the organisms responsible for cholera, typhoid fever, diphtheria, glanders, tetanus, tubercle, and suppuration were identified. Later discoveries in the final decade of the 19th century included the bacilli of plague (1894), influenza (1892), and dysentery (1898).

## Morphology and Reproduction
*Bacillus anthracis* is characterized as a rod or rodlet—an elongated cylindrical cell. In certain conditions, such as a three-hour culture in a drop of aqueous humour, these rodlets grow into septate, long *leptothrix*-like filaments.

A defining characteristic of *B. anthracis* is the formation of spores, which serve as a resting stage for the organism. The process begins with a minute granule appearing in the protoplasm of a vegetative cell. This granule enlarges over several hours, absorbing all the protoplasm and secreting a resistive thin envelope to become a ripe ovoid spore. These spores are smaller than the mother-cell and lie loosely within it.

## Resistance and Environmental Factors
The spores of *B. anthracis* exhibit significant resilience, capable of withstanding temperatures of 100°C and above. However, research conducted by Marshall Ward between 1892 and 1893 proved that these spores can be killed by exposure to rays of reflected light, specifically the ultra-violet and blue-violet rays of the spectrum, even at temperatures that are not injurious or are actually favorable to growth. Ward's experiments demonstrated that this bactericidal action occurs even in the absence of food materials, meaning it is not a result of the medium being poisoned.

The archive notes that the attenuation of virulence can also result from light exposure if the intensity is insufficient or the duration too short to kill the spores entirely. This highlights the importance of ventilation and lighting in dwellings and the purification of streams.

## Related Bacteriological Concepts
The study of bacteria during this era also involved the investigation of pleomorphism, or the mutability of shape. While some researchers like Billroth suggested all bacteria were different states of a single organism, it is generally agreed that while some forms show different cell types during their life-history, the majority are uniform.

Additionally, the development of the pasteurizer—named after Pasteur—was designed to destroy the tubercle bacillus in milk. Effective pasteurization requires specific temperatures, durations, and mechanical agitation to prevent the formation of a "scalded layer" or membrane, which can protect bacilli and allow them to resist heat. For example, heating milk in closed pasteurizers for at least twenty minutes at 140°F (60°C) is recommended to destroy tubercle bacilli without injuring the consistency or creaming properties of the milk.
