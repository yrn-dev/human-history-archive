## Bridge Engineering and Girder Weight
In the design of bridges, the weight of main girders ($W_g$) is calculated to carry their own weight as well as the live load ($W_l$) and the weight of the platform ($W_f$). The relationship is expressed as $W_g = (W_l+W_f)k/(1-k)$. Because $W_l+W_f$ is known during the design phase, the value $k(W_l+W_f)$ can be determined through a provisional design that neglects the weight of the girders. The final bridge members must then have sections larger than those in the provisional design by a ratio of $k/(1-k)$.

Empirical relations provided by Waddell allow for the comparison of girder weights per foot run. If $w_1$ and $w_2$ are weights for spans $l_1$ and $l_2$ with a live load $p$, the ratio is $w_2/w_1 = ½ [l_2/l_1+(l_2/l_1)^2]$. When live loads change to $p'$, the ratio $w_2'/w_2$ is $1/5(1+4p'/p)$.

Unwin (1869) provided a partially rational approximate formula for the weight of main girders ($w_3$):
$w_3 = (w_1+w_2)l^2/(Cds-l_2) = (w_1+w_2)lr/(Cs-lr)$
In this formula:
*   $w_1$ is the total live load per foot run.
*   $w_2$ is the weight of the platform per foot run.
*   $l$ is the span in feet.
*   $s$ is the average stress on the gross section of metal.
*   $d$ is the depth of the girder at the center.
*   $r$ is the ratio of span to depth ($l/d$).
*   $C$ is a constant based on the girder type.

For iron braced parallel or bowstring girders, $C$ ranges from 1500 to 1800, while for steel girders, it ranges from 1200 to 1500. A related constant, $K$, ranges from 6000 to 7200 for iron and 7200 to 9000 for steel bridges.

## Bending Moments and Shear in Bridges
The greatest bending moment on a beam occurs when one of the greatest loads is at the section. For a series of loads ($W_1, W_2, ... W_n$) traveling across a bridge, the position for the greatest bending moment at section $ab$ is satisfied when the load $W_x$ is at $ab$ and the following conditions are met:
*   $x W_1+W_2+... W_{x-1}$ is greater than $l W_1+W_2+... W_n$
*   $x W_1+W_2+... W_x$ is less than $l W_1+W_2+... W_n$

When loads $W_1, W_2, W_3$ traverse a girder at fixed distances $a$ and $b$, the bending moments describe parabolas ($M_1, M_2, M_3$) with middle ordinates of $¼W_1l, ¼W_2l,$ and $¼W_3l$.

Regarding shear, the greatest shear at a section $C$ may occur when $W_1$ is at $C$. If $W_1$ passes $C$, the shear may increase when $W_2$ reaches $C$, provided that $Ra/l+d > W_1$, where $R$ is the resultant of the loads and $d$ is the reaction at $B$ due to additional loads. If the load is distributed by flooring to points $A$ and $B$, the loads become $W(p-x)/p$ and $Wx/p$.

## Determination of Relative Density
The archive details several methods for determining the relative density or specific gravity of substances.

### Hydrostatic Weighing
This method involves weighing a solid in air ($W$) and then in water ($W_1$). The relative density is the quotient $W/(W - W_1)$. For solids lighter than water, a sinker is used; the relative density is then $W/(W + w - W_1)$, where $w$ is the weight of the sinker in water. 

Practical application requires suspending the solid by a fibre (such as platinum or silk) and immersing it in distilled water using a beaker or a specific gravity pan. To ensure accuracy, air bubbles are removed with a camel-hair brush, and porous solids are boiled to expel air.

### Other Density Methods
*   **Specific Gravity Bottle:** Used for insoluble solids available in small fragments. The relative density is $W/(W + W_1 - W_2)$, where $W$ is the solid's weight in air, $W_1$ is the bottle full of liquid, and $W_2$ is the bottle containing the solid and liquid.
*   **Weighing Equal Volumes:** Used for liquids. A glass vessel is weighed empty ($w$), filled with the liquid ($W$), and filled with a standard substance ($W_1$). The relative density is $(W - w)/(W_1 - w)$.
*   **Say's Stereometer:** A method to determine volume $u$ based on the equation $u = al^1(h - k^1)/k^1$. This method is susceptible to errors from moisture and variations in atmospheric pressure and temperature.

For high-accuracy absolute density ($\Delta(0^\circ/4^\circ)$), the formula $\Delta(0^\circ/4^\circ) = (\rho\alpha W - \delta W_1)/(W - W_1)$ is used, accounting for the density of air ($\delta$), the density of water ($\rho$), and the coefficient of cubical expansion of the body ($\alpha$).
