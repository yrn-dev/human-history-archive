# Chain Loaded Uniformly along a Horizontal Line

In the study of structural mechanics and bridge engineering, the behavior of a chain depends on the distribution of the load it carries. When the lengths of the links in a chain are assumed to be indefinitely short, the chain takes the form of mathematical curves known as catenaries under simple load distributions.

## Catenaries and Parabolas
A true catenary is the curve assumed by a chain that possesses a uniform weight per unit of length. However, for the construction of suspension bridges, the form generally adopted is the curve assumed by a chain under a weight that is uniformly distributed relative to a horizontal line. This specific curve is a parabola.

In such a system, the horizontal tension ($H$) at the vertex for a span ($L$), where the points of support are at equal heights, is expressed as $H = wL^2/8y$. Alternatively, if $x$ is defined as the distance from the vertex to the point of support, the expression is $H = wx^2/2y$. The value of $H$ is equivalent to the compression on the top flange or the maximum tension on the bottom flange of a girder with an equal span and similar loading, provided the girder's depth equals the dip of the suspension bridge.

## Mathematical Properties of the Parabolic Chain
For any point $F$ on the curve at a distance $x$ from the vertex, the horizontal component of the resultant remains unaltered. The vertical component ($V$) is the sum of the loads between the vertex ($O$) and point $F$, calculated as $wx$. 

The relationship between the horizontal tension ($H$) and the vertical force ($V$) is given by the ratio $H : V = wx^2/2y : wx = x/2 : y$. This confirms the curve is a parabola. The tension ($R$) at any point at a distance $x$ from the vertex is determined by the equation $R^2 = H^2 + V^2 = w^2x^4/4y^2 + w^2x^2$, which simplifies to $R = wx\sqrt{1 + x^2/4y^2}$.

Additionally, if $i$ is the angle between the tangent at any point with coordinates $x$ and $y$ measured from the vertex, then $\tan i = 2y/x$. The length of half the parabolic chain ($s$) is expressed as $s = x + 2y^2/3x$.

## Bridge Loading and Stresses
Suspension bridges and other structures are subject to various types of loads:
*   **Dead Load:** The total weight of the superstructure (excluding parts carried directly by piers), including wind bracing, flooring, and main girders. This is typically treated as uniformly distributed over the span.
*   **Live or Temporary Load:** For road bridges, this is the weight of a heavy wagon, traction engine, or a dense uniformly distributed crowd. For railway bridges, it is the weight of the heaviest likely train.
*   **Impact:** An allowance for dynamical action caused by irregularities in the permanent way, yielding of the structure, or lack of vertical balance in locomotive moving parts.
*   **Horizontal Pressure:** Pressure caused by wind blowing transversely to the span, which is significant in high and long bridges.
*   **Longitudinal Drag:** Friction from a braked train, estimated at about one-seventh of the train's weight.
*   **Centrifugal Load:** On curved bridges, the horizontal force exerted by a train is calculated as $wv^2/gr$ tons, where $w$ is the locomotive weight, $r$ is the radius of curvature, and $v$ is the velocity in feet per second.

## Environmental and Structural Factors
Temperature changes can set up stresses in suspension and arch bridges equivalent to external loads. In Europe, a temperature variation of $126^\circ\text{F}$ ($70^\circ\text{C}$) is commonly assumed, resulting in an expansion of approximately 1 inch per 100 feet. In steel structures, if expansion is prevented, a stress of one ton per square inch is set up for every $12^\circ$ change in temperature. To mitigate this, structures are generally anchored at one point and left free to move in other directions.

## Case Study: Tower Bridge, London
The Tower Bridge in London serves as an example of a suspension bridge featuring a secondary bascule bridge in the center span. The bridge has a total length of 940 feet, with approaches of 1260 feet to the north and 780 feet to the south. The width between parapets is 60 feet, narrowing to 49 feet across the center span.

The suspension chains are constructed as braced girders to maintain stiffness against unsymmetrical loading. These chains are carried across the center span as horizontal ties resting on high-level footway girders. These ties are connected to hanging chains via pins 20 inches in diameter, surrounded by half-rings 5 inches thick to distribute wear. The load at these joints is transmitted to the steel columns of the towers via rocker bearings.
