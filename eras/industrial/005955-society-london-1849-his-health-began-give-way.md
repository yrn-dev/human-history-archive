# Societies of London

The city of London has served as the center for a diverse array of organizations, ranging from financial associations and religious missions to scientific bodies and charitable societies. These organizations have played significant roles in the social, spiritual, and economic development of the region and beyond.

## Building Societies
In London, early financial organizations that would later be known as building societies often avoided that specific title. Instead, they utilized descriptive names such as "Investment Association," "Savings Fund," "Accumulating Fund," or "Mutual Associations." Some were identified as "Societies of Equality" or "Societies for obtaining freehold property."

The legal definition of these entities was clarified by the act of 1836, which described them as societies intended to allow every member to receive the value of a share or shares to purchase or erect a dwelling-house. The act also permitted members who did not wish to purchase property to receive the value of their shares, including interest payments made by those who had been advanced shares. While the term "Benefit Building Society" appeared in the 1836 act, it did not become the sole distinctive name for these societies in England until 1849. Despite the name, these societies typically did not engage in building operations themselves; rather, they functioned as a means for the investment of small savings and provided funds to members for the purchase of land or the construction of houses.

## Religious and Missionary Societies
London has been a hub for various religious movements and the administration of international missions.

### The Salvation Army
Founded by William Booth, who arrived in London in 1849, the Salvation Army evolved through several earlier iterations. Booth, originally a local preacher for the Wesleyan Methodists, was severed from that body due to his passion for open-air preaching. After a period with the Methodist New Connexion, he returned to London in 1864 to conduct services in tents and the open air. He founded a body that was known successively as the East London Christian Mission, the East London Revival Society, the Christian Mission, and finally, in 1878, the Salvation Army.

The organization is structured as a quasi-military body, with "Orders and Regulations" modeled on the British army. Its methods include:
* Conducting processions and outdoor meetings.
* Visiting prisons, private houses, and public-houses.
* Holding meetings in factories, theatres, and other unusual buildings.
* Utilizing everyday language and popular song-tunes.
* Encouraging every convert to act as a daily witness for Christ.

Early efforts were met with violent opposition from a "Skeleton Army" organized to disrupt meetings, leading to the imprisonment and fining of Booth's followers for breaking the peace, though such disorders had largely ceased by 1889.

### Other Religious Organizations
London is also the seat of the Baptist South African Missionary and Colonial Aid Society, which assists Baptist work in South Africa. Additionally, the city has been a center for the publication of works related to the British and Foreign Bible Society, including histories by G. Browne (1859) and J. Owen (1816-1820).

## Scientific and Professional Societies
London has hosted prominent scientific organizations, most notably the Geological Society of London. William Buckland, an English divine and geologist, served as the president of this society twice, in 1824 and again in 1840. 

Furthermore, the Society of Arts has recognized professional achievement in the city; for example, Sir Henry Doulton, a pottery manufacturer based in Lambeth, was awarded the Albert medal by the Society of Arts.
