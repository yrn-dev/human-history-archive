# Indian System of Triangulation

The Indian system of geodetic triangulation serves as the scientific foundation for exact surveys across a vast portion of Asia. By the close of the 19th century, the final links of the great geodetic triangulation of India were forged regarding the peninsula. This system provides the basis for exact surveys extending as far east as the eastern boundaries of Burma, at a longitude of approximately 100 degrees East.

## Extensions and Geographical Reach
Beyond the primary geodetic framework, the Indian triangulation has been pushed outwards in all directions through minor extensions. While these extensions have been carried out on systems involving varying degrees of irregularity, they have significantly increased the accuracy of Asiatic geographical surveys.

These extensions reach into the following regions:
* **West and South:** Through Baluchistan and Afghanistan to the eastern districts of Persia, and along the coast of Makran to the coast of Arabia.
* **North and East:** The system has included the farther mountain peaks of Nepal and has branched outwards into Siam and towards western China.

These far-reaching extensions provide the necessary basis for extensive exploratory surveys of a strictly geographical nature.

## Determination of Lhasa
A significant achievement of the Indian system of triangulation occurred in 1904. Following the mission of Sir Francis Younghusband to Lhasa, the triangulation system was extended to finally determine the geographical position of that city. This period of exploration also included valuable reconnaissance of the Indus and Upper Brahmaputra valleys, conducted by Captains C.G. Rawling and C.H.D. Ryder.

## Connection with Russian Surveys
While a direct process of earth measurement to bring Asia into connection with Europe via the expansion of principal triangulation is still awaited, a topographical connection has been established between the Indian and Russian surveys. 

This connection demonstrates that the deductive methods used by both countries to determine the co-ordinate values of fixed points are in sufficient agreement. Consequently, it is believed that no difficulties in adjustment between Russian and Indian mapping need be feared for the purposes of future Asiatic cartography.

## Longitude and the Telegraphic Method
To determine longitudes differentially with Greenwich, a more rigorous application of the telegraphic method has been employed since 1890. This process resulted in a slight correction of about 2" of arc to previous determinations made through Suez. This specific determination was executed through four distinct arcs:
1. Greenwich to Potsdam
2. Potsdam to Teheran
3. Teheran to Bushire
4. Bushire to Karachi

In the arc between Teheran and Potsdam (3000 m.), the current took 0^S.20 to travel. Despite uncertainty caused by the retardation of the current, the final value is likely correct to within 0^S.05. The final result of this determination placed the Madras observatory 2' 27" to the west of the position previously adopted based on absolute astronomical determinations.
