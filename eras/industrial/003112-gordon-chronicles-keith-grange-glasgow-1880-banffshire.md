# Banffshire

Banffshire is a north-eastern county of Scotland. It is bounded to the north by the Moray Firth, to the east and south by Aberdeenshire, and to the west by Inverness and Elgin. The county covers an area of 633-1/2 square miles, or 403,364 acres.

## Geography and Geology
The surface of the county is diversified. The northern half consists primarily of rich, highly-cultivated soil and open, undulating country. The southern half is mountainous, featuring fertile glens with extensive farms, as well as mountains covered in brown heath, forests, or a mixture of copse and rock. The principal mountains are located in the south, including Cairngorm (4,084 ft.), which is known for amber-coloured quartz crystals used in Scots jewelry, Ben Rinnes (2,775 ft.), Corryhabbie (2,563 ft.), Cook's Cairn (2,478 ft.), Carn an t-Saidhe (2,401 ft.), and the Buck of Cabrach (2,368 ft.).

The county's largest lochs are found in the extreme south: Loch Etchachan (3,100 ft.), Loch Avon (2,300 ft.), and Loch Builg (1,586 ft.). While no great rivers belong entirely to Banffshire, the Deveron forms the eastern boundary and the Spey forms the western boundary for considerable parts of their courses. The Spey and Deveron are famous for salmon, while other chief streams—including the Livet, Deskford (which features a series of cascades), Buckie, Isla, Fiddich, and Avon—are stocked with trout. Notable romantic scenery is found in the great glens, specifically Glen Rinnes, Glen Livet, Glen Isla, Glen Fiddich, Glen Barry, and Glen Avon.

Geologically, Banffshire is closely connected to Elgin and Aberdeenshire. Much of the area is occupied by crystalline schists of sedimentary origin from the Eastern Highland sequence. These include quartzite (Schiehallion), the main limestone, and phyllites, black schists, and slates with thin black limestone. These groups form subparallel belts trending south-west and north-east from the seacoast between Portsoy and Cullen, passing through Dufftown and Keith toward the head waters of the Avon beyond Tomintoul. 

The quartzite forms prominent ridges, such as the Binn of Cullen, the Knock Hill, Ben Aigan, Durn Hill near Portsoy, and ridges trending from Grange by Glen Fiddich toward Tomintoul. The main limestone has been worked at Dufftown, Keith, and Fordyce (near Grange, east of Keith). In the north-east, between Troup Head and Macduff (except for a small part at Gamrie), there is a large development of slate with pebbly grits and interbedded greywackes, which has been quarried for roofing. These metamorphic sediments have been pierced by basic and acid igneous intrusions.

## Population and Administration
As of 1901, the population of Banffshire was 61,488, down slightly from 61,684 in 1891, resulting in a density of 97 persons per square mile. In 1901, 499 persons spoke English and Gaelic. The chief towns are Banff (population 7,161 in 1901), Buckie (6,549), and Keith (4,753), followed by Cullen (1,936), Portsoy (1,878), and Dufftown (1,823).

Administratively, Banffshire forms a sheriffdom along with Kincardine and Aberdeen shires. A resident sheriff-substitute is based in Banff and also sits in Dufftown, Buckie, and Keith. The county returns one member to parliament, while the royal burghs of Cullen and Banff belong to the Elgin group of parliamentary burghs.

## Economy and Infrastructure
The county's trade and manufactures include engineering works in Keith, Portsoy, and Banff, as well as woollen factories in Gollachy, Rothiemay, and Dufftown. Distilleries are numerous and highly reputed. Fishing is a significant industry, with fishery districts centering in Buckie and Banff. Trade is conducted at numerous creeks and harbours along the coast, including Port Gordon, Cullen, Portsoy, Gardenstown, Buckie, Macduff, and Banff. White fishing occurs year-round, while the herring season runs from September to June. Additionally, granite is quarried and there are large deposits of limestone.

Communication is provided by the Highland and Great North of Scotland railways, which serve the chief towns and connect the county to Inverness, Nairn, Elgin, and Aberdeen.

## Education
Most schools in the county are under the jurisdiction of school boards. The county council uses a "residue grant" to subsidize classes in veterinary science, navigation, agriculture, laundry work, and cookery. Furthermore, teachers in Banffshire, along with those in Elgin and Aberdeenshire, benefit from a bequest of over £110,000 left by James Dick (1743-1828), a West India merchant. This bequest provides an annual income of £4,000 distributed to "dominies" who qualify via examination to promote higher learning.
