# Proposals for an Economical and Secure Currency

In his work *Proposals for an Economical and Secure Currency*, David Ricardo examines the mechanisms by which a bank achieves prosperity and serves its community. According to Ricardo, a bank would not be established if its only profits were those derived from the employment of its own capital. Instead, the real advantage a bank provides to the community begins when it employs the capital of others.

## The Role of Bank Capital and Deposits
A bank must possess sufficient capital of its own to establish the standing necessary to collect capital from other parties. However, the bank does not hoard this capital; it holds entrusted funds only until they can be utilized through advances. The money controlled by a bank—taking the form of issued notes or received deposits—is loaned out to borrowers who can demonstrate they are trustworthy.

Ricardo notes that while some deposits remain with the bank for a customer's ordinary everyday needs, the vast majority of deposits allow the bank to make advances to individuals who employ the funds in "reproductive industry." This type of industry is characterized by its ability to bring back a greater value than the original amount lent, thereby maintaining profitable enterprises and assisting the business development of the country. While some loans may be used for extravagance, Ricardo asserts these can only be a small proportion of the total, as capital can only truly be replaced through reproductive industry. In cases where a loan is repaid via the sale of a security, it is viewed as a transfer of capital from one hand to another; otherwise, the money must be made by the owner.

## Principles of Safe Banking
For a bank to conduct its business on safe lines, Ricardo establishes one absolute rule regarding loans, provided the security is complete: the advance should be of floating capital rather than fixed capital.

## Broader Currency Principles and Stability
The archive contains further context regarding the stability and nature of currency. The practical purpose of currency is to ensure that the money a person receives for their work or for relinquishing property can purchase a fairly equivalent amount of "consumable goods," which includes items such as housing, medical advice, lectures, and theatrical entertainment. A healthy state of currency exists when the community is assured that money will not deteriorate in purchasing power.

However, fluctuations occur based on the availability of consumable goods. An increase in these goods increases the purchasing power of currency (unless the amount of currency also increases). Conversely, a diminution of goods—such as the destruction caused by war—leads to a rise in prices and wages. For example, the explosion of a single large shell in a modern war destroys labor and material equivalent to a year's holiday for a man or a comfortable cottage, thereby diminishing the purchasing value of every coin globally.

## Currency Demoralization and Gresham's Law
A partially demoralized currency leads to rising prices and makes workers irritable and suspicious. In such conditions, "sharp" individuals seek to hold money for the shortest time possible, demanding the most for their "reality" and quickly reinvesting in reality to rid themselves of "perishable" currency paper. This results in suffering for those with saved accumulations and fixed incomes, as the real value of wages declines. An entirely demoralized currency would eventually end all trade and work that cannot be conducted via barter or payment in kind.

This instability is often linked to "Gresham's Law," a term suggested in 1857 by H. D. Macleod (though the principle was previously set forth by Copernicus and Oresme). The law is summarized as "bad money drives out good." Specifically, the worst form of currency in circulation regulates the value of the entire currency and drives other forms out. This occurs in three primary scenarios:
1. When debased or under-weight coin circulates alongside full-weight coin of the same metal.
2. When two metals circulate and one is undervalued relative to the other.
3. When inconvertible paper money circulates alongside metallic currency.

## Historical Standards and Experiments
Historically, nations have fluctuated between different standards. England accepted the gold standard by law in 1816. France and the United States legally established a double standard in 1803 and 1792, respectively. However, gold discoveries in Australia (1851) and California (1848) led to the displacement of silver by gold in France and the United States. In the U.S., a law in 1834 over-rated gold (1:16) compared to the French ratio (1:15 1/2), which expelled most circulating silver.

Other attempts at currency stability included Owen's experiments with "labour notes," which represented one, five, or twenty hours of work. Owen believed that economic justice was impossible while paying for work with money of fluctuating value.
