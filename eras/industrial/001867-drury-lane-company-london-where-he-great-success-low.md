# Drury Lane Theatre

The Drury Lane theatre in London served as a primary venue for English drama and opera over several centuries, hosting a wide array of prominent actors, managers, and playwrights. Its history is marked by the careers of celebrated tragedians and comedians, as well as periods of complex managerial transitions and artistic experimentation.

## Notable Actors and Performances
Throughout its history, Drury Lane was a center for some of the most recognized talents of the English stage. Barton Booth, educated at Westminster school and formerly of a theatrical company in Dublin, performed at Drury Lane for twenty years. Booth was highly regarded as a tragedian, particularly for his roles as Brutus, Hotspur, and the title character in Addison's *Cato*. His portrayal of the ghost in *Hamlet* was considered unsurpassed, and his Lear was compared to that of Garrick.

Spranger Barry, a Dublin-born actor and son of a silversmith, made his first London appearance at Drury Lane in 1746 as Othello. His success in *Macbeth* and *Hamlet* led him to alternate roles with Garrick, and his performance as Romeo was so successful it aroused Garrick's jealousy, eventually leading Barry to move to Covent Garden in 1750.

Other significant performers included Robert Baddeley, who became a regular member of the company in 1763 and achieved great success in servants' parts and low comedy. Baddeley was the original Moses in *The School for Scandal*. His wife, Sophia Baddeley, an actress and singer of great beauty, made her stage debut at Drury Lane on April 27, 1765, as Ophelia. Thomas King, engaged by Garrick after being spotted as a strolling player in Windsor, debuted at the theatre in 1748 as the Herald in *King Lear*. King later reached the peak of his reputation at Drury Lane when he created the role of Sir Peter Teazle in the 1777 first representation of *The School for Scandal*. Additionally, John Bannister, described as the best low comedian of his day, performed at Drury Lane in 1778 in James Miller's version of Voltaire's *Mahomet* as Zaphna.

## Management and Administration
The administration of Drury Lane saw various leaders with differing fortunes. John Bannister served as manager in 1802 and was considered successful in this role. Later, Alfred Bunn was appointed stage-manager in 1823. By 1833, Bunn undertook the joint management of Drury Lane and Covent Garden. This period was marked by significant conflict, including vigorous opposition and a longstanding quarrel with the tragedian Macready, which resulted in Macready assaulting Bunn. 

Bunn faced further difficulties with his company and the lord chamberlain, as well as keen rivalry from other theatres. Although a bill to abolish patent theatres was passed in the House of Commons, it was thrown out by the House of Lords following a petition by Bunn. Despite being declared a bankrupt in 1840, Bunn continued to manage Drury Lane until 1848. Artistically, his tenure was successful; nearly every leading English actor played under his management, and he attempted to establish English opera by producing the principal works of Balfe, translating most of the libretti himself.

## Playwrights and Literary Connections
Drury Lane was the site of both popular successes and volatile receptions for various writers. Hugh Kelly, an Irish dramatist, had his first comedy, *False Delicadey*, produced by Garrick at Drury Lane on January 23, 1768. While Samuel Johnson found the work "totally void of character," it was very popular. However, Kelly's second comedy, *A Word to the Wise*, produced at Drury Lane on March 3, 1770, caused riots during its first two performances and had to be abandoned.

The theatre also had early connections to the London landscape; in 1610, Sir Robert Drury of Hawsted provided an apartment in his large house in Drury Lane to the poet Donne and his wife. In the realm of early acting companies, Edward Kynaston, known for his beauty and his skill in female parts, was part of a company set up in the Cockpit in Drury Lane.

## Traditions and Legacy
The theatre maintained specific institutional traditions, some stemming from the bequests of its performers. Robert Baddeley, who died on November 20, 1794, left property to establish a home for decayed actors. He also bequeathed £3 per annum to provide cake and wine in the green-room of the Drury Lane theatre on Twelfth Night, a ceremony that remained a regular institution.
