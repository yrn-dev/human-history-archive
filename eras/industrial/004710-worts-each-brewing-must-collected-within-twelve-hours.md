# Wort in the Brewing Process

Wort is the liquid extracted from the mashing of malt and other materials, serving as the primary substance that is transformed into beer through fermentation. Its production, handling, and measurement are subject to both chemical requirements for quality and stringent legal regulations for taxation.

## Production and Processing
The creation of wort begins with the grinding of malt into "grist," which is then mixed with hot water in a mashing machine and transferred to a mash-tun. In the mash-tun, the starch of the malt is rendered soluble, resulting in clear wort. In some breweries, the filter press process is used instead of the ordinary mash-tun process; this allows for finer grists and more efficient washing of the wort from the filter cake, leading to increased yields.

Once extracted, the wort follows a specific sequence of processing:
1. **Boiling**: The wort passes to a copper (either open or closed) where it is boiled with hops for one to three hours. This process sterilizes the wort, concentrates it, and extracts hop resin (a preservative and bitter), hop tannin (which precipitates proteids), and volatile hop oil for aroma. Boiling also coagulates nitrogenous matter that would otherwise cause cloudiness in the finished beer.
2. **Separation and Cooling**: The boiled wort moves to a hop back to separate insoluble hop constituents. It is then passed through a cooler and a refrigerator. In some modern plants, hot wort trickles over pipes cooled by circulating water, and the cooled liquid is collected in trays.
3. **Collection and Fermentation**: The wort is moved to a collecting vessel—often used by revenue officers to assess duty—before finally entering fermenting vessels to become "green" beer.

## Chemical Composition and Quality
The nitrogenous constituents of malt are as critical to the wort as starch. Yeast requires nitrogenous compounds, such as amides, for vigorous fermentation. A primary goal for the maltster is to break down barley proteins so the wort provides maximum nutritive value for the yeast. Additionally, degrading these proteins is necessary to eliminate nitrogenous matter to ensure the beer is stable, while the presence of albumoses is believed to assist in the beer's foaming properties.

## Measurement and Gravity
In the brewing industry, "gravity" refers to the specific gravity of the wort, though the brewer's scale is 1000 times that of the physicist. For example, a wort with a physicist's specific gravity of 1.045 is referred to by a brewer as having a gravity of 1045, or "forty-five degrees of gravity." 

The "original gravity" is the specific gravity of the wort before fermentation begins. This measurement allows brewers to determine the percentage of solids in the original wort by dividing the excess of the gravity over 1000 by 3.86. In trade, gravity is often expressed in pounds per barrel, representing the weight excess of a barrel of wort (36 gallons) over a barrel of water (360 lb).

## Legal Regulations and Taxation
Brewing operations are governed by strict regulations, particularly regarding the recording of materials and worts for tax purposes. Under the act of 1880, specific equivalents were established: 42 lb of corn or 28 lb of sugar were deemed equivalent to one bushel of malt. Brewers were expected to produce at least one barrel (36 gallons) of worts at standard gravity for every two bushels of malt (or equivalents), minus a 4% allowance for wastage.

The regulations for mashing are highly stringent:
* **Notice**: A brewer must enter the day and hour for commencing mashing or dissolving sugar in a brewing book provided by the Inland Revenue at least 24 hours in advance.
* **Materials**: The quantity of sugar, corn, and malt to be used, as well as the hour when worts will be drawn off the grains, must be entered at least two hours before mashing.
* **Collection**: The worts of each brewing must be collected within twelve hours of the start of collection.
* **Storage**: After collection, worts must remain undisturbed in the same vessel for twelve hours unless an officer has already taken account of them.
* **Documentation**: The brewer must record the gravity and quantity of the worts before fermentation, the name and number of the vessel, and the date of entry.

Mixing worts from different brewings is prohibited unless each has been accounted for separately.
