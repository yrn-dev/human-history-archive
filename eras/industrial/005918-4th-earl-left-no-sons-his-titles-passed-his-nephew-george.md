# Succession of the 4th Earl of Buckinghamshire

The succession of the 4th Earl of Buckinghamshire is characterized by the transition of titles to a nephew due to the absence of direct male heirs. This event initiated a series of successions and name changes within the Hobart family.

## Succession of George Robert Hobart
The 4th Earl of Buckinghamshire left no sons. Consequently, his titles passed to his nephew, George Robert Hobart (1789–1849). George Robert Hobart was the son of George Vere Hobart (1761–1802), who had served as the lieutenant-governor of Grenada.

## The 5th Earl and the Hampden Inheritance
In 1824, George Robert Hobart, as the 5th Earl, inherited the Buckinghamshire estates of the Hampden family. Following this inheritance, he took the name of Hampden. This connection to the Hampden family originated from an ancestor, Sir John Hobart, 3rd baronet, who had married Mary Hampden around the year 1655.

## Subsequent Successions
Upon the death of the 5th Earl in February 1849, he was succeeded by his brother, Augustus Edward Hobart (1793–1884), who became the 6th Earl. Augustus Edward Hobart later took the name of Hobart-Hampden in 1878.

The 6th Earl was predeceased by two of his sons: Frederick John Hobart (1821–1875) and Vere Henry, Lord Hobart (1818–1875), the latter of whom served as the governor of Madras starting in 1872. The 6th Earl also had another son named Augustus Charles Hobart-Hampden, who was commonly known as Hobart Pasha.

When the 6th Earl died, he was succeeded by his grandson, Sidney Carr Hobart-Hampden (born 1860), who became the 7th Earl of Buckinghamshire. Sidney Carr Hobart-Hampden added the name Mercer-Henderson to his own.

## Historical Context of the Title
The Earldom of Buckinghamshire was first held by John Hobart (c. 1694–1756), a descendant of Sir Henry Hobart (died 1625). John Hobart was created Baron Hobart of Blickling in 1728 and later became the Earl of Buckinghamshire in 1746. He was succeeded by his eldest son, John (1723–1793), the 2nd Earl, who served as ambassador to Russia and lord-lieutenant of Ireland. The 2nd Earl died without sons, and the title passed to his half-brother, George (c. 1730–1804), who became the 3rd Earl.

Historically, some confusion regarding the title existed because, until 1784, the 2nd Earl of Buckinghamshire always signed himself as "Buckingham," and was known by that name by his contemporaries. This continued until George Grenville, Earl Temple, was created the marquess of Buckingham in 1784.
