# The Relationship Between Balzac and Madame Hanska

## Early Meetings and Engagement
The relationship between the writer Balzac and Madame Hanska began with a series of meetings in the 1830s. Balzac first met Madame Hanska in Neuchâtel in 1833, followed by a second meeting in Geneva later that same year. During this Genevan meeting of 1833, Madame Hanska formally promised to marry Balzac if her husband were to die. A third meeting took place in Vienna in 1835.

## Postponement and Courtship
The condition for the marriage was met at the end of 1841 upon the death of Madame Hanska's husband. Despite this, she did not permit Balzac to visit her until the following year had passed. When Balzac eventually traveled to St Petersburg, the engagement was renewed "after a fashion," but its actual fulfillment was postponed indefinitely. For several years, the couple met in various cities, including Rome, Paris, Brussels, Wiesbaden, and Baden.

## Marriage and Final Years
In September 1847, Balzac was invited to Madame Hanska's estate in the Ukraine, Wierzschovnia, on the definite footing of her future husband. This visit lasted more than two years, though it was interrupted by one trip to Paris and back. The wedding finally took place on March 14, 1850.

Following the marriage, the couple moved to a house in Paris located in the rue Fortunee, which Balzac had purchased for his wife and filled with his collections, arriving there at the end of May. However, Balzac's health had been ruined by his extensive travels across Europe, the cold of Russia, and his enormous labors.

## Death and Burial
Balzac died at half-past eleven on the night of Sunday, August 17. He was attended by his mother, but his wife was not present. He was buried on August 20 in Pere La Chaise. The pall-bearers included Dumas, Victor Hugo, the statesman Baroche, and Sainte-Beuve, the latter of whom was an enemy but acted generously in this instance. Victor Hugo delivered a speech at the burial.

## Literary Legacy and Documentation
The history of Balzac's life and works is documented in various bibliographies, including the *Histoire des oeuvres* attached to the *Edition definitive*. His *Comedie humaine* was first issued in sixteen volumes between 1842 and 1846. Later publications, such as the *Oeuvres posthumes* (specifically the second volume of *Lettres a l'etrangere* published in 1906), include illustrations and a portrait of Madame Hanska.
