# Sir David Brewster and Selected Events of 1824 and 1831

## Scientific Contributions of David Brewster
Sir David Brewster was a scientific discoverer whose work was characterized by an empirical method. Rather than focusing on mathematical genius or the ultimate explanation of phenomena, Brewster established laws through repeated experiment. He is recognized as the discoverer of double refraction by compression, optical mineralogy, and the law of polarization of biaxial crystals. 

Throughout his career, Brewster's stance on the nature of light remained nuanced; while he did not maintain the corpuscular theory until the end of his life, he also never explicitly adopted the undulatory theory. His contributions to the intellectual history of his age are considered by some, such as Forbes, to be of a different kind than those of Fresnel and Young, yet still foremost in rank.

## Literary and Editorial Work
Beyond his personal discoveries, Brewster provided significant service to science through extensive literary and editorial efforts. Starting in 1799 as a contributor to the *Edinburgh Magazine*, he became its editor at age twenty. In 1807, he undertook the editorship of the *Edinburgh Encyclopaedia*, a project that began appearing in 1808 and concluded in 1830; the scientific sections were particularly strong, with Brewster authoring many of the most valuable articles.

Brewster's editorial reach extended to other publications and references:
* **Journals:** In 1819, he co-established the *Edinburgh Philosophical Journal* with Robert Jameson. The first ten volumes (1819–1824) were joint efforts, after which Jameson edited alone from 1825 to 1826. In 1824, Brewster started the *Edinburgh Journal of Science*, editing sixteen volumes between 1824 and 1832.
* **Encyclopedias:** He was a leading contributor to the seventh and eighth editions of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, writing articles on Magnetism, Optics, Hydrodynamics, Electricity, Voltaic Electricity, the Microscope, and the Stereoscope.
* **Other Writings:** He contributed between three and four hundred papers to various learned societies and seventy-five articles to the *North British Review*.

## Major Publications of 1824 and 1831
Brewster produced several distinct works during the early 19th century. In 1824, he wrote the Introduction and Notes for Legendre's *Elements of Geometry*, which was translated by Carlyle. 

The year 1831 was particularly prolific for Brewster. He published the *Treatise on Optics* and the *Letters on Natural Magic*, the latter of which was addressed to Sir Walter Scott. Additionally, in 1831, he published a short popular account of the life of the philosopher Sir Isaac Newton in Murray's *Family Library*. This served as a precursor to his much more comprehensive 1855 work, *Memoirs of the Life, Writings and Discoveries of Sir Isaac Newton*, which was the result of over twenty years of research into original manuscripts.
