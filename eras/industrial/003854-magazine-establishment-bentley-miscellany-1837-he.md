# Magazines and Periodicals in the 19th Century

The 19th century saw a proliferation of magazines, journals, and miscellanies that served as primary vehicles for literary contributions, scientific records, and political commentary. These publications ranged from specialized botanical journals to humorous miscellanies and general literary reviews.

## Literary and Humorous Periodicals

A significant portion of 19th-century periodical literature focused on humor and grotesque storytelling. Richard Harris Barham, writing under the pseudonym Thomas Ingoldsby, began contributing to *Blackwood's Magazine* in 1826. Following the establishment of *Bentley's Miscellany* in 1837, Barham began providing the series of grotesque metrical tales known as *The Ingoldsby Legends*. These verses, characterized by their whimsicality of rhyme and underpinned by solid antiquarian research, became highly popular and were eventually published in collected form.

Other writers utilized magazines to publish a variety of poetic and prose works. Thomas Hood contributed to many contemporary periodicals and published his own *Hood's Magazine and Comic Miscellany* between 1844 and 1848. Similarly, the writer De Quincey contributed to several journals, including *Blackwood*, *Knight's Quarterly Magazine*, *Tait's Magazine*, and the *London Magazine*, the latter of which published his *Confessions* in 1821. During his twelve years in Edinburgh, De Quincey also wrote for the *Edinburgh Literary Gazette*.

## Scientific and Botanical Journals

The archive records a extensive series of specialized botanical publications during this era. *The Botanical Magazine* was produced in 38 volumes between 1827 and 1865, later supplemented by a two-volume *Companion to the Botanical Magazine* (1835-1836). Other significant botanical periodicals included *The Journal of Botany* (1830-1842), *The London Journal of Botany* (1842-1848), and the *Journal of Botany and Kew Garden Miscellany* (1849-1857).

## Academic and Regional Publications

University-affiliated and regional magazines provided platforms for intellectual and historical discourse. The *Dublin University Magazine* published papers by the Irish historian of philosophy William Archer Butler between 1834 and 1837, as well as a series of papers regarding the life and character of Giuseppe Balsamo (Cagliostro) in volumes lxxviii and lxxix. In the realm of ecclesiastical discourse, the *Irish Ecclesiastical Journal* published William Archer Butler's "Letters on Development" in 1845.

Regional news and literary outlets also played a role in the dissemination of work. Robert Burns's poems appeared in various publications, including *The Edinburgh Magazine*, *The Scots Magazine*, *The Caledonian Mercury*, *The Edinburgh Evening Courant*, *The Edinburgh Herald*, and *The Edinburgh Advertiser*. In London, his work appeared in *The Morning Chronicle* and *Stuart's Star and Evening Advertiser* (later known as *The Morning Star*). Additionally, the *Westmoreland Gazette* was edited by De Quincey for approximately a year and a half before he moved to London in 1820.

## Miscellanies and Collected Works

Beyond standard magazines, "miscellanies" served as collections of diverse writings. Examples include the *Harleian Miscellany* (1808) and Carlyle's *Miscellanies*, which contains an account of Giuseppe Balsamo. In the case of Robert Burns, a series of tracts issued between 1796 and 1799 was reissued in January 1800 as *The Poetical Miscellany*.
