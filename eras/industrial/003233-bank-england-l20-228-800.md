# Bank of England

The Bank of England serves as a central institution in the English banking system, particularly regarding the issuance of notes and the management of national circulation. Its role and scale have evolved significantly from the mid-19th century through the early 20th century.

## Note Circulation and the Bank Act of 1844
Prior to the implementation of the Bank Act of 1844, nearly all provincial banks in Wales and England possessed the privilege of issuing notes. During the parliamentary discussions of the Bank Act, these banks continued their usual operations. However, once the arrangements of the act were made public, nine banks—consisting of one joint-stock bank and eight private banks—ceased issuing their own notes before the act officially came into operation on October 12, 1844. Among these, one private bank closed and the Western District Joint-Stock Banking Co. was dissolved; the remaining seven banks began issuing Bank of England notes and were granted certain privileges for doing so.

The Bank Act of 1844 fixed the maximum circulation for English issuing banks based on the average circulation recorded during the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. At that time, the authorized issue for 207 private banks was £5,153,417, and for 72 joint-stock banks, it was £3,478,230, totaling £8,631,647.

By October 12, 1844, the actual monthly return of notes in circulation for England showed that the Bank of England had £20,228,800 in circulation, while private banks had £4,674,162 and joint-stock banks had £3,331,516.

## Growth and Institutional Expansion
The Bank of England has experienced substantial growth in its workforce and operational costs. At the commencement of its business, the bank employed fifty-four assistants with total salaries of £4,350. By 1847, the number of employees had risen to over nine hundred, with salaries exceeding £210,000. By 1867, the staff grew to more than one thousand persons, with salaries and wages reaching nearly £260,000, supplemented by approximately £20,000 in pensions for superannuated clerks. By 1906, the bank employed about 1,400 persons across its head office and eleven branches.

## Circulation Trends and Banking Infrastructure
Between 1844 and 1906, there was an apparent increase in note circulation of more than £6,000,000. This increase was primarily driven by the notes of the Bank of England, which offset decreases in English country circulation and increases in Irish and Scottish circulations. 

This trend is linked to the expansion of banking infrastructure in England and Wales. In 1844, there were 976 banking offices; by 1906, this number had increased to more than 5,880. Because each office must maintain till-money, and Bank of England notes almost always comprise a portion of those funds, it is probable that a significant part of the increased circulation is held within these bank tills. Consequently, the active note circulation of the United Kingdom may be only slightly larger than it was in 1844.

By 1906, the banking business in England was conducted primarily by approximately sixty joint-stock banks and ten private banks, though some joint-stock organizations functioned as private firms. While the total number of individual banks decreased, the number of offices increased.

## Government Revenue and Profits
The government derives revenue from the note circulation. For a typical year ending April 5, 1904, the government received £184,930 2s. 2d. from the Bank of England's profits of issue. Additionally, the government received £120,768 18s. 6d. as composition for duties on the notes and bills of country bankers and the banks of Ireland and England.
