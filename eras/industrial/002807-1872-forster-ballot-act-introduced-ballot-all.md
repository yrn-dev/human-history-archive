# The Ballot Act of 1872 and the Evolution of Secret Voting

The Ballot Act of 1872, introduced by W. E. Forster, established the use of the ballot in all municipal and parliamentary elections in Great Britain, with the exception of parliamentary elections for universities. Beyond the introduction of secret voting, the Act abolished the public nomination of candidates and addressed the expenses of elections and the offense of personation.

## Legal Framework and Enforcement in Great Britain
The Ballot Act created several new criminal offenses to protect the integrity of the electoral process. These included the fraudulent defacing, destroying, or forging of papers or official marks; the unauthorized supply of papers; the fraudulent insertion of non-official papers into the ballot box; and the unauthorized removal of papers from the station. Additionally, it became an offense to interfere with, open, or destroy boxes or packets of papers used for election purposes.

Penalties for these crimes varied by the role of the offender: officers and clerks could face imprisonment for two years, with or without hard labour, while others faced a term of six months.

The adoption of the secret ballot was not without criticism. Some argued it led to universal deception and hypocrisy, with Sydney Smith famously describing voters arriving at polls in sedan-chairs with closely-drawn curtains. However, the observed effect of the system was the gradual extermination of undue influence. Concerns regarding "the confessional"—that Catholic voters would be forced to reveal their votes to priests—were considered unfounded, as penitents are not bound to confess their vote, and confessors committing such a breach of secrecy would be committing a crime.

## Historical Context in Great Britain
The concept of the ballot was suggested in a political tract during the time of Charles II. It saw limited use in the Scots parliament of 1662 for the Billeting Act, a measure intended to ostracize political opponents like Lauderdale. In that instance, members wrote names of suspected persons in a disguised hand on paper, which were then sealed in a bag and later burned in the exchequer chamber to conceal the names. In 1705, Fletcher of Saltoun proposed secret voting to protect Scottish members from court influence.

By the early 19th century, demands for the ballot in parliamentary elections grew during reform agitations. The Benthamites advocated for the system in 1817, and banners calling for the ballot appeared at the Peterloo Massacre in 1819. O'Connell introduced a bill on the subject in 1830, and the original draft of Lord John Russell's Reform Bill also provided for its introduction. Despite ridicule from Sydney Smith, who called the ballot a "mouse-trap" or "dagger-box," support for the system increased in the House of Commons between 1833 and 1839.

## International Developments and the "Australian Ballot"
The "Australian ballot" originated in Australia, introduced by the Constitution Act of South Australia in 1856. In South Australia, returning officers initialed voting cards, which voters folded to hide their cross. In Victoria, under the Electoral Act of 1865, officers added a number corresponding to the voter's register number. In Tasmania, voters handed their cards to the officer to prevent double-voting or the use of non-official cards.

This system was tested in England at Manchester and Stafford in 1869. The Victorian model used cards with candidates' names printed in different colors to assist illiterate voters. In Manchester, this was found to be more rapid and less expensive than previous systems, and it decreased the risk of personation.

## Adoption in the United States
Early American voting was *viva voce* (by voice), though several colonies adopted written or printed ballots early on. By 1775, ballots were used in Pennsylvania, Delaware, North and South Carolina, and the New England states. By the time of the U.S. Constitution's adoption, *viva voce* voting remained only in Georgia, Virginia, and Maryland.

Initially, ballots were written by voters or provided by candidates and political committees. This made "mixed tickets" difficult and allowed for bribery and intimidation, as secrecy was nearly impossible.

Serious agitation for the Australian ballot began in 1885. Massachusetts enacted the first complete law in 1888, requiring the state to print and distribute alphabetical ballots. This was followed by a wave of adoption in 1889 across nine states, including Indiana, Michigan, Wisconsin, Minnesota, Tennessee, Missouri, and Montana. 

Different systems emerged:
* **Blanket Ballots:** Used in Massachusetts, where candidates were arranged by office.
* **Party-Group Systems:** Used in Indiana, Michigan, and Missouri, where candidates were arranged in party groups, allowing a voter to select an entire group with a single mark.
* **Separate Party Ballots:** Used in Connecticut, New Jersey, Missouri (after 1897), North and South Carolina, Georgia, and New Mexico.

By the 1890s, adoption continued in states such as Iowa (1892), Alabama and Kansas (1893), Virginia (1894), Florida (1895), and Louisiana and Utah (1896).

## Other European Systems
In Prussia, the ballot was introduced for municipal assemblies in 1808 via Stein's *Staedteordnung*. Under the German constitutions of 1867 and 1871, Reichstag elections were conducted by universal suffrage under the ballot.

Hungary's history was distinct; secret voting was unknown there before 1848. Early methods were primitive, involving voters placing a 4-to-6-foot rod into a colored box bearing a candidate's name. While the electoral law of 1874 abolished the ballot for parliamentary elections, it made it obligatory for town and county council elections.
