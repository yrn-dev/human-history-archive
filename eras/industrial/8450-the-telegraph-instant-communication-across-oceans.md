# The Electric Nerve: The Telegraph and the Conquest of Distance

## Overview
During the Industrial Revolution, the speed of information was limited by the speed of physical transport—be it a galloping horse or a steamship. The advent of the electrical telegraph in the 1830s and 1840s fundamentally decoupled communication from transportation, creating what historians often call the "annihilation of space and time." 

While Samuel Morse is the most recognized figure in the West, the telegraph was the result of concurrent breakthroughs by inventors like William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone in England. The technology transitioned from local lines to a global network, culminating in the mid-19th century with the laying of submarine cables. The transatlantic cable, completed successfully in 1866, reduced the communication time between London and New York from ten days (by ship) to mere minutes. This shift revolutionized global diplomacy, synchronized international financial markets, and altered the nature of journalism by giving birth to the "wire service."

## Key Facts
*   **The Economic Engine:** The telegraph was inextricably linked to the railroad. Telegraph lines were often laid alongside tracks, allowing dispatchers to manage train traffic and prevent collisions, while the railroads provided the right-of-way for the wires.
*   **Standardization of Time:** Before the telegraph, towns kept "local solar time." The need to coordinate telegraphic messages and train schedules forced the adoption of standardized time zones.
*   **The "Victorian Internet":** Historians often draw parallels between the telegraph and the modern internet. Both shifted the world toward a "real-time" existence and created new forms of coded language (Morse Code) to optimize bandwidth.
*   **Historical Debate:** There is an ongoing historiographical debate regarding the telegraph's impact on imperialism. Some scholars argue it was a tool of liberation by connecting distant peoples, while others, such as those focusing on the British Empire, argue it was primarily a tool of centralized control, allowing London to micromanage colonial administrations in India and Africa.

## Did You Know?
1. **The 1858 Failure:** The first transatlantic cable in 1858 worked for only three weeks. The chief electrician, Wildman Whitehouse, applied excessive voltage to boost the signal, which effectively "fried" the insulation.
2. **The Cost of Brevity:** Because telegraph companies charged by the word, a specialized "telegraphic style" developed—a precursor to modern texting—where unnecessary adjectives and articles were stripped away.
3. **The SS Great Eastern:** The ship used to lay the 1866 cable was the largest ship in the world at the time, designed by Isambard Kingdom Brunel; it was the only vessel capable of carrying the massive weight of the armored cable.
