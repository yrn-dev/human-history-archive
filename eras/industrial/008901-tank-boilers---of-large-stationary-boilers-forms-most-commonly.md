# Tank Boilers

Tank boilers represent one of two primary classes of boilers, distinguished by the fact that they contain relatively large quantities of water. This class is contrasted with water-tube boilers, where water is primarily held in numerous small tubes. Tank boilers are utilized in both marine and land-based applications.

## Design and Construction
The outer shell of a tank boiler is either partially or wholly cylindrical. This specific geometry is employed because it allows the boiler to achieve the necessary strength without requiring a large number of stays. These boilers are generally internally fired, with the furnace plates surrounded by water, which constitutes the most efficient part of the heating surfaces. 

After leaving the furnace, the products of combustion are directed into a chamber and then passed through either flues or numerous small tubes. This process transmits a portion of the heat of combustion to the water within the boiler. A general advantage of the tank boiler design is that heating surfaces are typically kept in close contact with water without the special circulation provisions required by water-tube designs.

## Large Stationary Tank Boilers
Among large stationary boilers, the "Lancashire" boiler and its modification, the "Galloway" boiler, are the most common forms. These units typically measure 26 to 30 feet in length with diameters ranging from 6-1/2 to 8 feet. The Lancashire boiler features two cylindrical furnace flues that extend the full length of the boiler.

### Pressure Specifications
Working pressures vary based on the engine type being supplied:
* **Older boilers:** Approximately 60 lb. per sq. in.
* **Compound engines:** 100 lb. to 120 lb. per sq. in.
* **Triple expansion engines:** 150 lb. to 170 lb. per sq. in.
* **Maximum constructions:** Some have been built for pressures of 200 lb. per sq. in.

### Flue and Tube Engineering
Furnace flues are constructed in sections of 3 to 3-1/2 feet. Each section is made from a plate bent into a cylinder with a welded longitudinal joint and flanges at both ends. These are connected via an "Adamson" joint, which prevents rivets or double-thickness plates from being exposed to fire and acts as a stiffening ring to prevent flue collapse.

To increase the heating surface, "Galloway" tubes—conical tubes with flanges at each end—are often fitted into the furnace flues. These tubes are designed so the large end's diameter is slightly greater than the small end's flange, allowing for easy removal and replacement. These tubes serve several purposes: they increase the heating surface, stiffen the flue, promote water circulation, and improve evaporative efficiency by mixing flue gases.

## Vertical and Small-Scale Boilers
In installations where floor space is limited, vertical cylindrical boilers are used. The simple vertical boiler features a plain, slightly tapering vertical fire-box and an internal smoke stack. Except in the smallest sizes, these are fitted with cross tubes (approximately 9 inches in diameter) that are slightly inclined to aid water circulation.

Other specialized vertical types include:
* **Tyne boiler:** Features a hemispherical furnace and an upper combustion chamber traversed by vertical water-tubes and four or more inclined water-tubes.
* **Victoria boiler:** Utilizes a hemispherical furnace and leads gases to an internal combustion chamber and then through horizontal smoke-tubes to a side smoke-box.
* **Cochran boiler:** Similar to the Victoria but features a "dry" back combustion chamber with firebrick-lined doors for easy access to tube ends.

## Boiler Fittings and Safety
All boilers are equipped with a stop-valve for regulating steam admission and a steam-gauge to indicate pressure. Safety is managed through one or two safety-valves. In stationary boilers, these are typically held by levers with weights, whereas marine and locomotive boilers use steel springs. At least one safety-valve includes easing gear for blowing off steam.

For maintenance, boilers include blow-out cocks for emptying and man-holes for cleaning and examination. Man-holes are usually oval, allowing doors to be placed on the inside so that steam pressure helps keep them shut. These doors are secured by bolts and "dogs" (cross-bars). In rare cases where doors are fitted on the outside, multiple bolts are used to ensure that a single bolt failure does not allow the door to be forced off.

## Specialized Small Boilers in Incubators
Small-scale boilers are also used to heat warm-water tanks in various incubators:
* **Graves's Incubator:** Uses a simple boiler heated by a lamp with a metal flap to regulate the flame size. An inlet tube carries heated water to the far end of the tank, while an outlet tube returns water to the boiler.
* **T. Christy's Incubator (1894):** Employs an external conical boiler containing two spaces: an outer space for heated water and a central air shaft heated by a lamp flame.
* **Other designs:** Some incubators use a boiler with an inner conical wall and an outer cylindrical division, where warmer water rises through an inlet pipe to be discharged into the tank.
