# League

In the technical field of basket-making, a "league" refers to a specific construction method where the bottom-stick and the stake are formed from a single, continuous rod.

## Basket-Making Framework and Construction
The construction of a basket involves several technical components and processes. The rigid framework of the side is created by driving stakes in at each side of the bottom-sticks and pricking them up. In contrast, "bye-stakes" are simply inserted into the "upsett." 

When a basket's bottom is constructed on a hoop, the butts of the stakes are "sliped," which involves cutting them away with a long cut using a shop-knife. These are then turned tightly around the hoop, a process described as being "scallomed" on.

## Technical Strokes and Plaiting
Various strokes are employed to construct an ordinary basket:
*   **Slew:** Two or more rods woven together.
*   **Rand:** Rods that are woven in singly.
*   **Fitch:** Two rods worked tightly and alternately one under the other, typically used for skeleton work such as waste-paper baskets or cages.
*   **Pair:** Two rods worked alternately one over the other, used for covers and bottoms of oval or round baskets.
*   **Wale:** Three or more rods worked alternately to form a binding course or string.

To finish or border off the work, basket-makers use various forms of tracking, roping, and plaiting.

## Foundation and Superstructure
The foundation of an ordinary oval basket is technically called the "slath." This is created by ranging bottom sticks in pairs on the floor, parallel to each other in the direction of the basket's longer diameter, forming a "woof." These are crossed at right angles by two pairs of the largest osiers. The workman places his feet on the butt ends of these osiers, which are woven alternately over and under the parallel pieces and their own butts. Other rods are then woven around the bottom until it reaches the sufficient size.

The superstructure is raised by sharpening the large ends of long, stout osiers to form the skeleton or stakes. These are forced from the edge toward the center between the bottom sticks and "upset" (turned up) toward the sides. Rods are woven between these stakes until the intended depth is reached. The edge is finished by turning the ends of the stakes behind and in front of each other to unite the structure firmly; this finished part is known as the "belly."

## Tools of the Trade
A basket-maker's kit is generally simple. Essential tools include:
*   A shop-knife for cutting material.
*   A picking knife for cutting off protruding tops and butts of rods.
*   Bodkins of various sizes.
*   A narrowly triangular flat piece of iron used to drive work closely together.
*   A "dog" or "commander" for straightening sticks.
*   A stout pair of shears.

Additionally, employers may provide a lapboard for fixing the upsetted bottom during siding and a vice or screw block for gripping cover and bottom sticks in square work. While some baskets can be made with only a bodkin and shop-knife, shapes or blocks are sometimes used in Europe for weaving the fabric.
