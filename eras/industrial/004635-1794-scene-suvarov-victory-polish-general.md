# Brest-Litovsk

Brest-Litovsk (known in Polish as *Brzesc-Litevski* and in the Chron. as *Berestie* or *Berestov*) is a strongly fortified town located in the government of Grodno. It is situated at the intersection of railways from Moscow, Kiev, Warsaw, and East Prussia, and at the junction of the Bug and the navigable river Mukhovets. The town is linked to the waterways connecting the Vistula and the Dnieper via the King's canal (also called the Brest-Litovsk canal), which spans 50 miles and utilizes the Bug and Mukhovets rivers.

## Geography and Demographics
The town is located at 52° 5' N. latitude and 23° 39' E. longitude, 137 miles by rail south of the city of Grodno. As of 1867, the population was 22,493, increasing to 42,812 by 1901. More than half of the 1901 population were Jews. The town serves as the seat for a Greek Catholic bishop and an Armenian bishop, the latter of whom holds authority over Armenians throughout the entire country. The town also contains a Jewish synagogue that was considered the first in Europe during the 16th century.

## Economy and Trade
Brest-Litovsk maintains an extensive trade in various commodities, specifically leather, tar, wood, hemp, flax, and grain.

## History
First mentioned at the start of the 11th century, the town has a history marked by repeated destruction and reconstruction. In 1241, the Mongols laid waste to the town, and it was not rebuilt until 1275. The Teutonic Knights burned the suburbs in 1379, and by the end of the 15th century, the khan of the Crimea destroyed the entire town.

During the reign of the Polish king Sigismund III, the town hosted diets. It also served as the meeting place for two significant councils of western Russian bishops in 1594 and 1596. The town was captured by the Swedes twice, first in 1657 and again in 1706.

## Military Conflicts of the Late 18th Century
In 1794, Brest-Litovsk was the site of a victory achieved by Suvarov over the Polish general Sierakowski. Following this period, the town was added to the Russian empire in 1795.
