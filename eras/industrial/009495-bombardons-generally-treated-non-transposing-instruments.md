# Bombardon

The bombardon, also known as the bass tuba, is a brass wind instrument serving as the bass and contrabass in military bands. In an orchestral context, it is referred to as the bass tuba. The term is derived from the Italian *bombardone*, which referred to the contrabass pommer (bombard) used as the bass in medieval orchestras before the invention of the fagotto. Additionally, the term is used to describe a bass reed stop on an organ with a 16 ft. tone.

## History and Development
The bombardon was the first bass wind instrument to be fitted with valves. In its early stages, it was known by several names, including *clavicor*, *bass horn*, or *corno basso*. This original *corno basso* featured a narrow bore and pistons that were set horizontally. It should be distinguished from the keyed bass horn, which eventually became the ophicleide.

In the Prussian Jager military bands, the keyed bass horn was introduced as the bass in 1829, followed by the bombardon (or valve-ophicleide) in 1831. By 1835, these were superseded in the Guards by the bass tuba, which was invented by J.G. Moritz and W. Wieprecht.

## Construction and Design
The modern bombardon exists in two primary forms:
*   **Upright model:** Utilized for stationary band music.
*   **Circular model (Helicon):** Designed to be worn around the body with the bell resting on the left shoulder, similar to the Roman *cornu*, making it more convenient for marching.

The bombardon is the bass of the euphonium and is a result of applying valves to the bugle family, a process that also produced saxhorns. A primary distinction between tubas (including the bombardon) and saxhorns is the width of the conical bore. Tubas possess a bore wide enough to produce fundamental sounds with immense power and a rich, full quality. 

In Germany and Austria, this distinction led to the classification of brass winds as "whole" instruments (*Ganze Instrumente*), such as the tubas, and "half" instruments (*Halbe Instrumente*), such as the narrow-bore saxhorns. While older instruments required a tube twice as long as an organ pipe to produce the same note (making the fundamental unobtainable), the bugle-based proportions of the tubas allow them to be half the length of those older instruments, corresponding directly to the length of an organ pipe of the same pitch.

## Technical Specifications and Operation
The bombardon has a chromatic compass spanning 3-1/2 to 4 octaves, with a harmonic series consisting of the 1st through the 8th harmonics. The instrument utilizes pistons or valves to obtain intervening notes; depressing these either admits wind into additional tubing to lower the pitch or cuts off a length to raise it. 

Most bombardons feature three or four pistons that lower the pitch by 1, 1/2, 1-1/2, and 2-1/2 tones. In Belgium, the pistons lower the pitch by 1, 1/2, 2, and 3 tones. In Germany and Austria, a "cylinder action" is frequently used, replacing the pump or piston with a four-way brass cock operated by a series of cranks and a key.

A significant technical challenge occurs with the lowest notes produced by the valves. Because the column of air is of such immense length, the lips often lack the power to set it in vibration at a rate slow enough to synchronize with such deep pitches. Consequently, these lowest notes are difficult to obtain and are often barely audible unless another bombardon doubles them an octave higher.

## Notation and Usage
In terms of musical notation, bombardons are generally treated as non-transposing instruments, meaning the music is written as it is sounded. The exception to this rule is found in Belgium and France, where transposition is the usual practice.

While the bombardon is used in Austrian military bands, it has been superseded in German military bands by a bass tuba that differs slightly in construction and form from the versions used in Austria, France, Belgium, and England.
