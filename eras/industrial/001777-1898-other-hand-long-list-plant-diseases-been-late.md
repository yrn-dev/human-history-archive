# Bacterial Diseases in Higher Plants

## Overview of Bacterial Plant Pathology
The study of bacterial diseases in higher plants has been a subject of scientific description, though it requires careful treatment due to lingering doubts regarding whether the described organisms are the actual causes of the diseases attributed to them. Historically, it was argued that the acid contents of plants provided immunity from bacterial diseases; however, it is now understood that many bacteria are capable of flourishing in acid media.

Another point of contention involved the ability of bacteria to penetrate plant structures. While it was once believed that bacteria could not penetrate the cell-walls bounding intercellular spaces even if they entered through the stomata, research has shown otherwise. Certain anaerobic forms can ferment cellulose, and other bacteria possess the power to penetrate the cell-walls of living cells. This was first described by Marshall Ward in 1887 regarding the bacteria of Leguminosae and was later confirmed by Miss Dawson in 1898.

## Primary Agents and Doubtful Cases
In recent years, a long list of plant diseases has been attributed to bacterial action, though these cases vary in certainty. Some instances are regarded as very doubtful, such as the slime fluxes of oaks and other trees, as well as the Sereh disease of the sugar-cane. In these cases, other organisms, including fungi and yeasts, play a role, and it is considered extremely improbable that bacteria are the primary agents. Instead, these are likely saprophytic forms that accessed rotting tissues already injured by other agents. Saprophytic bacteria may enter through punctures made by insects or travel down the dead hypha of an invading fungus. For example, while Aphides were once credited with the bacterial infection of carnations, more recent research by Woods concludes that Aphides alone are responsible for the disease.

Conversely, recent investigations have identified cases where bacteria are certainly the primary agents. These diseases are characterized principally by the wilting of shoots resulting from the stoppage of the vessels. When transverse sections of these shoots are cut, the vessels typically appear brown and are choked with a dark yellowish slime containing detectable bacteria. Examples of plants affected in this manner include cucumbers, potatoes, and cabbages. Bacterial action is also the seat of disease in certain plants such as tobacco and carnations.

## The Germ Theory of Disease
The understanding of plant and animal pathology is rooted in the broader "germ theory of disease," which gained general acceptance from approximately 1870 onwards. Before this, thinkers had noted resemblances between fermentation and certain diseases, suggesting that a contagium or virus might be a minute organism capable of reproduction. These notions were formalized through the ferment theory developed by Schwann (1837), Cagniard de la Tour (1828), and Pasteur.

Key milestones in the establishment of this theory include:
* **1850-1855:** P. F. O. Rayer and Davaine observed bacilli in the blood of animals that died of anthrax (splenic fever), a discovery later repeated by Pollender in 1855.
* **1863:** Davaine, influenced by Pasteur's fermentation research, proposed that anthrax bacilli caused splenic fever, which was subsequently proved via inoculation.
* **1870-1871:** Pasteur proved a silkworm disease was caused by a bacterium-like organism, and Oertel demonstrated that a known *Micrococcus* was intimately involved in producing diphtheria.
* **1872:** Cohn grouped several "pathogenous" Schizomycetes together.
* **1876:** Koch published observations on Davaine's bacilli, confirming their causal relation to splenic fever and discovering the organism's spores and saprophytic phase.

Through these developments, diseases such as tuberculosis, glanders, relapsing fever, fowl-cholera, and septicaemia were brought into the range of biology. It is now established that all infectious and contagious diseases result from the action of bacteria, or in some instances, protozoa or fungi.
