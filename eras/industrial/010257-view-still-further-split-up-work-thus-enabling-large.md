# Book-Keeping and Ledger Organization

Book-keeping is defined as a systematic record of business transactions. It is designed to be conveniently available for reference to individuals or corporations engaged in financial or commercial operations. The primary goals of this practice are to allow these entities to ascertain, with minimum dislocation to the business and minimum trouble, the detailed particulars of their transactions and the cumulative effect those transactions have on the business and its financial relations with others.

While book-keeping is sometimes described as an art and other times as a science, it is characterized as a growth rather than a discovery. Crude methods from the past have evolved gradually to meet the changing requirements of business. The ideal of any system is to achieve the maximum amount of record while requiring the minimum amount of labour. However, because dishonesty must be guarded against, an adequate system must allow records to be verified as a complete and true statement of transactions. This verification process is known as an audit. In large or public concerns, professional accountants typically perform audits. In cases where a large book-keeping staff is employed, the staff may be organized to check each other's work, a process referred to as an "internal check" or "staff audit."

## Ledger Subdivision and Staffing

To enable a large staff to be engaged simultaneously, the ledger is generally kept in sections to split up the work. This subdivision takes several forms:

**Cash and Bank Accounts**
The cash account and bank account are often bound together in a separate book called the *cash book*. This book uses parallel columns to show the movements of bank cash and office cash. A third column for discounts can be added to avoid the need for a separate *discount journal*. In recent years, many businesses pay all accounts by cheque and deposit all received moneys into banks without deduction, rendering the office cash account unnecessary except for petty payments. These petty payments are recorded in a *petty cash book*.

**Personal, Real, and Nominal Accounts**
Personal accounts, which are the most numerous, are frequently separated from nominal and real accounts. Personal accounts are further subdivided into *sales ledgers* (or *sold ledgers*) for customers and *purchases ledgers* (or *bought ledgers*) for trade creditors. 

Nominal and real accounts may be kept together in a *general ledger*, or further divided into a *nominal ledger* and a *private ledger*. This latter subdivision is rarely based on a scientific approach. For example, trading and profit and loss accounts are generally kept in the private ledger despite being nominal accounts. Conversely, bills payable and bills receivable accounts are usually kept in the nominal ledger. This arrangement minimizes clerical work for the private ledger, which is typically managed by a confidential employee or the principal himself.

To make these various ledgers self-balancing and to localize clerical errors, *adjustment accounts* are employed to complete the double-entry record in each ledger.

## Tabular Book-Keeping

Tabular book-keeping is a modification of standard methods intended to increase the speed at which results are available, reduce clerical work, and ensure results are handled with greater certainty. This device substitutes books ruled with numerous columns for more usual forms.

When applied to books of first entry, this system allows a single book to handle multiple classes of transactions. For instance, if a business is divided into several departments, separate columns for the sales of each department allow for the easy calculation of aggregate sales for each, which simplifies the preparation of departmental trading accounts. 

When applied to ledgers, the tabular system allows a single ledger opening to record a very large number of personal accounts, provided that the number of entries made against each individual is small.
