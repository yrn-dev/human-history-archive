# Connexions Between Furnaces and Combustion Chambers

In the construction of boilers, specifically those utilizing combustion chambers, there are two distinct types of connexion used to join the furnace to the combustion chamber. These designs differ in their riveting methods, structural stiffness, and the ease with which components can be replaced.

## Standard Flanged Connexion
In one type of connexion, the furnace is flanged at its crown portion. This allows it to be riveted to the tube plate. Simultaneously, the lower section of the furnace is riveted to the side plate of the combustion chamber, which is referred to as the "wrapper." While this design provides greater stiffness, it presents a disadvantage during maintenance; if the furnace suffers an accident, it cannot be removed from the boiler and replaced without disturbing the end plates.

## The Gourlay Back End
The second type of connexion is generally known as the "Gourlay back end." In this design, the end of the furnace is contracted into an oval conical form and then flanged outwards around its entire circumference. The tube plate is constructed to extend to the bottom of the combustion chamber, and the furnace is riveted directly to this tube plate. 

The primary advantage of the Gourlay back end is the ability to remove and replace a damaged furnace with one of the same design without disturbing the end plates. However, this configuration is not as stiff as the standard flanged design, necessitating the use of more longitudinal stays within the boiler.

## Application in Marine Boilers
These types of connexions are utilized in cylindrical multitubular return tube boilers, which are common in merchant steamers. These boilers can reach diameters of up to 17 feet and typically operate at pressures between 160 and 200 lb per sq. in., though some reach 265 lb per sq. in.

These boilers are categorized as either single-ended or double-ended:
*   **Single-ended boilers:** The most generally used type, where furnaces are fitted at one end only. They may contain between one and four furnaces, though three is the most common practice. Each furnace generally has its own separate combustion chamber, although in four-furnace models, one chamber may be shared by the two middle furnaces or by pairs of side furnaces.
*   **Double-ended boilers:** Furnaces are fitted at both ends. While some may have separate combustion chambers for each furnace, it is more common for one chamber to serve two furnaces (one at each end of the boiler).

## Furnace Construction and Materials
Furnaces used in smaller boiler sizes are often plain cylindrical types, with plate thickness ranging from 3/8 in. to 3/4 in. depending on the working pressure and furnace diameter. For high pressures and large furnaces, corrugated or ribbed furnaces are typically adopted because they are stronger against collapse than plain furnaces of the same thickness. Examples of these include the Deighton, Fox, and Morison types, which are made from plates rolled to a uniform thickness, formed into cylinders with welded longitudinal joints, and then corrugated. Other types are rolled with ribs or thickened portions at 9-inch intervals.
