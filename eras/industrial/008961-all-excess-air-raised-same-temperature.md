# Thermal Effects of Excess Air in Combustion

The use of air in combustion processes, particularly within boilers and furnaces, involves a critical balance between ensuring complete combustion and maintaining thermal efficiency. While oxygen is necessary to oxidize the combustible portions of fuel, the introduction of air in quantities exceeding the theoretical requirement—known as excess air—has significant impacts on the temperature and heat transmission of the system.

## Excess Air and Boiler Efficiency

The efficiency of a boiler is measured by the amount of steam produced relative to the amount of fuel consumed. To achieve high efficiency, a boiler must combine complete combustion with a heating surface sufficient to absorb heat, thereby reducing the temperature of funnel gases to a level near that of the steam. 

Completeness of combustion requires admitting considerably more air to the fire than is theoretically necessary. This ensures that furnace gases and air are thoroughly mixed before the temperature drops below the critical point where combustion ceases. While it was generally considered that the required excess air should nearly equal the theoretical amount, experience indicates that less is required if proper means are used to ensure an early, complete mixture of gases. The methods for achieving this mixture vary depending on the type of coal used; for instance, methods suitable for Welsh coal are unsuitable for Scottish or North Country coal.

## Thermal Impact of Excess Air

The introduction of excess air affects the internal temperature of the combustion system. Because all excess air must be raised to the same temperature as the gases that are actually burnt, an excess of air passing through the fire lowers the temperature within the fire and the flues. This reduction in temperature lessens the overall heat transmission. Furthermore, as this excess air leaves the boiler at a high temperature, it carries away a portion of the produced heat.

Consequently, reducing the amount of air can increase efficiency by increasing the fire temperature and reducing waste through the chimney, even if such a reduction results in a slight incompleteness of combustion.

## Combustion of Solid and Gaseous Fuels

The combustion of solid fuels, such as coal, cannot be performed using only the theoretically necessary quantity of atmospheric air. Instead, a considerable excess is required, often ranging from 50% to 100% or more. This is evidenced by the analysis of smoke gases. In a theoretical scenario where all oxygen is converted into $\text{H}_2\text{O}$ and $\text{CO}_2$, pure carbon would result in nearly 21 volumes % of $\text{CO}_2$, and ordinary coal would show about 18.5%. However, smoke gases from steam boilers frequently show only 10% to 13% $\text{CO}_2$, and gases from reverberatory furnaces often show less than 5%.

This indicates that the volume of smoke gases escaping into the air is 1.5 to 2 times—and in high-temperature operations, up to 4 times—greater than the theoretical minimum. Because these gases carry off heat, the loss is magnified as the temperature of the operation increases and the utilization of oxygen decreases. In metallurgical operations like glass-making, the proportion of fuel heat value utilized may fall below 5%, whereas best-constructed steam boilers with heat economizers can reach 70% to 75% utilization.

## High-Temperature Combustion and Flame Intensity

The calorific intensity of fuel is significantly enhanced when combustion is effected with air that has been previously heated to a temperature between that of boiling water and a dull red heat. This effect applies to both gaseous and solid fuels. In the case of carbonaceous fuel, the highest attainable flame temperature is approximately 2100°C to 2300°C, which is observed in the "eyes" or bright spots of blast furnace tuyeres. This is achieved under favorable conditions where the air is previously raised to high pressure and temperature.

To prevent the reduction of carbon dioxide, air is typically supplied to the fire in excess of what is required to burn the fuel. However, the presence of inert gas increases the volume of the flame, which causes a proportionate diminution of the heating effect. In specialized applications requiring intense local heat, such as the Deville furnace, dense coke, graphite, or gas retort carbon are used, and air is supplied under pressure.
