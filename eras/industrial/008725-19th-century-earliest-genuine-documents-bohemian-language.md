# The Earliest Genuine Documents of the Bohemian Language

The records of the Bohemian (or Czech) language are described as very ancient. While several documents were long believed to be among the oldest written examples of the language, 19th-century scholarship eventually distinguished between genuine ancient texts and later forgeries.

## Genuine Early Documents
The earliest genuine documents of the Bohemian language consist of several legends and hymns. Among the legends, those concerning St. Dorothy and St. Catherine are noted as having the greatest value. Additionally, several ancient epic fragments have been preserved, including *Tandarias a Floribella* and the *Alexandreis*.

Of significant historical importance is a rhymed chronicle, which is generally—though incorrectly—referred to as the chronicle of Dalimil. The author of this work likely lived during the reign of King John (1310–1346). The chronicle records Bohemian history from its earliest period up until the reign of King Henry of Carinthia, who was the immediate predecessor to King John. The text is characterized by a strong feeling of racial antipathy toward the Germans.

## 19th-Century Forgeries
During the early 19th century, certain manuscripts were produced that were later identified as forgeries. These include the so-called MSS. of Zelena Hora (Gruneberg) and Kralodvur (Koniginhof). 

The manuscript of Königinhof (Dvur Kralove) became widely known in the 19th century. In 1817, Wenceslas Hanka, who later served as the librarian of the Bohemian museum, claimed to have discovered an ancient manuscript containing lyric and epic poems in the church tower of Dvur Kralove. Although the philologist Dobrovsky expressed suspicions from the beginning, the document was accepted as genuine for a long time, leading to its translation into most European languages and frequent printing.

Doubts persisted and grew stronger after Hanka was convicted of fabricating other false Bohemian documents. The genuineness of the Kralodvorsky Rukopis was eventually disproved through a series of articles and works by Professors Masoryk, Gebauer, and Goll. Consequently, almost no Bohemian scholars of the present day believe the document is genuine.

## The 19th-Century Literary Revival
Following the deaths of Comenius, Slavata, and Skala, there was a considerable period during which no important works were written in the Bohemian language, a situation exacerbated by the Austrian government's efforts to discourage the language's use. A revival began at the start of the 19th century, driven by the romantic movement. 

This early revival was modest and initially focused almost exclusively on translations from foreign languages. Joseph Dobrovsky (1753–1829) was the first writer to draw attention back to the nearly forgotten language. While he wrote a history of Bohemian literature and a grammar of the language, these were mostly written in Latin or German; his Bohemian contributions were limited to a collection of letters and essays for the *Casopis Musea Kralovstvi Ceskeho* (Journal of the Bohemian Museum).

The broader revival of Bohemian literature and language is primarily attributed to four men:
* **Joseph Jungmann (1773–1847):** He published numerous translations of English and German writers and created a monumental Bohemian and German dictionary that aided the development of the language. He also wrote the *Dejepes literatury ceska* (history of Bohemian literature).
* **John Kolar (1793–1852):** Regarded as the greatest poet of the revival, his principal work was *Slavy dcera* (daughter of Slavia), a personification of the Slavic race.
* **Paul Joseph Safarik (1795–1861):** A prolific writer whose work *Starozitnosti Slovanske* (Slavic antiquities) attempted to record the literature and history of the early Slavs.
* **Francis Palacky (1798–1876):** Considered the greatest of Bohemian historians, most notably for his history of Bohemia from the earliest period to 1526.

Other significant writers of the early 19th century include the poets Macha, Tyl, Erben, and Francis Ladislav Celakovsky, the author of *Ruze stolistova* (the hundred-leaved rose). Karel Havlicek is also noted as the founder of Bohemian journalism.

## Later 19th-Century Developments
Bohemian literature expanded significantly in the latter part of the 19th century, particularly after the national university was founded in 1882. Notable authors from this period include:
* **John Neruda (1834–1891):** A talented writer of prose and poetry, known for the philosophical epic *Kosmicke basne*.
* **Julius Zeyer (1841–1901):** Author of the novel *Jan Maria Plojhar* and the epic poem *Vysehrad*.
* **Svatopluk Cech:** A poet inspired by national enthusiasm, known for *Basne otroka* and *Vaclav z Michalovic, Lesetinsky Kovar*.
* **Jaroslav Vrchlicky (b. 1853):** A brilliant dramatist and poet who wrote *Poute k Eldoradu*, *Rok na jihu*, and *Sonety Samotare*.
* **Other Novelists:** Alois Jirasek (also a dramatist), Jacob Arbes (author of *Romanetta*), and Vaclav Hladik (author of *Evzen Voldan*).

In the field of history, Wenceslas Tomek wrote the *Dejepis miesta Prahy* (history of the town of Prague). Jaroslav Goll wrote extensively on the Bohemian Brethren and co-founded the *Cesky Casopis Historicky* (Bohemian Historical Review) in 1895 with Dr. Anton Rezek. Other contributors to the study of Bohemian history include Professor Joseph Kalousek, who studied the ancient constitution (*Statni pravo*), and Professors Niederle and Pic, who published archaeological studies on the earliest period.
