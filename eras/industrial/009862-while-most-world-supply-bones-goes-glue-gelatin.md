# Industrial Applications of Animal Bone

The industrial utilization of animal bone has been developed to a degree where nearly every constituent of the bone—including mineral matter, fat, and gelatinous substances—is recovered for commercial use. While a significant portion of the global bone supply is directed toward gelatin and glue works, other specific parts and processes allow for a wide array of secondary products.

## Manufacture of Bone Articles
Leg and thigh bones, referred to as "knuckles" and "marrows," are specifically used to create bone articles, such as knife-handles and buttons. The processing of these bones differs from that used in glue-works. First, a saw is used to remove the ends, and the bones are steeped in a 1% brine solution for three to four days to separate fibrous matter. They are then simmered in water for approximately six hours to remove gelatinous matter and fat. This process leaves the bones firm enough to be worked with a lathe. After being scrubbed and dried, they are transferred to the bone-worker. Additionally, bone is used as a material in marquetry, where thin pieces are combined with other materials like ivory or brass and laid upon a matrix with glue to form ornamental designs.

## Glue and Gelatin Production
Small bones often serve as the raw material for glue. In glue-works, the fat is first removed and supplied to the candle and soap trades. The remaining bone is treated for glue, and the final residue is processed into manures. In the gelatin industry, mineral matter is recovered from a hydrochloric acid solution. The liquors are filtered and mixed with a neutralizer, such as milk of lime, to precipitate bicalcium phosphate ($\text{CaHPO}_4$), which then becomes tricalcium phosphate ($\text{Ca}_3(\text{PO}_4)_2$) with additional lime. This resulting product is used in the manufacture of phosphorus and as a valuable manure.

## Chemical By-products and Carbonization
Some industrial practices involve extracting only about half of the gelatinous matter from degreased bones and carbonizing the residue. This is done by heating bones in horizontal cast-iron retorts for twelve to thirteen hours. The process yields 55% to 60% carbonized bone, known as animal charcoal, which is used for clarifying and filtering. 

The vapors released during this carbonization are condensed into two layers:
*   **Tarry Layer:** This is redistilled to produce "bone oil" (consisting of pyridine derivatives and fatty amines) and "bone pitch," which is used in the manufacture of black varnishes.
*   **Ammoniacal Liquor:** This is distilled with steam and received in sulphuric acid to produce ammonium sulphate, primarily used as a manure.

Inflammable gases evolved during carbonization and distillation are purified and used for illuminating or motive purposes.

## Mineral Recovery and Fertilizers
The principal inorganic constituent of bone is the calcium orthophosphate known as the normal salt, $\text{Ca}_3(\text{PO}_4)_2$. This mineral is the primary component of commercial "bone-ash." In certain regions, particularly South America, bone residues are burned in a retort to create this white ash, which contains 70-80% tricalcium phosphate and is used for high-grade superphosphates and manure. 

Other methods of producing fertilizer include grinding bone residues into a meal. This meal is either used directly or treated with sulphuric acid to create "superphosphate of lime," a more soluble form of phosphate that is more readily assimilated by growing plants.

## Associated Fat and Oil Recovery
The fat skimmed from bones during the simmering process is either bleached for use in fine soaps or expressed as unbleached oil to serve as an adulterant for other oils. The solid matter, or stearine, is supplied to candle-makers. In the broader meat-packing industry, such as that seen in Illinois between 1870 and 1905, fats are processed into soap, lard, and butterine.
