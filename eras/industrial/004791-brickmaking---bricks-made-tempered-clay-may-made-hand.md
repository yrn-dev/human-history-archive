# Brickmaking

Brickmaking is an ancient industrial art practiced by all civilized nations of antiquity. The process involves the shaping and drying—and often the firing—of clay to create durable building blocks.

## Historical Development
The earliest known burnt bricks were produced in Babylonia, where the method of burning dried clay blocks to create strong materials was likely discovered. Evidence shows that well-burnt bricks were produced there more than 6,000 years ago and were used extensively during the time of Sargon of Akkad (c. 3800 B.C.). The Babylonians and Assyrians achieved high proficiency in the craft, including the creation of bricks with enamel or coloured glaze for wall decoration. It is believed that the knowledge of brickmaking and glazed bricks traveled east from Babylonia through northern India and Persia to China. While the Great Wall of China utilized both burnt and unburnt bricks, this occurred at a later period (c. 210 B.C.).

In Egypt, brickmaking was the primary occupation of the Bani Israel during their bondage. These bricks, known as "adobes," were made from Nile river mud mixed with water and chopped reeds or straw, which acted as a binding material to increase plasticity. These were shaped by hand or in moulds and were sun-dried rather than burnt.

The Greeks produced three kinds of bricks, though they were not used extensively; the walls of Athens on the side toward Mount Hymettus are among the most significant Greek brick structures. The Romans later mastered the art, likely acquiring knowledge from Greece and Egypt. Around the beginning of the Christian era, the Romans revived and extended the manufacture of bricks, introducing kiln-burning and careful clay selection. They spread these methods across western Europe, including Britain and Germany.

## Brickmaking in Britain
After the Roman withdrawal from Britain, the art of making burnt bricks was largely lost until the 13th century. During this interval, builders often salvaged bricks from Roman ruins, such as those at St Albans Abbey and Colchester. Little Wenham Hall in Suffolk (c. A.D. 1210) is one of the earliest post-revival brick buildings, though bricks did not enter general use until the 15th century.

Under Henry VIII, brickmaking reached a high level of perfection, likely due to the influence of workmen from Flanders, as seen in the older sections of Hampton Court Palace and St James's Palace. Until the Great Fire of London in 1666, smaller dwellings were typically timber-framed with lath and plaster. Following the fire, bricks became the primary material for ordinary buildings. In 1625, a statute regulated brick sizes to a standard of 9 x 4½ x 3 inches. Additionally, a tax on bricks was levied in 1784, which remained in effect until 1850.

## Materials and Composition
Bricks are made from clays resulting from the decomposition of siliceous and felspathic rocks. These range from soft "alluvial" clays found near the surface to hard, rock-like shales. Older sedimentary deposits often require heavy machinery or long "weathering" (exposure to sun, frost, and rain) to become plastic.

The strength of brick clay is usually linked to the percentage of hydrated aluminium silicates. All such clays contain some free silica (sand) and a small amount of undecomposed felspar. Oxide of iron is a critical ingredient, typically ranging from 2% to 10%, which determines the brick's hardness, durability, and colour. A concentration of 5% to 8% oxide of iron generally produces a red brick. Higher temperatures or the presence of 3% to 4% alkalis can make the colour darker or more purple, while an excess of alumina tends to make the colour brighter and lighter.

## Manufacturing Methods
Bricks are categorized by the state of the clay used: plastic or semi-plastic.

### Hand-Making
The method for hand-making bricks is globally consistent. Tempered clay is pressed into a four-sided wooden or metal mould. To prevent sticking, the moulder dips the case in water or a mixture of water and sand. The clay is dashed into the mould, pressed into the corners by hand, and leveled with a wooden strip called a "strike." If the mould is placed on a "stock-board" with a central elevated tongue, a hollow called a "frog" is created in the bottom of the brick.

### Machine-Making
Machine-made bricks may be produced via two primary methods:
*   **Plastic Method:** Tempered clay is processed through grinding-mills (rollers or mortar pans) and pug-mills. A pug-mill uses a revolving shaft with cutters to knead the clay and force it through a delivery nose. This continuous mass of clay is then cut into bricks using a wire cutting frame, resulting in "wire-cuts."
*   **Semi-Plastic Method:** This method uses ground shale or hard clay that is moistened enough to bind under pressure. The material is crushed under heavy rollers in an iron grinding-pan, sifted through a screen to remove coarse particles, and stamped out by heavy presses. While semi-plastic bricks are not as strong as the best plastic bricks, this method allows for the use of clay-rocks that are otherwise impracticable and eliminates the need for ageing or weathering.
