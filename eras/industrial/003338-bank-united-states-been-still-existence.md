# Bank of the United States

## Establishment and Purpose
The first Bank of the United States was incorporated in 1791 as a national bank of issue. It served as an essential component of the financial system organized by Alexander Hamilton to manage the finances of the Federal government under the constitution of 1789. The bank was established with a capital of $10,000,000, which was divided into 25,000 shares valued at $400 each.

The institution performed several primary functions: it discounted commercial paper, issued circulating notes, and provided assistance to the government in its financial operations. While the government initially subscribed to one-fifth of the bank's capital, it did so through a roundabout process that effectively resulted in the bank loaning that amount to the treasury.

## Government Relations and Financials
The bank provided various loans to the government, and by the end of 1795, these obligations had reached $6,200,000. To settle these debts, the government gradually sold its bank stock, completing the disposal of all holdings by 1802 and realizing a profit of $671,860.

Although the bank did not release regular reports, a statement provided by Gallatin to congress on January 24, 1811, detailed the bank's resources. At that time, the bank held total resources of $24,183,046. This sum consisted of $5,009,567 in specie, $14,578,294 in discounts and loans, and $2,750,000 in United States stock.

## Charter Expiration and Controversy
The bank's charter expired in 1811. The renewal of this charter became the subject of a party contest, which ultimately prevented its renewal. The bank faced attacks on two primary fronts:
* **Constitutional grounds:** Critics argued that congress lacked the power to create such an institution.
* **Ownership grounds:** The bank was attacked due to foreign ownership, despite the fact that the twenty-five directors were required to be citizens of the United States and foreign shareholders were prohibited from voting by proxy.

## Impact on the War of 1812
The absence of the Bank of the United States created significant financial difficulties for the government during the war with Great Britain, which began the year after the charter expired. Consequently, the government was forced to rely on state banks during the War of 1812.

In 1814, state banks suspended specie payments. This suspension made the transfer of funds between different parts of the Union very difficult for the treasury, as notes from one section of the country did not circulate easily in another. Gallatin recorded the opinion that the suspension of specie payments might have been prevented had the former Bank of the United States still been in existence at that time.
