# Combination Bridges

A combination bridge is a structural design constructed using a mixture of materials, specifically timber and steel. In these structures, the tension members are generally composed of steel, while the compression members are typically made of timber.

## Regional Usage and Materials
Combination bridges remain in significant use on the Pacific coast, a region where steel works are distant but excellent timber is readily available. The specific materials used in these bridges can vary based on the component's function. For example, while compression members are generally timber, certain elements—such as bottom chord panels next to river piers and struts—may be constructed of steel. Additionally, iron is used for tension members, steel for pins, and cast-iron for post shoes and chord blocks. Other components, including the railing, floor, and floor beams, are constructed of timber.

## Case Study: Roseburgh, Oregon
An example of this construction is the combination bridge at Roseburgh, Oregon. This structure is a cantilever bridge with a total distance of 584 ft. between its anchor piers. The bridge consists of several distinct sections:
*   **Shore arms:** 147 ft. span.
*   **River arms:** 105 ft. span.
*   **Suspended girder:** 80 ft. span.

## Historical Context of Bridge Materials
The use of combination materials follows a broader evolution of bridge engineering. In medieval and classical times, bridges were built from masonry, timber, and later, concrete or brick. Wrought iron began to be utilized in the late 18th century, initially in combination with cast iron or timber. 

By the early 19th century, wrought iron was used extensively for suspension road bridges. In the middle of the 19th century, entirely wrought iron structures were erected, such as the great girder bridges at Saltash (near Plymouth) and over the Menai Strait. From 1880 onwards, mild steel—which possesses greater toughness and tenacity than wrought iron—largely superseded wrought iron, except for girders with a span of less than 100 ft.

## Evolution of Truss and Girder Designs
Early American timber bridges served as models for later metal trusses. Between the end of the 18th century and the middle of the 19th century, large-span timber bridges were common in America, such as the Bellows Falls bridge (two spans of 184 ft.) and the Colossus bridge (a flat arched truss with a 340 ft. span). 

Starting in 1840, American designs adopted trusses that combined timber with cast-iron shoes and wrought-iron tension-rods. Notable examples include:
*   **The Howe truss (1830):** Featured timber chords and a lattice of timber struts with vertical iron ties.
*   **The Pratt truss (1844):** Featured inclined ties and vertical struts.

As railway loads increased, some Howe trusses were stiffened with timber arches on each side of the girder, though this composite system was considered fundamentally defective due to the indeterminate distribution of loading between the two independent systems.

## Modern Material Transitions
The introduction of metal allowed for the construction of great spans ranging from 500 to 1800 ft. In addition to steel and iron, the latest material changes include the use of armoured concrete, ferro-concrete, or concrete strengthened with steel bars, specifically for arched bridges.
