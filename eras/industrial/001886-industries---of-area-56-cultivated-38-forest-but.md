# Global Industrial and Agricultural Profiles

The following records detail the industrial and agricultural characteristics of various regions, highlighting the relationship between land use—specifically cultivation and forest cover—and the resulting economic outputs.

## European Regional Industries

In Baden, 56.8% of the area is cultivated and 38% is forest. While the agricultural industry previously provided the majority of the country's wealth, it is now matched or surpassed by industrial output. Key manufactured goods include chemicals, paper, leather, tobacco, china, glass, jewellery, clocks, silk ribbons, and cotton and woollen goods, as well as machinery. Beet sugar is also produced on a large scale. In the Black Forest, inhabitants are noted for producing musical boxes, organs, toys, and wooden ornaments. Mannheim serves as the chief manufacturing town and a major emporium for goods exported via the Rhine.

Bohemia is highly developed agriculturally, with over 50% of its area under cultivation. Notable fertile regions include the "Garden of Bohemia" (Leitmeritz), "Paradise" (Teplitz), and the "Golden Road" (Koniggratz). Principal crops include wheat, barley, rye, and oats, though beetroot cultivation has increased due to competition from Hungarian wheat. Potatoes serve as the staple food. The Saaz district is known for hops, and the region produces high-quality flax and abundant fruit, particularly plums. Forests cover 29.01% of the area. Other industries include bee-cultivation, the rearing of geese (especially in the south), and pisciculture. Silkworm rearing has also seen fair success.

In the province of Kuopio, Finland, only 2.3% of the surface is under cultivation, with chief crops being oats, barley, rye, and potatoes. Forests occupy 2,672,240 hectares. Industrial establishments include distilleries, paper-mills, saw-mills, tanneries, chemical works, and engineering works. Domestic industry focuses on the preparation of sledges, carts, and other wooden goods. Mining is significant, with 38,800 tons of iron ore extracted annually, supporting ten iron-works that produce 12,000 tons of pig iron and 6,420 tons of iron and steel.

In another region where the climate is severe and the Bora wind affects the south, only 14.8% of the land is cultivated, and crops are insufficient for local needs. Forests occupy 44.4%. The timber trade is of considerable importance, and flax is grown in large quantities. While general industry is not well developed, linen and lace weaving are pursued as household industries. Mining produces coal, zinc, copper ore, iron, and mercury, the latter of which is extracted at Idria.

## Asian Agricultural and Forest Industries

In the District of Broach, the land is rich and highly cultivated, consisting of an alluvial plain without forests. The chief industry is dealing in cotton, managed by a guild. This is supported by cotton mills in Broach city and various factories for pressing and ginning cotton. Principal crops include pulse, wheat, millet, and cotton.

Burma possesses some of the finest forests in British India, serving as a primary global source of teak for shipbuilding. Teak is preferred over oak for iron ships because its essential oil preserves iron and steel. The government strictly preserves these forests through a forest department that manages conservation, prevents fires, and plants young trees. Commercial timber requires a license. Beyond teak, valuable woods include *padauk*, *pyingado* (ironwood used for railway sleepers), *sha* (cutch), and india rubber. In Lower Burma, Sulpiz Kurz enumerated some 1,500 species of trees in 1877.

In the southern Malay Archipelago, Java is the most advanced, best cultivated, and most thickly peopled island, with a population chiefly occupied with agriculture. In contrast, Sumatra is thinly peopled, with dense forests covering most of the surface and cultivation limited mostly to low lands in the central volcanic region.

## Americas: Forest and Mineral Exploitation

Bolivia derives its greatest immediate profits from forest products. Rubber-collecting, which began between 1880 and 1890, was the most profitable industry, reaching an annual output of nearly 3,500 metric tons before its best forests were transferred to Brazil in 1903. Rubber-producing *Hevea* is still found in the Madre de Dios and Beni forests. Another long-standing industry is the collection of cinchona bark for quinine sulphate; however, this industry is destructive, and the forests are diminishing.

Costa Rica is essentially agricultural, with more than two-thirds of the population consisting of peasant proprietors. Coffee, planted in 1838, is grown on the San Jose plateau in volcanic ash soil. Bananas are grown over increasing areas, while the coast-lands produce indigo, cotton, sugar-cane, vanilla, and cocoa. The Atlantic slopes contain virgin forests with ebony, rubber, rosewood, mahogany, cedar, and precious dye-woods like fustic. While stock-farming is relatively undeveloped, the state imports sheep, cattle, horses, and swine from the United States and Europe. Mining has focused on gold, silver, and copper, though silver and copper mines were abandoned by the beginning of the 20th century.
