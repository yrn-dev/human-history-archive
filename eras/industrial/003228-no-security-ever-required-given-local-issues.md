# Local Note Issues and Security in the United Kingdom

## Security of Local Issues
In the United Kingdom, no security was ever required to be given for local issues. While the acts of 1844-1845 compelled Scottish and Irish banks to hold specie against notes issued beyond the legal limit, this coin was not considered a security for those notes. The state's only recognition of the duty to render note-holders secure occurred through the legislation of 1879. This legislation established that note issues were a first charge, with unlimited liability, on the total assets of joint-stock banks that had accepted limited liability for other areas of their business.

The lack of sufficient recognition regarding this duty is noted as a real disadvantage to England. Consequently, the provincial note issue—which provided a considerable advantage to customers and was a convenient power for banks to possess—was swept away without any attempt to remedy its deficiencies. In contrast, the security of note issues for national banks in the United States, which were made against government bonds, has scarcely ever been questioned, despite potential objections to a note circulation secured by such bonds.

## The Bank Act of 1844 and Provincial Banks
The Bank Act of 1844 was designed to concentrate note issues within the Bank of England to secure its monopoly as the sole issuer in Wales and England. This resulted in nearly all provincial banks in England losing their right of issue by 1906. This loss of local issues deprived English industries and banking of advantages that had been present in Ireland and Scotland. It is suggested that if English country banks had been permitted to associate and retain their issues, as Scottish banks did, powerful banks would have been established throughout Wales and England much earlier, and recent amalgamations would have occurred on terms more favorable to the public.

## The Role of the Bank of England
Under the act of 1844, the Bank of England became the sole source for obtaining legal tender notes, a position of significant importance during times of pressure. Between 1844 and 1906, the government granted authority to supply notes exceeding the limit fixed by the act on only three occasions, following requests from the bank. In these instances, the Bank of England became the center of interest, as a "treasury letter" was required to permit an excess issue.

## Issues on Securities and International Comparisons
There have been discussions between the government and bankers regarding granting the Bank of England an automatic power to augment the note issue on securities during necessary periods, similar to the powers of the Bank of Germany (*Reichsbank*). Such plans were hindered by the government and treasury requiring an extremely high rate of interest on advances. For example, a proposal by Mr. Lowe (later Lord Sherbrooke) in 1873 to charge 12% failed because such a high rate is typically associated with business risks and tends to intensify panic.

Conversely, the arrangement between the *Reichsbank* and the treasury of the German empire aimed to prevent panic and provide required banking accommodation. This was achieved by charging a moderate rate, generally 5%, though it reached 7% for a short time in 1899. Between 1881 and the end of 1906, the duty on the extra issue of the Bank of Germany totaled £839,052, providing funds for the relief of taxation while business continued normally.

Regarding the Bank of England, the act of 1844 remains unaltered. The issue on securities originally allowed was £14,000,000, which was increased to £18,450,000 by March 29, 1901. Due to this increase, the bank now holds £4,450,000 less gold against issued notes than it would have if the 1844 security arrangements had remained unchanged.
