# Gouldsbury Mission to Eastern Akim, Juabin and Kumasi

## Overview of the Mission
In late 1875, the colonial government dispatched Dr. V.S. Gouldsbury on a diplomatic mission to Kumasi, Juabin, and Eastern Akim. The primary objective of this mission was to repair the effects caused by the previous inaction of the colonial government. However, the mission was ultimately unsuccessful.

## Political Context and Colonial Inaction
The mission took place in the aftermath of the war of 1873-74. One consequence of that conflict was that several states dependent on Ashanti declared their independence and requested British protection. The colonial office refused to grant this protection, and this period of inaction contributed to the reconsolidation of Ashanti power. 

Following the war, the Ashanti deposed Kofi Karikari and placed his brother, Mensa, on the "golden stool," which served as the symbol of sovereignty. Mensa's reign was characterized as tyrannous, marked by repeated human sacrifices and the violation of almost every article of the Fomana treaty. Specifically, the indemnity payments were largely ignored; only 4,000 oz. of gold were paid out of the stipulated 50,000 oz.

## Geography and Regional Administration
The mission's destinations were located within a region characterized by dense forest land, estimated at fully 12,000 sq. m. Kumasi, the capital of Ashanti, is situated on a low rocky eminence in a clearing of this forest. It serves as the distributing center for the hinterland and the whole of Ashanti.

The Ashanti people are divided into numerous tribes, including the Juabin and the Kumasi, among others such as the Bekwai, Adansi, Kokofu, Mampon, Nsuta, Nkwanta, Dadiassi, Daniassi, Ofinsu, and Adjisu. While each tribe possesses its own king, the king of Kumasi has been recognized as the paramount king of Ashanti since the beginning of the 18th century.

## Regional Infrastructure and Communication
Travel to these regions during the period of the mission and subsequent years relied on tortuous forest paths too narrow for wheeled vehicles, or the use of palanquins carried by four people. While a 141 m. road was eventually cut from Cape Coast to Kumasi, and a railway was completed in 1903, earlier communications were limited. The region is traversed by various rivers, including the Prah and the Volta; however, with the exception of the Volta, these rivers were navigable only by canoes.
