# RULE IX.--CARGO, SHIP'S MATERIALS, AND STORES BURNT FOR FUEL

Rule IX governs the specific conditions under which the burning of a ship's cargo, stores, or materials for fuel is admitted as General Average (G.A.). This rule applies when such items are necessarily burnt for fuel for the common safety during a time of peril.

## Conditions for Admission as General Average
Under Rule IX, the burning of cargo, ship's materials, and stores is admitted as G.A. only when an ample supply of fuel had been provided for the voyage. While the loss of these materials is recognized as a sacrifice for the common safety, the rule stipulates a specific financial adjustment regarding the fuel that should have been available. The estimated quantity of coals that would have been consumed is calculated based on the price current at the ship's last port of departure on the date of her leaving. This amount is then charged to the shipowner and credited to the G.A.

## Related Provisions for Common Safety
Rule IX exists within a broader framework of rules concerning sacrifices and expenses incurred for the common safety. For example, Rule II provides that damage done to a ship and cargo by a sacrifice made for the common safety is made good as G.A. Similarly, Rule III allows for G.A. compensation for damage caused by water or beaching during the extinguishing of a shipboard fire, though it excludes compensation for portions of the ship or cargo that were actually on fire.

Other rules address the movement of cargo to ensure safety or facilitate repairs. When a ship is ashore, the discharge of cargo, bunker coals, and ship's stores to float the vessel allows the loss, damage, and extra costs of lightening, reshipping, and lighter hire to be admitted as G.A. Furthermore, Rule X (a) allows expenses for entering a port of refuge—and subsequently leaving it—if such action was necessary for the common safety.

## Cargo Handling and Repairs in Ports of Refuge
When a ship enters a port of refuge or returns to a port of loading due to extraordinary circumstances, accident, or sacrifice, several associated costs may be admitted as G.A.:
* **Discharging and Reloading:** The cost of discharging cargo is admitted if it was necessary for common safety or to enable repairs to the ship that were required for the safe prosecution of the voyage. This applies whether the repairs occur at a port of refuge or a port of call. Consequently, the costs of reloading, storing the cargo on board, and any associated storage charges are also admitted.
* **Damage During Handling:** Under Rule XII, damage or loss of cargo necessarily caused during the acts of discharging, storing, reloading, and stowing is made good as G.A., provided the costs of those measures were themselves admitted as G.A.
* **Crew Maintenance:** The wages and maintenance of the master, officers, and crew during the extra period of detention in a port of refuge are admitted as G.A. until the ship is ready to proceed.

However, these admissions are limited. If a ship is condemned or the voyage is abandoned, storage expenses and crew wages incurred after the date of condemnation or abandonment are not admitted as G.A.

## Fuel Types and Ship Efficiency
While Rule IX specifically mentions the burning of materials and coals, maritime history shows a transition in fuel types. In large passenger steamers, particularly those in the Atlantic trade operating at 20 knots and above, oil fuel offers advantages over coal. Using a consumption ratio of 3 to 2 (coal to oil), oil fuel can increase dead weight cargo by 1,000 tons and provide an additional 100,000 cubic feet of measurement cargo. This is achieved by storing oil in double bottom spaces previously used for water ballast, leaving ordinary bunkers free. Oil fuel also provides increased speed, cleanliness, and faster bunkering, though the installation of oil-tight tanks, pumps, and valves is costly.

In warships, fuel choice is driven by strategy and fighting efficiency rather than economy. Liquid fuel can reduce fuel weight by 30% and eliminate the need for stokers, who often make up more than half the ship's company. This reduction in crew allows for a doubling of the radius of effective action regarding consumable stores. Additionally, 36 cubic feet of stored oil possesses the same practical calorific value as 67 cubic feet of coal. Despite these gains, coal has been used in the side bunkers of unarmoured ships as a form of protection against shell and shot, a benefit not provided by liquid fuel.
