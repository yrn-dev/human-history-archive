# Wrought Iron and Steel Plate Web Girders

Wrought iron and, subsequently, steel plate web girders were utilized extensively for the construction of railway bridges in England. This widespread adoption followed the construction of the Menai and Conway bridges. The design process for these structures led to the first understanding of the proper function of the vertical web located between the top and bottom flanges of a girder.

## Engineering and Economic Considerations
In the design of plate web girders, there is a relationship between the depth of the girder and the stress resisted by the flanges; the stress varies inversely as the depth increases. While increasing the depth of the girder would be economical regarding the flanges, it necessitates a much heavier web. Consequently, a specific ratio of depth to span must be established to achieve maximum economy.

For plate webs, this ratio could not be economically increased beyond 1/12 to 1/15. The necessity for a considerable excess of material in plate webs is due to two factors: the need to stiffen the web against buckling and the requirement for additional thickness to reduce the effects of corrosion. For example, the Britannia bridge featured a depth to span proportion of 1/16.

## Material Evolution and Comparison
The history of bridge construction transitioned from timber, masonry, brick, and concrete in classical and medieval times to the use of wrought iron in the late 18th century. Initially, wrought iron was used in combination with cast iron or timber. While cast iron was used for early railway bridges and is now limited to arched bridges of moderate span, wrought iron became a primary material for 19th-century suspension road bridges and large girder bridges, such as those at Saltash and the Menai Strait.

From 1880 onwards, mild steel—which possesses greater toughness and tenacity than wrought iron—began to supersede wrought iron. Currently, wrought iron is only used for girders with a span of less than 100 feet; for spans exceeding this length, mild steel is used. The introduction of these metals enabled the construction of great spans ranging from 500 to 1,800 feet.

## Structural Variations and Efficiency
While plate web girders are now restricted to spans of less than 100 feet, other designs offer better material efficiency. Framed, braced, open web, or lattice girders are more economical than solid web girders, except when used for small spans. In America, these types of girders were used from the beginning, following the general design of earlier timber bridges.

Compared to arches or chains, girder superstructures require more material because a girder acts as a combination of both an arch and a chain. However, girders offer distinct advantages: they are easier to erect and impose only a vertical load on abutments and piers, avoiding the horizontal thrust associated with suspension chains or arches.

## Applications and Examples
The expansion of railways since 1830 necessitated a vast number of girder bridges. Notable examples include:

*   **The Britannia Bridge:** This structure consists of a pair of tubular girders with plate sides stiffened by angle irons. Each girder is 1,511 feet long and weighs 4,680 tons. To prevent buckling and facilitate painting, the top and bottom were constructed as cellular.
*   **The Saltash Bridge:** Built shortly after the Britannia bridge, this design utilized a curved top boom (an elliptical wrought iron tube) and a bottom boom consisting of a pair of wrought-iron link chains, which obviated the need for a web except in cases of non-uniform loading.
*   **The Ohio Bridge:** Constructed in 1888, this is likely the largest girder span, featuring a center girder 545 feet long and 84 feet deep. It utilizes mild steel for the majority of the structure, with wrought iron used for the wind-bracing, stringers, and cross girders.

Beyond bridges, steel girders are employed in building construction for double flooring. In these instances, steel girders span from wall to wall, with wood plates bolted to their flanges to receive single fir joists.
