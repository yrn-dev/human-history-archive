# British Military Operations and the Ashanti Rebellion

## The Campaign to Crush the Rebellion
Following the relief of Kumasi, British forces focused on the task of crushing a rebellion. Under the command of Colonel Willcocks, the military force was expanded to include 3,368 natives, consisting of Sikhs from Central Africa and Yaos. This native force was supported by 134 British officers and 35 British non-commissioned officers. Additionally, Ashanti levies were utilized in the operations.

The campaign reached a decisive point on September 30, when the Kumasi were completely defeated at Obassa. This victory led to the surrender of many rebel chiefs; the final two remaining in the field were captured on December 28. Peace was restored by the opening of 1901. The total casualties for the campaign, which included those who died from disease, numbered 1,007. Among the British officers, nine were killed in action, six died of disease, and forty-three were wounded. For his leadership, Colonel Willcocks was created a K.C.M.G. and received a promotion.

## Ashanti Sovereignty and the Golden Stool
The Ashanti political structure centered on the "golden stool," which served as the symbol of sovereignty. Following the war of 1873-74, the Ashanti deposed Kofi Karikari and placed his brother, Mensa, on the golden stool. Mensa's reign was characterized as tyrannous and marked by repeated human sacrifices. He also violated nearly every article of the Fomana treaty; of the 50,000 oz. of gold stipulated as an indemnity, only 4,000 oz. were paid.

In 1883, a revolution displaced Mensa, and he was succeeded by Kwaka Dua II, who died in June 1884 shortly after his election. The former king, Kofi Karikari, also died in June 1884. This period of disruption led to a desolating civil war. Eventually, Prince Prempeh (who took the name Kwaka Dua III) was chosen as king on March 26, 1888. His election was sanctioned by an officer of the Gold Coast government, as the colonial government had been forced to intervene due to troubles in the Gold Coast.

## Conflicts with the British Protectorate
While Prempeh initially brought peace and prosperity, fresh trouble emerged in 1893 between the Ashanti and tribes of the protectorate, leading Prempeh to order the closure of roads to traders. Because the country was included in the British sphere of influence by international agreement, the British government intervened. A mission was sent to Prempeh demanding he accept a British protectorate, receive a resident at Kumasi, and fulfill the 1874 treaty.

Prempeh refused to treat with the governor of the Gold Coast and instead sent informal agents to England, though the secretary of state refused to receive them. Prempeh failed to reply to the demands of the British mission within the three-week grace period, which expired on October 31, 1895.

## Earlier Anglo-Ashanti Wars
Earlier conflicts were driven by territorial disputes and the captivity of Europeans. A primary cause of the 1873-74 war was the British acquisition of Elmina fort from the Dutch on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti viewed the Elmina as their subjects and resented the British occupation of what they considered Ashanti territory. Additionally, the Ashanti held four Europeans captive in Kumasi: Monsieur M.J. Bonnat, a French trader, and members of the Basel mission—Mr. J. Kuhne and Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer and his wife.

On January 22, 1873, an Ashanti force invaded the British protectorate, defeating the Fanti and encamping at Mampon. In response, the British parliament voted £800,000 for an expedition led by Sir Garnet Wolseley. Wolseley landed at Cape Coast on October 2 and marched toward Kumasi, while Captain John Glover cooperated from the east with native levies.

Earlier hostilities in 1824 saw Sir Charles M'Carthy, the first crown-appointed governor, defeated by the Ashanti at Essamako on January 21. The Ashanti force of 10,000 men overwhelmed Sir Charles's 500. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed, and the governor's skull was later used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. Peace was not purchased until 1831, when the king paid 600 oz. of gold to administrator George Maclean and sent his son as a hostage.

## Kumasi
Kumasi serves as the capital of Ashanti in British West Africa. Situated on a low rocky eminence in a clearing of dense forest, the town's name (more correctly Kum-ase) refers to the okum trees found in its streets. As of 1906, the population was 6,280, exceeding 12,000 when including suburbs. 

The town features a fort built in 1896, which serves as the residence of the chief commissioner and can house a garrison of several hundred men. Other infrastructure includes European and native hospitals, stations for the Wesleyan and Basel missions, and officers' quarters. While native houses are built of red clay and often richly ornamented, the principal Ashanti chiefs own large houses built in the European style, which are leased to strangers.
