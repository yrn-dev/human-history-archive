# Mahommedans and Parsees in Bombay

## Population and Social Influence
Bombay is characterized by a vast variety of racial and ethnic types due to its central position between the East and West. Among the large sections of its population—which include Hindus, Mahommedans, Parsees, Jains, and Christians—the Parsees are noted for being one of the smallest groups, numbering only some 46,000. Despite their small size, they are among the most influential. Parsee millionaires own most of the great business houses and have founded most of the large charities. 

The city's population also includes other diverse groups such as the dominant Mahratta race (next to the European rulers), Arab traders from the Gulf, Afghans and Sikhs from northern India, Bengalis, Rajputs, Chinese, Japanese, Malays, negroes, Tibetans, Sinhalese, and Siamese.

## Religious and Cultural Practices
The Parsees maintain specific funerary customs at the Parsee Towers of Silence, located towards the northern end of Malabar hill. In these towers, the Parsees expose their dead until vultures devour the flesh; subsequently, the bones are cast into a well where they crumble into dust.

In terms of official recognition, the British government confers the title of Khan Bahadur upon both Mahommedans and Parsis.

## Governance and Representation
The municipal government of Bombay, framed by an act of the Bombay legislative council in 1888, consists of a town council and a municipal corporation. The corporation is composed of 72 members. While 16 are nominated by the government, the remainder are elected by various bodies: 36 by ratepayers, 16 by justices of the peace, 2 by the chamber of commerce, and 2 by the senate of the university. The membership of this corporation includes Europeans, Hindus, Mahommedans, and Parsees.

## Education and the University of Bombay
The Bombay University was constituted in 1857 as an examining body, modeled after the university of London. It is a body corporate consisting of fellows, a vice-chancellor, and a chancellor; the governor of Bombay serves as the chancellor *ex officio*. 

The city hosts several chief educational institutions, including:
* The government Elphinstone College
* The Grant medical college
* The government law school
* The Sir Jamsetjee Jeejeebhoy school of art
* The Victoria Jubilee technical institute
* Two missionary colleges, namely Wilson and St Xavier

By 1905, the total number of educational institutions in the region reached 10,194, serving 593,431 pupils. However, census data from 1901 indicates a high rate of illiteracy, with nearly 24 million people illiterate out of a population of 25-1/2 millions.

## Historical Context
The island of Bombay was originally composed of seven islands and was inhabited by the Kolis—a race of fishermen and husbandmen—who occupied the area probably around the beginning of the Christian era. The city's name is derived from Mumba, a form of the goddess Parvati. 

The area was part of the dominions of several dynasties, including the Rashtrakutas, Chalukyas, Mauryas, and Satavahanas. During the Maurya and Chalukya period (450-750), the principal place in the harbour was the city of Puri on Elephanta Island. The first town built on Bombay Island, Mahikavati (Mahim), was founded by King Bhima (likely of the house of the Yadavas of Deogiri) following a raid into the Deccan by Ala-ud-din Khilji in 1294. Mahikavati remained under Hindu rule until 1348, when it was captured by a Mahommedan force from Gujarat. The islands remained part of the kingdom or province of Gujarat until 1534, when Sultan Bahadur ceded them to the Portuguese.
