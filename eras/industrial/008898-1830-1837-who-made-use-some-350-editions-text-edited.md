# Literary Editions and Textual Scholarship

The process of establishing and maintaining accurate literary texts involves the study of various editions, the comparison of manuscripts, and the application of critical scholarship. This practice ranges from the collection of multiple versions of a single work to the creation of definitive critical texts.

## The Role of Editions in Textual Study
In the field of book-collecting, some collectors seek to acquire as many editions of a single work as possible. While the acquisition of late editions may sometimes only serve as an index of an author's popularity, intermediate editions can be highly valuable for studying the development of a final text. This is particularly true for liturgical works, translations of the Bible, and editions published during the lifetime of the author. Generally, early editions—specifically first editions—are regarded with the most interest and command the highest prices, although an edition that introduces important textual changes may also gain prestige.

## Critical Texts and Scholarly Editions
Establishing a critical text often requires the exhaustive comparison of existing versions. For example, Berriat Saint-Prix established the critical text of the works of Boileau in *Oeuvres de Boileau* (4 vols., 1830-1837), utilizing approximately 350 different editions. Boileau himself is noted as the first to reduce French versification to rule and emphasize the value of workmanship. His "favourite" edition, *Oeuvres diverses* (1701), was later reprinted with notes and variants by Alphonse Pauly in 1894.

In the study of Dante's *Commedia*, Carl Witte made the first serious recent attempt to establish an accurate text in 1862. This edition served as the basis for the Oxford edition of Dante's complete works in 1896 and subsequent issues; Dr. Toynbee's 1900 text also followed the Oxford version with some modifications.

## Variorum and Collected Works
Variorum editions aim to summarize and represent the conclusions of the best authorities regarding textual, critical, and annotative matters. Horace Howard Furness, an American Shakespearian scholar, produced a new Variorum edition of Shakespeare's plays beginning in 1871. His work included *Romeo and Juliet* (1871), *Macbeth* (1873), *Hamlet* (1877), *King Lear* (1880), *Othello* (1886), *The Merchant of Venice* (1888), *As You Like It* (1890), *The Tempest* (1892), *A Midsummer Night's Dream* (1895), *The Winter's Tale* (1898), *Much Ado about Nothing* (1899), *Twelfth Night* (1901), and *Love's Labour's Lost* (1904). A noted inconsistency in Furness's work is that he used the First Folio text as his basis starting with *Othello* (1858), whereas he used the Cambridge (Globe) editors' text for other plays.

The works of Robert Burns have also seen numerous collected editions. While W. Craibe Angus listed 930 items in *Printed Works of Robert Burns* (1899), key editions include the 1800 version edited anonymously by Dr. Currie, the 1834 eight-volume set by Allan Cunningham, and the 1834-1836 edition by James Hogg and William Motherwell. Sir H. Nicolas provided an improved text in the second "Aldine Edition" (1839) by utilizing many original manuscripts. Robert Chambers's *Life and Works of Robert Burns* (1851-1852) presented the works chronologically, though the text was bowdlerized.

## Translation and Commentary
Translations often rely on specific editions to ensure accuracy. A translation of Plutarch was made from the 1839 edition by C. Sintenis, which was compared against the 1826 edition by G.H. Schaefer. Commentaries further enhance these texts; for the *Commedia*, Scartazzini's commentary is the most voluminous since the 15th century. In the case of Robert Burns, William Motherwell provided notes attempting to trace the sources of the poet's songs.

## Editorial Corrections in Historical Texts
Modern reprints of historical works often include corrections based on comparisons with later editions or external sources. In the *History of Greece* by George Grote, editors have corrected printer errors and Greek text by comparing volumes with later editions and the Perseus project. Such corrections include adjusting references (e.g., changing "Thucyd. vi, 69" to "Thucyd, i, 69") and updating spellings to maintain consistency, such as changing "Phokæn" to "Phokæan."
