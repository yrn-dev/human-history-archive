# Naval and Military History Sources and Marine Forces

The study of naval and military history is supported by a variety of specialized memoirs, chronicles, and institutional records. Among these, *Beatson's Naval and Military Memoirs* (London, 1804) serves as a primary reference for the earlier campaigns of figures such as Lord Hood. Other significant works include James's *Naval History* and the French accounts provided by Troudes in *Batailles navales de la France* and Chevalier's histories of the French navy during the Republic and the American War of Independence.

## Privateering and Naval Warfare
Historical naval conflicts were often characterized by the prominence of privateering. During certain periods, Spanish forces in the West Indies and at home, as well as French forces in all seas, operated with great success. An example of this was Mahe de la Bourdonnais's attack on Madras, which was largely a privateering venture. 

The British responded with vigor, though the total number of captures by Spanish and French corsairs was likely higher than those made by the British. This disparity is attributed to two factors: the higher volume of British merchant ships available to be taken, and the fact that the British government had not yet strictly enforced the use of convoys.

## Evolution of the Royal Marine Forces
The Royal Marines have evolved from a purely military force into a specialized organization. Historically, the force was a military body applied to naval purposes, appearing and disappearing in accordance with the state of war. Its primary functions were three-fold: fighting aboard ships, seizing and holding land positions to aid naval operations, and maintaining ship discipline by providing an example of expert arms handling for seamen.

In early periods, marines were organized into regiments embarked in fleets, and their officers were interchangeable with those of the line and guards. Notable figures such as George Rooke (later Admiral Sir George Rooke) and John Churchill (later the Duke of Marlborough) served as ensigns of marines. During this era, the navy did not utilize marines during peacetime.

## The Royal Marine Artillery
Until 1804, the marines functioned as an infantry force. However, improvements in artillery at the end of the 18th century led to the temporary use of Royal Artillery detachments in the fleet. Due to friction between artillery and naval officers, the Royal Marine Artillery (RMA) was raised in 1804 upon the recommendation of Nelson. The RMA was responsible for the equipment and operation of field-guns ashore and larger ordnance afloat, while also serving as gunnery instructors.

The corps faced a period of instability in the 19th century. In 1831, the RMA was abolished without warning, and the marine force reverted to a single infantry corps. Shortly thereafter, the Admiralty began restoring the capability by converting one company of each infantry marine division into artillery. These companies were eventually formed into a separate division in 1859, and the name Royal Marine Artillery was officially restored in 1862.

## Organization and Traditions
The modern designation for the combined force is the Royal Marine Forces, consisting of two corps. The Royal Marine Artillery is headquartered at Eastney, Southsea, and wears blue uniforms with red facings. The Royal Marine Light Infantry (RMLI) is based at Devonport, Chatham, and Gosport, wearing red uniforms with blue facings. Both corps share a common badge—a globe surrounded by a laurel wreath—and the motto "Per mare per terram."

Due to an original association with the London train bands, the Royal Marine Forces hold the privilege of marching through the city of London with bands playing, bayonets fixed, and colours flying. This privilege is shared with the Royal London Militia, the East Kent Regiment (formerly the Buffs), and the 3rd Battalion Grenadier Guards.

## Notable Military Figures and Contributions
The intersection of naval and military expertise is exemplified by figures such as Sir Howard Douglas (1776-1861). A British general and son of Admiral Sir Charles Douglas, he served in the Royal Artillery and later as a major in the York Rangers. Douglas contributed significantly to military science through his writings, including an *Essay on the Principles and Construction of Military Bridges* (1816) and a *Treatise on Naval Gunnery* (1820), the latter of which became a standard textbook.
