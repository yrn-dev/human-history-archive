# Universalist Magazine

The *Universalist Magazine* was a denominational publication founded in 1819 by Hosea Ballou, an American Universalist clergyman. In the years following its establishment, the publication was renamed *The Trumpet*.

## Founder and Editorial Influence
The magazine was founded and edited by Hosea Ballou (1771–1852), who is recognized as a principal American expositor of Universalism. Born in Richmond, New Hampshire, Ballou was a self-educated man and the son of a Baptist minister, Maturin Ballou. After converting to Universalism in 1789, Ballou served as a pastor in various locations, including Dana, Massachusetts; Barnard, Vermont; Portsmouth, New Hampshire; and Salem, Massachusetts. From December 1817 until his death in 1852, he served as the pastor of the Second Universalist Church in Boston.

Ballou's editorial work with the *Universalist Magazine* and *The Trumpet* formed part of a larger body of denominational literature that served as his great contribution to his Church. In addition to this magazine, he founded and edited *The Universalist Expositor* in 1831, which was later renamed *The Universalist Quarterly Review*.

## Theological Context
The literature produced by Ballou, including his editorial work, reflected a specific theological direction within American Universalism. While John Murray has also been called "the father of American Universalism," Ballou differed from Murray's theology by opposing trinitarian views and legalism. Furthermore, Ballou sought to divest Universalism of every trace of Calvinism. His theological positions were further detailed in his written works, such as *Notes on the Parables* (1804), *A Treatise on Atonement* (1805), and *Examination of the Doctrine of a Future Retribution* (1834).

## Related Denominational Literature
The *Universalist Quarterly Review* (formerly *The Universalist Expositor*) continued the tradition of denominational publishing. This publication was edited by the elder Hosea Ballou in association with his grand-nephew, also named Hosea Ballou (1796–1861). The younger Ballou was a prominent figure in the faith who preached in Stafford, Connecticut, as well as in Roxbury and Medford, Massachusetts. He later became the first president of Tufts College in 1853, having urged the necessity of a Universalist denominational college in 1847.
