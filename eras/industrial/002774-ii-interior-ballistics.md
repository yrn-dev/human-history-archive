# Interior Ballistics

Interior ballistics is a branch of ballistics—the science of throwing projectiles or warlike missiles—that focuses on the analysis of powder-gas pressure within the bore of a gun. While exterior ballistics examines the motion of a projectile after it has received its initial impulse, interior ballistics investigates the dynamics of the shot's movement up the bore and the requisite powder charge needed to achieve an initial velocity without causing undue strain to the gun.

## Theoretical Framework
A gun is conceptualized in interior ballistics as a simple heat-engine or thermo-dynamic machine. Unlike ordinary gas or steam engines that operate in periodic cycles, a gun performs its work in a single stroke. The study of interior ballistics involves investigating the relations between the temperature, volume, and pressure of the powder-gas inside the bore, as well as the work realized through the expansion of the powder and the resulting stress placed upon the material of the gun.

## Explosives and Propellants
For six hundred years, black gunpowder was the primary propellant used in artillery. In recent years, it has been replaced by modern explosives, such as cordite, which is adopted in the British service. Cordite is produced by squeezing a viscous composition through a die to create short lengths of cord. The size of the cordite is designated by the diameter of the hole in hundredths of an inch; for example, cordite size 30 is squeezed through a hole 0.30 inches in diameter.

Modern explosives offer several military advantages over black powder, most notably being nearly smokeless. They are capable of producing a much larger volume of gas at higher pressures and temperatures—more than threefold that of older propellants—which allows the necessary charge to be reduced in proportion. While the thermochemical properties of an explosive's constituents set the upper limits for pressure, temperature, and volume, practical application requires extensive experimentation.

## Pressure and Chamber Dynamics
A primary challenge in interior ballistics is adjusting the gravimetric density (G.D.) of the charge relative to the weight of the shot. If a shot were unable to move, the closed chamber pressure resulting from the explosion of a charge (at a G.D. of 0.5) could reach nearly 49 tons per square inch, a level that is not safe. However, because the shot advances during the combustion of the cordite, the maximum pressure can be kept within safe limits.

The design of the chamber and the G.D. of the charge are determined by estimating the maximum pressure that will be reached in the bore. This estimation relies on the experimental determination of the size of the grain and the time of burning under varying density and pressure. To approximate these results, practitioners employ empirical formulas based on practical experience.

## Work and Efficiency
The work realized by the expansion of the powder is used to increase the velocity of the shot. For instance, a realization of 2826 foot-tons of work can increase a shot's velocity from 1020 to 2250 feet per second (f/s) at the muzzle. 

In practical application, some work is lost to friction within the bore. This is measured by the "factor of effect" (f). For example, if the actual muzzle velocity is 2250 f/s while the range table indicates 2150 f/s, it may be determined that approximately 10% of the work is lost to friction, resulting in a factor of effect of f = 0.9.
