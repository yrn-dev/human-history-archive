# Corrosion

Corrosion refers to the chemical degradation of materials, particularly metals, often occurring in industrial settings such as boilers or chemical processing vessels. In these environments, the prevention of corrosion is essential to maintain the structural integrity and efficiency of the equipment.

## Corrosion in Boilers
In the context of boiler systems, corrosion can occur when chemicals are used to purify or soften feed-water. It is critical that these chemicals, and the products they form, do not have a corrosive effect on the boiler-plates. A significant amount of corrosion has been attributed to oxygen from the air, which enters the boiler in solution within the feed-water. To mitigate this, current best practices involve delivering feed-water into the boiler at specific positions. This ensures that air evolved during heating passes directly into the steam space, preventing it from becoming disengaged on the under-water surfaces of the boiler.

To protect boiler-plates where corrosion is feared, zinc slabs are typically fitted into the water spaces. Experience indicates that rolled zinc is preferable to cast zinc. These slabs are secured on studs that are kept bright to ensure direct metallic contact between the zinc and the boiler-plate. This setup creates galvanic action; the zinc acts as the negative metal and is dissolved, while the metal of the boiler shell is kept electro-positive. It is necessary to ensure that fragments of zinc breaking off as the metal wastes away do not fall upon the boiler's heating surfaces.

## Water-Softening and Impurities
The need for purification arises because impurities in feed-water do not evaporate with steam and instead become concentrated. These impurities—primarily magnesia or lime salts—often precipitate as mud or scale on heating surfaces. While mud and soluble impurities can be removed via blow-off cocks, scale requires periodical cleaning. Excessive incrustations are dangerous as they obstruct heat transmission, causing plates to become overheated and weakened.

To prevent this, feed-water is often passed through a "softener." In this process, chemical reagents are mixed with measured quantities of water while the temperature is raised using other means or exhaust steam. This precipitates most impurities or converts them into more soluble salts that remain in solution until they are blown out. The water is then filtered before entering the boiler. Depending on the level of impurity, certain boiler types are preferred; for very impure water, Lancashire, Cornish, and Galloway boilers are used because they are easier to clean than water-tube or multitubular boilers.

## Corrosion in Chemical Industry and Materials
In the chemical industry, corrosive actions on metal are managed through the use of specialized equipment and materials. The autoclave—a strong, closed metal vessel used to heat liquids above their boiling points under pressure—is sometimes provided with renewable linings in cases where the substances being treated exert a corrosive action on the metal.

Certain materials are utilized for their resistance to corrosive substances. Asbestos, for example, possesses a partial resistance to the attack of acids. Because of this property, as well as its low thermal conductivity and ability to withstand high temperatures, it is used as a filtering medium for corrosive liquids and for jacketing steam-pipes and boilers.

## Other Corrosive Substances
Certain chemical compounds are characterized by their corrosive nature. Corrosive sublimate, also known as mercuric chloride or perchloride of mercury ($\text{HgCl}_2$), is a white solid. It can be produced commercially by heating a mixture of common salt and mercuric sulphate, causing the mercuric chloride to sublime and condense into small rhombic crystals. In medicine, corrosive sublimate is applied as a caustic, antiseptic, stimulant, and astringent.
