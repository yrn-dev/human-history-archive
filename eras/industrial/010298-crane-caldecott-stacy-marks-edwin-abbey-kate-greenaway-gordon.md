# Crane, Caldecott, Stacy Marks, Edwin Abbey, Kate Greenaway, and Gordon Browne

The artists Walter Crane, Randolph Caldecott, H. Stacy Marks, Edwin Abbey, Kate Greenaway, and Gordon Browne are recognized as modern practitioners whose handicraft in the creation of book-plates makes their work worth collecting. These artists contributed to a period of significant development in English illustration, particularly in the realm of children's literature and the advancement of colour-printing.

## Kate Greenaway
Born in London on March 17, 1846, Kate Greenaway was the daughter of John Greenaway, an engraver on wood and draughtsman. Her formal artistic education included studies at the Slade School, "Heatherley's" life classes, and South Kensington. Greenaway began exhibiting water-colour drawings at the Dudley Gallery in London in 1868.

She first gained an extraordinary vogue through her Christmas cards, which were praised by artists and the public for their charm of draughtsmanship, purity, humour, and quaint beauty of design. Her primary subjects included landscape, flowers, children, and young girls. Encouraged in this style by H. Stacy Marks, R.A., she resisted pressures to return to a more conventional manner of art.

Greenaway's illustrations for children, such as those for *Little Folks* (1873), attracted significant attention. In 1877, she began drawing for the *Illustrated London News* and sold drawings at the Dudley Gallery for £54, while her Royal Academy picture sold for eighteen guineas. Her most successful works include:
*   *Under the Window* (1879): Reported sales of 150,000 copies, with editions issued in German and French. Greenaway received £1130 in profits for this title.
*   *The Birthday Book*: Produced a profit of £1250.
*   *Mother Goose*: Produced a profit of £905.
*   *Little Ann*: Produced a profit of £567.

These four books alone provided a clear return of £8000. Her "toy-books" are credited with creating a revolution in illustration and were sold in American cities and every European capital.

## Randolph Caldecott
Randolph Caldecott (1846–1886) was an English illustrator, painter, and sculptor born in Chester on March 22, 1846. Before entering the art profession, he worked as a bank clerk from 1861 to 1872 in Manchester and Whitchurch, Shropshire. He moved to London in 1872 to study at the Slade School.

Caldecott became a member of the Royal Institute of Painters in Water-colours and contributed to *Punch* and the *Graphic*. His work is characterized by a genial and humorous faculty. His notable book illustrations include:
*   *Old Christmas* (1876) and *Bracebridge Hall* (1877) by Washington Irving.
*   *North Italian Folk* (1877) by Mrs Comyns Carr.
*   *Breton Folk* (1879) by Henry Blackburn.
*   *The Harz Mountains* (1883).
*   *Some Aesop's Fables with Modern Instances, &c.* (1883).
*   Various "picture-books" for children, including *The House that Jack Built* and *John Gilpin*, starting in 1878.

In his illustrations for *Bracebridge Hall*, Caldecott began replacing the pencil with pen and ink, creating a more independent style of draughtsmanship. His work also showed early signs of Japanese influence, utilizing easy freedom of outline and washes of conventional colour. Caldecott died in Florida on February 12, 1886, following prolonged suffering.

## Walter Crane and Colour-Printing
Walter Crane designed illustrated nursery rhymes on lines similar to the idyllic renderings of children produced by Kate Greenaway. Along with Caldecott and Greenaway, Crane's children's books are considered among the finest examples of colour-printing produced outside of Japan. These works, generally printed by Edmund Evans, achieved a high level of excellence through the combination of bold, simple outlines and flat masses of pleasant colour. Later, in 1896, Crane published *Decorative Illustration of Books*.

## Influence and Development of Style
The transition in illustration during the 1870s and 1880s saw a shift from black-and-white line-work to pigment. While some argued this depressed artistic value, several artists of great ability continued to innovate. Following the independent style of draughtsmanship established by Caldecott, other artists such as Hugh Thomson and Harry Furniss followed. This development was further advanced by the more elaborate and mobile penmanship of E. A. Abbey, which pushed illustration toward greater illusion and fineness.
