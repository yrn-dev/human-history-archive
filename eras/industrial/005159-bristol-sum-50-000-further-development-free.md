# Bristol

Bristol is a city whose greatness has been derived entirely from trade. It was not a shire town, nor was it the site of a great military post or religious house. Its growth began around the year 1000 as a Saxon settlement located at the junction of the rivers Avon and Frome.

## Early History and Governance
During the reign of Canute, Bristol served as a market for English slaves and began a wool trade with Ireland. By the time of the Domesday survey, it was a royal borough governed by a king-appointed reeve. Following a rebellion against William II, the town and castle were granted to Robert FitzHamon, and later, through marriage in 1119, Bristol became the dowry of Earl Robert of Gloucester.

A significant turning point occurred with the charter granted by John in 1190, which provided burgesses with various protections and rights, including the ability to hold by burgage tenure and the restriction of the sale of corn, wool, cloth, and leather to burgesses only. Around this time, a commune was established on the French model; Robert FitzNichol became the first mayor in 1200, chosen by the merchants of the merchant gild rather than an overlord.

The city's boundaries and legal status evolved through various charters. A 1373 charter made Bristol a county in itself, independent of county courts, and extended its boundaries to include Redcliffe and the waters of the Severn and Avon. This charter established a council of forty and divided the town into five wards. Later, a 1499 charter granted by Henry VII altered the corporation's basis by stipulating that aldermen be elected by the mayor and council. In 1664, Charles II granted a formal charter of incorporation.

## Trade and Exploration
Bristol's prosperity was heavily tied to its maritime activities. In the 14th century, cloth manufacture developed, with Thomas Blanket establishing looms in 1337. By 1353, Bristol was designated as one of the Staple towns. Merchants exported cloth to the Levant, Spain, and France, and traded wool with the Baltic. The Society of Merchant Venturers, whose records began in 1467, became so influential that by 1500 it directed all the city's foreign trade and held a lease on port dues.

The city played a conspicuous role in voyages of discovery. John Cabot, a citizen of Venice, sought letters patent from Henry VII, which were issued on March 5, 1496, authorizing him and his sons to discover unknown lands. Under these terms, merchandise from visited countries was entered at Bristol free of duty, while one-fifth of net gains went to the king. These explorations led to further trade development; in the 16th century, Bristol traded with the Canaries, Spain, and Spanish colonies in America, and participated in attempts to colonize Newfoundland. During the 17th century, the city engaged in the trade of African slaves. In more recent history, a weekly steamer service from Limon was established to Bristol and Manchester to facilitate the banana trade.

## Public Buildings and Institutions
The city's public architecture is often overshadowed by its ecclesiastical buildings. Notable structures include:
* **The Council House:** Located at the "Cross" of four main thoroughfares, it dates from 1827, was enlarged in 1894, and houses the city archives.
* **The Guildhall:** A modern Gothic building.
* **The Exchange:** A corn-market designed by architect John Wood in 1743.
* **Colston Hall:** Used for meetings and concerts, named after Edward Colston (d. 1721), a benefactor of the city.

Bristol is also home to institutions considered originals of their kind, such as the city free library (1613) and a bank near St Stephen's church that claims to be the first savings-bank established in England (1812). In 1893, the corporation took over the reference library and Bristol museum. An art gallery presented by Sir William Henry Wills opened in 1905, and the central library opened in 1906.

The development of the city's free libraries was significantly aided by Vincent Stuckey Lean (d. 1899), who bequeathed £50,000 to the corporation of Bristol. This sum was intended for the further development of the free libraries, with a specific focus on the sustenance and formation of a scientific and standard general reference library.
