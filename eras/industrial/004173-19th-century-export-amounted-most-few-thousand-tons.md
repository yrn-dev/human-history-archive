# Global Trade and Export Trends (19th to Early 20th Century)

The transition from the 19th century into the early 20th century was marked by significant shifts in industrial output, the expansion of maritime logistics, and the evolution of agricultural exports across various global regions.

## Industrial and Mineral Exports
In England, the growth of Barrow-in-Furness was primarily driven by the presence of pure haematite iron ore. At the start of the 19th century, the export of this ore to the midlands and South Wales furnaces was limited to a few thousand tons. By 1847, this figure had risen to approximately 50,000 tons, though the town's population at that time remained small at 325. The subsequent opening of the Furness railway in 1846 and the establishment of iron works by Messrs Schneider & Hannay in 1859 led to further growth. By 1866, the Barrow Haematite Steel Company began producing steel via the Bessemer process. Over time, shipbuilding—specifically the work of Vickers, Sons & Maxim—surpassed steel works in importance.

In Spain, the Basque Provinces emerged as major centers for iron mining and sea fisheries. By 1905, these provinces produced 5,302,344 tons of iron, with over five million tons originating from Biscay. This represented more than half of Spain's total iron production of 9,395,314 tons. Of this total, 5,845,895 tons were exported to England. Additionally, the province of Guipuzcoa was known for the swords of Mondragon and the small-arms factory at Eibar, where Senor Zuloaga revived the artistic inlaying of gold and silver in iron and steel during the 19th century.

In Sardinia, the town of Iglesias served as the center of a mining district. In 1905, Sardinia extracted 170,236 tons of minerals valued at £765,054, including 99,749 tons of calamine zinc, 26,051 tons of blende zinc, 15,429 tons of lignite, and 24,798 tons of lead. A total of 118,009 tons were exported by sea from Portoscuso, with zinc primarily destined for Dunkirk, Bordeaux, and Antwerp.

## Agricultural and Commodity Trade
Burma's primary exports were timber and rice. In 1805, rice exports for coastal and foreign trade totaled 1,419,173 tons (valued at Rs.9,77,66,132), increasing to 2,187,764 tons (valued at Rs.15,67,28,288) by 1905. While England took the largest share, rice was also consumed in Holland, Italy, Belgium, Germany, and France. Trade was occasionally affected by famines; for example, over one million tons of rice were shipped to India during a famine in 1900. Other Burmese exports included jade, petroleum, cotton, caoutchouc (india-rubber), and cutch dye. Teak exports were valued at Rs.1,34,64,303 in 1895 and Rs.1,31,03,401 in 1905.

In the Basque Provinces and the Pays Basque, cider production was gradually replaced by wine-growing during the 19th century. Similarly, Cadiz maintained a trade in exporting olives, salt, figs, canary-seed, ready-made corks, and wine (principally sherry from Jerez). However, Cadiz experienced a decline due to the 19th-century acquisition of independence by Spanish colonies and the Napoleonic wars.

## Dairy and Specialized Goods
The United Kingdom's inability to produce sufficient dairy for its population led to increased imports. Between 1891 and 1900, butter imports rose from 102,500 tons to 170,700 tons. Denmark showed the most significant development in supply, increasing its exports by 28,678 tons over that decade, followed by Holland (6,589 tons) and Russia (7,207 tons). Conversely, Sweden's exports declined, sending 1,400 tons less in 1900 than in 1891.

Cheese imports into the UK also shifted. Between 1891 and 1900, the United States shipped 10,000 tons less cheese to British ports. France's supply dropped from over 3,000 tons in 1891 to below 2,000 tons in 1900, while Belgium's supply rose from less than 1,000 tons to 2,600 tons. Dutch cheese imports remained relatively stable, moving from 15,300 tons in 1891 to 17,000 tons in 1900.

In the luxury goods sector, Lyons remained a leading European silk market. In 1905, its special office examined over 4,700 tons of silk, with two-thirds coming from Japan and China. Traders in Lyons re-exported seven-twelfths of this silk.

## Shipping and Logistics
The trade of the lower Danube saw significant improvements in efficiency. Between 1847 and 1860, an average of 203 British ships entered the Danube, averaging 193 tons each. By 1893, this increased to 905 vessels totaling 1,287,762 tons. Between 1861 and 1902, the total tonnage of ships frequenting the Danube increased five-fold, and the mean size of individual ships increased ten-fold. Cereal exports (mostly wheat) from the Danube averaged 13,000,000 quarters annually between 1901 and 1905, five times the average seen between 1861 and 1867.
