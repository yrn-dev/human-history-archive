# Fermenting

Fermenting is the process by which wort is converted into beer. This transformation is driven by the action of living yeast cells, which split the sugar contained in the wort into carbonic acid and alcohol, accompanied by several subsidiary reactions. Within the broader scope of brewing, fermentation is the fifth of seven main processes, following grinding, mashing, boiling, and cooling, and preceding cleansing and racking and storing.

## Systems of Fermentation

There are two primary systems of fermentation: top fermentation and bottom fermentation. Top fermentation is the system employed in the United Kingdom. In this process, wort—generally at a temperature of approximately 60° F.—is "pitched" with liquid yeast, also known as "barm," at a rate of 1 to 4 lb per barrel, depending on the strength and type of beer. 

As fermentation progresses, a slight froth or scum appears on the surface, developing into a light curly mass called a "curly head" or "cauliflower." This evolves into a more solid "rocky head," which eventually shrinks into a compact "yeasty head" that emits hissing bubbles of gas. This stage marks the beginning of "cleansing," where the yeast is separated from the liquid. Because temperatures rise considerably during this process, vessels are often equipped with "attemperators"—pipes through which cold water runs to ensure temperatures do not exceed a maximum of 70-75° F.

Bottom fermentation is used for continental "lager" type beers and is also the common system in America. In the decoction system of bottom fermentation, pitching and fermentation occur at very low temperatures in small vessels. Fermenting cellars are maintained at about 37-38° F., and the wort temperature does not rise above 50° F. The yeast remains at the bottom of the tun. Primary fermentation in this system lasts approximately eleven to twelve days, compared to three days in the English system.

## Specialized Brewing Methods

### Modern Lager Production
Some modern lager brewing, such as the process introduced by Messrs Allsopp in Great Britain, utilizes closed cylindrical sheet-steel vessels coated in glass enamel. In this method, wort ferments under reduced pressure while a vacuum pump removes generated carbonic acid, replacing it with cool sterilized air. With fermenting cellars kept at 40° F. and the use of a pure culture bottom yeast, fermentation is accelerated and finished in about six days. Following this, the beer is cooled to 40° F. and moved via filtered air pressure to store tanks for a secondary fermentation lasting three weeks. The beer is then cooled, filtered, racked, and bottled under counter pressure to prevent gas escape.

### American and International Variations
American brewing typically combines bottom fermentation with infusion mashing. This process uses a very low initial heat of about 100° F. for one hour, followed by a rapid increase to 153-156° F. and a final increase to about 165° F. This allows the use of malt that would be considered unfit for English breweries.

Other notable fermented liquids include:
*   **Saké:** A Japanese beer prepared in three stages. First, *Koji* is created by treating steamed rice with *Aspergillus oryzae* to convert starch to sugar. Second, *Koji* is added to a paste of fresh-boiled starch to create *moto*, with fermentation lasting 30 to 40 days. Finally, more rice, *Koji*, and water are added for a secondary fermentation of 8 to 10 days before the liquid is filtered and heated.
*   **Weissbier:** Produced largely in Berlin from a mash of one part barley malt and three parts wheat malt. This involves symbiotic fermentation using both a yeast and a fission fungus (*lactic acid bacillus*), often assisted by adding white wine to the yeast.

## Chemical and Enzymatic Processes

Vigorous fermentation requires nitrogenous compounds; yeast assimilates these in simple forms like amides. Maltsters aim to break down barley proteins to maximize nutritive value for the yeast and eliminate nitrogenous matter to ensure the beer remains stable. Additionally, albumoses may assist in the beer's foaming properties.

The process involves a wide array of enzymes. In the malt and mash-tun, cytase dissolves cell walls, while Diastase A liquefies starch and Diastase B saccharifies it. Proteolytic enzymes (peptic and pancreatic) break down proteins. During the fermentation of the wort and yeast, catalase splits peroxides, invertase inverts cane sugar, glucase splits maltose into glucose, and zymase splits sugar into carbonic acid and alcohol.

## Post-Fermentation and Storage

Once fermentation and cleansing are complete, the beer is "racked off" into trade casks or storage vessels. "Running" beers, such as mild ales, may be sent out within ten days of mashing. In contrast, "pale" and "stock" ales are stored for six weeks to three months. Some beers undergo "dry hopping" (adding hops in the cask) or "priming," where a small amount of sugar solution is added to rapidly produce "condition" in flat beers or running beers.

In the Stone Square System used in northern England, wort is pumped between two superimposed cool stone vessels. Once fermentation is complete, the yeast is removed from the top square.
