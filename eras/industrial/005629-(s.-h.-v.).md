# Ballistic Tables and Calculations

Ballistic tables are used to determine the flight characteristics of projectiles, such as shots and rifle bullets, by calculating variables like time of flight, distance, and velocity. These calculations rely on the ballistic coefficient and experimental data regarding air resistance.

## The Ballistic Coefficient and Time of Flight
The ballistic coefficient, denoted as $C$ (also referred to as driving power), is defined by the formula $C = w/nd^2$, where $w$ represents the weight of the shot in pounds, $d$ is the diameter in inches, and $n$ is a value determined by experiment. For example, $n = 0.8$ is considered a good average for modern rifle bullets.

To determine the time $t$ in seconds required for a shot's velocity to fall from an initial velocity $V$ to a final velocity $v$, the ballistic table uses a standard shot for which $C=1$. The time interval $\Delta T$ for the velocity to drop by $\Delta v$ is calculated as $\Delta T = \Delta v/gp$, where $p$ is a function of velocity determined experimentally. The total time for a standard shot is expressed as $T(V) - T(v) = \int_{v}^{V} dv/gp$. For any specific shot with a ballistic coefficient $C$, the actual time of flight is $t = C[T(V) - T(v)]$.

## Distance and Advance
The advance of a shot, $s$, is calculated using the sum of all values of $\Delta S$ up to an assigned velocity. For a standard shot where $C=1$, the advance $\Delta S$ is defined as $\Delta S = v\Delta v/gp = v\Delta T$. The distance between two assigned velocities $V$ and $v$ is $S(V) - S(v) = \int_{v}^{V} vdv/gp$. For a shot with a ballistic coefficient $C$, the total advance is $s = C[S(V) - S(v)]$.

## The Degree Table and Direction of Motion
The degree table, attributed to Sir W. D. Niven, F.R.S., determines the change in the direction of motion of a shot flying nearly horizontally as its velocity changes from $V$ to $v$. This involves the angle $i$ (in radians) that the tangent of the trajectory makes with the horizon. In direct fire, where the trajectory is flat, the relationship is expressed as $di/dt = g/v$.

The change in inclination can be measured in radians ($I$) or degrees ($D$). The difference in radians is $I(V) - I(v) = \int_{v}^{V} dv/vp$, and the difference in degrees is $D(V) - D(v) = 180/\pi [I(V) - I(v)]$.

## Trajectory and Range Applications
In range table results, the line of sight is often considered horizontal. Given a ballistic coefficient $C$, an initial velocity $V$, and a range $X$ (in feet), the final velocity $v$ is found via $S(v) = S(V) - X/C$. The time of flight is then $T = C\{T(V) - T(v)\}$.

The total deviation in the range is the sum of the angle of departure ($\phi$) and the angle of descent ($\beta$), calculated as $\delta = \phi + \beta = C\{D(V) - D(v)\}$. To divide this deviation, the vertex $A$ is identified as the point of half-time. The velocity $v_0$ at the vertex is found using $T(v_0) = 1/2\{T(V) + T(v)\}$. Consequently, $\phi = C\{D(V) - D(v_0)\}$ and $\beta = C\{D(v_0) - D(v)\}$.

## Direct Fire and Altitude Functions
Siacci's altitude-function is utilized in direct fire to determine the required angle of elevation $\phi$ and the angle of descent $\beta$ for a given range. In direct fire, real velocities $V$ and $v$ are treated as undistinguishable from pseudo-velocities $U$ and $u$. For small angles, these are expressed as:
* $\sin 2\phi = 2C [I(V) - \Delta A/\Delta S]$
* $\sin 2\beta = 2C [\Delta A/\Delta S - I(v)]$

## Practical Examples
Calculations for different projectiles demonstrate the impact of the ballistic coefficient:
* **6-inch shot (100 lb):** With a velocity drop from 2150 to 1600 f/s and $C = 2.894$, the time $t$ is 3.307 seconds and the distance $s$ is 6114 feet (2038 yards).
* **Rifle bullet (0.303-in calibre, 1/32 lb):** For the same velocity drop and $C = 0.426$, the time $t$ is 0.486 seconds and the distance $s$ is 900 feet (300 yards).
