# American and British Navies

The naval forces of the United States and Great Britain have a history characterized by periods of conflict, strategic competition, and eventual consolidation and cooperation. Their development reflects differing national priorities, ranging from the British necessity of imperial existence to the American focus on protecting commerce and establishing a separate military structure.

## The British Navy and Imperial Defense
For Great Britain, naval power is viewed as a fundamental necessity for existence rather than a luxury. Because the main thoroughfares of the British Empire are the world's oceans, a strong navy is considered the primary requirement for defense. Historically, the British adhered to the "two-power standard," a defensive axiom stating that the British navy should exceed the joint strength of the two next most powerful navies by a 10% margin.

The financial burden of maintaining this floating armament was colossal and was borne almost exclusively by taxpayers in the British islands until the end of the 19th century. Between 1895 and 1902, self-governing colonies began contributing to the imperial navy's maintenance. These contributions included annual subsidies from the Australasian colonies (initially £126,000, later increased to £240,000), the Cape Colony (£30,000, later £50,000), Natal (£35,000), and Newfoundland (£3,000). In 1908-1909, the Commonwealth also provided an Australian torpedo-boat flotilla.

## The United States Navy and Marine Corps
The United States navy was organized in 1798. Early naval officers, such as Commodore William Bainbridge (1774-1833), operated in an era where American trading vessels faced interference from both French and British cruisers. During this period, American vessels were often excluded by navigation laws from commerce with the British West Indian Islands, leading to contraband commerce. Naval activities also included protecting merchant ships in the Mediterranean; the U.S. initially paid tribute to the dey of Algiers to avoid capture but eventually used force against Algiers and Tunis.

Concurrent with the establishment of the navy was the formation of the United States Marine Corps in 1775. A separate military body under the control of the Navy Department, the Marine Corps was organized based on the British model. By an Act of Congress on March 3, 1899, its strength was fixed at 211 officers and 5,920 non-commissioned officers and men. Marines serve as riflemen in action and perform sentry, police, and orderly duties on shore stations and ships. Most marines serve three of every four years of their enlistment at sea.

## Naval Technology and Ordnance
A significant development in naval weaponry was the carronade, a short, light piece of ordnance designed by General Robert Melville in 1759. Though called the "smasher" by its inventor, it was not adopted by the British navy until 1779, when it was cast at the Carron works in Scotland. While the carronade had a limited range, it was highly destructive at close quarters. 

The use of carronades created misleading official classifications of ship strength. For example, a 38-gun frigate that carried ten carronades actually possessed 48 pieces of ordnance. This discrepancy in reported versus actual strength applied to British ships and later extended to American and French navies as they adopted the weapon.

In later years, the British admiralty experimented with liquid fuel at sea. While the British identified oil sources in Trinidad and Burma, they lacked the "gushing" wells found in America. The use of liquid fuel was also adopted by other powers; the Dutch navy combined coal and liquid fuel furnaces, which accelerated the speed of some destroyer-type vessels by nearly two knots.

## Cooperation in the World War
During the World War, the American and British navies moved toward a state of practical consolidation. According to reports by Admiral H.T. Mayo, United States naval activities in Europe—specifically in France, Great Britain, and Ireland—were characterized by cooperation with allied navies.

American naval operations in the region included:
* The deployment of destroyers on the coast of Ireland and at Brest.
* The establishment of air stations in Ireland and an aviation base at Eastleigh.
* The operation of a seaplane station at Killingholme and a northern bombing group of seaplanes.
* The use of subchasers at Plymouth and Corfu.
* The maintenance of forces at Gibraltar and a base at Cardiff, Scotland.
* The implementation of a cross-channel transport service and mine-laying operations.

Despite this operational synergy, cultural differences remained. Observations from the period noted that British uniforms were considered superior in cut and dress—featuring a rolling collar—compared to the American uniforms, which were criticized for a high standing collar that was viewed as more of a parade uniform than a practical one.
