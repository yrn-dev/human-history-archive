# Sir David Brewster and the History of Scientific Method

## Life and Academic Career
Sir David Brewster (1781–1868) was a Scottish natural philosopher born on December 11, 1781, in Jedburgh. The son of a highly reputed grammar school rector, Brewster was sent to the University of Edinburgh at age twelve. Although he completed a theological course and was licensed to preach, his lifelong inclination toward natural science—fostered by his early intimacy with James Veitch of Inchbonny, a skilled telescope maker—led him away from the clerical profession.

Brewster's academic and professional ascent was marked by several prestigious appointments. In 1838, he became the principal of the united colleges of St Leonard and St Salvator at St Andrews. Later, he served as the principal of the University of Edinburgh until shortly before his death on February 10, 1868, at Allerly, Melrose. His contributions were recognized with a knighthood and the decoration of the Guelphic order of Hanover in 1831. He was also elected as one of eight foreign associates of the Institute of France in 1859, succeeding J.J. Berzelius.

## Scientific Discoveries and Methodology
Brewster's scientific approach was empirical, relying on repeated experiment rather than mathematical genius. He did not provide ultimate explanations for the phenomena he studied, nor did he explicitly adopt the undulatory theory of light, though he did not maintain the corpuscular theory throughout his life.

His primary inquiries focused on optics, specifically:
*   The laws of polarization by reflection and refraction.
*   The discovery of biaxial crystals (crystals with two axes of double refraction) and the relationship between optical structure and crystalline forms.
*   The production of double refraction via irregular heating and the polarizing structure induced by pressure and heat.
*   The laws of metallic reflection and experiments regarding the absorption of light.

For these achievements, he received the Copley medal (1815) and the Rumford medal (1818) from the Royal Society of London. In 1816, the French Institute awarded him a prize for two of the most important physical science discoveries in Europe. To the general public, Brewster was best known for the rediscovery of the kaleidoscope around 1815 and the invention of the lenticular stereoscope. While Sir Charles Wheatstone discovered the principle of the stereoscope in 1838 using mirrors, Brewster is credited with suggesting the use of lenses to unite dissimilar pictures.

## Literary and Editorial Contributions
Brewster was a prolific writer and editor. Beginning in 1799 as a contributor to the *Edinburgh Magazine* (which he edited at age twenty), he later edited the *Edinburgh Encyclopaedia* from 1807 to 1830. He co-founded the *Edinburgh Philosophical Journal* in 1819 with Robert Jameson and later established the *Edinburgh Journal of Science* in 1824. He was also a major contributor to the seventh and eighth editions of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, writing articles on Magnetism, Electricity, Hydrodynamics, Optics, the Microscope, and the Stereoscope.

Among his separate works are the *Treatise on Optics* (1831), *Letters on Natural Magic* (1831), *The Martyrs of Science* (1841), and *More Worlds than One* (1854). His most significant biographical work was the 1855 *Memoirs of the Life, Writings and Discoveries of Sir Isaac Newton*, which resulted from over twenty years of research into original manuscripts.

## Influence on Scientific Organization
Brewster recognized the necessity of regular communication between scientists. Through a suggestion in the *Quarterly Review* for an association of philosophers, clergy, gentry, and nobility, he helped facilitate the creation of the British Association for the Advancement of Science. He played a primary role in shaping its constitution alongside Sir John F. W. Herschel and Charles Babbage. Brewster served as the president of the British Association in 1849.

## Context of the Scientific Method
The archive records a broader historical debate regarding the "Father of Experimental Philosophy," Francis Bacon (1561–1626). While some view Bacon as the founder of the scientific method, others, such as Sir R. A. Gregory, describe him as the "apostle" rather than the founder. Bacon's *The New Atlantis* envisioned an organized "palace of invention" for the pursuit of knowledge.

The application of Baconian induction and observation spread through English thought, typified by John Locke's *Essay concerning Human Understanding* (1690) and the activities of the Royal Society. However, the effectiveness of Bacon's rules has been contested. Liebig argued that little science actually proceeds according to Bacon's rules, though other critics, such as Fischer and Sigwart, suggest that Liebig's attacks on Baconian methods were unjustifiable and based on a misunderstanding of the true nature of the method. Other scholars, including Jevons, Brewster, and Ellis, are cited in the archive's records concerning judgments of Bacon's method.
