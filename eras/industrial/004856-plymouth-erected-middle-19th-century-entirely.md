# Wrought Iron Bridges of the 19th Century

## Evolution of Bridge Materials
Historically, bridges were constructed using masonry or timber during medieval and classical periods. These materials were later supplemented or replaced by concrete and brick. Toward the end of the 18th century, wrought iron began to be utilized, often in combination with cast iron or timber. During this same period, cast iron was employed for arches, and cast iron girders were used in the construction of some early railway bridges. In contemporary practice, cast iron is reserved for arched bridges of moderate span.

## 19th Century Developments
The early part of the 19th century saw the large-scale application of wrought iron in the construction of suspension road bridges. By the middle of the 19th century, this material was used for major girder bridges, specifically those located at Saltash near Plymouth and over the Menai Strait; these structures were entirely of wrought iron. Following these developments, wrought iron girder bridges became extensively used for railway systems.

## Transition to Steel and Concrete
The dominance of wrought iron ended with the introduction of mild steel from 1880 onwards. Because mild steel possesses greater toughness and tenacity, it wholly superseded wrought iron, except in cases where girders have a span of less than 100 ft. More recently, the materials used for arched bridges have evolved to include armoured concrete, ferro-concrete, or concrete strengthened with steel bars. The use of metal has been the primary factor enabling the construction of great spans ranging from 500 to 1800 ft.
