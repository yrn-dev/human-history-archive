# BOMBAY FURNITURE

Bombay furniture, specifically referred to as "Bombay blackwood furniture," describes a broad category of articles produced in India. While the city of Bombay is a primary center of manufacture, these items are also produced in the towns of Ahmedabad and Surat.

## Materials and Construction
The primary material used in this furniture is blackwood, also known as Shisham (_Dalbergia_). This timber is characterized by its dark color and hard grain. When treated properly, the wood is capable of achieving a beautiful natural polish.

In addition to standard furniture, "Bombay boxes" are produced. These items feature wood inlaid with geometrical patterns. The materials used for this distinctive and peculiar inlay work include:
* Ivory
* Ebony
* Sandal wood
* Sapan wood
* Stags' horns
* Wire

## Design and Artistry
The quality and form of Bombay furniture vary significantly. Experts, including Sir George Birdwood, have suggested that some pieces are clumsy and inelegant because the original models used for the furniture were Dutch. 

Conversely, smaller items—such as ornamental stands, small tables, and flower stands—are noted for having exceedingly graceful contours and are highly valued by collectors. At its highest level, the carving is described as lace-like. This artistry is further enhanced by the ingenuity of the craftsmen, who adapt their detailed designs to the specific purpose of the article they are creating.

## Production and Tradition
The manufacture of the most artistic pieces is handled by a special class of workmen who follow inherited traditions. These traditions are often highly specific; a craftsman may know only one particular design, which was passed down from his father, who had received it from his father before him.

In recent years, there have been efforts under European auspices to modernize the industry. These efforts have seen a measure of success by integrating elements of native work into furniture featuring Western designs. Despite these attempts at modernization, conventional patterns remain the dominant standard in the industry.
