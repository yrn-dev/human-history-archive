# Starch Degradation and the Theory of Brown and Morris

The scientific understanding of brewing chemistry involves the study of physiological chemistry, particularly regarding the growth, harvesting, and conversion of barley into malt, as well as the processes of mashing, fermenting, and conditioning beer. A central focus of this field is the analysis of extractive matters in beer and wort, which are primarily transformation products of starch.

## Historical Perspectives on Starch Conversion
Early scientific beliefs held that the action of diastase on starch first produced a gummy substance known as dextrin, which was subsequently transformed into glucose. This view was challenged in 1860 by F.A. Musculus, who demonstrated that dextrin and sugar are produced simultaneously. Later, between 1872 and 1876, Cornelius O'Sullivan provided definitive proof that the sugar produced during this process was maltose. O'Sullivan also identified that the temperature of the mash significantly influences the character of the resulting products. This foundational work was further extended by Horace T. Brown and J. Heron.

## The Brown and Morris Theory
Brown and Morris developed a theory regarding the degradation of starch that provides a rational and consistent explanation for the phenomena accompanying the transformation of starch by diastase. While this theory is not universally accepted, it serves as a practical working hypothesis for regulating and controlling fermenting and mashing operations.

According to Brown and Morris, a starch molecule is composed of five amylin groups. Each of these groups corresponds to the molecular formula $(C_{12}H_{20}O_{10})_{20}$. In this structure, four amylin radicles are grouped centrally around a fifth group.

When diastase acts upon starch-paste, the complex molecule undergoes hydrolysis. This process splits the molecule into four groups of amyloins, while the fifth central group remains unchanged under brewing conditions. This remaining unchanged group forms the substance identified as stable dextrin. The chemical reaction for this hydrolysis is represented as:

$5(C_{12}H_{20}O_{10})_{20} + 80 H_2O \text{ (starch and water)} = 80 C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} \text{ (maltose)} + (C_{12}H_{20}O_{10})_{20} \text{ (stable dextrin)}$

## Chemical Indicators of Degradation
The progress of starch degradation, whether caused by diastase or acids, can be monitored through several chemical reactions:

*   **Iodine Reaction:** Starch-paste and soluble starch (the first product of degradation) produce a deep blue coloration when mixed with an iodine solution. As degradation continues and products become more soluble and diffusible, this blue color changes to purple, then to a reddish color, and finally disappears entirely.
*   **Optical and Reducing Power:** As the process of hydrolysis proceeds, the optical rotating power decreases while the cupric reducing power (specifically towards Fehling's solution) increases.
