## Henry Thomas Buckle and the Events of 1859
Henry Thomas Buckle (1821–1862) was an English historian and the author of the *History of Civilization*. Born in Lee, Kent, Buckle spent seventeen years devoting ten hours a day to the preparation of his great historical work. After revising the first volume for six years, it was published in June 1857, bringing him social and literary celebrity.

On April 1, 1859, Buckle suffered a "crushing and desolating affliction" with the death of his mother. This loss immediately influenced his writing; while concluding a review of J.S. Mill's *Essay on Liberty*, Buckle included an argument for immortality. He based this argument on the impossibility of living if the separation from the beloved dead were final, and on the yearning of affections to regain communion. This perspective was notable because Buckle had previously maintained that history proves the "extreme fallibility of consciousness."

Earlier, on March 1, 1858, Buckle had delivered his only public lecture at the Royal Institution, titled *Influence of Women on the Progress of Knowledge*. This lecture was published in *Fraser's Magazine* in April 1858 and was later reprinted in the first volume of his *Miscellaneous and Posthumous Works*.

## Academic and Literary Publications (1855–1860)
The late 1850s saw the release of several scholarly works across different disciplines:

*   **Philology:** Wilhelm Paul Corssen (1820–1875), a German philologist, published *Uber Aussprache, Vocalismus, und Betonung der lateinischen Sprache* between 1858 and 1859. This treatise on the pronunciation and accent of Latin was regarded as one of the most masterly and erudite works in its field.
*   **History:** J. E. J. Quicherat published an edition of the *Historiae* by Basin between 1855 and 1859. Basin's work, *Historiae de rebus a Carolo VII. et Ludovico XI. Francorum regibus eorumque in tempore in Gallia gestis*, is noted for its historical value, despite Basin's dislike for Louis XI.
*   **Drama:** The German dramatist Julius Roderich Benedix (1811–1873) published *Der mundliche Vortrag* in Leipzig between 1859 and 1860.
*   **Philosophy:** Between 1859 and 1860, Foucher de Careil published unedited writings of Descartes in two parts, utilizing copies that Leibnitz had taken from original papers.
*   **Epigrams:** The year 1859 saw the publication of *Les Guepes gauloises: petit encyclopedie des meilleurs epigrammes, &c., depuis Clement Marot jusqu'aux poetes de nos jours* by Sauvage.

## Miscellaneous and Posthumous Works
The archive contains various references to "miscellaneous" and "posthumous" collections across different authors:

**Honoré de Balzac**
The bibliography of Balzac is complex. While the *Edition definitive* (from 1869 onwards) provides miscellaneous works in four volumes, earlier editions varied. The *Oeuvres posthumes* began appearing in 1899 with an instalment of *Lettres a l'etrangere*, followed by a second volume in 1906.

**Butler**
Several pieces ascribed to Butler appear in miscellaneous or posthumous collections. This includes *Mola Asinarum, on the unreasonable and insupportable burthen now pressed ... upon this groaning nation ...* (1659), which is included in his posthumous works (though some attribute it to John Prynne). Additionally, his work is found in the 1704 *Miscellaneous works, written by ... George Duke of Buckingham ... also State Poems ...*.

**Other Authors**
*   **Julius Roderich Benedix:** His work *Die Shakespearomanie* (1873), which criticizes the extreme adoration of Shakespeare, was published posthumously.
*   **Descartes:** His collected works include so-called posthumous works, such as *The Search for Truth by the Light of Nature* and *The Rules for the Direction of the Mind*, published in Latin in 1701.
*   **Thomas Basin:** In addition to his *Historiae*, Basin produced other miscellaneous writings, including a *Breviloquium* (an allegorical account of his misfortunes) and a defense of Joan of Arc.
*   **Augustus De Morgan:** His miscellaneous writings include a *Treatise on the Globes, Celestial and Terrestrial* (1845) and the *Book of Almanacks*.
