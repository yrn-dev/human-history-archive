# Victorian London: Life in the Industrial Capital

## Overview
During the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), London evolved into the undisputed center of the global economy, serving as the heart of the British Empire. This era was defined by a jarring dichotomy: unprecedented wealth and technological advancement existing alongside systemic squalor and extreme poverty. As the Industrial Revolution accelerated, London’s population exploded, straining its medieval infrastructure and leading to the birth of modern urban planning.

The city was a hub of innovation, hosting the Great Exhibition of 1851, which showcased the world's industrial prowess. However, the "Great Stink" of 1858 revealed the cost of this growth, as the Thames became an open sewer. This crisis prompted Joseph Bazalgette to engineer a massive intercepting sewer system, a feat of Victorian engineering that fundamentally changed public health.

Historians continue to debate the "standard of living" during this period. While some argue that real wages rose and the middle class expanded, others point to the "urban penalty"—the fact that life expectancy in the crowded slums of East London was significantly lower than in rural areas due to cholera, tuberculosis, and malnutrition.

## Key Facts
*   **Social Stratification:** The city was sharply divided. The West End was the domain of the aristocracy and the rising bourgeoisie, while the East End became a densely packed area of factories, docks, and tenements.
*   **The Fog:** London was famous for its "pea-soupers"—thick, yellowish smog caused by the combustion of soft coal in millions of domestic fireplaces and industrial furnaces.
*   **Transport Revolution:** The transition from horse-drawn omnibuses to the underground railway allowed the city to expand geographically, creating the first true "commuters."
*   **Public Health:** The transition from the "Miasma Theory" (disease caused by bad air) to "Germ Theory" revolutionized medicine and urban sanitation.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Mudlarks:** Poor children and adults, known as "mudlarks," would scavenge the muddy shores of the Thames at low tide for scrap metal, coal, or jewelry to sell for food.
2. **The Penny Post:** The introduction of the Uniform Penny Post in 1840 democratized communication, making it affordable for the working class to send letters.
3. **The Crystal Palace:** The building for the 1851 Great Exhibition was made of cast iron and plate glass, materials that symbolized the industrial age's mastery over nature.
