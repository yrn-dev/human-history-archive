# Established Church and Related Parliamentary Debates

The history of the Established Church in parliamentary discourse is characterized by conflicting views on church property, clerical control, and the funding of religious institutions. These debates often intersected with broader movements for religious freedom and the reduction of public expenditure.

## Opposition to the Irish Established Church
During the mid-19th century, the Irish Established Church was a subject of significant parliamentary contention. John Bright, a Nonconformist, spoke out against the Irish Established Church as part of his broader political activity. His stance was aligned with his opposition to church-rates and his advocacy for religious freedom, principles upon which he was returned for Manchester in the election of 1852.

In contrast, other members of Parliament maintained a conservative view of church assets. In May 1824, a vehement and eloquent speech was delivered against a motion by Joseph Hume that sought a reduction of the Irish Church establishment. This opposing argument maintained the doctrine that church property is as sacred as private property, defending the establishment in its most conservative form.

## Clerical Control and National Education
The intersection of church authority and state education was another point of friction in the House of Commons. John Bright, acting from his position as a Nonconformist, spoke against the clerical control of national education. This opposition to ecclesiastical influence was mirrored in his reaction to other religious legislation; for instance, he opposed Lord John Russell's Ecclesiastical Titles Bill, describing it as a "little, paltry, miserable measure" and predicting that it would fail.

## Church-Rates and Local Agitation
Beyond the halls of Parliament, the issue of church-rates sparked local movements. In 1838 and 1840, John Bright led movements against the Rochdale church-rate. During one such instance in 1840, Bright delivered a speech from a tombstone in a churchyard to voice his opposition. This local activism preceded his later parliamentary efforts, where he continued to speak against church-rates on a national level.

## Related Parliamentary Motions and Alliances
The debates surrounding the church often occurred alongside motions regarding public spending and social legislation. John Bright supported a motion by Richard Cobden for the reduction of public expenditure. While Bright and Cobden were generally close allies—voting in opposite lobbies only twice in twenty-five years of parliamentary life—they did disagree on the Maynooth Grant during the 1845 session, with Cobden voting for the grant and Bright voting against it.
