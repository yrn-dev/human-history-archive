# Industrial and Technical Applications of Wire

Wire serves a diverse range of functions across various engineering and industrial sectors, from the construction of massive suspension bridges to the precision requirements of scientific instruments and agricultural fencing.

## Bridge Construction and Cable Engineering
In the construction of large-span bridges, such as those crossing the East River in New York, steel wire is utilized to create massive supporting cables. The Brooklyn Bridge, begun in 1872, employs four cables, each 15¾ inches in diameter. These cables consist of 19 strands, with each strand containing 278 parallel steel wires of 7 B.W.G. The use of parallel wires was preferred by Roebling because they are 10% stronger than twisted wires.

The Williamsburg Bridge, begun in 1897 and opened in 1903, utilizes a similar but larger cable system. Its four cables are 18¾ inches in diameter, with each cable weighing approximately 1116 tons. Each cable is composed of 37 strands of 208 wires, totaling 7696 parallel steel wires. These wires are No. 8 B.W.G., which is approximately 3/16 inches in diameter. The specifications for this wire required a tensile strength of 89 tons per square inch, as well as an elongation of 2½% in 5 feet and 5% in 8 inches. To protect the cables, they are sheathed in sheet iron and wrapped in cotton duck that has been soaked in varnish and oxidized oil.

## Agricultural Fencing and Regulation
Wire is extensively used in the United States and England for field fencing. While barbed wire—defined as wire with jagged projections or spikes—was historically popular with railroads, there has been an increasing demand for meshed wire field fencing since 1899. This shift is attributed to the high cost and scarcity of labour and the decline in the construction of rail fences. Meshed wire is often viewed as a superior enclosure for hogs and sheep compared to barbed wire. To ensure greater stability, the fabric of meshed wire has been produced using wires of larger diameters than in the past.

In England, the use of barbed wire is governed by the Barbed Wire Act 1893. This law allows local authorities to require land occupiers to remove barbed wire fences adjoining highways if the wire is likely to be injurious to animals or persons lawfully using the road. If the occupier fails to comply with a written notice or a court order, the local authority may execute the removal and recover the expenses from the occupier.

Production statistics for the United States show a significant rise in wire fencing. Barbed wire production grew from 5 tons in 1874 to 200,000 tons by 1900 and 250,000 tons by 1907. During the same period, meshed field fencing rose from 50,000 tons in 1900 to 425,000 tons in 1907.

## Scientific and Meteorological Instrumentation
Steel wire is critical for meteorological services in America and Europe that use kites to measure air velocity, humidity, and temperature. These services typically use modifications of the box-kite invented by L. Hargrave. Because wind pressure is a greater hindrance to reaching high altitudes than the weight of the line, steel piano wire is used for its high strength and small size. For example, a wire with a 1/32 inch diameter weighs about 16 lb. per mile and can withstand a strain of 250-280 lb. before breaking. In 1905, at the Prussian Aeronautical Observatory in Lindenberg, a train of six kites reached an altitude of four miles using a wire length of slightly over nine miles.

In chemical analysis, specifically the electrolytic method for copper ores, platinum wire is used to connect electrodes to the battery poles. In this process, copper is deposited on a platinum cylinder over a period of two to twelve hours.

## Miscellaneous Mechanical Uses
Wire is employed in various other technical applications:
*   **Maritime:** Cables for the British mercantile marine and navy are marked by twisting pieces of iron wire around the studs of the links to indicate the number of lengths of cable that have been deployed.
*   **Transportation:** Bicycle and tricycle wheels use high-tenacity steel wire for spokes. These are screwed to a specific initial tension to create circumferential compression on the rim. The best spokes are "butted," meaning they have a larger diameter at the ends than in the middle.
*   **Manufacturing:** In the production of "wire-cut" plastic bricks, a wire cutting frame with tightly stretched wires is used to cut a continuous mass of clay delivered from a pug-mill into individual bricks.
*   **Recreation:** A historical form of "lawn-billiards" used cues ending in wire loops that were slightly smaller in diameter than the wooden balls used in the game.
