# Book-plates

The book-plate, also known as the *ex-libris*, is a printed label used to indicate the ownership of individual volumes. It serves a similar purpose to the hand-painted armorial or symbolical personal devices found in medieval manuscripts, mirroring the relationship between the printed page and the work of a scribe.

## Origins and Early Development
The history of the book-plate begins in Germany, where the earliest known examples were produced. According to Friedrich Warnecke of Berlin, the oldest movable *ex-libris* are hand-painted woodcuts depicting a shield of arms supported by an angel. These were pasted into books presented to the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim by Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach around 1480.

Germany remained the primary center for book-plates long before the practice spread to other nations. The German style is characterized as heavy and over-elaborated, a quality that did not affect neighboring countries. Notable artists contributed to this tradition; Albrecht Durer engraved at least six plates between 1503 and 1516 and provided designs for many others. Other artists ascribed with notable plates include Hans Holbein, Lucas Cranach, and the "Little Masters," including Jost Amman, Virgil Solis, the Behams, Georg Hupschmann, Matthias Zundt, and Saldorfer.

The spread of the book-plate to other regions occurred gradually:
*   **France:** The oldest discovered example is from 1529, belonging to Jean Bertaud de la Tour-Blanche.
*   **England:** The earliest example is a gift-plate from Sir Nicholas Bacon.
*   **Holland:** The earliest known plate is from 1597, belonging to Anna van der Aa.
*   **Italy:** An example is attributed to the year 1622.
*   **America:** The earliest known example is a plain printed label from 1679 belonging to John Williams.

## English Book-plates
English book-plates reflect the prevailing tastes in decorative art from the late Elizabethan to the late Victorian periods. While a gorgeous hand-painted armorial device attached to a folio once belonging to Henry VIII exists in the British Museum, it is not considered a book-plate in the modern sense. The earliest dated English plate is that of Sir Thomas Tresham (1585).

Until the last quarter of the 17th century, authentic English plates were limited in number and remained remarkably simple, lacking German elaborateness. They were typically plain armorials featuring symmetrical mantling and occasional wreaths or palms. Following the Restoration, book-plates became established accessories for well-ordered libraries. This period introduced two major changes: the use of tincture lines and dots (a method devised by Petra Sancta in *Tesserae Gentilitiae*) and more elaborate mantling resembling the periwigs of the era. This style was imported from France, and until the French Revolution, English decoration generally followed French taste with a delay of several years.

## Styles and Composition
In old examples, the style of decoration serves as the primary criterion for dating. While many older and modern plates are pictorial, specific English genres emerged:
*   **Library interiors and book-piles:** Exemplified by the *ex-libris* of William Hewer (1699), secretary to Samuel Pepys.
*   **Portrait-plates:** Notable examples include those of John Gibbs (the architect) and Samuel Pepys.
*   **Allegories:** Engraved by George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth.
*   **Landscape-plates:** Produced by wood engravers of the Bewick school.

In these pictorial styles, the armorial element is usually secondary.

## Modern Developments
Historically, the creation of book-plates was left to heraldic stationers. In recent years, however, the practice has been recognized as a minor branch of higher art. Modern designs tend to emphasize symbolic or allegorical elements over purely heraldic ones.

The popularization of the book-plate—once restricted to important collections or ancestral libraries—has been aided by process work, which made the reproduction of elaborate designs cheaper and easier. While most modern plates are reproduced by process, some artists still use the graver. Notable modern English artists include G.W. Eve, C.W. Sherborn, Warrington Hogg, John Leighton, John Vinycomb, Charles Ricketts, J. Forbes Nixon, Erat Harrison, J.D. Batten, and Robert Anning Bell. In America, prominent engravers include E.D. French of New York City, K.W.F. Hopson of New Haven, and J.W. Spenceley of Boston.
