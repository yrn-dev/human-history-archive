## Brewing Chemistry and the Degradation of Starch

In the study of brewing technology, Horace T. Brown (working with J. Heron) extended the research of Cornelius O'Sullivan regarding the influence of mash temperature on the character of products. Brown and Morris developed a theory concerning the degradation of starch, which serves as a practical working hypothesis for regulating and controlling fermenting and mashing operations, although the theory is not universally accepted.

According to the theory proposed by Brown and Morris, a starch molecule is composed of five amylin groups. Each of these groups corresponds to the molecular formula $(C_{12}H_{20}O_{10})_{20}$. The structure consists of four amylin radicles grouped centrally around a fifth. 

When diastase acts upon starch-paste, the complex molecule undergoes hydrolysis. This process splits the molecule into four groups of amyloins, while the fifth central group remains unchanged under brewing conditions. This unchanged central group forms the substance known as stable dextrin. The reaction is represented by the following equation:
$5(C_{12}H_{20}O_{10})_{20} + 80 H_2O \text{ (starch and water)} = 80 C_{12}H_{22}O_{11} \text{ (maltose)} + (C_{12}H_{20}O_{10})_{20} \text{ (stable dextrin)}$.

## English Literature and Linguistics

The archive identifies a William Morris as a remarkable writer who, along with John Ruskin, John Addington Symonds, and Walter Pater, gave the fine arts a significant place in English literature. William Morris, along with Ruskin, shared a discipleship with Carlyle and Kingsley.

Additionally, a Rev. Dr. Morris is recorded as a scholar of the English language. His contributions include:
*   Editing works for the Early English Text Society, including a publication in 1866 and another in 1865.
*   Authoring *Historical Outlines of English Accidence*, which contains extended lists of foreign words in the English language.
