# Parliamentary Representation and Boroughs in English History

The history of English parliamentary representation is characterized by the evolution of county and borough seats, the rise of "rotten boroughs," and subsequent legislative reforms that altered the distribution of political power.

## The Evolution of Cornish Representation
Cornwall provides a notable example of the expansion and eventual contraction of parliamentary seats. In 1295, Edward I summoned two members for the county, a number that remained constant until 1832. During the same period, six boroughs—Helston, Bodmin, Truro, Lostwithiel, Liskeard, and Launceston—were granted similar privileges. 

Between 1547 and 1584, the crown sought to augment its power against the House of Commons by granting the franchise to fifteen additional towns and villages. Consequently, by 1821, Cornwall sent forty-four members to parliament. The region became notorious for its "rotten boroughs." For instance, in an area near Liskeard that would be represented by only one member after 1885, there were eighteen members returning from nine parliamentary boroughs on the eve of the Reform Act, despite a population of only 14,224. 

The Reform Act of 1832 led to significant changes: Grampound had already lost both its members in 1821, and fourteen other Cornish boroughs followed. Disfranchised boroughs in 1832 included Camelford, Bossiney, East Looe, West Looe, Fowey, and Lostwithiel. However, some boroughs such as St Ives, Liskeard, Launceston, Helston, and Bodmin remained separately represented until 1885. Until that time, Penryn with Falmouth and Truro each returned two members.

## Regional Borough Histories and Representation
Various English towns experienced fluctuating periods of parliamentary representation:

*   **Honiton:** Founded before 1217 by William de Vernon, the borough returned two members in 1300 and 1311. Representation ceased until 1640, after which it returned two members until it was disfranchised in 1868.
*   **Bridgnorth:** After becoming a royal borough in 1102, it returned two members to parliament starting in 1295. This continued until 1867, when it was reduced to one member, before being disfranchised in 1885.
*   **Horsham:** This borough sent two representatives to parliament from 1295 until 1832, when the number was reduced to one. It was eventually disfranchised in 1885.
*   **Ilchester:** Parliamentary representation began in 1298, and the town returned two members until 1832.
*   **Great Marlow:** Two burgesses sat in parliament from 1300 to 1309. Representation lapsed until 1621. Following the Reform Bill of 1832, the borough boundaries were enlarged, but representation was reduced to one member in 1867 and merged into the county in 1885.
*   **Buckinghamshire:** First represented by two members in 1290, the total return for the county and its boroughs reached fourteen members by 1603. The Reform Act of 1832 reduced this to eleven, and the Redistribution of Seats Act of 1885 deprived the boroughs of representation, leaving the county with three members for three divisions.

## Notable Members of Parliament
The archives record several individuals who held parliamentary seats during the 18th century:

**John Dunning, 1st Baron Ashburton (1731–1783)**
An English lawyer who established a reputation through his work for the British East India Company in 1762 and his counsel for Wilkes in 1763. Dunning was appointed solicitor-general in December 1767, a post he held until May 1770. He was first chosen as the member for Calne in 1768 and represented that borough until his promotion to the peerage. A member of the opposition, he carried a motion in 1780 stating that the influence of the crown had increased and ought to be diminished. In 1782, he was appointed chancellor of the duchy of Lancaster.

**Augustus John Hervey, 3rd Earl of Bristol (1724–1779)**
A naval officer who served with distinction in the West Indies and the Mediterranean. Hervey served as a member of parliament for Bury from 1757 to 1763. After a brief period as a member for Saltash, he again represented Bury from 1768 until 1775, when he succeeded his brother in the peerage. He served as a lord of the admiralty from 1771 to 1775.

**William Dowdeswell (1721–1775)**
A Whig politician who represented the family borough of Tewkesbury from 1747 to 1754. From 1761 until his death, he was a representative of Worcestershire. He served as chancellor of the exchequer in 1765 under the marquess of Rockingham. After refusing to take office under Lord Chatham in 1766, he led the Rockingham party in the House of Commons.
