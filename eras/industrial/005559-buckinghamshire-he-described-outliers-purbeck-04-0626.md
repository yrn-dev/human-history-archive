# Purbeck and Portland Formations

The Purbeck and Portland formations are significant geological strata found in England, notably in the counties of Dorsetshire and Buckinghamshire. These beds are characterized by their fossil content and their extensive use in the building and decorative arts.

## Geological Distribution and Characteristics

In Dorsetshire, the Purbeck Beds form an elevated plateau south of the Weald Clay strip, resting upon Portland Sand and Portland Stone. The geology of the Isle of Purbeck is marked by a faulted anticline; denudation has brought Wealden beds to light in the vale between Swanage and Lulworth, and similar processes have exposed them at East Chaldon. The vertical and highly inclined strata of this anticline are visible near Ballard Point and Gad Cliff, where a fractured fold transitions into an "overthrust fault."

The Portland beds are located beneath the Purbeck formations. In the Isle of Portland, both the Portland and Purbeck formations are well exposed. The Portland beds are known for yielding freestones. Beneath these lies the Kimmeridge Clay, which contains "Coal" bands and forms a lower platform near the village of Kimmeridge.

In Buckinghamshire, the geological structure is characterized by a general south-easterly dip. The northern half of the county consists of Jurassic strata, while Cretaceous rocks predominate in the south. The vale of Aylesbury contains Gault, Kimmeridge Clay, and Oxford Clay, which are covered by various outliers of Lower Greensand, Purbeck, and Portland beds. In this county, the Portland beds are calcareous in their upper portions and sandy below, with an outcrop running from Thame through Aylesbury. The Purbeck beds in Buckinghamshire are freshwater and are found capping the ridge between Whitchurch and Oving.

## Paleontology and Fossils

The formations are highly fossiliferous. In the Isle of Purbeck, remains of the *Iguanodon* have been recovered from the Wealden beds. A notable feature of the Lower Purbeck is the "Dirt bed," which represents the remains of a Jurassic forest and can be observed on the Isle of Portland and near Mupe Bay.

The geologist Peter Bellinger Brodie (1815–1897) conducted extensive observations in these districts. In the Vale of Wardour, Brodie discovered an isopod in the Purbeck Beds named *Archaeoniscus Brodiei* by Milne-Edwards. Brodie also published descriptions of the Portland and Purbeck outliers in Buckinghamshire.

## Economic Use and Industry

The quarrying of these stones is a primary industry in the Isle of Purbeck and the Isle of Portland.

### Building Stone and Freestone
The Isle of Portland supplies a white freestone used in many of London's finest buildings. Similarly, the Portland beds in Buckinghamshire are quarried for building stone. In the Isle of Purbeck, the Middle Purbeck building stone is extensively quarried.

### Purbeck Marble
The Upper Purbeck *Paludina* marble is a famous decorative material consisting of the shells of a fresh-water snail, *Paludina carinifera*, embedded in limestone of a greenish, grey, or blue hue. This material was favored by medieval architects for sepulchral monuments and slender clustered columns. Notable examples of its use are found in the Temple Church, Westminster Abbey, and the cathedrals of Lincoln, Worcester, Winchester, and Salisbury.

### Other Industrial Materials
The Purbeck district also produces a white pipeclay used in the manufacture of china, which is exported to the Potteries of Staffordshire. Additionally, potters' clay is raised in the area around Corfe Castle.

## Regional Context: The Isle of Purbeck

The Isle of Purbeck is a district in eastern Dorsetshire. Corfe Castle, a town in this district, is situated in a gap within the hills that rise in the center of Purbeck. The town's economy is centered on the raising of potters' clay and the quarrying of Purbeck stone. The region's geography includes the Purbeck Hills, with the Lower Greensand cropping out on their south side at Worbarrow Bay and Punfield Cove.
