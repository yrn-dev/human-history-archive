# Equivalent Load in Tons per Foot Run

In the engineering of bridges, the "equivalent load in tons per foot run" is a uniform load used to represent the straining action—specifically the maximum bending moments—produced by concentrated loads, such as locomotives or heavy vehicles. Because actual rolling stock varies in weight and distribution, engineers often utilize an equivalent uniform load ($w_e$) to simplify the design process.

## Railway Bridge Live Loads
The live load on a railway bridge is defined as the weight of the heaviest train capable of crossing the structure. In early girder bridge designs, the live load was typically assumed to be a uniform load of 1 ton per foot run for each line of way. This was based on locomotives of 4 ft. 8½ in. gauge that weighed between 35 and 45 tons, and heavily loaded wagon trains that did not exceed three-quarters of a ton per foot run.

Modern traffic requires higher load capacities because engines and wagons have increased in weight. Furthermore, it is recognized that concentrated loading at the axles creates greater straining action, particularly in short bridges, than a uniformly distributed load of the same total weight.

### Locomotive Weight Variations
Data provided by W.B. Farr regarding heavy English locomotives illustrates the variety of loads:
*   **Passenger Engines:** Total weights range from 84.35 to 98.90 tons, with tons per foot over all ranging from 1.58 to 1.71 and tons per foot of wheel base between 1.92 and 2.04. Maximum axle loads range from 16.00 to 19.00 tons.
*   **Goods Engines:** Total weights range from 75.65 to 78.80 tons, with tons per foot over all between 1.50 and 1.54 and tons per foot of wheel base between 2.00 and 2.03. Maximum axle loads range from 13.65 to 16.00 tons.
*   **Tank Engines:** Total weights range from 47.00 to 60.80 tons, with tons per foot over all between 1.55 and 1.70 and tons per foot of wheel base between 2.23 and 3.03. Maximum axle loads range from 15.29 to 17.54 tons.

In the United States, some locomotives are significantly heavier. A consolidation engine may weigh 126 tons with a length of 57 ft. over buffers, averaging 2.55 tons per foot run. J.A.L. Waddell's classification system for railways identifies a lightest class (locomotive and tender of 93.5 tons, average load 1.8 tons per foot run) and a heaviest class (locomotive and tender of 144.5 tons, average load 2.77 tons per foot run). American ore wagons may weigh up to two tons per foot run when loaded.

## Calculation of Equivalent Uniform Loads
To determine the equivalent uniform load that produces bending moments as great as actual wheel loads, engineers may use diagrams of bending moments. For various spans, the equivalent uniform load per track is as follows:
*   **5.0 ft span:** 7.6 tons per ft. run
*   **10.0 ft span:** 4.85 tons per ft. run
*   **20.0 ft span:** 3.20 tons per ft. run
*   **30.0 ft span:** 2.63 tons per ft. run
*   **50.0 ft span:** 2.24 tons per ft. run
*   **100.0 ft span:** 1.97 tons per ft. run

In Austria, official regulations specify live loads per foot run and per track based on span:
*   **3.3 metres (1 ft):** 4.6 tons per ft. run
*   **6.6 metres (15 ft):** 3.1 tons per ft. run
*   **16.4 metres (10 ft):** 1.5 tons per ft. run
*   **65.6 metres (20 ft):** 1.2 tons per ft. run
*   **98.4 metres (30 ft):** 1.2 tons per ft. run

## Road Bridge Loads
For road bridges, live loads may consist of a dense crowd of people, treated as a uniform load of 80 to 120 lb per sq. ft. However, traction engines and wagons often produce greater straining action. In some manufacturing districts, provisions are made for loads of 30 tons.

In Switzerland, roads are classified into three categories:
1.  **Main Roads:** Crowd load of 92 lb per sq. ft. or a wagon of 10 tons per axle (13 ft. wheel base).
2.  **Secondary Roads:** Crowd load of 72 lb per sq. ft. or a wagon of 6 tons per axle (10 ft. wheel base).
3.  **Other Roads:** Crowd load of 51 lb per sq. ft. or a wagon of 3 tons per axle (8 ft. wheel base).

J.C. Inglis has calculated equivalent loads per foot of span for two English cases: (a) a traction engine and boiler trolley, and (b) a traction engine and trucks loaded with granite. For these cases, the equivalent loads for different spans are:
*   **10 ft span:** Case a: 1.75; Case b: 3.25
*   **20 ft span:** Case a: 0.95; Case b: 1.7
*   **30 ft span:** Case a: 0.70; Case b: 1.3
*   **40 ft span:** Case a: 0.73; Case b: 1.2
*   **50 ft span:** Case a: 0.72; Case b: 1.15

## Dead Loads and Additional Stresses
Beyond live loads, engineers must account for the dead load, which includes the weight of main girders, flooring, and wind-bracing. In railway bridges, sleepers and rails weigh 0.2 to 0.25 tons per foot run per line, while rail and cross girders weigh 0.15 to 0.2 tons. A footway adds approximately 0.4 ton per foot run.

Additional considerations include:
*   **Impact Stresses:** Empirical allowances are added to live load stresses: 20% for floor stringers, 15% for floor cross girders, 10% for 40-ft main girders, and 5% for 100-ft main girders.
*   **Longitudinal Drag:** Friction from braking is estimated at one-seventh of the train's weight.
*   **Centrifugal Load:** On curved bridges, the horizontal force is calculated as $wv^2/gr$ tons.
