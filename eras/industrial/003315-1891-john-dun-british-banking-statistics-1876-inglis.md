# British Banking and the Contributions of Sir Robert Harry Inglis Palgrave

The history of British banking is documented through a vast array of parliamentary reports, statistical analyses, and theoretical treatises. Central to the professionalization and study of the field in the late 19th and early 20th centuries were figures such as Sir Robert Harry Inglis Palgrave and various statistical works that tracked the evolution of the banking system from the era of goldsmiths to the modern era of regulated bank notes.

## Sir Robert Harry Inglis Palgrave
Sir Robert Harry Inglis Palgrave, F.R.S., was a prominent figure in British finance and economic literature. He served as the Director of Barclay & Co., Ltd., Bankers, and held the position of Editor of the *Economist* from 1871 to 1883. His contributions to the field include the editorship of the *Dictionary of Political Economy* and the authorship of *Notes on Banking in Great Britain and Ireland, Sweden, Denmark and Hamburg*.

Palgrave's work often intersected with official government inquiries. In 1876, he published a privately circulated work titled *Analysis of the Minutes of Evidence taken before the Select Committee of the House of Commons on Banks of Issue, 1875*, which included a selection of evidence from that committee. He also authored *Bank Rate and the Money Market in England, France, Germany, Holland and Belgium, 1844-1900*, published in 1903.

## The Evolution of Bank Issue and Provincial Banking
The structure of banking in England and Wales underwent significant changes due to legislative action. Prior to 1844, the majority of provincial banks functioned as banks of issue. However, the Act of 1844 restricted their ability to issue notes. 

During a banking inquiry in 1875, Sir R. H. Inglis Palgrave provided evidence regarding the impact of this legislation, stating that these provincial banks held a property in their issues that they could utilize but were unable to sell. Data from Appendix 14 of the 1875 House of Commons report on banks of issue illustrates a sharp decline in the proportion of notes in circulation relative to bank deposits between 1844 and the time of the inquiry. 

This trend was observed across twenty-one banks located in various environments, including manufacturing centers, agricultural districts, and mixed industrial-commercial districts. In some instances, deposits increased three-fold, five-fold, seven-fold, or nearly twelve and a half times. Consequently, the circulation-to-deposit ratio dropped significantly; for example, one bank saw its ratio fall from one-third of deposits to 9%, while another dropped from 22% to 1.5%.

## Historical Foundations and Banking Literature
The origins of the banking system can be traced back to goldsmiths who kept "running cashes," as noted in the *London Directory* of 1677. Of the firms listed in that directory, five were still operating in 1876. By the late 19th century, only three of these—or their direct descendants—remained in business: Child & Co. of Temple Bar, Martin & Co. of Lombard Street (as Martin's Bank, Ltd.), and Hoare & Co. of Fleet Street. Other firms, such as Barnetts and Hoare & Co., were absorbed by other entities, with Barnetts being taken over by Lloyds Bank in 1884 and the latter by the Capital and Counties in 1878.

The academic and professional study of these systems is reflected in a wide range of literature. Early works include *De Monetarum Augmento, variatione et diminutione, Tractatus varii* (1509) and a 1697 proposal to supply the King with twelve to fourteen million in money. Later influential texts include Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873), which provided a picture of the city at that time, and John Dun's *British Banking Statistics* (1876). Other notable contributions include the works of J. W. Gilbart, Thomson Hankey's *Principles of Banking* (1867), and the writings of Ernest Seyd on the Bankers' Clearing House System and the Bank of England Note Issue.

## Professional Organizations and Reform
The late 19th century saw the rise of formal banking institutions and leadership. John Lubbock, 1st Baron Avebury, an English banker and politician, was elected the first president of the Institute of Bankers in 1879. Lubbock was instrumental in several banking reforms and carried the Bank Holidays Act 1871 through parliament. His career spanned various roles, including the presidency of the London Chamber of Commerce (1888–1892) and chairman of the London County Council (1890–1892).
