# Mashing

Mashing is a primary process in brewing that focuses on extracting soluble matters pre-existent in malt using water at an adequate temperature. The process also converts insoluble nitrogenous compounds and insoluble starch into products that are soluble and partly fermentable. Because the character of the beer to be brewed is largely determined in the mash-tun, mashing is considered the most important of the brewing processes.

## The Mashing Process and Equipment
In modern brewing practice, the mashing "liquor" (water) and the malt are introduced into the mash-tun simultaneously via a mashing machine. This machine is typically a cylindrical metal vessel equipped with a screw and a central shaft. The crushed malt, known as the grist, enters from a grist case above while the liquor is introduced from the back; the rapid rotation of the screw ensures a thorough mixture as the materials travel through the machine.

The mash-tun itself is a large vessel made of wood or metal. It features a false bottom consisting of plates with numerous slits or small holes, which allows for the separation of the spent grains from the liquid portion of the finished mash, called the "wort." To ensure the grist and liquor are intimately mixed, the tun is fitted with a stirring apparatus known as rakes. Additionally, an automatic sprinkler called a sparger is used to wash out remaining wort from the grains. The sparger consists of hollow arms radiating from a center; when water is admitted, the central vessel rotates, covering the tun in a constant fine spray.

Once the grist and liquor are in the tun and the rakes have made the mash homogeneous, the rakes are stopped and the mash is allowed to rest, usually for approximately two hours. Subsequently, "taps are set" to establish communication between the mash-tun and the receiving vessel, and the sparger is activated to separate the extract from the grains. The total amount of water used ranges from two to three barrels per quarter (336 lb) of malt.

## Temperature and Quality Control
The quality and type of beer produced depend almost entirely on the mashing temperature and the kind of malt employed. Brewing science utilizes four theoretical combinations of kiln heats and mashing temperatures:
1. High kiln heats and high mashing temperatures.
2. High kiln heats and low mashing temperatures.
3. Low kiln heats and high mashing temperatures.
4. Low kiln heats and low mashing temperatures.

Temperature specifically affects diastase, the enzyme responsible for converting insoluble starch into sugar, intermediate products, and soluble dextrin. Higher temperatures "cripple" the action of diastase, resulting in more non-fermentable dextrinous matter and less fermentable maltose. Consequently, beers that must be "dry" and keep well, such as stock or pale ales, require high mashing temperatures. Conversely, mild running ales—which are sweet and intended for rapid consumption—are produced using low mashing temperatures. 

Typical temperature ranges for English beers are limited, generally between 140° and 160° F. Specific examples include:
* **Stouts and black beers:** 148° to 150° F.
* **Pale or stock ales:** 150° to 154° F.
* **Mild running beers:** 154° to 149° F.

## Variations in Mashing Methods
### Raw Grain and Decoction
When raw grains such as rice or maize are used, the process is modified. These grains are typically gelatinized in a separate vessel called a cooker or converter using high-temperature steam, sometimes with the addition of malt meal. After about half an hour, this mass is mixed into the main mash shortly before the taps are set. Alternatively, the limited-decoction system allows for a percentage of raw grain to be used directly in the mash-tun, removing the need for a converter.

In the full decoction system, grist is first mashed with cold water, then heated to 85-100° F. One-third of the mash (the *Dickmaische*) is transferred to a mash copper, heated to about 165° F until transparent, and then boiled for fifteen to forty-five minutes. This is returned to the tun to raise the overall temperature to 111-125° F. This process is repeated for a second "thick mash." Finally, a third portion called the *Läutermaische* (clear mash) is withdrawn and boiled to precipitate coagulable albuminoids before being returned to the tun. The final heat in this system is slightly over 160° F.

### The Filter Press Process
The standard mash-tun process requires coarse grists to ensure drainage, which can lead to incomplete starch conversion and inefficient wort extraction. To solve this, the filter press process (introduced to Great Britain by P. Meura) allows the malt to be ground quite fine. While an ordinary mash-tun may still be used for the initial mashing, the separation of clear wort from solids occurs in a filter press. This method retains the finest particles and allows for easier washing of the filter cake, resulting in increased yields.

## Role in the Brewing Cycle
Mashing is the second of seven main brewing processes. The sequence begins with grinding (where malt is crushed by smooth rollers), followed by mashing. After mashing, the clear wort moves to the copper for boiling with hops, then to the hop back for separation of insoluble constituents, the cooler, the refrigerator, a collecting vessel for duty assessment, and finally to fermenting vessels to become "green" beer. The final stages are cleansing, and racking and storing.
