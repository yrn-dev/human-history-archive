# The Development of the Ballot System in America

## Early Voting Practices
In the earliest American elections, voting was conducted *viva voce*. However, several colonies implemented written or printed ballots early on. By 1775, these were in use in Pennsylvania, Delaware, North Carolina, South Carolina, and the New England states. New Jersey introduced ballots in 1776, followed by New York in 1778. Consequently, when the United States constitution was adopted, *viva voce* voting remained the standard for public elections only in Georgia, Virginia, and Maryland.

Among the states that later entered the Union, Arkansas, Missouri, Kentucky, and Illinois did not have a ballot system upon their admission. Maryland, Georgia, and Illinois adopted the ballot during the first half of the 19th century, with Arkansas doing so in 1846. In Missouri, ballot-voting appeared in some localities in 1845 but was not generally adopted until 1863. Virginia did not provide for voting by ballot until 1869, and Kentucky continued *viva voce* voting until 1819.

## The Pre-Reform Era
Before the adoption of the Australian system, most states had laws regarding the count of the vote and the form of ballots, but no one was tasked with printing or distributing them at polling places. In primitive town meetings, voters wrote their own ballots or used printed ones provided by candidates. As elections evolved, political committees for various parties took over the preparation and distribution of ballot-tickets. 

This system created several issues: it was difficult for voters to cast a mixed ticket, and because ballots were received within a few feet of the polls, secrecy was nearly impossible. This environment made bribery and intimidation both frequent and easy.

## Introduction of the Australian Ballot
Serious agitation for a system similar to the Australian ballot did not begin in the United States until 1885, though it had been introduced in Canada following its adoption in Great Britain. In 1887, the legislatures of Michigan and New York urged bills for the Australian ballot, but neither became law. That same year, a Wisconsin law for cities with populations over 50,000 incorporated some features of the system.

The first complete law was enacted by Massachusetts in 1888. This statute required the state to print and distribute ballots containing the names of all candidates for each office arranged alphabetically; electors then marked the names of the candidates for whom they wished to vote. The movement for reform gained powerful impetus following the presidential election of 1888, where it was alleged that large sums of money were raised on an unprecedented scale to purchase votes.

## Practical Demonstration and Rapid Adoption
The new ballot system had its first practical demonstration at the Massachusetts election of 1889. Its success led to rapid adoption across other states. In 1889, nine states enacted new ballot laws: Montana, Tennessee, Missouri, Minnesota, Wisconsin, Michigan, Indiana, Rhode Island, and Connecticut. 

The implementations varied:
* **Massachusetts Plan:** This blanket ballot arranged candidates by office. It was adopted by the aforementioned states (excluding those using party-groups), as well as Vermont, Mississippi, Wyoming, and Washington in 1890. Later, in 1891, it was adopted in Oregon, Texas, Kentucky, South Dakota, North Dakota, New Hampshire, Nebraska, and Arkansas.
* **Party-Group System:** This system arranged candidates in party-groups. It was used from the start by Maryland and was adopted in 1890 by Vermont (which switched from the Massachusetts plan), Michigan, and Missouri. In 1891, it was followed in West Virginia, Pennsylvania, Ohio, Maine, Illinois, Delaware, and Colorado. Indiana's 1889 law also used this system, allowing a single mark to vote for all candidates in a party-group.
* **Other Variations:** California used the Massachusetts arrangement of names but added a list of party names; marking one party name cast a vote for all its candidates. Pennsylvania listed candidates not in a party-group in alphabetical order. New York and New Jersey's 1890 laws provided official ballots for each party but allowed ballots obtained outside polling booths.

## Later Expansions and Modifications
Between 1892 and 1896, further states adopted the Australian system: Iowa (1892), Kansas and Alabama (1893), Virginia (1894), Florida (1895), Utah and Louisiana (1896). In 1895, New York replaced separate party ballots with a blanket ballot, arranging candidate names in party columns.

Missouri was the only state to abandon the blanket ballot after adopting it, returning to separate ballots in 1897 with no provision for secret marking booths. As of the archive's records, the system of separate party ballots continued to be used in Georgia, North and South Carolina, Missouri, New Jersey, Connecticut, and New Mexico.

## Physical Characteristics of the American Ballot
Due to the high number of officials elected simultaneously, the American ballot differs significantly from the British version. In a New York quadrennial presidential election, a voter may choose thirty-six presidential electors, a congressman, a governor, a lieutenant-governor, five other state officers, members for each house of the state legislature, various judges, a county-clerk, and a sheriff. This results in party columns 2 to 3 feet in length and ballot papers 18 to 20 inches wide. Voters enter a booth to mark either a full party column or individual candidates across different columns.
