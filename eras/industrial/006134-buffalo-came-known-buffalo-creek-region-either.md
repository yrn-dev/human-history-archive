# Buffalo

## Early History and Settlement
The site of Buffalo was first visited by Jesuit missionaries and French trappers. In 1679, La Salle constructed a ship named the "Griffin" on the east bank of the Niagara river at the mouth of Cayuga Creek; in the same year, he built Fort Conti at the mouth of the river, though it was burned. In 1687, the marquis de Denonville established a fort in his own honor at the mouth of the river, which served as the predecessor to the fortifications later known as Fort Niagara. This area remained a site for military operations until the end of the War of Independence.

By 1784, the area became known as the "Buffalo Creek region." This name likely derived from an Indian chief, or possibly from herds of bison or buffalo that Indian tradition suggests frequented the creek's salt licks. The first permanent white resident was Cornelius Winney, an Indian trader who built a cabin near the mouth of the creek around 1788-1789.

The land was originally part of the Phelps-Gorham Purchase and was later acquired around 1793 by the Holland Land Company as part of the Holland Purchase. Joseph Ellicott, the company's agent and known as the "Father of Buffalo," laid out a town between 1801 and 1802. He named the town New Amsterdam, a designation used in company records until approximately 1810, though the names Buffalo or Buffalo Creek remained more popular. The village served as the Niagara county seat starting in 1808, and the town of Buffalo was officially erected two years later.

## Military Conflict
During the second war with Great Britain, Buffalo and the region surrounding Niagara Falls were centers of active military operations, situated across the Niagara river from the British Fort Erie. In October 1812, Lieutenant Jesse D. Elliott captured the "Caledonia" and "Detroit" from Buffalo. Later, on December 30 and 31, 1813, the settlement was captured, sacked, and almost completely destroyed.

## Commerce and Transportation
Buffalo is a major industrial and commercial center due to its location on the principal northern trade route between the East and West and its proximity to Chicago, Boston, and New York. It serves as a distributing center for raw products from the West and manufactured goods from the East, as well as a trans-shipment point between rail and lake transport.

The city's commercial growth was first spurred by the 1825 completion of the Erie Canal, which terminates here. Lake commerce is also vital; almost all major Great Lakes steamship lines have an eastern terminus in Buffalo, providing freight and passenger connections to Milwaukee, Detroit, Cleveland, Chicago, and the "Head of the Lakes" (Duluth-Superior). The latter is served by the Great Northern Steamship Company. The port handles more than 5,000,000 tons annually and possesses a waterfront of roughly 20 miles. Federal government improvements to the harbor include piers, inner breakwaters, and a 4-mile outer breakwater of cement and stone costing over $2,000,000. Additionally, the International bridge from Squaw Island, completed in 1873 at a cost of $1,500,000, connects the city to the Canadian shore.

Rail transportation is extensive, with over 500 miles of track within city limits and 14 trunk lines passing through or terminating in the city.

## Industry and Trade
Buffalo is one of the world's primary markets for flour and grain. In 1843, Joseph Dart built the first grain elevator. By 1906, these elevators had annual receipts exceeding 200,000,000 bushels and a capacity of 20 to 30 million bushels. Annual flour receipts are approximately 10,000,000 barrels. Other major commerce includes:
* **Livestock:** Over 10,000,000 head are handled annually in 75 acres of stock-yards at East Buffalo; the city also hosts the largest horse market in America.
* **Lumber:** Annual receipts average 200,000,000 feet.
* **Other:** Fish (15,000,000 lb annually), coal, and iron ore (some of which is handled at Tonawanda).

In 1908, the Buffalo Creek customs district reported imports valued at $6,708,919 and exports at $26,192,563.

## Education and Public Institutions
The city's educational system includes high and grammar schools, a state normal school, a city training school for teachers, kindergartens, and night schools. The University of Buffalo, organized in 1845, includes schools of medicine (1845), pharmacy (1886), law (1887), and dentistry (1892). Other institutions include the Martin Luther Seminary (1854), Canisius College (a Jesuit institution for men established in 1870), and various academies such as St. Joseph's Collegiate Institute and the Academy of the Sacred Heart.

The Buffalo public library, founded in 1837, contains about 300,000 books and pamphlets in a building erected in 1887. Other libraries include the Grosvenor (75,000 volumes), the Law library (17,000), the Catholic Institute (12,000), and the Buffalo Historical Society library (26,600).

Public health and social services are provided by numerous hospitals, including the Buffalo General, the Children's, the German, and the United States Marine hospital. Charitable institutions include the St. Joseph's and St. Vincent's orphan asylums and the Home for the Friendless. The Charity Organisation Society, founded in 1877, was the first of its kind in the United States.

## Architecture and City Layout
Buffalo is noted for residential sections featuring detached houses with attractive lawns. Key buildings include:
* **Government:** The Federal building ($2,000,000) and the city and county hall ($1,500,000), which features a 245-foot clock tower.
* **Commercial/Social:** The Ellicott Square building (one of the world's largest office structures), the Masonic temple, the chamber of commerce, and various hotels such as the Iroquois and Lafayette.
* **Culture:** The Albright art gallery and the Buffalo Historical Society building in Delaware Park.
