# Walter Bagehot

Walter Bagehot (1826–1877) was an English economist and publicist who served as the editor of the *Economist* newspaper from 1860 until his death. Born on February 3, 1826, in Langport, Somerset, to a father who was a banker, Bagehot was characterized by a personality that combined practical common-sense, philosophic reflectiveness, brilliant wit, and a buoyant humor. While he was politically affiliated with the Liberal party, he possessed a conservative disposition and frequently commented with sarcastic boastfulness on the tenacity and stupidity of the English mind regarding its adherence to old ways in both the country and the city.

## Education and Professional Career
Bagehot earned his degree from London University in 1848 and was called to the bar in 1852. Despite these qualifications, he early dedicated his energies to literature rather than pursuing a large fortune in business or a high position in politics. He joined his father's banking firm, Stuckey & Co., in the west of England. For a significant portion of his life, while concurrently editing the *Economist*, he managed the bank's London agency, attending to its London affairs and lending surplus money in "Lombard Street." Additionally, he served as an underwriter at Lloyd's, though he did not engage in the active detailed business, which was handled for him by proxy.

## Political and Social Connections
Bagehot's entry into the *Economist* began in 1858, around the time he married the daughter of the publication's first editor, the Right Hon. James Wilson. Through this connection, Bagehot gained access to the inner circles of political life. He maintained an intimate friendship with Sir George Cornewall Lewis and remained in constant communication with permanent heads of state departments and political chiefs, including Grant Duff, Robert Lowe, and Gladstone. 

His influence extended into the financial sector, where he was intimate with the directors and governor of the Bank of England, as well as leading commercial and banking magnates. His membership in the Political Economy Club further bridged his contacts between the city and the political world.

## Literary Contributions and Style
Bagehot's writing is noted for a conversational, epigrammatic style that provides a peculiar zest to his work. He is credited with applying a quality of scientific, dispassionate description to subjects that were not previously viewed as scientific studies. This approach is most evident in two of his most reputable works: *The English Constitution* (1867) and *Lombard Street* (1873). 

In *The English Constitution*, Bagehot eschewed the approach of a lawyer using law books; instead, he described the constitution based on its actual working, which he had witnessed through his contact with the heads of government departments, ministers, and the society in which politicians moved. This resulted in a description of the methods and springs of action characterized by vivid freshness.

## Intellectual Interests and Statistics
Bagehot was regarded as an ideal editor due to his fullness of ideas. He often proposed concepts for books that he did not write himself. One such idea was a history of recent politics that utilized knowledge of the family histories and connections of English politicians to provide new insights. He noted this after a specific bill's passage through parliament, where the chancellor of the exchequer—a relative of the permanent head of the department—took over the management of the measure after the head of the department in the House of Commons failed, allowing the chancellor to implement his own legislative ideas. Bagehot also expressed a desire for a book detailing the differences in the fundamental ideas and forms of the administrative systems of Scotland and England, noting that no judicial or other officers in one were represented by corresponding functionaries in the other.

As a journalist, Bagehot provided assistance to the study of statistics. Although he was a most excellent mathematician, he was averse to the manipulation of figures and boasted of an inability to add up. However, he was exceptionally careful regarding the logic of the figures used in his articles, which he scrutinized thoroughly. He frequently cautioned that figures were merely illustrative and did not establish an argument on their own, emphasizing that a subject could not be studied using only accounts and figures.
