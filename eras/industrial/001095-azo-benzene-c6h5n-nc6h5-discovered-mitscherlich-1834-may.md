# Azo-benzene

Azo-benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N:NC}_6\text{H}_5$) is a chemical compound discovered by E. Mitscherlich in 1834. It is characterized as a yellow substance and is classified as a chromogen—a compound containing a chromophorous group (in this case, $-\text{N:N}-$) that, while not a dyestuff itself, can generate one through the introduction of auxochromous salt-forming atomic groups such as $\text{NH}_2$ or $\text{OH}$.

## Physical Properties and Preparation
Azo-benzene crystallizes from alcohol as orange-red plates. It has a melting point of $68^\circ\text{C}$ and a boiling point of $293^\circ\text{C}$. Chemically, it does not react with alkalis or acids; however, when reduced with zinc dust in an acetic acid solution, it yields aniline.

There are three primary methods for the preparation of azo-benzene:
*   The reduction of nitrobenzene using caustic soda and zinc dust in an alcoholic solution.
*   The oxidation of aniline using sodium hypobromite.
*   The condensation of aniline with nitrosobenzene in a solution of hot glacial acetic acid.

## Amino-azo Compounds
Amino-azo compounds are typically red or yellowish-brown. Their specific shade is influenced by their chemical structure: the presence of additional amino groups results in browner shades, while the introduction of alkylated amino groups produces redder shades. These compounds generally crystallize well and are easily reduced. When they are heated with aniline hydrochloride and aniline, they yield indulines.

Specific examples of amino-azo-benzenes include:
*   **Amino-azo-benzene** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{.N}_2\text{.C}_6\text{H}_4\text{NH}_2$): This compound crystallizes in yellow needles or plates and melts at $126^\circ\text{C}$. Its constitution is evidenced by the fact that it yields meta-phenylene diamine and aniline upon reduction with stannous chloride, and it can be prepared by reducing nitro-azo-benzene with ammonium sulphide.
*   **Diamino-azo-benzene (Chrysoidine)** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{.N}_2\text{.C}_6\text{H}_3(\text{NH}_2)_2$): First prepared by O. Witt in 1877, it is obtained by coupling meta-phenylene diamine with phenyl diazonium chloride. It forms red octahedra and is used to dye wool and silk yellow.
*   **Triamino-azo-benzene** ($\text{NH}_2\text{.C}_6\text{H}_4\text{.N}_2\text{.C}_6\text{H}_3(\text{NH}_2)_2$): Also known as vesuvine, phenylene brown, Manchester brown, or Bismarck brown, it is prepared by the action of nitrous acid on meta-phenylene diamine. It forms brown crystals soluble in hot water and dyes mordanted cotton a dark brown.

Alkylated amino-azo-benzenes are formed by coupling diazonium salts with alkylated amines, provided there is a free para position relative to the amino group. Research by A. Merz and H. Goldschmidt indicates that the velocity of this formation depends on the nature of the reagents rather than concentration.

## Oxyazo Compounds
Oxyazo compounds are prepared by adding a diazonium salt solution to a cold, slightly alkaline phenol solution. The diazo group typically occupies the para position relative to the hydroxyl group; if this is blocked, it occupies the ortho position. It never enters the meta position directly.

*   **Para-oxyazobenzene (benzene-azo-phenol)** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N:N(1).C}_6\text{H}_4\text{.OH(4)}$): An orange-red crystalline compound with a melting point of $154^\circ\text{C}$, prepared by coupling phenol with diazotized aniline in alkaline solution.
*   **Ortho-oxyazobenzene** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N:N(1)C}_6\text{H}_4\text{.OH(2)}$): Obtained by E. Bamberger, this compound crystallizes in orange-red needles and melts at $82.5\text{--}83^\circ\text{C}$. Reduction with zinc dust in dilute sal-ammoniac solution yields aniline and ortho-aminophenol.
*   **Meta-oxyazobenzene** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N:N(1)C}_6\text{H}_4\text{.OH(3)}$): Obtained in 1903 by P. Jacobson through the hydrolysis of benzene-azo-meta-anisole with aluminium chloride. It melts at $112\text{--}114^\circ\text{C}$.

The constitution of these compounds has been debated. Some chemists view them as true azophenols, while others suggest a quinonoid structure (quinone hydrazones). J. T. Hewitt found that para-oxyazobenzene reacts with dilute nitric acid to give benzene azo-ortho-nitrophenol, a reaction quinones do not undergo.

## Mixed Azo Compounds and Derivatives
Mixed azo compounds feature an azo group ($\text{.N:N.}$) united with an aliphatic radical on one side and an aromatic radical on the other. These are often formed by the union of a diazonium salt with the sodium or potassium salt of a nitroparaffin.

*   **Benzene-azo-methane** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{.N}_2\text{.CH}_3$): A yellow oil that boils at $150^\circ\text{C}$ and is volatile in steam.
*   **Benzene-azo-ethane** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{.N}_2\text{.C}_2\text{H}_5$): A yellow oil boiling at approximately $180^\circ\text{C}$. It converts into the isomeric acetaldehyde-phenylhydrazone when left with 60% sulphuric acid.

Other mixed derivatives include **diazobenzenecyanide** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_2\text{.CN}$), an unstable oil, and **phenyl-azo-carboxylic acid** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{.N}_2\text{.COOH}$), which crystallizes in orange-red needles.

## Related Nitrogen Compounds
*   **Diazo-amines** ($\text{R.N:N.NHR}_1$): Yellow crystalline solids obtained by the action of primary amines on diazonium salts. Diazoamino benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{.N:N.NHC}_6\text{H}_5$) melts at $96^\circ\text{C}$ and explodes at slightly higher temperatures.
*   **Diazoimino benzene** ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_3$): A yellow oil with a stupefying odour that boils at $59^\circ\text{C}$ (at 12 mm) and explodes when heated.
*   **Azoxy Compounds** ($\text{R.[=N.O.N].R}'$): Yellow or red crystalline solids resulting from the oxidation of azo compounds or the reduction of nitroso or nitro compounds. Azoxybenzene ($\text{(C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N)}_2\text{O}$) melts at $36^\circ\text{C}$ and yields aniline and azobenzene upon distillation.
