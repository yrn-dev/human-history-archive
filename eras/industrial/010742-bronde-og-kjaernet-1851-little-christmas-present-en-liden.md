# Christmas Traditions and Literature

The celebration of Christmas is reflected in a wide array of cultural practices, literary works, and legal designations across different nations and eras, ranging from the development of carols and folklore to the establishment of public holidays.

## Christmas Carols and Poetry
Christmas carols have a long history, with numerous extant examples dating back to the 15th century that exhibit the characteristics of folksongs. Examples include "The Camel and the Crane," "I saw three ships come sailing in," and the "Cherry-tree Carol" (also known as "Joseph was an old man"), the latter of which is based on a legend found in the Coventry mystery plays. 

During the 16th century, household accounts of Tudor sovereigns indicate that carol-singing was a popular activity. The 17th century saw the enrichment of Christmas literature through poems intended for reading rather than singing, produced by authors such as George Herbert, Ben Jonson, Southwell, Crashaw, George Wither, and Milton. Robert Herrick produced a particularly rich collection, including the jovial "Come, bring with a noise," which may have been written to a dance tune. In the 18th century, Charles Wesley wrote "Hark, how all the welkin rings," more commonly known as "Hark, the herald angels sing."

Modern developments include a revival of carol-singing often derived from medieval Latin Christmas hymns. Modern poetry also includes Swinburne's ballad "Three Damsels in the Queen's Chamber." Various collections have been compiled over time, such as T. Wright's *Songs and Carols* (1847), W. Sandys' *Christmastide, its History, Festivities and Carols* (1852), and *Carols for Christmastide* (1853-1854) by J.M. Neale and T. Helmore.

## Folklore and Cultural Figures
In Italy, the figure of Befana serves as a female counterpart to Santa Claus (St Nicholas). Her name is a corruption of *Epifania* (Epiphany). According to tradition, Befana was too occupied with house duties to watch the Three Wise Men of the East pass by on their way to adore the Saviour, claiming she would see them upon their return. Because they returned by a different route, she was punished by being forced to search for them forever. On Twelfth Night or Epiphany, she acts as a fairy godmother, filling children's stockings with presents. Despite this role, Italian mothers have also used her name as a bogey to frighten infants. Historically, it was customary to carry an effigy of Befana through towns on the eve of the Epiphany.

## Legal and Administrative Observances
In the United Kingdom, the Bank Holidays Act 1871 established specific close holidays for banks in Scotland, England, and Ireland. Prior to 1834, the Bank of England closed on approximately thirty-three saints' days and anniversaries, but this was reduced in 1834 to four days: May 1st, November 1st, Good Friday, and Christmas Day. 

Under the 1871 Act, Christmas Day remains a bank holiday in Scotland and England/Ireland. In Scotland, if Christmas Day falls on a Sunday, the following Monday is observed as the bank holiday. The law stipulates that no person is compelled to perform an act or make a payment on a bank holiday that they would not be compelled to do on Good Friday or Christmas Day.

## Other References
The term "carol" also appears in an architectural context, though unrelated to the holiday. It refers to a small oratory or chapel enclosed by screens, or specifically to the separate seats near cloister windows used by monks for study.
