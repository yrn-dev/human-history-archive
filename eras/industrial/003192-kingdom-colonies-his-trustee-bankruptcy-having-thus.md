# Bankruptcy Law and Administration

Bankruptcy law governs the process of distributing a debtor's assets among creditors when insolvency occurs. While systems vary by nation, the English model has served as a foundation for many regions, particularly within the British Empire.

## English Bankruptcy Law and Administration
English bankruptcy legislation has undergone significant evolution, often marked by instability. The Act of 1869, intended to remedy previous failures, was later denounced by Joseph Chamberlain, President of the Board of Trade, as "the most unsatisfactory and most unfortunate" attempt to address the subject. Under the 1869 Act, the registrar of the court adjudicated creditors' claims and presided over meetings, while the bankrupt underwent a public examination. However, the Act was criticized because provisions regarding private arrangements—specifically petitions for liquidation by "arrangement" or composition—allowed a majority in number and three-fourths in value of creditors to resolve matters without judicial investigation of claims.

The Act of 1883 shifted the focus from mere legal procedure to commercial legislation aimed at enforcing commercial morality. A primary feature of this Act was the separation of judicial and administrative functions, transferring the latter to the Board of Trade. This led to the creation of the bankruptcy department of the Board of Trade, headed by an inspector-general in bankruptcy. This department supervises administrative work, audits trustee accounts, and can remove trustees for unfitness, neglect, or misconduct. Additionally, official receivers are appointed to manage estates interimly and oversee the conduct of bankrupts.

## The Role and Duties of the Trustee
The English act vests all of a bankrupt's property—whether located in the United Kingdom or the colonies—in a trustee in bankruptcy. Because the trustee denudes the bankrupt of all assets, the bankrupt's discharge is recognized in colonial courts.

The definition of "property" includes assets the bankrupt truly owns, as well as property in their possession or disposition for trade or business where they are the reputed owner. However, the doctrine of reputed ownership has been restricted by court decisions and trade customs, such as "hire and purchase" contracts. 

The trustee's title relates back to the date of the first act of bankruptcy committed within three months before the bankruptcy petition. This allows the trustee to invalidate payments or assignments made to creditors during that period if the creditor knew of the act of bankruptcy. Similarly, any payment made to give a creditor preference over others within three months of the petition is void. Settlements of property are void if made within two years of bankruptcy, unless they were for valuable consideration in good faith or for marriage. Settlements made within ten years are void unless the settlor was solvent at the time and the interest passed to the trustees upon execution of the deed.

Trustees must maintain a record book and a cash book. All funds must be paid into the "Bankruptcy Estates Account" at the Bank of England under Board of Trade control; payments to a trustee's private account are strictly forbidden. Trustees may disclaim property subject to onerous obligations, such as rent, sometimes with court leave.

## Distribution of Assets and Costs
Certain claims receive priority during the distribution of the estate. These include:
*   Taxes and local rates for up to one year.
*   Wages and salaries for four months (up to £50), with a limit of two months (up to £25) for ordinary workmen.
*   Agricultural labourers' claims for up to one year's wages under specific lump-sum contracts.
*   Balances due to friendly societies or savings banks if the bankrupt held an office of trust.

Landlords may distrain for unpaid rent on goods remaining on their premises, though the Act of 1890 limits this to six months' rent due before adjudication. Conversely, some claims are postponed until general creditors are paid, including loans from a married woman to her husband for business.

Regarding costs, a remunerated trustee cannot claim costs for duties they should have performed themselves. All agent and solicitor bills must be taxed according to prescribed scales and authorized before work begins.

## Release of the Trustee
Once property is realized and distributed, the trustee applies to the Board of Trade for release. This requires sending notices and final accounts to each creditor. The Board of Trade may grant or withhold the release after considering a report and any objections. A granted release removes the trustee from office, at which point the official receiver becomes the trustee again to handle any unforeseen circumstances.

## International Systems
### Germany
Governed by a code passed in 1877, German law distinguishes between simple and fraudulent bankruptcy. Simple bankruptcy involves offences like extravagance, gambling, or disorderly book-keeping. Fraudulent bankruptcy involves the concealment of property, falsifying books, or illegal preferences, and is punishable by penal servitude or imprisonment. Creditors may appoint a committee to advise the trustee, and assets are typically sold by auction for rapid liquidation. A composition may be accepted by a majority in number and three-fourths in value of creditors, subject to court approval.

### Italy
Under the Commercial Code of 1883, only "merchants"—those habitually engaged in commercial business, including companies—can enter bankruptcy court. Proceedings can be initiated by the debtor, a creditor for a commercial debt, or the court. Bankruptcy is declared only upon proof of insolvency. A trustee is appointed to sell all movable and immovable property by auction. Arrangements can close a bankruptcy if they have the assent of creditors representing three-fourths of the indebtedness.

### British Empire
Many colonies, such as Australia and New Zealand, modeled their laws on the English system. Victoria, South Australia, and Western Australia follow existing English acts, while Queensland, Tasmania, and New South Wales follow the 1869 Act. Notably, New South Wales, Victoria, and New Zealand require bankrupts to apply for discharge within a fixed period or face contempt of court, addressing a deficiency in the English system where 70,000 undischarged bankrupts remained in the UK.
