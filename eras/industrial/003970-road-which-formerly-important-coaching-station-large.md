# Road and Coaching History

The development of roads and the systems of carriage transport have historically served as primary means of communication and commerce across various regions, facilitating the movement of people, mail, and goods before the widespread adoption of rail and telegraph systems.

## Coaching Stations and Road Networks
Certain towns developed specifically as hubs for road travel. Chipping Barnet (also known as High Barnet), a market town in Hertfordshire, England, is located on the Great North Road. It formerly served as an important coaching station. Similarly, in France, the village of Cuvilly is situated on the old Flanders road, which was previously utilized by stage-coaches that stopped at a local Post-House.

In other regions, road infrastructure varied in quality. In Bolivia, public highways were constructed to connect large cities and frontier points, supporting subsidized stage coaches. However, these roads were often rough, and river crossings were considered dangerous and difficult. In Asiatic Turkey, the town of Bashkala serves as a military station with roads leading to Mosul, Urmia in Persia, and Van.

## Evolution of Public Carriages
Public carriages for hire, known as hackney coaches, were established in London in 1625. Despite opposition from the king and court, who feared the vehicles would break up the roads, their numbers grew from a restricted limit of fifty in 1635 to 300 by 1650, and 700 by 1694. Many of these early hackneys were repurposed private coaches from the gentry and nobility; specialized smaller coaches for hackney use were not built until 1790.

In Paris, hackney carriages were introduced by Nicholas Sauvage during the minority of Louis XIV. Because Sauvage lived at the sign of St Fiacre on the rue St Martin, these vehicles became known as *fiacres*. By the mid-18th century, the *cabriolet de place* (later shortened to "cab") gained popularity in Paris, with 1,150 such vehicles plying the streets by 1813. The original cabriolet was a hooded gig with room for a driver and one passenger. Later iterations included a four-wheeled version for two persons introduced by Mr Boulnois, and a four-person version by Mr Harvey, which served as the prototype for the London "four-wheeler."

## Stage-Coach Travel and Speed
Stage-coach services provided long-distance connectivity. In 1673, coaches ran from London to Exeter, York, and Chester, with each route employing forty horses and carrying six inside-passengers; the journey to Exeter took eight days. By 1706, the trip from London to York was reduced to four days, with departures every Monday, Wednesday, and Friday. In the same year, a coach traveled from London to Birmingham, departing Monday and arriving Wednesday. In 1784, coaches became universal at a speed of 8 miles per hour.

The volume of traffic increased significantly with the development of specific destinations. The erection of the pavilion at Brighton by the prince of Wales (later George IV) in 1786 led to a surge in traffic, resulting in 70 coaches daily visiting and leaving the town by 1820. By 1835, there were 700 mail coaches operating across Ireland and Great Britain. This growth was facilitated by the road construction system introduced by Mr McAdam.

## Coach-Building and Vehicle Types
Coach-building emerged as a sophisticated industry. In the 18th century, there was a rapid development in sumptuous state carriages for European courts. Notable examples include the 1696 imperial family carriage of Vienna, designed in the style of Louis XIV with panels painted by nymphs in the style of Rubens and a large imperial crown on the roof. Other significant builds include the 1757 state coach of the city of London and the 1761 royal state coach of England for King George III.

In England, the Society of Arts encouraged improvements in coach-building through money prizes. While French builders were noted for their elegant taste and finish, English carriages were preeminent in constructive quality. Various types of carriages became vogue, including:
* **Popular styles:** The victoria (and double victoria), sociable, brougham, landau, and landaulet.
* **Four-in-hand and road coaches:** These were built on a smaller scale than in the past, with a standard roof height of 6 feet 8 inches.
* **Other vehicles:** The char-a-banc, mail phaeton, wagonette, four-wheel dog-cart, polo-cart, ralli-cart, and governess-car. The tandem-cart was attempted but became almost obsolete in England.

Coach-building also existed as a local industry in other parts of the world, such as in the city of Bendigo, Victoria, Australia.
