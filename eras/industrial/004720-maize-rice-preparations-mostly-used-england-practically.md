# Maize and Rice Preparations in English Brewing

In the brewing industry of Great Britain, maize and rice are utilized as adjuncts or substitutes for barley malt. While raw grains—including rice, maize, wheat, and unmalted barley—are not used extensively in Great Britain, they are employed more frequently in America, where brewers may use these materials for 50% or more of the composition.

## Nature and Processing of Preparations
The preparations of maize and rice used in England consist practically of pure starch. This is achieved by removing substantially all of the water, oil, and other subsidiary constituents of the grain. In the case of maize, the germ must be eliminated because it contains a proportion of oil with a somewhat unpleasant flavour, which would otherwise make the material unfit for use in the mash-tun.

The processing of maize involves several specific steps:
1. Degerming.
2. Unhusking.
3. Wetting.
4. Application of a temperature sufficient to rupture the starch cells.
5. Drying.
6. Rolling the material into a flaky condition.

Rice is treated in a similar manner to produce flaked goods.

## Application in the Brewing Process
When raw grains are used, the mashing process is modified. Maize, rice, or other grains are typically gelatinized in a separate vessel known as a cooker or converter using high-temperature steam. This process may occur with or without the addition of some malt meal. After approximately half an hour, the gelatinized mass is mixed with the main mash shortly before taps are set, allowing the highly disintegrated starch to be converted rapidly. Alternatively, the limited-decoction system allows for a fair percentage of raw grain to be used directly in the mash-tun, removing the need for a separate converter.

## Use in Specific Beer Types
Flaked maize and rice are used in various beer styles:
* **Pale Ales:** These are produced from pale malt alone, or a combination of pale malt with glucose, invert sugar, rice, or a small amount of flaked maize.
* **Running Beers (Mild Ale):** These are made using a mixture of amber and pale malts, flaked goods, and sugar.
* **Stout:** These are produced from a mixture of roasted (black), amber, and pale malts, sometimes with the addition of flaked maize or a small amount of sugar.

## Relation to Glucose Production
In England, purified maize and rice, along with sago, are generally the materials used to manufacture glucose. Glucose is produced directly from starch via the action of dilute boiling acid, which converts the starch first into maltose and dextrine, and then into glucose.

## Statistical Context of Malt Substitutes
Historically, sugar was the only allowed substitute for malt in the United Kingdom prior to the repeal of the Malt Acts. However, preparations of maize, rice, and unmalted corn later gained favor. Statistics from 1905 indicate that 1,348,558 cwt. of rice, maize, and similar materials were used. 

The overall use of substitutes in brewing increased significantly between 1878 and 1905. In 1878, substitutes accounted for 6.05% of the total material used in brewing (with 3,825,148 bushels of sugar and 59,388,905 bushels of malt). By 1905, the percentage of substitutes rose to 23.22%, with sugar usage reaching 15,706,413 bushels and malt usage at 51,942,368 bushels.
