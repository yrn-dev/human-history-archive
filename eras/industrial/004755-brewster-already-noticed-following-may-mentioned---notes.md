# Sir David Brewster

Sir David Brewster was a scientist and editor whose contributions to the intellectual history of his age were marked by empirical research and the popularization of scientific instruments. His approach to discovery was characterized by repeated experiment rather than mathematical genius, and he did not contribute to the ultimate explanation of the phenomena he studied. Throughout his life, he neither explicitly adopted the undulatory theory of light nor maintained the corpuscular theory to the end.

## Scientific Discoveries and Research
Brewster's primary scientific inquiries focused on optics and the properties of light. His most significant work involved the laws of polarization by reflection and refraction, as well as the discovery of the polarizing structure induced by pressure and heat. He is credited with discovering the connection between the polarizing angle and the refractive index, the production of double refraction by irregular heating, and the discovery of biaxial crystals (crystals with two axes of double refraction). His research also extended to the laws of metallic reflection and experiments regarding the absorption of light.

In the field of color theory, Brewster proposed three "primaries"—red, blue, and yellow—though these were later referred to as "impossible" by others, such as Chevreul.

## Optical Instruments
Brewster achieved significant fame among the non-scientific public through his work with optical devices. Around 1815, he rediscovered the kaleidoscope, which became highly sought after in America and England. Later, between 1849 and 1850, he contributed to the development of the stereoscope. While the principle of the stereoscope was discovered and applied by Sir Charles Wheatstone in 1838 using mirrors, Brewster is credited with the invention of the lenticular stereoscope by suggesting the use of lenses to unite dissimilar pictures.

## Literary and Editorial Contributions
Brewster's service to science extended beyond his own discoveries into extensive literary and editorial work. He began as a contributor to the *Edinburgh Magazine* in 1799 and served as its editor at age twenty. In 1807, he became the editor of the *Edinburgh Encyclopaedia*, contributing many of its most valuable scientific articles between 1808 and 1830. He also wrote articles on Magnetism, Electricity, Optics, Hydrodynamics, the Microscope, the Stereoscope, and Voltaic Electricity for the seventh and eighth editions of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*.

In 1819, he co-founded the *Edinburgh Philosophical Journal* with Robert Jameson, editing it until 1824. Following this, he started the *Edinburgh Journal of Science*, editing sixteen volumes between 1824 and 1832. Brewster was a prolific writer for learned societies and reviews, contributing between three and four hundred papers to various society transactions and seventy-five articles to the *North British Review*.

Among his separate works, his most significant was the biography of Sir Isaac Newton. After publishing a short popular account in 1831, he released the comprehensive *Memoirs of the Life, Writings and Discoveries of Sir Isaac Newton* in 1855, the result of over twenty years of research into original manuscripts. Other notable works include:
* *Notes and Introduction to Carlyle's translation of Legendre's Elements of Geometry* (1824)
* *Treatise on Optics* (1831)
* *Letters on Natural Magic* (1831), addressed to Sir Walter Scott
* *The Martyrs of Science, or the Lives of Galileo, Tycho Brahe, and Kepler* (1841)
* *More Worlds than One* (1854)

## Professional Career and Honors
Brewster's editorial roles allowed him to communicate with eminent scientists, leading him to suggest the creation of an association for philosophers, clergy, gentry, and nobility. This suggestion helped lead to the formation of the British Association for the Advancement of Science, which held its first meeting in York in 1831. Brewster, along with Sir John F. W. Herschel and Charles Babbage, played a primary role in shaping its constitution. He later served as the association's president in 1849.

His academic and professional honors include:
* The degree of LL.D. from Marischal College, Aberdeen (1807).
* Membership in the Royal Society of London and the Copley medal (1815).
* The Rumford medal (1818).
* A prize of one-half of three thousand francs from the French Institute (1816) for two of the most important discoveries in physical science in Europe.
* Knighthood and the decoration of the Guelphic order of Hanover (1831).
* Appointment as principal of the united colleges of St Leonard and St Salvator, St Andrews (1838).
* Election as one of eight foreign associates of the Institute of France (1849).
* Principal of the University of Edinburgh (appointed 1859).

Sir David Brewster died on February 10, 1868, at Allerly, Melrose.
