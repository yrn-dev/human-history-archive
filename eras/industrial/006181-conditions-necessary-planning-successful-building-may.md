# Planning a Successful Building

The planning and execution of a successful building require a combination of specific environmental conditions, professional expertise, and adherence to regulatory frameworks. The process involves a transition from conceptual planning and site analysis to the creation of detailed technical specifications.

## Essential Conditions for Success
The conditions necessary for planning a successful building can be summarized into five primary requirements:
1. Ease of access.
2. Good light.
3. Good service.
4. Pleasing approaches and environment.
5. True economy with minimum cost.

In the specific case of office buildings, an additional requirement is the ease of rearrangement to accommodate different tenants.

## The Role and Expertise of the Architect
An architect must possess practical acquaintance with all trades or arts employed in building and all modes of operation. A master of the profession must be able to ascertain the quantities of work to be done and minutely estimate the absolute cost of executing a proposed structure beforehand. While the measurement of work is usually performed by a quantity surveyor at an advanced stage, the architect typically cubes a building at a price per foot cube.

Once the architect has obtained the approval of the client and the consent of building authorities, they may face challenges regarding air space, party walls, or ancient lights from adjoining owners. In the city of London, these disputes can lead to significant losses for the client and long suspensions of work.

## Site Preparation and Documentation
Proper site analysis is critical to the planning process. If the soil is uncertain or the site is large, trial holes should be sunk immediately following the approval of sketch plans. For leasehold properties, the architect must negotiate approval from freeholders and senior lessors before serving required notices regarding party walls.

Documentation must be precise. Contract plans should include elevations, sections, and plans of all building parts, with levels provided from a datum line. Large-scale details of principal portions are required in addition to general drawings. If existing buildings are on the site, they must be carefully surveyed with accurate detail plans, particularly concerning the rights of adjoining owners and easements. Site plans must also display the various levels of the ground.

Following the approval of plans by all concerned parties, the architect writes the specification. This document describes the materials to be used, how they are to be mixed, and how the works are to be executed, specifying every portion of work and every trade. Typically, two copies are made: one for the builder and a signed contract copy for the architect.

## Regulatory and Legal Constraints
Buildings must be planned on clearly defined lines according to the rules of controlling authorities. Examples of such governance include:
* **Educational and Religious Buildings:** In England, public schools are governed by the Board of Education, while churches are governed by financing societies, most notably the Incorporated Church Building Society.
* **Industrial Buildings:** Factories in France and England must meet separate acts.
* **Residential Buildings:** In London, dwelling-houses are subject to building acts governing drainage, wall thickness, roof inclination, materials, and fire escape. These laws specifically forbid timber-framed buildings.
* **Fire Safety:** Fire insurance companies set requirements based on the building's size and trade. In London, if a combined shop and dwelling-house exceeds 10 squares (1,000 sq. ft. super), the stairs and a large portion of the building must use fire-resisting materials. Similarly, stairs and corridors in certain London flats must be fire-resisting, though timber buildings are permitted in parts of New York.
* **Public Spaces:** Theatres and public buildings in New York, Paris, and London must conform to regulations regarding seating, exits, and the stage (including flies and scenery docks).

## Specialized Planning: Military and Colonial Structures
Military planning often relies on standardized systems to ensure efficiency and avoid fresh designs for every building. In the British system, the *Barrack Synopsis* provides particulars on authorized buildings, fittings, and the cubic contents of rooms. These are supplemented by "Standard Plans" that illustrate convenient arrangements based on previous successful designs, which are then modified for local conditions. These designs prioritize sound sanitary construction and convenience of plan over ornamentation.

Specialized military facilities include:
* **Army Schools:** Planned similarly to civil elementary schools but with slightly more liberal accommodation.
* **Garrison Churches:** Built when troops cannot attend neighboring places of worship.
* **Detention Barracks:** Constructed according to Home Office regulations.
* **Support Facilities:** Every station requires an engineer office with workshops and a building yard. Large stations may require supply depots (with bakeries and abattoirs), sewage disposal works, and special water, gas, or electricity schemes.

In colonial stations, the *Barrack Synopsis* is modified for climate. In tropical or sub-tropical regions, planning focuses on the freest possible circulation of air. This is achieved by raising inhabited buildings some 4 ft. off the ground, arranging the aspect to catch prevailing winds, and using verandahs to protect walls from direct sunlight. Additionally, kitchens are usually kept detached from dwelling-houses in hot countries.
