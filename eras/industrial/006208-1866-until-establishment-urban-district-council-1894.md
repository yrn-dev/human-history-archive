# Local Government Transition: 1866–1894

The period between 1866 and 1894 marked a transition in the administrative governance of certain districts, specifically regarding the shift from local boards to urban district councils.

## Governance of Builth
In the town known in Welsh as Llanfair (yn) Muallt (St Mary's in Builth), the administration was managed by a local board starting in 1866. This system of governance remained in place until 1894, when an urban district council was established. Following this change, the urban district was made conterminous with the civil parish. In 1898, the area was renamed Builth Wells.

The town's location is rooted in the ancient territorial division of Buallt, which Nennius described as an independent principality in the early 9th century and later a cantrev. Historically, the district became a lordship marcher annexed to Brecknock during the Norman invasions of the late 11th century. It later became part of the shire of Brecknock following the Act of Union in 1536.

## The Local Government Act 1894
The establishment of urban district councils was part of a broader change in parochial government introduced by the Local Government Act 1894. This act specifically altered the government of burial grounds. Under this legislation, the powers, liabilities, property, and duties previously held by burial boards in urban districts were transferred, or allowed to be transferred, to the district councils. In rural parishes, these responsibilities were transferred to parish meetings and parish councils.

## Burial Ground Administration
The 1894 changes followed a series of statutes known as the Burial Acts, which spanned from 1852 to 1900. These acts were created to address the overcrowded state of burial grounds and churchyards. The goal was to provide new burial grounds and close those deemed dangerous to public health. 

A central authority, the home office (which for most purposes became the Local Government Board), was established to superintend these grounds to maintain public decency and protect health. The Local Government Board held the power to:
* Close any burial ground via an order in council.
* Grant necessary consent for the opening of new burial grounds.
* Direct the inspection of cemeteries.
* Regulate burials in common graves within statutory cemeteries.
* Compel those in charge of burial places or vaults to take necessary steps to prevent them from becoming injurious to health.

Under these regulations, the vestry of an ecclesiastical or common-law parish was authorized to provide a new burial ground if the existing one was unavailable. These grounds could be partly or wholly consecrated, and chapels could be provided for burial services. Management was handled by a burial board composed of ratepayers elected by the vestry.
