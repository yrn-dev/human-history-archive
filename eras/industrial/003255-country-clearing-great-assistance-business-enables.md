# Clearing Systems and Business Assistance

Banking clearing systems are mechanisms designed to settle accounts between financial institutions, thereby reducing the friction of commercial transactions. These systems have evolved in various forms across different nations, providing significant assistance to business development by streamlining the movement of capital and the processing of payments.

## The London Clearing House
In England, city banks had associated themselves into a "Clearing House" by 1776, although records from the Grasshopper bank suggest a less organized system of settlement existed as early as 1773, noting a quarterly charge for the use of a "Clearing-room." By 1810, a dedicated house was acquired for this purpose, with forty-six banking houses settling their accounts there.

The membership of the Clearing House evolved over time. In 1844, twenty-six of the forty-nine London banks were members, though at that time membership was restricted to private banks. Joint-stock banks were admitted in 1854. By 1906, the membership consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank. Certain institutions remained outside this body, including banks in Charing Cross and Fleet Street. The Bank of England was never a member, although it "clears on one side," meaning its claims on clearing bankers are handled through the Clearing House, while the bankers' claims on the Bank of England are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times daily.

A significant advancement occurred in 1858 through arrangements made by Sir John Lubbock (Lord Avebury), which added the clearing of country cheques. This "country clearing" provided great assistance to business by allowing a cheque drawn on a distant village in England to be handled as conveniently as one drawn on London.

## Clearing Systems in the United States
The United States developed a system of clearing cheques that reached a higher level of development than almost any other country, with the possible exception of Great Britain. By 1907, clearing-houses existed in approximately 112 leading cities. For the year ending September 30, 1907, the aggregate clearings totaled $154,662,515,258.

The New York Clearing-House managed a substantial portion of this volume; in 1906, its clearings accounted for 67.2% of the total clearings across 55 of the larger cities. The volume of these clearings is subject to great fluctuation based on the prosperity of the country's business and the volume of transactions on the stock exchange. For example, data from New York shows that average daily clearings rose to $115,885,794 in 1873 during a period of great business activity, but dropped to $74,692,574 in 1874 during an industrial depression. Other fluctuations include a dip to $82,789,480 in 1885 due to a bank panic and a peak of $342,422,773 in 1906 driven by stock-market activity.

## International Variations and Specialized Banking
Different nations have employed various methods to assist business and stabilize trade:

*   **Germany:** The Reichsbank, managed by a directory appointed by the chancellor of the empire, assists business primarily through its note circulation and the "Giro-Verkehr" (clearing system), which it has greatly developed. This was made possible by the uniformity of coinage introduced by laws between 1871 and 1873.
*   **France:** Business transactions in France are typically liquidated using notes of the Bank of France rather than cheques. The bank's large reserve of specie—which in 1906 averaged L115,000,000 in gold and L42,000,000 in silver—has provided stability to French trade and allowed the bank to avoid the frequent fluctuations in discount rates seen in England. This system provides a great advantage to French industry through low interest rates on borrowed capital.
*   **Hamburg:** The Bank of Hamburg historically aided commerce by creating a "money of account" or international coinage. This allowed merchants to avoid the delays and expenses of verifying various foreign coins that differed in purity, weight, and intrinsic value, thereby contributing to the city's prosperity.

## The Role of Capital in Business Development
The broader function of banks in assisting the community involves the employment of capital. According to David Ricardo, a bank's real advantage to the community begins when it employs the capital of others rather than just its own. Banks collect deposits and issue notes, which are then loaned to trusted borrowers.

While some deposits are held for the customer's everyday wants, the majority enable banks to make advances to those engaged in "reproductive industry." This type of industry is defined as that which brings back a greater value than the original loan and maintains profitable enterprises, thereby assisting the business development of the country. To maintain safe business lines, it is suggested that advances should consist of floating rather than fixed capital.
