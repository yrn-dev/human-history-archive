# Bombay City

## Geography and Status
Bombay City serves as the capital of the Bombay Presidency and is the primary seaport for western India. It is located at 18 deg. 55' N. and 72 deg. 54' E. The city is situated on an island of the same name, which is part of a group of islands that have been connected to the mainland via causeways. The city covers an area of 22 square miles.

## Population and Urban Growth
As of the 1901 census, the population of the town and island was 776,006, with a subsequent estimate in 1906 placing the population at 977,822. At the time of the 1901 census, Bombay was the second most populous city in the Indian empire, trailing behind Calcutta.

The city experienced a period of unrestricted development following the annexation of the Deccan and the British victory over the Mahrattas. During this era, specifically in 1819, the city's fortunes were vigorously fostered by Mountstuart Elphinstone. By 1838, the population had reached 236,000. Over the following fifty years, the population more than doubled, reaching 821,000 by 1891.

## Economic Drivers and Infrastructure
The rapid growth of Bombay was driven by several key factors:
*   **Railways:** The first railway line was completed in 1853, establishing the city as a railway centre.
*   **Maritime Trade:** The opening of the Suez Canal enhanced its advantages as a port.
*   **Industry:** The foundation of cotton factories gave the city a monopoly over the cotton industry.

Despite these advantages and its proximity to Europe, Bombay's growth is counteracted by the fact that it lacks a great waterway similar to the Brahmaputra or the Ganges, and the region it serves is not as fertile as the valley of the Ganges. Nevertheless, the city remains a strong competitor to Calcutta regarding commercial prosperity and population.

## Economic Fluctuations and Challenges
The city's prosperity has faced intermittent interruptions. In 1866-1867, a financial crisis occurred due to a fall in cotton prices following the termination of the American war. While Bombay recovered and reached a peak of prosperity by 1891, the following decade was marked by significant disruption. 

A plague caused great havoc to both trade and the population. This resulted in a 6% decline in population and a 7% decline in exports. During this same period of decline for Bombay, the exports of Calcutta rose by 38%.
