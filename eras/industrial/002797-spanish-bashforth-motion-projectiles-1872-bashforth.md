# Ballistics and the Work of F. Bashforth

Ballistics is the science of throwing projectiles or warlike missiles. The field is divided into two primary branches: interior ballistics and exterior ballistics. Interior ballistics focuses on the analysis of powder-gas pressure within the bore of a gun to determine the charge of powder required to achieve a specific initial velocity without causing undue strain to the weapon. Exterior ballistics considers the motion of the projectile after it has received its initial impulse, moving freely under the influence of gravity and air resistance to determine the requirements for hitting and destroying a specific object.

## The Experiments of F. Bashforth
The foundation for the construction of ballistic tables and the understanding of air resistance is based on a series of experiments conducted between 1864 and 1880 by the Rev. F. Bashforth, B.D. Bashforth determined that air resistance cannot be represented by a simple algebraical law across a wide range of velocity. To address this, he abandoned a priori theoretical assumptions in favor of experimental measurement.

Bashforth utilized a chronograph and equidistant electric screens equipped with vertical wire or threads to measure the velocity of shot and air resistance as the projectile flew nearly horizontally. By applying formulae from the calculus of finite differences to the chronograph records, he was able to infer the retardation and velocity of the shot, and consequently, the resistance of the air. His findings were detailed in his *Report on the Experiments made with the Bashforth Chronograph* (1865-1870), his *Final Report* (1878-1880), and the book *The Bashforth Chronograph* (1890).

## Coefficients of Projectile Motion
In the study of exterior ballistics, several factors are used to calculate the resistance of the air (R). These are combined into a single "coefficient of reduction" (n), expressed by the formula $n = [\kappa] [\sigma] [\tau]$.

*   **Coefficient of Shape ($\kappa$):** This factor accounts for the reduction of air resistance achieved by giving modern projectiles a sharper point and greater length, and by removing projecting studs. For a projectile with an ogival head struck with a radius of 2 diameters, Bashforth assigns $\kappa = 0.975$. For spherical shot, $\kappa$ is not constant, though it averages 1.7. Flat-headed projectiles used at proof-butts have a $\kappa$ of approximately 1.8, or 2 on average.
*   **Coefficient of Steadiness ($\sigma$):** Introduced by Bashforth to account for the superior centering provided by breech-loading systems. This value may vary during flight, as a shot may be unsteady immediately after leaving the muzzle before steadying like a spinning-top. Additionally, $\sigma$ may increase as a gun wears out over time.
*   **Atmospheric Factor ($\tau$):** This becomes important in high-angle, long-range fire where projectiles reach attenuated strata of the atmosphere. Conversely, for calculations involving shooting underwater, $\tau$ is taken to be approximately 800.

## Application and Ballistic Tables
Bashforth's experimental results, though originally conducted with muzzle-loading guns and old-fashioned projectiles, can be applied to modern designs using a well-chosen value for $n$. For example, a value of 0.8 or less is considered a good average for modern rifle bullets.

To construct a ballistic table, the time (t) required for a shot of diameter $d$ and weight $w$ to fall from an initial velocity $V$ to a final velocity $v$ is determined, assuming horizontal motion and ignoring the curving effect of gravity. The "ballistic coefficient" (or driving power) of the shot is defined as $C = w/nd^2$.

## Interior Ballistics and Pressure Measurement
To measure pressure within the bore of a gun, a crusher-gauge is employed. This device records maximum pressure via the compression of an internal copper cylinder and can be placed in the base of the shot or the powder-chamber. In the research of Sir Andrew Noble, chronographic appliances and plugs were used in the side of experimental guns to register the passage of the shot, providing an independent control for the crusher-gauge records.

Preliminary pressure determinations are often made by exploding a powder charge in a closed vessel to vary the density of the powder-gas. This method, used by Benjamin Robins in 1743 and Count Rumford in 1792, was later revived by Dr. Kellner, who used bicycle ball-bearing steel spheres as safety-valves to check crusher-gauge indications.
