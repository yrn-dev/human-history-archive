# London Banking and Bankers' Clearing House System

The London banking system utilizes a sophisticated mechanism for the settlement of transactions, primarily centered around the London Clearing House. This system allows for the vast majority of large transactions in the United Kingdom to be settled via cheque through a series of ledger transfers, reducing the reliance on specie and notes to the payment of fractional amounts.

## The London Clearing House: History and Evolution
Purely city banks had associated themselves into a "Clearing House" by 1776, though evidence from the books of the Grasshopper suggests a less organized system of settlement existed as early as 1773, noting a quarterly charge for the use of a "Clearing-room." In 1810, a dedicated house was acquired for this purpose; at that time, forty-six banking houses settled their accounts through the system.

Membership requirements evolved over the 19th century. In 1844, only private banks were permitted as members, and of the forty-nine banks in London at that time, twenty-six were connected to the Clearing House. Joint-stock banks gained entry to the body in 1854. By 1906, the membership consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank. Certain institutions remained outside the system, including banks located at Charing Cross or Fleet Street.

The Bank of England has never been a member of the Clearing House. However, it "clears on one side," meaning its claims on clearing bankers are processed through the Clearing House, while the claims of clearing bankers against the Bank of England are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times daily.

## Operations and Impact
The system is facilitated by the fact that every bank in the United Kingdom maintains an agent in London. A significant expansion occurred in 1858 when arrangements made by Sir John Lubbock (Lord Avebury) added the clearing of country cheques. This "country clearing" provided a major boost to business by allowing cheques drawn on distant English villages to be handled as conveniently as those drawn on London banks.

The volume of turnover processed by the London Clearing House has grown substantially over time. According to the *Appendix to the Second Report, Committee of House of Commons, Banks of Issue* (1841), the figures were as follows:
*   **1839:** £954,401,600 (29 banks)
*   **1840:** £978,496,800 (29 banks)
*   **1899:** £9,150,269,000 (19 banks)
*   **1900:** £8,960,170,000 (19 banks)
*   **1906:** £12,711,334,000 (18 banks)

It is estimated that the total yearly turnover in cheques—including those from clearing houses in Scotland, Ireland, and large English towns, as well as internal bank branch settlements—is at least twice as great as the amount registered by the London Clearing House alone.

## Legal Framework and Banker Protections
The operation of the banking system is governed by various statutes regarding the liability and protection of bankers. Under the Stamp Act 1853 (sec. 19), bankers paying a draft or order payable to order on demand were relieved of liability if the endorsement was unauthorized or forged. This protection was extended to cheques by sec. 60 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882.

Regarding crossed cheques, sec. 79 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 stipulates that a banker who pays a crossed cheque contrary to its crossing is liable to the true owner for any resulting loss. Conversely, a banker who pays in accordance with the ostensible tenor of a crossed cheque is protected under sec. 80 and the proviso to sec. 79.

Bankers are also granted qualified immunity under sec. 82 for the collection of crossed cheques for customers. To qualify for this protection, as clarified in *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* (1903), the banker must:
*   Receive the cheque and money from a customer with whom they have existing, definite business relations.
*   Take the cheque already crossed generally or specially to themselves.
*   Act in good faith and without negligence, exercising due care for the true owner.
*   Ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct and not take "account payee" cheques for any other account.
*   Act strictly as an agent or "conduit pipe" for the customer; crediting cheques as cash before receiving the money is considered taking them as an owner, which removes this protection.
