# British Steamers on the Tigris

## Introduction and Establishment
British steamers were first introduced to the Tigris as a result of an expedition led by Colonel F. R. Chesney in 1836. Following this expedition, British steamers were permitted to navigate the river, and a British gunboat was stationed before the residency.

## Steam Communication and Traffic
Steam communication is maintained between Bagdad and its port, Basra, which is located on the Shatt el-Arab, slightly more than 50 miles from the Persian Gulf. Bagdad itself is approximately 500 miles from the Persian Gulf when following the course of the river. This communication is served by two lines of steamers: one Turkish and one English.

The British service consists of two steamers that maintain a weekly connection with Basra. However, these two vessels are considered quite inadequate for the volume of freight traffic moving between the two cities. In contrast, the Turkish service employs a more numerous fleet of vessels, but these are poorly handled, inadequately equipped, and so small that they are used for freight or passenger transport only by those unable to secure the services of the British steamers.

## Navigation Challenges
Navigating the Tigris from Bagdad to Korna is characterized as slow and uncertain for several reasons:
* **Geography:** The river runs through an absolutely flat country of alluvial soil, making it prone to changing its channel.
* **Seasonal Variations:** During flood time, the area can become a huge lake, making it extremely difficult to locate the channel. In the dry season (autumn and winter), there is a constant danger of grounding on shifting shoals and flats.
* **Human Intervention:** Inhabitants along the eastern bank frequently dig new canals for irrigation, which reduces the river's water levels and contributes to the shifting of the channel.

While steamers operate on the lower sections of the river, there are no steamers on the Tigris above Bagdad. In these upper reaches, sailing vessels of 30 tons or more navigate to Samarra and beyond. For local service in the immediate vicinity of Bagdad, the *kufa* is used—a circular basket-work boat covered in bitumen, capable of carrying a dozen men and five or six horses. These boats are depicted on old Assyrian monuments. Another ancient craft used in the region is the *kellek* raft.

## Infrastructure and Logistics
The town of Kut-el-Amara, located on the east bank of the Tigris where the Shatt-el-Haï leaves the stream, serves as an important Turkish post for controlling the lower Tigris. It also functions as a coaling station for the steamers plying between Bagdad and Basra.

## The Euphrates Expedition and the Loss of the Tigris
Colonel Chesney also commanded the Euphrates expedition. In May 1836, a violent hurricane occurred while the steam-boats *Tigris* and *Euphrates* were passing over the rocks of Es-Geria. While the *Euphrates* was secured safely, the *Tigris* struck the bank with great violence. Heavy gusts of wind drove the vessel rapidly along the bank, making it impossible to secure. The *Tigris* perished in the storm, resulting in the loss of twenty men. Colonel Chesney escaped by swimming to shore in darkness. He reported seeing the keel of the *Tigris* uppermost near the stern for an instant as the vessel sank bow foremost.
