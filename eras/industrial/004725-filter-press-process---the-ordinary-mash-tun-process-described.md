# The Filter Press Process

The filter press process is a method of brewing introduced into Great Britain by the Belgian engineer P. Meura. It was developed to address specific inefficiencies found in the ordinary mash-tun process, particularly regarding the grinding of malt and the extraction of wort.

## Comparison with the Ordinary Mash-Tun Process
In the ordinary mash-tun process, brewers are limited to using coarse grists. This limitation leads to a loss of extract through two primary means. First, the sparging process—the method used to wash out the final portions of the wort—is considered somewhat inefficient. Second, when the malt is "steely" or hard, the conversion of starch is not entirely complete.

The filter press process overcomes these disadvantages by allowing the malt to be ground quite fine. While an ordinary mash-tun may still be utilized for the mashing stage, the actual separation of the solid matter from the clear wort is performed within the filter press. This equipment is capable of retaining the finest particles with ease. Furthermore, washing the wort from the filter cake within the presses is a simple procedure, and experience indicates that this results in markedly increased yields.

## The Mashing Process
Mashing is regarded as the most important of the brewing processes because the character of the beer is largely determined within the mash-tun. The process consists of using water at an adequate temperature to extract soluble matters already present in the malt and to convert insoluble nitrogenous compounds and starch into products that are soluble and partly fermentable.

In modern practice, the "liquor" (water) and the "grist" (crushed malt) are introduced simultaneously into the mash-tun via a mashing machine. This machine is typically a cylindrical metal vessel equipped with a screw and a central shaft. The grist enters from a case above while the liquor is introduced from the back; the rapid rotation of the screw ensures a thorough mixture as the materials travel.

The mash-tun itself is a large wooden or metal vessel. It features a false bottom made of plates with slits or small holes to separate the spent grains from the "wort" (the liquid portion of the finished mash). To ensure the grist and liquor are intimately mixed, the tun is fitted with stirring apparatus known as rakes. To recover the wort remaining in the grains, an automatic sprinkler called a sparger is used. The sparger consists of hollow, perforated arms radiating from a center; as water is admitted, the central vessel rotates, covering the entire tun in a constant fine spray.

## General Brewing Sequence
The filter press process fits into a broader sequence of seven main brewing processes:
1. **Grinding**: Malt is cleaned, graded, and crushed in a malt mill.
2. **Mashing**: Grist and liquor are mixed in the mashing machine and then pass to the mash-tun to render the starch soluble.
3. **Boiling**: Clear wort is passed to a copper to be boiled with hops.
4. **Cooling**: The wort passes through a hop back to separate insoluble hop constituents, then through a cooler and a refrigerator.
5. **Fermenting**: After passing through a collecting vessel for duty assessment by revenue officers, the wort enters fermenting vessels to become "green" beer.
6. **Cleansing**: The green beer is cleansed.
7. **Racking and Storing**: The final stage of the process.

## Technical Considerations in Mashing
The quality and type of beer depend on the mashing temperature and the kind of malt used. For example, English beers generally range from 140° to 160° F. Specific types require different temperatures:
* **Stouts and black beers**: Usually 148° to 150° F.
* **Pale or stock ales**: 150° to 154° F.
* **Mild running beers**: 154° to 149° F.

The temperature affects the enzyme diastase, which converts insoluble starch into soluble sugar and dextrin. Higher temperatures "cripple" the diastase, resulting in more non-fermentable dextrinous matter. Consequently, a "dry" beer that must keep (like a stock ale) requires a high mashing temperature, whereas a sweet mild running ale intended for rapid consumption is produced at lower temperatures.
