# Macaulay, Ward, Hume, and Berkeley

The intellectual and political history of Great Britain has been shaped by a variety of thinkers and statesmen whose contributions range from the foundations of modern idealism and psychological philosophy to the practicalities of parliamentary representation and colonial administration.

## Berkeley and the Foundations of Idealism
Berkeley was a pivotal figure in English philosophy who sought to utilize the conclusions implicit in Locke's starting point to disprove systems that excluded intelligence, design, and freedom from the formation of things, which instead viewed a "stupid, unthinking substance" as the origin of all beings. Berkeley established the position that all knowledge is relative to the subject, asserting that no object can be known except through the forms provided by memory, imagination, notions, inference, and the powers of sense-perception.

This perspective marked a significant step in speculation, effectively excluding ordinary dogmatic dualism from philosophy. Berkeley contended that matter was an abstraction, a view that suggested the entire conception of a world of objects is an inference from subjective modifications, which are the only immediately certain objects of knowledge. In terms of literary style, Berkeley is associated with the "aristocratic style" of 18th-century English prose.

## Hume and the Analysis of Impression
Hume extended Berkeley's contentions, showing that if matter is considered an abstraction, the mind must also be considered one. This line of thinking signaled a school of reaction to "external reality" or common sense. Hume's work highlighted the implications of Berkeley's view, demonstrating that there seemed to be nothing one could confidently affirm except the order of one's own impressions.

In the study of the relation between mental images and sense-impressions, Hume held that the distinction between a percept and an image is one of quality rather than degree. He argued that the distinctive character of a percept is the "liveliness and force" with which it strikes the mind. Literarily, Hume is noted for a "limpid and flowing" manner of prose. Additionally, M.A.S. Hume is recorded as the author of *Great Lord Burghley* (1898), which drew heavily from the author's *Calendar of Simancas MSS*.

## Macaulay and Political Life
Macaulay was a figure of significant literary and political activity in the 19th century. He was returned for Leeds in 1832, largely through the influence of Edward Baines. In his political career, Macaulay maintained a resolution to always provide an independent vote, a stance that meant he could potentially disagree with colleagues and be forced to leave his position.

Macaulay's literary contributions were varied. In 1834, he published an essay regarding a powerful speaker in the House who possessed an intuitive faculty for reply. He also wrote a "brilliant but misleading" essay regarding the biography of Burghley written by Nares. Despite his literary success, Macaulay viewed his writing as a relaxation rather than a means of support, expressing horror at the idea of becoming a "bookseller's hack."

Due to the precarious nature of his income from office and his pen, and the sinking fortunes of his family, Macaulay accepted a seat in the supreme council of India under the new India Act. This office provided a salary of £10,000, and in February 1834, he sailed for Calcutta accompanied by his sister, Hannah.

## Ward and Psychological Distinction
In the analysis of mental processes, Ward is cited regarding the "superior steadiness" of impressions compared to images. This distinction explains why, while observing a set of surroundings, various images of different scenes may pass through the mind, yet the actual scene before the eyes remains immediately distinguished. This steadiness is attributed in part to the fact that perception possesses a clear localization that images lack, as images of even familiar scenes are often inaccurate and vague.
