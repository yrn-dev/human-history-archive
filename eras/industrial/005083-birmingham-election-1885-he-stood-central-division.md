# Birmingham Parliamentary Representation and Development

## Parliamentary Evolution and the 1885 Divisions
Birmingham was first enfranchised in 1832, at which time two representatives were assigned to the city. The first individuals elected to these positions were Joshua Scholefield and Thomas Attwood, both of whom were leaders of the Political Union. The number of assigned members increased to three in 1867.

In 1885, the number of representatives was further increased to seven. To accommodate this expansion, seven corresponding parliamentary divisions were created:
* Central
* Bordesley
* East
* Edgbaston
* North
* South
* West

## Political Figures and Reform
The city's political history is closely linked to the reform movements of the 19th century. John Bright emerged as a popular hero and a recognized leader of the Liberal party in both the country and parliament. In June 1865, following the dissolution of parliament, Bright was returned for Birmingham without opposition. He later supported the government under Lord John Russell, though Russell's fourth Reform Bill was defeated by the Adullamites. Bright subsequently roused the great towns to demand reform, describing the accession of the Derby-Disraeli ministry as a "declaration of war against the working classes."

Following the passage of the Reform Bill, which included a clause for minority representation, Bright was returned for Birmingham again in the autumn of 1868 along with two Liberal colleagues. During this period, he served as the president of the Board of Trade under Mr. Gladstone and became a member of the privy council.

Additionally, Joseph Chamberlain played a significant role in the town's affairs. Serving as mayor from 1873 to 1876, Chamberlain was a principal factor in the foundation of Birmingham University, the Corporation Street improvement, and the municipalization of the water and gas supply.

## Municipal Growth and Geography
The city has experienced rapid population growth over three centuries. In 1700, the population was approximately 15,000; by 1801, it had grown to 73,000. This growth continued into the next century, reaching 478,113 in 1891 and 522,204 by 1901. The total area of the city is 12,639 acres.

Under the Provincial Local Government Board Act of 1891, the city's boundaries were made conterminous for municipal, school board, and parliamentary purposes. This act added four local board districts to the city for local government: Saltley, Harborne (Staffordshire), Balsall Heath (Worcestershire), and the rural hamlet of Little Bromwich (Warwickshire). Although these districts remained in their respective counties for the exercise of freehold votes, they were declared to be in the county of Warwick by the act.

## Urban Structure and Suburbs
Central Birmingham is connected to its suburbs via motor omnibuses and electric tramways operated by the corporation. The residential districts of Harborne and Edgbaston, located south-west of the center and approached via Broad Street, are primarily inhabited by the richer classes and maintain a semi-rural character. This area includes the botanical gardens, Calthorpe Park, the Warwickshire county cricket ground, and the private grounds of Edgbaston Hall.

Other notable districts include:
* **East:** Saltley, Small Heath, Sparkbrook, and Balsall Heath.
* **South-East and East:** The residential suburbs of Moseley and Yardley. Highbury, the seat of Joseph Chamberlain, is located between Moseley and King's Heath.
* **South:** The manufacturing districts of Selly Oak and Bourneville, with Northfield and King's Norton further south in Worcestershire.
* **West:** The borough of Smethwick adjoins the city, with continuous streets extending toward Dudley, Oldbury, and West Bromwich. This area includes Handsworth and Soho.
* **North and North-East:** Aston Manor (a municipal borough) and Perry Bar lie to the north, while a populous district extends toward Sutton Coldfield to the north-east.

## Religious and Cultural Institutions
Birmingham has a long tradition of Nonconformity and Puritanism. The Unitarians, the oldest established body in the city, replaced their original building in 1885 with a handsome structure on Bristol Road known as the Old Meeting. Other significant religious sites include the New Meeting in Moor Street and the church of the Messiah in Broad Street, which contains a monument to Dr. Joseph Priestley.

Other religious presence in the city includes:
* **Wesleyan Methodists:** Established by John Wesley in 1745; they opened a Renaissance-style central hall in 1903.
* **Society of Friends:** Their first meeting-house dates to approximately 1690.
* **Baptists:** First erected a chapel in Cannon Street in 1738.
* **Roman Catholics:** Now a bishopric, featuring the cathedral of St Chad (designed by A.W. Pugin) and the Benedictine Abbey at Erdington.
* **Other:** The city also contains Presbyterian places of worship and a synagogue.

Cultural assets include the Central free library, various other free libraries with reading rooms and lending departments, and the Birmingham library, which was founded in 1798 by Dr. Priestley.
