# Baby-Farming

## Definition and Nature of the Practice
Baby-farming is a term generally used to describe the practice of taking in infants to nurse for payment. The term typically carries an implication of improper treatment. Historically, the practice was often linked to the issue of illegitimacy, as baby-farms frequently accepted children without question in exchange for a lump sum payment. In such instances, it was to the pecuniary advantage of the baby-farmer to hasten the death of the child.

Beyond the care of illegitimate infants, the practice was also utilized by mill-hands and factory operatives who placed their children out by the day. Because these children were often viewed as a drain on resources or a burden, parents did not always inquire closely into the quality of care provided. In some cases, the sums paid for the maintenance of these children were ridiculously insufficient.

## Risks and Health Impacts
A select committee appointed by the House of Commons in 1871 investigated the best means of preventing the destruction of infants put out to nurse for hire. The committee identified several factors that led to the wasting away, convulsions, and diarrhoea of these children, including:
* Wilful neglect
* Bad air and crowded rooms
* Lack of cleanliness
* The use of drugs and opiates
* Improper and insufficient food

Evidence from the Magdalene home and the Foundling hospital suggests that the high mortality rate among these children was partly explained by the fact that they were hand-nursed rather than breast-nursed. In contrast, children nursed by their mothers in the workhouse generally survived.

## Legal Regulations in England and the United States
In England, the findings of the 1871 committee led to the act of 1872. This legislation mandated the compulsory registration of all houses that received more than one child under the age of one year for a period exceeding twenty-four hours. Prior to these regulations, high-profile trials involving Mary Hall and Margaret Waters had drawn attention to the "infamous relations" between baby-farming houses and lying-in houses in London.

In the United States, laws vary by state:
* **Massachusetts:** A law from 1891 requires anyone receiving an infant under three years of age for board or adoption to determine if the child is illegitimate. If the person knows or believes the child is illegitimate, they must notify the State Board of Charity. The board's officers may inspect buildings where such infants are boarded and remove children if neglect, abuse, or other causes make removal necessary to preserve the infant's life.
* **New York:** The state penal code (s. 288, subsec. 4) requires a licence for baby-farming. This licence must be issued by the board of health of the town or city where the children are kept. Licensed individuals are required to maintain a register that includes the names and ages of all children boarded there, children born on the premises, and the names and residences of parents (where known). The register must also track the time of reception, the reasons for discharge, and details regarding any child under five years of age who is indentured, taken away, adopted, or given out, including the residence and name of the adopter.

## Regulations in Australia and New Zealand
Several states in Australia have implemented measures to prevent the evils associated with baby-farming:
* **South Australia:** Under the State Children Act of 1895, the State Children's Council oversees infants under two years who are boarded out by their mothers. Women who take in infants as boarders, known as "foster-mothers," must be licensed. The licence specifies the number of children the foster-mother is authorized to keep. These women must maintain a register detailing the child's name, age, and place of birth, as well as the description, names, and addresses of the parents or other parties involved in the delivery or receipt of the child. The register must also include the date of delivery or receipt, the name of any attending medical practitioner, and particulars of any illness or accident.
* **New South Wales:** The Children's Protection Act of 1892, along with 1902 amendments, requires the licensing of foster-mothers and state supervision of boarding homes.
* **Victoria:** An act was passed in 1890 specifically for the "protection of infant life."

Similar legislation exists in New Zealand, specifically the "Infant Life Protection Act 1896" and the "Adoption of Children Act 1895."
