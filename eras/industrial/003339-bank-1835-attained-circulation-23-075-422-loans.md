# Banking and Financial Institutions in the 19th and Early 20th Centuries

## The Bank of the United States
The Bank of the United States was a national bank of issue established as a core component of the financial system organized by Alexander Hamilton under the 1789 constitution. Incorporated in 1791, the bank began with a capital of $10,000,000, divided into 25,000 shares valued at $400 each. Its primary functions included discounting commercial paper, issuing circulating notes, and assisting the Federal government with financial operations.

The government initially subscribed to one-fifth of the bank's capital through a process that effectively resulted in the bank loaning that amount to the treasury. By the end of 1795, other loans to the government had increased the total obligation to $6,200,000. To settle these debts, the government sold its bank stock, completing the disposal by 1802 for a profit of $671,860. A statement submitted to Congress by Gallatin on January 24, 1811, indicated the bank had resources totaling $24,183,046, consisting of $14,578,294 in loans and discounts, $5,009,567 in specie, and $2,750,000 in United States stock.

By 1835, the bank had reached a circulation of $23,075,422, deposits of $5,061,456, and loans of $59,232,445. The institution was eventually destroyed due to the open enmity of President Jackson. In 1833, Jackson suspended the deposit of public money in the bank's custody, a policy known as the "removal of the deposits." While Clay and Webster led the political opposition to this move, Jackson maintained public support. The bank's Federal charter expired in 1836. President Nicholas Biddle obtained a state charter from Pennsylvania to continue business, but this was unsuccessful, and the bank entered liquidation in 1841.

## State Banking in the United States
Following the decline of the Bank of the United States, banks chartered by separate states served as rivals and successors. Early banking in undeveloped areas of the country was often speculative and unsound. The most successful systems were concentrated in New England and New York, where the country's surplus capital was primarily located. Conversely, banking in poorer and newer sections was the least successful, hampered by inexperience and a lack of capital for tools of exchange.

Before the Civil War, the power of note-issue was more critical than the deposit system, which was not yet fully developed. In 1835, the combined capital and circulation of state banks was approximately $335,000,000, while deposits were only $83,000,000. By 1907, this ratio had shifted significantly; national banks had deposits of $4,322,000,000 compared to $1,430,000,000 in circulation and capital.

State bank circulation fluctuated over time, reaching a maximum of $149,185,890 in 1837, dropping to a minimum of $58,563,608 in 1843, and rising again to $238,677,218 by 1863. Between 1835 and 1863, the number of state banks grew from 704 to 1,466. During this period, capital stock rose from $231,250,337 to $405,045,829, and deposits increased from $83,081,365 to $393,686,226.

## Regional and International Banking Examples
In some regions, state-led financial policies proved unsuccessful. One state legislature chartered a bank in 1819 based on state credit; it failed, and the state borrowed money in 1831 to redeem its depreciated notes. A second state bank chartered in 1835 suspended payment two years later and was liquidated in 1843. This state also attempted internal improvements, including a loan for the Illinois and Michigan canal in 1836 and a $10,000,000 appropriation for railroads and other improvements in 1837. These efforts failed, leading to a heavy debt that was not practically extinguished until 1880.

In Ireland, the limit of authorized circulation established by Peel's Act of 1845 was maintained. As of 1906, several joint-stock banks operated there, including the Bank of Ireland (est. 1783), the National Bank (est. 1835), and the Ulster Banking Co. (est. 1836).

In Austria-Hungary, the monetary system transitioned toward the krone in 1892, with the krone divided into 100 heller. To facilitate this, the bank was required to buy gold and coin it into ten- or twenty-krone pieces. A loan of 150 million gulden at 4% was arranged through Rothschild, providing the gold necessary to strike over 34 million kronen, which were used to pay off *Staatsnoten*.

## Modern Money Markets and War Loans
By the late 19th century, the London money market evolved. Walter Bagehot's 1873 work *Lombard Street* described a system where discount houses quoted "allowance" rates for loans at call and short notice. By 1888 or 1889, bill-brokers began allowing rates for "money for the night." This fugitive money often came from temporary balances held for foreign governments or subscriptions to public loans.

Modern methods of managing market disturbances were exemplified by Japanese war loans in 1904, 1905, and 1906. These were issued by three banks: a Japanese institution representing the government, a leading London bank, and the principal British bank operating in China. To minimize market disturbance, "application money" deposited by investors was lent back into the short loan market almost immediately. The Japanese government managed its resulting huge balances considerately, transferring a portion of the funds to the Bank of England.
