# Scotland and Ireland

## Banking Legislation and Note Issue
Under the acts of 1844-1845, Sir Robert Peel established a banking policy for Scotland and Ireland that differed from the policy implemented in England. In these two countries, banks were permitted to exceed their authorized note issues, provided they held specie (gold) equal to the amount of the excess. Additionally, no restrictions were placed on bank amalgamations in Scotland and Ireland, and notes for amounts less than £5 continued to be allowed.

The purpose of requiring banks to hold specie against notes issued in excess of the legal limit was to ensure that the country's circulating medium—composed of both notes and specie—would fluctuate in the same manner as a purely metallic circulation. Sir Robert Peel stated in a speech on May 6, 1844, that this legislation was intended to "ensure the uniform equivalency of bank notes to coin." According to a speech delivered on April 25, 1845, the specific amount of specie to be held was determined by the average amount of the note-issue for the preceding four weeks. This specie was placed in the banks for this specific purpose rather than serving as a special security for the note circulation.

## Impact on the Scottish Banking System
The limitation of note-issuing power to those Scottish banks that already possessed it in 1845 had a significant effect: no important bank has been established in Scotland since that time. Because notes are so extensively used in ordinary business in Scotland, banks without the power to issue them cannot practically supply the needs of their customers or carry on business. 

Despite this limitation on the number of banks, there has been no deficiency in banking accommodation for the population. In fact, Scotland has a larger number of banking offices in proportion to its population than Ireland, England, or Wales. Currently, ten large banks in Scotland possess the power to issue notes, a capability that has greatly assisted the large proportion of local branches in the country.

## Irish Banking and Circulation
In Ireland, six of the nine banks possess the power to issue notes. The Irish banking landscape includes the Bank of Ireland and various private and joint-stock banks. In one recorded instance, the Bank of Ireland held £3,597,850 and private and joint-stock banks held £2,456,261, totaling £37,276,254.

By May 1907, the figures for Irish banks were reduced. Twelve private banks had an authorized issue of £482,744 but an actual issue of only £122,536. Seventeen joint-stock banks had an authorized issue of £1,084,836 and an actual issue of £437,693. The disparity between authorized and actual circulation exists because the circulation of these banks is limited to very small areas. Notes from country banks are almost unknown outside the immediate neighborhood of their issuance; while they may be payable in London, cashing them often presents considerable difficulty.

## Comparative Monetary Statistics
The average circulation for the United Kingdom in 1906 was £43,372,000, with Scotland accounting for £7,477,000 and Ireland accounting for £6,452,000 (compared to £29,443,000 in England). Other figures indicate that Scotland's note circulation reached £135,042,000 and Ireland's reached £73,707,000, while the Isle of Man had £898,000.

## Agriculture and Natural History
### Fruit Culture in Ireland
According to data from the Irish Board of Agriculture through the end of 1907, Ireland's fruit culture is divided between orchard and small fruits. Orchard fruit totals 6,543 statute acres, dominated by apples (5,829 acres), followed by pears (224), plums (223), damsons (138), and other kinds (129). While Ireland grows 41 times fewer pears and 70 times fewer plums than England, it is ahead of Scotland and Wales in pear production. In apple production, Ireland grows about one-thirty-third the quantity of England, but it is approximately 2,000 acres ahead of Wales and nearly 5,000 acres ahead of Scotland.

Small fruit culture totals 4,906 acres, including strawberries (994), gooseberries (675), raspberries (374), black currants (234), red and white currants (159), and mixed fruit (2,470). Ireland is far behind Scotland in the culture of raspberries and strawberries, though it is close in the production of gooseberries and currants. There are over 62,000 holdings in Ireland between 1 and 5 acres, totaling 224,000 acres.

### Botany and Mineralogy
The buckthorn shrub (*Rhamnus cathartica*), which is native to England, is not wild in Scotland and is rare in Ireland. 

In terms of mineralogy, Scotland's Glencoe region is the source of Withamite, a variety of the mineral Epidote. Withamite is characterized by a straw-yellow to carmine-red color and is strongly pleochroic.
