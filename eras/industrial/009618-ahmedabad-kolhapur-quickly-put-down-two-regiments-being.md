# Ahmedabad and Kolhapur

Ahmedabad and Kolhapur are significant locations within the Bombay Presidency of British India. While Ahmedabad serves as a major industrial and administrative district in the northern Gujarat division, Kolhapur is a prominent native state located within the Deccan division.

## Ahmedabad: Industry and Administration
Ahmedabad is one of the twenty-six districts of the Bombay Presidency. It is a center of significant industrial development, particularly in the growth and manufacture of cotton, where large steam mills have been established. The city is also known for the manufacture of silk goods and is famous for its carved wood-work, with many houses featuring elaborate carvings.

The district is also a site for government salt works at Udu and Kharaghoda, which export salt by rail to central India and Gujarat. In terms of infrastructure, the railway line passing through the native state of Idar—located 64 miles northeast of Ahmedabad—runs through Ahmedabad via Parantij.

## Kolhapur: The Native State
Kolhapur is a native state in the Deccan division of the Bombay Presidency and is the principal state under the political control of the Bombay government. It is ranked as the fourth most important of the Mahratta principalities, following Baroda, Gwalior, and Indore. The state covers an area of 3,165 square miles, including its feudatories or *jagirs*, and had a population of 910,011 in 1901.

The geography of Kolhapur ranges from the Western Ghats in the west—characterized by wild hill slopes, valleys, and rich forests—to a well-cultivated, treeless plain in the east. The western hills contain ancient Mahratta strongholds, including Rungna, Bavda, Vishalgarh, and Panhala. The state's economy is supported by stone quarries and the production of agricultural goods such as tobacco, cotton, safflower, vegetables, millets, rice, and sugar-cane. Iron ore is found in the hills, though local smelting has declined due to competition from European imports.

The rajas of Kolhapur are descended from Raja Ram, a younger son of Sivaji the Great. Historically, the British government conducted expeditions against Kolhapur in 1792 and 1765 due to the prevalence of piracy. In the early 19th century, military operations and the appointment of a British management officer were necessitated by the misgovernment of the chief. In more recent years, the state has been governed on the pattern of British administration under Raja Shahu Chhatrapati, who succeeded to the throne by adoption in 1884. The state's institutions include a technical school, an agricultural school, a high school, Rajaram college, and training schools for mistresses and masters.

The town of Kolhapur (also known as Karvir) serves as the terminus for a branch of the Southern Mahratta railway. As of 1901, the town had a population of 54,373 and contains numerous Buddhist remains, indicating its history as an important religious center.

## Regional Stability and Crisis
The Bombay Presidency experienced various periods of unrest and health crises. During the tenure of Lord Elphinstone (1853–1860), the presidency generally avoided a general rising during the Mutiny, although local and isolated rebellions occurred in the southern Mahratta country and Gujarat among the Bhils. Specifically, outbreaks among the troops in Kolhapur, Ahmedabad, and Karachi were quickly suppressed, resulting in the disbanding of two regiments.

In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, both Ahmedabad and Kolhapur were affected by broader regional crises. The bubonic plague, which first appeared in Bombay City in September 1896, spread to the surrounding country. While some districts in Gujarat escaped, the mortality rate was very heavy in the native state of Kolhapur. Additionally, the state of Kolhapur has suffered from both plague and famine in recent years.
