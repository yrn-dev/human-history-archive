# Sind-Pishin Railway

The Sind-Pishin railway is a strategic rail system connecting the plains of Sind to the central plateau of Baluchistan. It serves as a primary link between Jacobabad and Sibi and the city of Quetta, traversing a region that has historically been significant for British campaigns in Afghanistan.

## Route and Alignment
The railway originates in the plains and ascends to the plateau. From Sibi, the line initially ran south-west, skirting the hills to Rindli and following the course of the Bolan stream to its head on the plateau. However, this original alignment was abandoned due to the destructive effects of floods. The current alignment follows the Mashkaf valley, which debouches into the plains near Sibi. From the head of the Mashkaf, the line is carried to a junction with the Bolan at Mach.

An alternative route from Sibi to Quetta exists via the Harnai valley, located to the north-east of Sibi. This line enters the hills at Nari, running in the opposite direction to the Bolan route. While the Harnai route is longer, it is utilized for all ordinary traffic, while the Bolan loop is reserved for emergency use.

## Geography and Environmental Challenges
The railway traverses a diverse and often hostile landscape. The approach to Baluchistan is facilitated by Kach Gandava, an alluvial bay extending 130 meters from the Indus to Sibi. This area is characterized by a climate that prevents European occupation, featuring poisonous winds, dust-storms, and extreme temperatures that never sink below 100°F in summer and drop below freezing in winter.

The railway's paths are dictated by the drainage basins of the Bolan and Nari, which drain south-eastwards from the Quetta-Pishin plateau. Both routes are susceptible to terrific floods caused by torrents of rainfall from narrow valleys with bare, flat-backed limestone slopes. 

On the Bolan route, the railway encounters significant geological obstacles. At the Khundilani gorge, the valley is enclosed by conglomerate cliffs rising to 800 feet. At Sir-i-Bolan, the passage between limestone rocks is so narrow that it hardly admits three persons riding abreast. The pass is subject to extreme weather, with very high summer temperatures and extreme winter cold, including destructive ice-cold winds.

## Strategic and Regional Impact
The Sind-Pishin railway acts as a geographical divider within Baluchistan. To the north of the line lie the highlands of the Suliman system, primarily the home of the Pathan highlander. To the south lies a block of territory roughly 300 meters by 300 meters, primarily inhabited by the Baluch and Brahui.

The railway has also had a stabilizing effect on the local population. The Bugti tribe, a Baluch tribe of Rind (Arab) origin numbering about 15,500, occupies the hills between Jacobabad and Sibi. Historically known as hereditary robbers, the Bugtis have become fairly orderly since the construction of the railway, which completely outflanks their country. Similarly, since the founding of the Quetta agency in 1877, the pass has been secured from the plundering bands of Baluch marauders, specifically the Marris.

## Terminus and Surroundings
The railway leads to Quetta, which sits 5,500 feet above sea level on an open plain formed by high-level tributaries of the Lora. Quetta is situated on the central dominant water-divide of Baluchistan and serves as the base of the Kandahar highway. The city is overlooked by several grand peaks and precipices ranging from 10,500 to 11,500 feet in height, including:
* **Khalifat** to the east (flanking the Harnai loop)
* **Takatu** to the north
* **Chahiltan (Chiltan)** to the south-west
* **Murdar** to the south
