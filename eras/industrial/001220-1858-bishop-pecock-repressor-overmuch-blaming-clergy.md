# Bishop Pecock's Repressor of Overmuch Blaming of the Clergy

Bishop Pecock's *Repressor of Overmuch Blaming of the Clergy* is a significant work of 15th-century English prose. Written by Reginald Pecock (c. 1395–c. 1460), the text is noted for its logical structure and its place within the transition of the English language.

## Author and Intellectual Style
Reginald Pecock was characterized as a man possessing a singularly acute and logical mind. He placed great value on his faculty for discovering arguments that others had overlooked and took pride in his dialectic skill. These intellectual traits are reflected in the *Repressor*, which is noted for its general arrangement and structure. Pecock presented his ideas simply and clearly, avoiding excessive excrescences or digressions. While his sentences were occasionally too long, his prose style is described as being more similar to modern prose than any other writing produced before the age of Elizabeth. To lighten his style, Pecock frequently employed illustrative figures of speech to clarify his ideas.

## Linguistic Significance
The *Repressor* serves as a primary example of 15th-century English prose, a period generally considered barren in literature and a time of linguistic transition. During this era, the technique of prose composition had received little serious consideration, and it is doubtful whether prose was viewed as a medium for artistic expression. Most prose of the time was written for educational or religious purposes.

Pecock is one of only five writers from this century deemed noteworthy by archival sources. His writings, specifically those produced around 1450 against the Wycliffites, provide evidence of the evolution of the English language. In these texts, verbal inflections in *-en* were becoming obsolete. Additionally, Pecock utilized southern pronouns, such as *her* and *hem*, rather than the northern equivalents *their* and *them*.

## Editorial History
In 1860, the *Repressor of Overmuch Blaming of the Clergy* was edited by Dr. Babington. This editorial project was undertaken by Babington at the request of the Master of the Rolls. Dr. Babington was a versatile scholar known for his work in botany, ornithology, and Greek scholarship, as well as his editions of other historical texts, such as the first two volumes of Higden's *Polychronicon* (1858).
