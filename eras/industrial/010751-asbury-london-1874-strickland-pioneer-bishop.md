# Francis Asbury

## Early Life and Conversion
Francis Asbury was an American clergyman born on August 20, 1745, at Hamstead Bridge in the parish of Handsworth, near Birmingham, in Staffordshire, England. Born to poor parents, Asbury attended the village school of Barre for a brief period of study. At the age of fourteen, he was apprenticed to a maker of tongues, also known as "buckle chapes." 

Asbury became converted to Methodism during his thirteenth year, and it is considered probable that his parents were among the early converts of Wesley. By the age of sixteen, he had become a local preacher. Described as a fluent and simple speaker, his success led John Wesley to enroll him as a regular itinerant minister in 1767.

## Missionary Work in America
In 1771, Asbury volunteered for missionary work in the American colonies, arriving in Philadelphia in October of that year. At the time of his arrival, Methodism had been introduced to the colonies for only three years, and the number of converts was fewer than 300. Asbury's efforts infused new life into the movement, and within one year, the membership of the various congregations more than doubled. 

In 1772, Wesley appointed Asbury as "general assistant" to oversee the work in America. Although Thomas Rankin, an older preacher, superseded him in 1773, Asbury remained practically in control of the operations.

## The War of Independence and Exile
During the War of Independence, Methodists numbered several thousand but were unjustly suspected of Loyalism. This suspicion arose primarily from their refusal to take the prescribed oath. While many ministers, including Thomas Rankin, returned to England, Asbury remained in the colonies due to his sense of duty and sympathy. Despite being threatened and arrested once, he continued his itinerant preaching. Eventually, hostility from the authorities in Maryland forced Asbury into a two-year exile in Delaware, where he remained active though quiet.

## Leadership and Episcopacy
In 1782, Asbury was reappointed to supervise the affairs of Methodist congregations in America. In 1784, John Wesley took the radical step of disregarding the authority of the Established Church by appointing Francis Asbury and the Rev. Thomas Coke as "bishops" or superintendents of the church in the United States. In his capacity as Bishop, Asbury ordained others, including Peter Cartwright in 1806. Asbury died in 1816.

## Legacy and Commemorations
The life and work of Francis Asbury have been documented in several historical accounts and biographies, including:
* *The Pioneer Bishop; or, The Life and Times of Francis Asbury* by W.P. Strickland (New York, 1858).
* *Bishop Asbury* by F.W. Briggs (London, 1874).
* *Asbury and His Co-Laborers* (2 vols.) by W.C. Larrabee (Cincinnati, 1853).
* *Heroes of Methodism* by J.B. Wakeley (New York, 1856).
* *Francis Asbury* by H.M. Du Bose (Nashville, Tenn., 1909).

Additionally, Asbury's own *Journals* (3 vols., New York, 1852) serve as a history of his life work and a commentary on the industrial and social history of the United States during its first forty years.

His name is preserved in various American institutions and locations:
* **Asbury Park**: A city in Monmouth county, New Jersey, founded in 1869 in honor of the Rev. Francis Asbury. It was incorporated as a borough in 1874 and chartered as a city in 1897.
* **De Pauw University**: A Methodist Episcopal institution in Greencastle, Indiana, which was founded in 1837 as Indiana Asbury University before being renamed in 1884. The university includes the Asbury College of Liberal Arts.
