# Military and Political Developments in Austria-Hungary (1868–1900)

## Military Organization and Expansion
During the late 19th century, Austria sought to strengthen its military resources in response to a disturbed European political climate and the growth of military establishments in other nations. Despite financial difficulties that limited the scale of these measures, compulsory military service was introduced in 1868 for both Hungary and Austria. The total army available for wartime was set at 800,000 men.

The military structure consisted of a joint army under a joint ministry of war, supplemented by separate militias and ministers for national defence in each part of the monarchy. In Hungary, this national force was known as the *honved* and remained distinct from the ordinary army. In Austria, the *Landwehr* was organized as part of the standing army, with the exception of the Tirol and Dalmatia, which maintained separate local militias.

## Structural Reforms and Mobilization
The military underwent several reorganizations to improve efficiency and mobilization:
* **1882:** Compulsory service was extended to include Bosnia and Herzegovina. The army adopted a local organization and army corps based on the Prussian plan, which aided speedy mobilization, though it created a risk that national or local spirits might enter the army.
* **1886:** A law established the *Landsturm*, which provided for the organization and arming of the entire male population up to age forty-two during emergencies.
* **1889 and 1892-1893:** The annual number of recruits was increased.

Despite these changes, the overall increase in the army establishment of Austria-Hungary remained small compared to other continental powers.

## Language and National Tension
A significant point of contention within the monarchy was the use of German as the common language for the entire army. The government and the monarch viewed this as a vital point of unity and a remaining remnant of German ascendancy, refusing to grant concessions on the matter. However, there were signs in recent years of attempts to tamper with this requirement, particularly through protests in the Hungarian parliament.

## Political Instability and the Reichsrath
The political environment was characterized by conflict between German and Czech factions within the Reichsrath. A primary source of dispute was Paragraph 14 of the Constitutional law of 1867. This clause allowed the emperor to proclaim orders in cases of pressing necessity if the Reichsrath was not in session, provided they were signed by the whole ministry and presented to the Reichsrath within four months.

Germans contended that applying this clause to the *Ausgleich* (the periodical financial and economic settlement) was invalid. This dispute led to the retirement of Thun in September 1899. His successor, Count Clary, attempted to restore peace by withdrawing the offending ordinances. However, Clary was forced to resign after less than three months because the Czechs used obstruction to prevent the passage of an excise law necessary for agreements with Hungary, making it impossible for him to govern without breaking a pledge not to use Paragraph 14.

Following Clary's resignation, the emperor appointed a ministry of officials who utilized Paragraph 14 for state purposes before making way for a ministry under Herr v. Koerber.

## Civil Unrest in Bohemia
In the early months of 1900, conditions were initially more peaceful under Koerber. However, tensions resurfaced as the Czechs demanded the restoration of their language within the internal service of Bohemia. On June 8, 1900, noise and disturbance were used to oblige the president to suspend proceedings.
