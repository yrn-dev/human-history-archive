# Pierre Simon Ballanche

Pierre Simon Ballanche (1776–1847) was a French philosopher associated with the theocratic school. Born in Lyons, Ballanche was described as naturally highly-strung and delicate. His early life was marked by profound emotional distress caused by the horrors of the siege of Lyons and an unsuccessful love affair, the latter of which he faced with fortitude.

## Personal Characteristics and Intellectual Style
As a man, Ballanche was characterized as enthusiastic and warm-hearted. However, he was endowed with a too-vivid imagination, which resulted in strange thoughts expressed through bizarre language. Because of this, providing a connected account of his views is considered difficult. It is suggested that his theories should be studied in relation to his life history, as the stages of his life curiously parallel his theory regarding the progress of man: the fall, the trial, and the perfection.

## Philosophical Framework
Ballanche belonged to the theocratic school of thought. This school stood in opposition to the rationalism of the previous age, emphasizing the principle of authority and placing order and revelation above progress, freedom, and individual reason. Ballanche attempted to create a system that united the valuable elements of these opposing modes of thinking.

He shared several core beliefs with other theocratists:
* **Society and Individualism:** He believed individualism was an impracticable view, asserting that man exists only through and in society.
* **Divine Origin:** He held that the origin of society was not the result of human effort or desire, but rather a direct revelation from God.
* **Language:** Following De Bonald, Ballanche reduced the problem of the origin of society to the origin of language, which he viewed as a divine gift.

## Theory of Progress and Palingenesis
While Ballanche agreed with the theocratists on the divine origin of language, he diverged by finding a "germ of progress" within that revelation. He theorized that in the primitive state of man, thought and speech were identical. Over time, these two separated as language evolved from being only spoken to being written and eventually printed. 

This separation broke the primitive unity and the original social order. Consequently, new institutions emerged. Ballanche believed that through these institutions, thought could draw closer to a final unity, a process he termed *palingenesis*. He posited that the volition of primitive man was once one with the volition of God, but later broke into separate volitions that opposed the divine will. Through the trials and oppositions of the world, these volitions work toward a second, more complete harmony.

According to Ballanche, humanity passes through three distinct stages:
1. The fall from perfection.
2. The period of trial.
3. The final return to perfection or re-birth.

He believed the obscure outlines of the fall and primitive society could be traced in mythical records. In actual history, he saw the conflict of two great principles, exemplified by the Roman plebeians and patricians. Ballanche regarded this distinction of caste as the original state of historical society.

## Major Works and Recognition
Ballanche's primary work is the *Palingenesie*, which was designed in three parts:
* ***L'orphee***: This completed section deals with the prehistoric period of the world before religion arose.
* ***La formule***: An intended effort to deduce a universal law from historical facts (exists only in fragments).
* ***La ville des expiations***: A sketch of the ultimate state of perfection toward which humanity moves (exists only in fragments).

The general introduction to the *Palingenesie* is considered perhaps the most valuable part of the work. His final work, *Vision d'Hebal*, was intended as part of *La ville des expiations*. It features the chief of a Scottish clan who possesses second sight and provides semi-prophetic utterances regarding the course of world history.

In 1830, a collected edition of his works was begun in nine volumes, though only four were published. A second edition consisting of six volumes was published in 1833. In 1841, Ballanche was elected to the French Academy. He died in 1847.
