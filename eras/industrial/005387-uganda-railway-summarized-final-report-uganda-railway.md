# Uganda Railway

The Uganda Railway was a major engineering project undertaken by the British government to connect the Indian Ocean coast at Mombasa to Victoria Nyanza. The construction of the line was a central focus of the administration from 1896 until its practical completion in 1903.

## Origins and Purpose
The impetus for the railway grew from a desire to open up the interior of the country and suppress the slave trade. In July 1890, representatives of various powers met in Brussels and agreed to common efforts to end the slave trade; the construction of a railway from Mombasa to Victoria Nyanza was seen as a material step toward this objective. 

Initially, the Imperial British East Africa Company sought imperial aid for the project because its own resources were inadequate. While a preliminary grant for a survey was nearly agreed upon and the company's efforts were praised by Prime Minister Lord Salisbury, the initial scheme failed in parliament. Subsequently, the railway was built entirely at the cost of the British government.

## Technical Specifications and Route
The railway is a mountain line spanning 584 miles with a metre (3.28 ft.) gauge, distinguishing it from the 3 ft. 6 in. gauge used for the South, Central African, and Sudan lines. The line features steep gradients of one in fifty and one in sixty.

The route begins at Mombasa, crossing to the mainland via a half-mile bridge. The terminus at Mombasa is located at the harbour of Kilindini. From there, the line ascends the plateau to the Kikuyu Escarpment, reaching an altitude of 7,600 feet above sea level at a distance of 346 miles from the start. The railway then descends across ravines using viaducts to reach the valley floor at 6,011 feet before ascending the Mau escarpment. The highest point of the line is the summit of the Mau escarpment at 8,321 feet. In the final 100 miles, the elevation drops to 3,738 feet at the station at Port Florence on the Kavirondo Gulf of Victoria Nyanza.

## Construction and Cost
A preliminary survey for the line was executed in 1892, and construction began in 1896. The project was managed by engineer Sir George Whitehouse, who remained in charge until his resignation in March 1903. The first locomotive reached Victoria Nyanza on December 26, 1901.

The total cost of construction was £5,331,000, which averages to approximately £9,500 per mile.

## Impact and Development
The completion of the railway had significant economic and social effects on the region:

*   **End of Slave Trade:** By eliminating the need for the carriage of goods by men, the railway dealt a final death-blow to the slave trade in that part of East Africa.
*   **Occupation of Uganda:** Prior to the railway, the cost of transporting goods from the coast was prohibitive at £60 per ton. The railway facilitated the continued development and occupation of Uganda, which had previously been an almost impossible task.
*   **European Colonization:** As the line reached high plateaus, the administration discovered sparsely populated areas with excellent climates. These conditions, combined with the new transport facilities, made it practicable to open the country to European settlers. This led to land applications, such as one in April 1902 by the East Africa Syndicate for 500 square miles, as well as proposals for a large Jewish settlement.
*   **Urban Growth:** Several flourishing towns developed along the railway. The most significant was Nairobi, located 327 miles from Mombasa and 257 miles from Port Florence; it was chosen as the administrative capital of the protectorate in 1907.
*   **Revenue:** The railway contributed to a substantial increase in trade and government revenue. When railway figures were first included in the protectorate's totals for 1906-1907, revenue rose to £461,362.
