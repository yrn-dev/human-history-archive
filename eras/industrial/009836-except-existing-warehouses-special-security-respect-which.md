# Warehouses

Warehouses serve various functions across different historical and geographical contexts, ranging from state-regulated facilities for the collection of customs duties to large-scale industrial storage and specialized residential or commercial structures.

## Bonded Warehouses
A bonded warehouse is a facility established by private enterprise or the state where goods liable to duty are stored until the required duty has been paid. This system was developed to address several inconveniences associated with the previous English system, which required duties to be paid immediately upon importation or via a bond with security. Under the old system, importers often struggled to find sureties or were forced to sell goods quickly during market depressions to raise the necessary funds. Additionally, the requirement for lump-sum payments increased the price of goods due to interest on the capital required, and the high capital needed for heavily taxed articles stifled competition. There were also difficulties in granting exact equivalent drawbacks to exporters for goods that had already paid duty.

To prevent revenue frauds and resolve these issues, Sir Robert Walpole proposed a warehousing system for wine and tobacco in his 1733 "excise scheme." Although initially unpopular, the system was adopted in 1803. Under an act of that year, imported goods were placed in warehouses approved by customs authorities, and importers provided "bonds" for payment upon the removal of the goods, giving rise to the term "bonded." 

The Customs Consolidation Act 1853 removed the requirement for giving bonds and established provisions for securing customs duties, which were later contained in the Customs Consolidation Act 1876, the Customs and Inland Revenue Act 1880, and the Revenue Act 1883. These facilities are known as "king's warehouses" and are defined by the 1876 act as any place approved by the commissioners of customs or provided by the crown for the security of goods and their due duties. While goods are "in bond," owners may perform processes to prepare them for market, including the roasting of coffee, the manufacture of certain tobacco, the repacking and mixing of tea, and the bottling, mixing, vatting, and racking of spirits and wines. Specific allowances are made for leakage, evaporation, and waste resulting from these processes.

## Industrial and Port Warehousing
In Birkenhead, which evolved from a commercial offshoot of Liverpool, huge sheds and warehouses were built along the quays to store freight. The Birkenhead Dock Warehousing Company opened its first warehouse in 1847, which had a capacity of 80,000 tons of goods. The area includes "corn warehouses" traversed by a canal and equipped with mechanical grain-elevators, as well as cold storage for dead meat and extensive lairages for live-stock. These properties currently fall under the authority of the Mersey Docks and Harbour Board.

In the context of international agreements following world war events, Germany was required to maintain existing free zones in its ports as of August 1, 1914. These zones were to be maintained with shipping, packing, and warehouse facilities without discrimination. Charges in these zones were limited to the expenses of use and administration. Goods brought into these free zones from Germany, or leaving them for German consumption, remained subject to ordinary export and import taxes.

## Legal and Regulatory Oversight
Historically, certain organizations held the power to inspect warehouses to ensure quality and prevent fraud. In London, the Skinners' Company—an association of furriers and skin dressers established by royal charter under Edward III—initially focused on preventing sellers from passing off old furs as new. A century later, the company received charters granting them the power to inspect workrooms, open markets, and warehouses.

## Warehouses in Japan
In historical Japan, warehouses were regarded as distinct from the land on which they stood. While the transfer of building-land (takuchi) required official approval, warehouses and dwellings could be disposed of at pleasure. This differed from the tenure of uplands (onchi), which were held in perpetuity by families provided they planted a specific number of lacquer and mulberry trees according to the grade of the tenant family.
