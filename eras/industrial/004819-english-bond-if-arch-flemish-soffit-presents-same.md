# English Bond and Brickwork Arches

In brick masonry, the term "bond" refers to the arrangement of bricks within a wall or arch. Among the various methods of bonding, English bond and Flemish bond are two primary techniques used to ensure structural integrity and aesthetic appeal.

## English Bond
English bond is regarded as the most perfect bond in use. It is characterized by the arrangement of bricks in alternate courses of headers (bricks laid with their ends to the front) and stretchers (bricks laid with their long sides to the front). This method combines the advantages of both types of arrangements to provide the greatest degree of strength and compactness. Consequently, English bond is the preferred choice for engineering works and buildings erected for manufacturing purposes.

In walls that are a multiple of a whole brick in thickness, the front and back elevations of the same course appear similar. However, in walls where an odd half-brick is required for thickness, the appearance of opposite sides of a course is inverted. To initiate the regular bond near a quoin or vertical end, "queen closers"—portions of a brick equal to a half-header in width—are placed next to the first header. This practice is preferred over using a three-quarter brick to maintain uniformity of appearance and keep whole bricks on the returns.

## Flemish Bond and Variations
Flemish bond differs from English bond by placing headers and stretchers alternately within the same course, resulting in a consistent appearance on each face. This variety requires the use of closers. Because Flemish bond involves a higher number of joints and a comparatively weaker transverse tie, it is not as strong or perfect as English bond. However, because the face joints present a neater appearance, it is generally selected for the external walls of domestic and other buildings where a good visual effect is desired.

Certain variations of this bond exist:
*   **Single Flemish Bond:** A compromise where bricks are laid as Flemish bond on the face but as English bond in the backing. This combines the aesthetic benefits of the former with the structural benefits of the latter and removes the need for bats in the interior of the wall.
*   **Other Bonds:** "Stretching bond," used only for walls half a brick thick, consists entirely of stretchers. "Heading bond," used for footings, corbels, projecting mouldings, and work curved too sharply for stretchers, consists entirely of headers.
*   **Interior Bonding:** In thick walls (three bricks thick or more), "diagonal bond" or "herring bone" may be used in the interior to save labor without losing strength, provided the wall is at least 27 inches thick.

## Brick Arches and the Soffit
The soffit is the underside of an arch. In architecture, a "caisson" may refer to a coffer or sunk panel located in the soffit of an arch or vault. The name of an arch's bond is derived from the arrangement of headers and stretchers on its soffit. Therefore, an arch built in English bond will show the same arrangement on its underside as a wall built in English bond. Similarly, an arch built in Flemish bond will have a soffit that presents the same appearance as the elevation of a Flemish bond wall.

Brick arches are classified into three types:
1.  **Plain Arches:** Built of uncut bricks where the wedge shape is created through mortar, resulting in wedge-shaped joints. To ensure strength, these are often built in independent rings of half-brick thickness.
2.  **Rough-cut Arches:** Bricks are cut with an axe into a wedge form, used where a strong arch with a neat appearance is required.
3.  **Gauged Arches:** Composed of soft bricks cut and rubbed to templates to form perfectly fitting voussoirs. These are set in lime putty with joints no wider than 1/32 inch. For stability, the sides of the voussoirs are gauged hollow and grouted with Portland cement to create joggle joints.

## Construction and Support
Arches are supported during construction by "centering." A "turning piece" is a specific type of centering used for turning an arch over; it is 4-1/2 inches wide on the bed or soffit. For spans up to 15 feet, thin ribs are used; for spans above 15 feet, ribs of 4 inches or more are required. Laggings—small strips of wood—are nailed to the ribs to create the building surface. For gauged arches, laggings are nailed close together and planed off.

In some instances, lintels made of stone, iron, steel, or concrete are used over square-headed openings instead of arches. Unless these lintels are made of steel or reinforced concrete, they should have relieving arches turned immediately over them.
