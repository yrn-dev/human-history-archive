# Impact of Railway Development on Urban and Regional Growth

The expansion of railway networks has served as a primary catalyst for economic flourishing, population growth, and the opening of new territories across various global regions. From the development of colonial protectorates to the expansion of established cities, the introduction of rail transport has frequently altered the commercial and social trajectory of a location.

## Regional Development in India and Asia

In British India, the town and district of Bahraich, located in the Fyzabad division of the United Provinces on the river Sarju, experienced a period of flourishing following the opening of the railway. Bahraich is notable for containing the tomb of Masaud, a champion of Islam killed in battle by confederate Rajputs in 1033, a site visited by both Hindus and Mahommedans. The town also houses a Mussulman monastery, the ruins of a nawab of Oudh's palace, and an American Methodist mission. As of 1901, its population was 27,304.

Similarly, the city of Benares maintains a flourishing trade with its surrounding country via both river and rail. It serves as a junction for the East Indian and Oudh & Rohilkhand railways, utilizing a steel girder bridge over the Ganges consisting of seven spans, each 350 feet in length. Benares is known for manufactures such as gold filigree, silk brocades, gold and silver thread, lacquered toys, and embossed brass vessels, though the archive notes that its famous brasswork has greatly degenerated.

In Japan, the town of Fukui in the province of Echizen has achieved a flourishing condition with a population exceeding 50,000 and several local industries, most notably paper manufacture. This revival followed severe disturbances in 1891-1892, during which a chasm over 40 meters long opened across the Neo valley. Fukui is currently served by railway communication. Additionally, Fukuoka in the province of Mutsu (North Nippon) serves as a railway station on the main line connecting Tokyo to Aimori Ura Bay.

## Infrastructure and Urban Expansion in the West

In the United States, Los Angeles has seen rapid population growth, rising from 11,183 in 1880 to 319,198 by 1910. The city is served by multiple railway systems, including the Atchison, Topeka & Santa Fé, the Southern Pacific, and the San Pedro, Los Angeles & Salt Lake railways. Urban mobility is further supported by five systems of electric railways—with 300 meters of track within the city and 700 meters within a 30-meter radius—as well as subways extending to the western limits and inclined railways ascending Court Street Hill and Third Street Hill.

In Europe, the Peiraeus, which had been a small fishing village known as Porto Leone after its destruction by the Romans in 86 B.C., was revived after Athens became the capital in 1833. Now one of the most flourishing commercial towns in the Levant, the Peiraeus is connected by railway to the principal towns of the Morea and Corinth. The archive notes that the town's importance will be further enhanced once a proposed connection with the Continental railway system and northern Greece and Thessaly is completed.

In Germany, the city of Bonn has seen the removal of the Sterntor to improve communication with the terminus of the light railway to Cologne and the rapidly increasing western suburbs. In England, the market-town of Basingstoke is served by the Great Western railway and the London & South-Western railway. However, the introduction of railway competition led to the loss of trade for the Basingstoke canal, which had opened around 1794.

## Colonial Expansion and Territorial Opening

In East Africa, the construction of the Mombasa-Victoria Nyanza railway between 1896 and 1903 fundamentally changed the administration of the protectorate. While the region was initially viewed primarily as a high road to Uganda, the railway's reach into the high plateaus revealed large, sparsely peopled areas with excellent climates favorable for European colonization. The transport facilities provided by the completed railway made it practicable to open the country to settlers. This led to land applications, including a 500 square mile grant sought by the East Africa Syndicate in April 1902 and a proposed scheme for a large Jewish settlement.

In Burma, railway construction has been the chief advance in internal communications, surpassing the existing road networks. Key lines include the 1877 route from Rangoon to Prome (161 miles) and the 1884 route from Rangoon to Toungoo (166 miles). The network was later extended to Mandalay, with the Mu Valley railway reaching Myitkyina (752 miles from Rangoon) and the Mandalay-Lashio railway covering 178 miles. By 1904-1905, the total length of open lines was 1,340 miles, though communication remained incomplete in areas like the southern Shan States.
