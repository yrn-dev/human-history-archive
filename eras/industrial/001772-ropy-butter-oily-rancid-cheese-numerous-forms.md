# Dairy Produce: Butter and Cheese

The production and quality of butter and cheese are fundamentally linked to the biological properties of milk and the industrial processes used to refine it. From the microscopic influence of bacteria to the economic structures of creameries and cheeseries, these products represent a complex intersection of science and commerce.

## Bacteriology and Product Quality
No milk is entirely free from bacteria, as they are always present in the external orifices of the milk-ducts. While some bacteria induce the "turning" or souring of standing milk, others are responsible for the fermentations required to produce butter and cheese. The search for specific bacteria has led to the use of pure cultures in American dairies to achieve a peculiarly fine flavour in certain butters, and in Denmark, where such cultures and specific treatments are used to suppress the forms of bacteria that cause rancidity, allowing butter to keep fresh longer.

Conversely, researchers have sought to identify the germs that cause "diseases" or undesirable changes in dairy products. These include bacteria that render milk "ropy" and butter "oily" or "rancid." Cheese, in its various forms, contains myriads of bacteria; some are known to be involved in the ripening process and other changes. Practical applications of these discoveries from the decade 1890–1900 have been applied to products such as Edam cheese. Additionally, the Japanese produce cheeses through the bacterial fermentation of boiled Soja beans.

## Composition and Characteristics of Butter
Theoretically, butter should consist almost entirely of fat, though in practice it typically ranges from 83% to 88% fat, with 10% to 15% water. It also contains milk-sugar (0.2% to 0.8%) and casein (0.5% to 0.8%). Casein is considered an objectionable ingredient and is usually the cause of rancidity. Butter that is badly worked or washed, leaving behind too much buttermilk and casein, possesses inferior keeping qualities. However, overworking or overwashing can remove the delicacy of flavour.

Well-made butter is firm, not greasy, and possesses a "grain" that allows it to cut cleanly and break with a granular fracture similar to cast-iron. Brine is used for washing to introduce a small amount of salt, which develops flavour and acts as a preservative. "Streaky" butter may result from an uneven distribution of salt or curd left behind due to imperfect washing.

## Varieties of Cheese
Cheese production varies widely by region and method. In France, pressed varieties with hard rinds include:
* **Gruyere:** A pale-yellow cheese with holes of varying sizes, produced in Switzerland and the Jura Mountains district.
* **Cantal:** A barrel-shaped cheese of lower quality from the midland districts.
* **Roquefort:** A small, soft, white cheese veined with greenish-blue mould, weighing between 4 and 5 lb. It is made from the milk of ewes in the department of Aveyron and cured in natural mountain caves.
* **Port Salut:** A modern, thin, flat cheese originating in the abbey of the same name in Mayenne.

Soft unpressed cheeses include Brie, Camembert, and Coulommiers, as well as Pont l'Eveque and Livarot. These are shaped in moulds and cured on straw mats until they reach a consistency similar to butter. Other soft varieties intended to be eaten fresh, such as cream cheese, include Neufchatel, Gervais, and Bondon.

## Industrial Production and Economics
The system of the "cheesery" or cheese factory is of American origin, credited to Jesse Williams near Rome, Oneida county, N.Y. By 1866, nearly 500 such factories existed in New York state. In England, co-operative cheeseries appeared by 1870 in Derby and Longford. The first creamery in the U.S. was built by Alanson Slaughter near Wallkill, N.Y., in 1861.

In England, cheeseries and creameries often work in conjunction with the milk trade. A factory serves as an economic safety-valve, converting surplus milk into butter or cheese when the milk market is overstocked. However, the rise of these factories and the expanding urban trade in country milk have led to a diminution of home dairying and farmhouse production.

## Trade and Regulation in the United Kingdom
The Sale of Food and Drugs Act 1899 (effective January 1, 1900) regulates the dairy trade to prevent adulteration. The act requires clear marking for margarine, margarine-cheese, and "machine-skimmed milk." An article is deemed adulterated if any substance is mixed in or abstracted to injuriously affect its quality. Specifically, Section 8 limits the butter-fat content in margarine to no more than 10%.

Regarding trade volumes, imports of butter expanded rapidly between 1887 and 1896, doubling in quantity. While cheese imports also increased, they did so at a slower rate than butter. By 1899, butter imports exceeded cheese imports by fully a million cwt. The growth in cheese demand among industrial classes may have been checked by the availability of cheap imported fresh meat.

Between 1890 and 1899, the decennial averages for United Kingdom production were 7,906,874 tons of milk, 83,992 tons of butter, and 141,412 tons of cheese. It is estimated that 32% of total milk yield is used for butter-making and 20% for cheese-making, with one ton of milk producing approximately 80 lb. of butter or 220 lb. of cheese.
