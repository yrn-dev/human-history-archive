# England and the Antiquarian Reaction

## The Development of Book-Collecting
In the history of civilization, the growth of book-collecting as it is understood in the modern sense is a relatively recent phenomenon. While early indications of this tendency existed during the Roman Empire and toward the end of the Middle Ages, earlier works focused primarily on the enjoyment of literature. For example, the *Philobiblon*, attributed to Richard de Bury and written in 1345, deals almost exclusively with the delights of literature. Later, in the century and a half following, Sebastian Brant wrote an attack on the "book-fool," arguing that possessing books is a poor substitute for actual learning.

Modern book-collecting required a sufficient abundance of books to be available, similar to how the collection of old china required the proliferation of cups and saucers. Even after the invention of printing reduced the cost of books by approximately 80%, collectors did not appear immediately. Many early owners of significant libraries were not collectors in the antiquarian sense. For instance, Jean Grolier (1479-1565) bound books for himself and his friends to encourage the best printers of his era and to share the latest Renaissance scholarship, rather than out of antiquarian instinct.

## Notable English Book-Owners
In England, several prominent figures assembled extensive libraries containing the best books of their day across all departments of learned literature. These individuals included Archbishop Cranmer, Lords Arundel and Lumley, and Henry, prince of Wales (1594-1612). Their approach to these volumes was characterized by aesthetic and personal branding; they placed their books in handsome leather jackets and adorned them with heraldic badges and their own coats of arms.

## Antiquarian Curiosity in British History
Antiquarian interest has also extended to the study of early British political figures. Carausius, a British emperor, became a favorite object of antiquarian curiosity due to the preservation of a large number of his medals. This has led to sagacious accuracy in the investigation of his life and actions. Dr. Stukely, specifically, devoted a large volume to Carausius, though some of his conjectures have been viewed as fanciful.

Regarding the status of England in the early fourth century, the orator Eumenius sought to exalt the glory of the hero Constantius through the importance of the conquest of England. However, historical perspectives suggest that a century and a half prior to this, England hardly paid for its own establishment, making such commendations difficult to conceive.

## Overseas Enterprises and Communication
In a broader political context between 1848 and 1878, the mechanical revolution did not immediately produce revolutionary changes in the political history of Europe, as the Great Powers continued to operate within similar boundaries and formalities as in pre-revolutionary times. However, significant changes in condition and method occurred in the overseas enterprises of Britain and other European powers. These changes were driven by the increased certainty and speed of telegraphic communications and transport, which also prompted reactions from Africa and Asia.
