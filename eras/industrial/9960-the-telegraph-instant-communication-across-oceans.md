# The Electric Nerve: The Telegraph and the Shrinking World

## Overview
The invention and deployment of the telegraph during the Industrial Revolution represented one of the most profound shifts in human history: the decoupling of communication from transportation. For millennia, information could only move as fast as a horse, a ship, or a human runner. The telegraph changed this by utilizing electrical impulses to transmit coded messages across wires, effectively creating a "Victorian Internet."

While Samuel Morse is often credited with the invention in the United States, the technology emerged through parallel efforts in Britain by William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone. The true geopolitical transformation, however, occurred with the laying of submarine cables. The quest to connect Europe and North America across the Atlantic was a feat of industrial endurance. After several failed attempts in the 1850s—where cables snapped or succumbed to electrical failure—a permanent link was finally established in 1866. This reduced the communication time between London and New York from ten days (by steamship) to mere minutes.

Historians often debate the extent to which the telegraph "globalized" the world. Some argue it was the primary catalyst for modern imperialism, allowing colonial powers to manage distant territories with unprecedented precision. Others suggest it primarily served the interests of capital, revolutionizing stock markets and commodity pricing by eliminating the "information lag" that previously allowed merchants to speculate on delayed news.

## Key Facts
*   **The Code:** Morse Code utilized a system of dots and dashes (short and long signals) to represent letters, making it possible to transmit complex language via simple electrical on/off switches.
*   **Economic Impact:** The telegraph gave birth to the modern news agency. Organizations like Reuters and the Associated Press emerged to gather and sell "instant" news to newspapers.
*   **Diplomatic Shift:** The "speed of diplomacy" accelerated. During the Crimean War, the telegraph allowed governments to communicate with generals in the field, though this often led to micromanagement from distant capitals.

## Did You Know?
1. **The "Great Eastern":** The massive ship used to lay the 1866 cable was the largest ship in the world at the time; it was the only vessel capable of carrying the immense weight of the armored cable.
2. **Gutta-Percha:** Submarine cables were made possible by gutta-percha, a natural latex from Malaysian trees that provided the necessary waterproof insulation for underwater wires.
3. **The First "Hackers":** Early telegraph operators often developed their own unofficial shorthand and "slang" to speed up transmission, bypassing official protocols.
