# Chemical and Physical Processes Involving Liquid Solutions

The manipulation of liquids, including their precipitation, distillation, and density measurement, is a fundamental aspect of various chemical and physical procedures. These processes often involve precise timing, specific temperature controls, and the use of specialized apparatus to isolate substances or determine material properties.

## Precipitation and Chemical Synthesis
In the preparation of certain chemical salts, the timing of sedimentation is critical. For example, in the production of ammonium azoimide ($\text{N}_3\text{.NH}_4$), diazohippuramide is boiled with alcoholic ammonia until ammonia no longer escapes. Following this reaction, the resulting liquid is allowed to stand for twelve hours. This period allows for the precipitation of hippuramide, after which the clear alcoholic solution is decanted. To further isolate the ammonium salt, four times the volume of ether is added to the solution, causing the salt to precipitate. The final product is then filtered, washed with ether, and air-dried. This salt is noted for being extremely explosive and feebly alkaline, though it is readily soluble in water.

Other synthesis processes utilize temperature and filtration. Mineral kermes (antimony trisulphide) is produced by adding 60 grammes of powdered antimony trisulphide to a boiling solution of 1280 grammes of crystallized sodium carbonate in 12,800 grammes of distilled water. After boiling for one hour, the liquid is filtered while hot. As the liquid cools slowly, the kermes deposits as a brown-red velvety powder that is insoluble in water.

## Distillation and Industrial Extraction
The processing of degreased bones involves the extraction of gelatinous matter and the carbonization of the residue. This operation, conducted in horizontal cast-iron retorts, takes approximately twelve to thirteen hours. The vapours produced during this carbonization are condensed in vertical air condensers, resulting in a liquid that separates into two distinct layers:

*   **Upper Tarry Layer:** This layer is floated off and redistilled. The resulting distillate, known as "bone oil," consists primarily of pyridine derivatives and fatty amines and is characterized by a disgusting odour. The remaining residue is called "bone pitch," used in the manufacture of compositions such as black varnishes.
*   **Lower Ammoniacal Liquor:** This layer is transferred to stills and distilled with steam. The ammonia is received in sulphuric acid to produce ammonium sulphate, which is drained, dried, and used primarily as manure.

## Density Determination Methods
Several methods exist for determining the density of liquids and the substances contained within them.

### The Diffusion Column
The "diffusion column" method involves creating a liquid column with a density that varies uniformly from approximately 3.3 to 1. This is achieved by adding benzene (five times the volume) to a small amount of methylene iodide in a long, tightly corked test tube, which is then left to stand for several hours. To determine the density of a test crystal, the crystal is introduced into the column. Using areometrical beads—hollow glass beads of variable density—the experimentalist identifies two beads ($d_1$ and $d_2$), one floating above and one below the crystal. By measuring the distances ($l_1$ and $l_2$) from the crystal to these beads, the density of the crystal is calculated as $(d_1l_2 + d_2l_1)/(l_1 + l_2)$.

### The Westphal Balance and Specific Gravity Bottles
The Westphal balance determines liquid density by weighing a glass plummet in air, in a standard liquid, and in the experimental liquid. The apparatus uses a steelyard with a heavy bob, a pointer, and riders of determined weights to bring the beam to equilibrium.

Alternatively, a "specific gravity bottle" (capacity 10 to 100 cc) is used. The bottle is cleaned with distilled water, hydrochloric acid, and soda, then dried and weighed. It is filled with distilled water, brought to a specific temperature in a thermostat, and weighed again. This process is repeated using the liquid under examination to determine its density.

## Electrolytic Precipitation
In the electrolytic method for assaying copper ores, 0.5 to 5 grammes of ore are decomposed using a mixture of 10 cc. of sulphuric acid and 10 cc. of nitric acid. Once the liquid is cold, it is diluted with cold water and heated to dissolve soluble salts before being diluted to approximately 150 cc. The solution is placed in a beaker with two concentric platinum cylinders acting as electrodes. The smaller cylinder typically serves as the negative electrode. A current is passed through the liquid for a period of two to twelve hours until all copper is precipitated. The resulting deposit is bright rosy red in colour.
