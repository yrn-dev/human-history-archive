# French Guiana as a Site of Deportation and Colonization

French Guiana, a territory located in South America, served as a significant site for French colonial interests and as a destination for political and criminal deportation from the late 18th century through the 19th century. By 1815, it remained one of the few overseas possessions held by France, alongside one or two West Indian islands.

## Penal Colonies and Criminal Deportation
France began utilizing deportation for criminals as early as 1763 with the establishment of a penal colony in French Guiana. This initial venture failed disastrously; an expedition of 14,000 individuals, described as the most evil elements of the Paris population, resulted in the death of every person sent. A second attempt in 1766 met with the same miserable outcome.

Further failures occurred in the 19th century, most notably a scheme in 1823 by the philanthropist Baron Milius. Milius attempted to create a community on the banks of the Mana through the marriage of degraded women and exiled convicts, a project that resulted in ghastly horrors. Although publicists and the government formally condemned the principle of deportation for a time, it was reintroduced into the French penal code in 1854. This reintroduction was framed as a means of regenerating criminals and creating rich colonies. However, the pestilential climate and malarious conditions of the equator rendered these efforts futile. Many of the thousands exiled there faced certain death, and agricultural developments, including plantations and farms, proved to be a dead loss due to the unkindly soil. Eventually, the French government ceased sending native-born French exiles, sending instead convicts of Asiatic blacks, Anamites, and Arab subject races, though this also failed to achieve successful colonization.

## Political Deportation during the Revolutionary Period
During the French Revolution, French Guiana was used as a destination for the deportation of political prisoners and religious figures. Following the annexation of Belgium and the principality of Liège on October 1, 1795, the French National Convention oversaw a period of harsh treatment in the conquered territories. Catholic worship was suppressed and replaced by the worship of the "Goddess of Reason," and priests were either sentenced to death or deported to the islands of Oléron and Ré, or to French Guiana.

The territory also became a destination for French politicians targeted during internal power struggles. During the *coup d'etat* of the 18th Fructidor (September 4) 1797, several high-profile figures were arrested and deported:

*   **Francois, Marquis de Barbe-Marbois**: A politician and former intendant of San Domingo who had been suspected of royalist sympathies due to his moderation in the Council of the Ancients. He was transported to French Guiana in 1797 before being transferred to Oleron in 1799.
*   **Francois, Marquis de Barthelemy**: A diplomat and member of the Directory elected through royalist influence. He was deported to French Guiana following the 18 Fructidor *coup d'etat* but managed to escape, traveling first to the United States and then to England.

Other political figures narrowly avoided this fate; for instance, Du Pont, a member of the Council of Five Hundred and a prominent member of the reactionary party, only escaped transportation to Cayenne through the influence of M.J. Chenier following the republican triumph of 18 Fructidor.

## Regional Context
French Guiana existed alongside other European colonial holdings in South America. By 1815, the region included British Guiana and Dutch Guiana (the latter comprising Surinam and Essequibo). The Dutch had established their presence in Guiana as compensation after resigning claims to Brazil in 1661, exploiting the region for great profits until the French invasion of Holland in 1794 led to a general reorganization of their possessions.
