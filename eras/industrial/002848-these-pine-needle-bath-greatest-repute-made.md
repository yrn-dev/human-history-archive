# Pine-Needle Baths and Related Applications

## Overview of Balneotherapeutics
Balneotherapeutics, derived from the Latin *balneum* (bath) and the Greek *therapeuein* (to treat medically), is the medical treatment of disease through the internal and external use of mineral waters. This practice is distinct from hydrotherapy, which utilizes pure water. While originally focused on mineral waters, the term has expanded to encompass general spa treatments. These include the drinking of waters, the use of natural vapour baths, hot baths, and the application of various types of sand and mud.

Mineral waters typically contain calcium, magnesium, sodium, and iron, which combine with acids to form carbonates, sulphides, sulphates, and chlorides. Other therapeutic elements occasionally present include iodine, bromine, manganese, potassium, lithium, and arsenic. The primary gases found in solution are nitrogen, oxygen, carbonic acid, and sulphuretted hydrogen, though helium and argon are found in "thermal sulphur waters" and "simple thermal" waters.

## Medicated and Aromatic Baths
To modify or increase the influence of mineral waters, some spas employ "medicated" or "aromatic" baths. These involve mixing substances into the water to produce a specific effect on the peripheral nerves and the skin.

Among these, the "pine-needle" bath is the most highly reputed. It is prepared by adding a tincture of an alcoholic extract, fir wood oil (a mixture of ethereal oils), or a decoction made from the young shoots or needles of pines and firs. It is believed that the volatile ethereal constituents of these additives penetrate the skin to stimulate the peripheral nerves and cutaneous circulation, eventually being eliminated through ordinary channels.

Similar therapeutic effects can be achieved by adding aromatic herbs such as thyme or camomile. The preparation for a full-sized bath involves tying 1-1/2 to 2 lb of herbs in a muslin bag and infusing them in a gallon of boiling water. The resulting infusion is then expressed and added to the bath. Astringent baths are prepared using a similar method.

## Regional Pine Forests and Environments
Pine forests are found in various global regions, contributing to both the economy and the atmosphere of the landscape. In the United States, the eastern pine belt consists of loblolly and long-leaf pine, covering approximately 3,900 sq. m. The south-western pine belt, covering about 4,200 sq. m., contains the world's heaviest growth of long-leaf pine timber, with some short-leaf pine interspersed. In the north-western portion of the state, short-leaf growth is especially heavy. Other varieties include curly pine, which is fairly abundant, and pond pine, found only near the Pearl river.

In Europe, the town of Marienbad in Bohemia, Austria, is surrounded on most sides by fragrant pine forests on gently sloping hills. Similarly, the Thüringer Wald features roads lined with lofty pines and solemn, dark green firs, where the air is characterized by the smell of juicy pine-needles and damp earth.

## Historical and Material Uses of Pine
Historically, pine trees have been used for construction and artistry. In one account, pine trees brought from Aurea Chersonesus were used to support a palace and temple, as well as to create psalteries and harps for the hymns of the Levites. This specific wood was described as whiter and more shining than the wood of the fig tree, and was noted as being distinct from the trees referred to as pine by merchants.
