# George Boole and Augustus De Morgan

George Boole and Augustus De Morgan were prominent English mathematicians and logicians who contributed significantly to the development of symbolic reasoning and logical theory. With the exception of De Morgan, Boole was likely the first English mathematician to write upon logic since the time of John Wallis.

## George Boole: Life and Mathematical Career
George Boole (1815–1864) was born in Lincoln on November 2, 1815. The son of a studious tradesman who provided his first lessons in mathematical science, Boole's extraordinary mathematical abilities did not appear early in his life; his initial favorite subject was classics. He did not begin studying higher mathematics until age seventeen, and his progress was hindered by a lack of efficient help. 

Boole spent his professional life in the scholastic profession, starting as an assistant-master in a private school at Doncaster around age sixteen. He later established a school in Lincoln, which he subsequently moved to Waddington. In 1849, he was appointed professor of mathematics at the Queen's College in Cork. In 1855, he married Mary Everest, who later wrote educational works based on her husband's principles.

Boole's published output consisted of approximately fifty scattered articles and a few separate publications. His earliest paper, "Theory of Analytical Transformations," appeared in the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* in 1839. He contributed twenty-two articles to the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* and its successor, sixteen papers to the *Philosophical Magazine*, and six important memoirs to the *Philosophical Transactions* of the Royal Society. During his lifetime, he completed two systematic treatises: the *Treatise on Differential Equations* (1859) and the *Treatise on the Calculus of Finite Differences* (1860).

## Boole's Logical System
Boole's work in logic was driven by a profound confidence in symbolic reasoning. In the spring of 1847, he published a pamphlet titled *Mathematical Analysis of Logic*. Although he later viewed this as a hasty and imperfect exposition, it displayed an originality that is noted by competent readers. His mature views were presented in the larger 1854 work, *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, on which are founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities*.

Boole did not view logic as a branch of mathematics, but he identified a deep analogy between algebraic symbols and those representing logical forms and syllogisms. He proposed that logic could be seen as mathematics restricted to the quantities 0 and 1. In this system:
* **Unity (1)** represents the universe of thinkable objects.
* **Literal symbols** (such as x, y, z, v, u) represent elective meanings similar to adjectives and substantives. 

For example, if x represents "horned" and y represents "sheep," the combination of x and y performed on unity represents the class of "horned sheep." Boole demonstrated that these elective symbols follow the same primary laws of combination as algebraical symbols, allowing them to be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided. Consequently, "1 - x" represents all things in the world except horned things, and "(1 - x)(1 - y)" represents all things that are neither horned nor sheep.

Furthermore, Boole developed a general symbolic method of logical inference in *Laws of Thought*, showing how to draw any conclusion logically contained within premises through purely symbolic treatment. The second part of this work attempted to discover a general method in probabilities to determine the probability of an event logically connected to a system of given events.

## Augustus De Morgan: Life and Contributions
Augustus De Morgan (1806–1871) was born in June 1806 in Madura, Madras presidency. His father, Colonel John De Morgan, served the East India Company. De Morgan's early education included Latin, Greek, and some Hebrew. He entered Trinity College, Cambridge, at age sixteen and a half, studying mathematics partly under Sir G. B. Airy. He earned a Trinity scholarship in 1825 and placed fourth in the mathematical tripos in 1827. However, he was prevented from obtaining a fellowship or an M.A. degree due to his conscientious objection to signing the required theological tests.

De Morgan became a professor of mathematics at the University of London (University College) at age twenty-two. Though he resigned in 1831 following a disagreement with the governing body, he resumed the professorship in 1836 after his successor drowned.

De Morgan's mathematical contributions include memoirs on the "Foundation of Algebra" in the *Cambridge Philosophical Transactions*, which influenced Sir W. Rowan Hamilton's work on quaternions. His other works include *Elements of Arithmetic* (1830), *The Elements of Trigonometry and Trigonometrical Analysis* (1837), *The Elements of Algebra* (1835), and *Trigonometry and Double Algebra* (1849).

## De Morgan's Logical Reform
De Morgan is remembered as a logical reformer who emphasized the importance of logical training. In 1839, he wrote *First Notions of Logic* for students of Euclid. In October 1846, he published a paper in the *Transactions of the Cambridge Philosophical Society* regarding the principle of the quantification of the predicate. This discovery led to a controversy with Sir W. R. Hamilton, though Hamilton eventually recognized the independence of De Morgan's discovery.

De Morgan believed that logicians had erred by neglecting mathematics and mathematicians had erred by neglecting logic. He sought to reconcile the two. While his system was described by Hamilton as "horrent with mysterious spiculae," it is noted that the real analogy between quantitative and qualitative reasoning is more effectively sought in the works of Boole.
