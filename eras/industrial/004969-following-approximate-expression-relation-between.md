# Approximate Expressions and Relations in Science and Engineering

The use of approximate expressions is a fundamental component of various scientific and mathematical disciplines, allowing for the calculation of complex physical phenomena where rigorous exactness is either impracticable or computationally exhaustive. These approximations are applied across fields ranging from celestial mechanics and structural engineering to geodesy and meteorology.

## Celestial Mechanics and the Problem of Three Bodies
In the study of planetary motion, the general law of gravitation indicates that planets deviate from the elliptical paths described by Kepler's laws due to the mutual attraction they exert upon one another. While Isaac Newton conducted investigations into these effects in the *Principia*, his geometrical methods provided only rude approximations. The subsequent "problem of three bodies" sought to determine the motions of three bodies under mutual attraction with a higher degree of rigor.

One established relation is the law of the conservation of the centre of gravity, which states that regardless of the number of interacting bodies, the centre of gravity of the entire system moves in a straight line with a constant velocity, expressed through three equations for the rectangular co-ordinates.

To solve the problem of planetary positions, a synthetic approach is employed:
1. Planets are first conceived as moving in invariable elliptic orbits to obtain approximate positions.
2. These positions are used to express the mutual pull between planets.
3. This pull determines variations in the ideal elements, which are represented by integration as algebraic expressions of time and starting elements.

Because the initial pull is based on the supposition of unvarying orbits rather than actual positions, the results are not rigorously exact. Further approximations are made by correcting the expression of the pull using the previously determined variations. In theory, these successive approximations can be continued indefinitely, but in practice, they are stopped when the outstanding error is less than that of the best observations.

## Orbital Velocity and Kepler's Third Law
The relation between a planet's mean distance and its periodic time, as approximately expressed by Kepler's third law, is derived from the laws of centrifugal force. Centrifugal force varies directly as the product of the distance of the moving body from the centre of motion and the square of its angular velocity. For a body to revolve around a central mass (M), the centrifugal force must be balanced by the attraction of that mass. If $n$ represents angular velocity and $a$ represents distance, this balance is expressed by the equation $an^2 = M/a^2$, which simplifies to $a^3n^2 = M$, where $M$ is a constant.

Additionally, at any given distance from the sun, there is a specific velocity a body would have if it moved in a circular orbit. If a body is projected with this velocity in any direction, the point of projection will be at the end of the orbit's minor axis. This is the only point of an ellipse where the distance from the focus equals the semi-major axis, and thus the only point where the distance from the sun equals the mean distance.

## Structural Engineering and Geodesy
In the context of suspension bridges, the relation between the length of a half parabolic chain ($s$) and its dip ($y$) is expressed as $s = x + 2y^2/3x$. The tension at any point at a distance $x$ from the vertex is determined by the equation $R^2 = H^2 + V^2 = w^2x^4/4y^2 + w^2x^2$, resulting in $R = wx\sqrt{1+x^2/4y^2}$.

In geodesy, approximate expressions are used to determine the difference in latitude and longitude between a point and the foot of a perpendicular (a great circle). The difference in latitude in seconds is approximately $x^2 \tan[\phi] \csc 1'' / 2[\rho][\nu]$, where $x$ is the length of the perpendicular, $[\rho]$ is the radius of curvature to the meridian, $[\nu]$ is that of the normal terminated by the minor axis, and $[\phi]$ is the latitude of the foot of the perpendicular. The difference in longitude in seconds is approximately $x \sec[\rho] \csc 1'' / [\nu]$.

## Meteorology and Geography
In meteorology, Buys Ballot's Law provides an approximate rule for the Northern Hemisphere: "Stand with your back to the wind; the low-pressure area will be on your left-hand." This law, recognized by J.H. Coffin and W. Ferrel, is reversed in the Southern Hemisphere. In low latitudes, however, the angle between the wind and the barometric gradient is not a right angle.

In geography, approximate lines are used to define physical and political boundaries. Wallace's line, indicated by naturalist A.R. Wallace, follows approximately 120 deg. E. through the Straits of Macassar, separating Asiatic flora and fauna to the west from Australian elements to the south and east. Similarly, the boundary between the Shan States and Tongking is defined by the Mekong river for some 200 m., with the Burmese boundary leaving the Mekong at approximately 20 deg. N. lat.
