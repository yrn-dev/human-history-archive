# The Collected Works of Barthelemy and Mery (1831)

The *Oeuvres* of Auguste Marseille Barthelemy and J. P. A. Mery were collected and published in 1831 in a four-volume set. This collection featured a notice written by L. Reybaud. Further information regarding both authors can be found in the work *Barthelemy et Mery etudies specialement dans leurs rapports avec la legende napoleonienne*, written by Jules Garsou and published in volume lviii of the *Memoires* of the Academie Royale... de Belgique.

## Auguste Marseille Barthelemy
Auguste Marseille Barthelemy (1796–1867) was a French satirical poet born in Marseilles. He maintained an exceptionally intimate collaboration with his friend and compatriot, J. P. A. Mery (1798–1866), to the extent that their individual personalities are difficult to distinguish within their joint works.

Barthelemy initially established a local reputation as a poet before moving to Paris. He first gained the favor of the court with the 1825 publication *Le Sacre de Charles X*. However, he soon shifted his efforts toward the opposition party. In 1825, he produced a clever political satire titled *Les Sidiennes*.

## Joint Works and Political Satire
Barthelemy and Mery produced a series of joint works that achieved immediate and pronounced success. These included:
* *La Villeliade ou la prise du chateau de Rivoli* (1827), which required fifteen editions within a single year.
* *La Corbiereide* (1827).
* *La Peyronneide* (1827).
* *Biographie des quarante de l'academie francaise* (1826).
* *Napoleon en Egypte* (1828), which saw nearly a dozen editions in one year.

The authors also wrote *Fils de l'homme*, a poem concerning Napoleon's son, the duke of Reichstadt. The publication of this work led to Barthelemy being fined 1,000 francs and imprisoned in 1829.

## Later Career and Political Shifts
Following the Revolution of 1830, which liberated Barthelemy from prison, he and Mery wrote *L'Insurrection* to celebrate the triumph of the people. Between March 1831 and April 1832, the pair issued a weekly series of verse satires called the *Nemesis*, which targeted the ministers and government of Louis Philippe. During this period, the small pension Barthelemy received was stopped.

After the *Nemesis* ceased publication, suspicions arose that Barthelemy had been paid to remain silent. In 1832, he published an anonymous poem that supported certain government acts that were particularly obnoxious to the Liberal party. This change in political alignment destroyed his influence, and his subsequent writings went unnoticed. For several years following this shift, Barthelemy refrained from satirical writing and received a handsome pension from the government.

## Other Related Figures
The archive contains records of other individuals named Barthelemy, distinct from the satirical poet:
* **Francois, Marquis de Barthelemy (1747 or 1750–1830):** A French politician and diplomat who served as minister plenipotentiary in Switzerland and negotiated the treaties of Basel (1795). He was a member of the Directory, was deported to French Guiana after the *coup d'etat* of 18 Fructidor, and later entered the senate in 1800. He eventually became a peer of France.
* **Abbe Jean Jacques Barthelemy:** The uncle of Francois Barthelemy, who educated him for a diplomatic career. His own *Memoires sur la vie de l'abbe Barthelemy* (1824) included a notice by Lalande, and his *Oeuvres completes* (1821, 4 vols.) included a notice by Villenave. He is also cited in discussions regarding the number of Spartan tribes in *Voyage du Jeune Anacharsis*.
