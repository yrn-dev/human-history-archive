# Gipsies

## Origins and Migration
The Gipsies are described as a refugee Eastern people who appeared in Europe between the end of the fourteenth and the early fifteenth centuries. Their arrival in Greece was a side result of Mongol perturbations during the fourteenth century, specifically those led by Timurlane. While they were believed to be Egyptians—a persuasion they themselves accepted and disseminated, which led to the name "Gipsy"—their leaders identified themselves as "Counts of Asia Minor."

It is probable that the Gipsies had been drifting through Western Asia for several centuries before the massacres of Timurlane drove them across the Hellespont. They may have been displaced from their original homeland on the Bactrian steppes by the great cataclysm of Jengis, or perhaps even earlier, following a similar drifting pattern to that of the Ottoman Turks, though with less success.

## Distribution and Cultural Identity
The Gipsies spread slowly westward across Europe, existing as fragments of nomadism within a society defined by cities and ploughs. They sought harbor in neglected patches, wild woodlands, by hedgerows, and upon European commons. Different European nations applied various labels to them: the French called them "Bohemians," while the Germans referred to them as "Tartars" and "Hungarians."

Although they do not appear to have maintained a true tradition of their origins, their history is indicated by a distinctive language. This language is likely North Indian in origin and contains many North Indian words, as well as significant Persian and Armenian elements. In the modern era, they are found in all European countries, working as beggars, fortune-tellers, showmen, horse-dealers, pedlars, and tinkers.

## Linguistic and Regional Presence
Beyond Europe, Gipsy dialects are noted in other regions. In the presidency encompassing areas such as Gujarat, Sind, and the Deccan, there are approximately 30,000 Gipsy dialect speakers.
