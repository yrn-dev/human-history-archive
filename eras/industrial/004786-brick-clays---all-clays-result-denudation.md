# Brick Clays

Brick clays are materials resulting from the decomposition and denudation of siliceous and felspathic rocks. They consist of fine insoluble particles that are carried by water in suspension and deposited in geologic basins based on their degree of fineness and specific gravity. These deposits were formed across all geologic epochs, ranging from the Cambrian to the Recent.

## Classification and Composition
Clays used for burnt bricks are divided into two principal types based on their chemical composition:

1.  **Ordinary Brick Clays**: These consist primarily of hydrated aluminium silicates (the "true clay substance"), with varying amounts of sand, undecomposed grains of felspar, and carbonate or oxide of iron. They contain only a small percentage of carbonate of lime and typically burn to colors such as salmon, buff, or red.
2.  **Marls (Lime Clays)**: These contain a considerable percentage of carbonate of lime, often between 15% and 30%, and can contain as much as 40% chalk. Marls are less widely distributed than ordinary brick clays and burn to a distinctive sulphur-yellow color.

The strength of a brick clay—specifically its tensile strength and plasticity—is usually linked to a higher percentage of hydrated aluminium silicates. Additionally, oxide of iron is a critical ingredient, typically present in amounts from 2% to 10%. This oxide determines the hardness, durability, and color of the burnt brick, with colors ranging from chocolate to light buff. For example, a clay with 5% to 8% oxide of iron generally produces a red brick. However, if the clay contains 3% to 4% alkalis or is fired too hard, the color becomes darker and more purple. Conversely, an excess of alumina tends to make the color brighter and lighter.

## Geological Formations and Properties
Brick clays vary in hardness depending on their formation. Alluvial and drift clays are soft, plastic, and found near the surface; these were the only clays used for brickmaking until modern times because they are easily worked and require little preparation. In contrast, older sedimentary deposits, such as slates and shales, are hard and rock-like. These older "rocky clays" often require heavy machinery to process, though they can be brought to a plastic state through grinding in water or through long weathering—exposure to sun, frost, and rain.

Specific geological examples include the London Clay, a marine deposit 500 feet thick consisting of brown or blue clay with sandy layers, which is frequently used for Roman cement, coarse pottery, and bricks.

## Processing and Preparation
The method of preparing clay depends on the material's initial state:

*   **Marls**: Because limestone pebbles in marls can convert to quicklime during firing and shatter the brick upon exposure to weather, they must be removed. This is achieved by squeezing the plastic marl through iron gratings or using a grinding-mill with heavy iron rollers to crush the pebbles. In some cases, an artificial "malm" is created by mixing ordinary brick clay with ground chalk in water until it reaches a cream-like consistency. This mixture is passed through a sieve onto a drying-bed, mixed with fine ashes and water, and tempered.
*   **Hard Clays and Shales**: These are processed using grinding-mills (either rollers with graduated spaces or "mortar pan" types with iron bottoms) to crush the material. This disintegration accelerates the weathering process.
*   **Plastic Clays**: If the clay is already plastic, it may go directly into a pug-mill, unless it contains pebbles larger than a quarter of an inch.

## Manufacturing Methods
Bricks are produced using three primary methods:

**Hand-molding**: Tempered clay is pressed by hand into a wooden or metal four-sided mould. To prevent sticking, the moulder dips the case in water or a mixture of water and sand. The clay is leveled with a wooden strip called a "strike." A "frog" (a hollow in the bottom of the brick) is created by placing the mould on a stock-board with a central elevated tongue.

**Machine-made Plastic Bricks**: These use a pug-mill, which consists of a trough with a central shaft equipped with knives and cutters that knead the clay and force it through a delivery nose. The clay emerges as a continuous mass, which is then cut into bricks using a wire cutting frame. These are known as "wire-cuts."

**Semi-plastic (Dust-made) Bricks**: Common in districts using shales, these bricks are stamped from ground shale powder that has been moistened enough to adhere under pressure. The shale is crushed in an iron grinding-pan and sifted through a screen to remove coarse particles. These bricks are formed using heavy-duty presses capable of applying greater pressure than those used for plastic clay.
