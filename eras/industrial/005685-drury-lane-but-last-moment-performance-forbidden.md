# Drury Lane

Drury Lane is a location in London associated with residential history and a long tradition of theatrical performance.

## Early Residential History
In 1610, Sir Robert Drury of Hawsted, a wealthy gentleman, owned a large house in Drury Lane. He provided an apartment in this residence to the poet Donne and his wife. During his time there, Sir Robert suffered the loss of his only daughter, Elizabeth Drury. In 1611 and 1612, Donne published an extravagant elegy and a work titled *Progress of the Soul* regarding her death. In late 1611, Sir Robert Drury visited Paris, taking Donne with him.

## The Drury Lane Theatre: Performers and Productions
The Drury Lane theatre served as a primary venue for some of England's most prominent actors and dramatists.

### Notable Actors
Many distinguished performers were associated with the company. Edward Kynaston, known for his beauty and as one of the best male actors of female parts, appeared in a company set up in the Cockpit in Drury Lane. Robert Baddeley, who began his career as a valet and cook to Samuel Foote, became a regular member of the Drury Lane company in 1763, finding great success in servants' parts and low comedy. He later originated the role of Moses in *The School for Scandal*.

Spranger Barry made his London debut at Drury Lane in 1746 as Othello. His success in roles such as Romeo, *Hamlet*, and *Macbeth* created a rivalry with David Garrick. Similarly, Thomas King was engaged by Garrick for Drury Lane after being discovered as a strolling player in Windsor. King debuted in 1748 as the Herald in *King Lear* and later achieved significant acclaim as Lord Ogleby in *The Clandestine Marriage* (1766) and as Sir Peter Teazle in *The School for Scandal* (1777).

The theatre also featured prominent actresses. George Anne Bellamy played Juliet to Garrick's Romeo at Drury Lane during a period of rival performances with Spranger Barry at Covent Garden.

### Dramatists and Musical Works
The theatre produced a variety of plays and musical adaptations. Hugh Kelly's first comedy, *False Delicacy*, was produced by Garrick at Drury Lane on January 23, 1768. While Samuel Johnson criticized it for being "totally void of character," the play was popular. However, Kelly's second comedy, *A Word to the Wise*, produced on March 3, 1770, was abandoned after causing riots during its first two performances.

Musical contributions include the work of Charles Burney, who wrote music for Thomson's *Alfred*, produced at Drury Lane on March 30, 1745. In 1766, Burney also produced *The Cunning Man*, an English adaptation of J.J. Rousseau's operetta *Le Devin du village*.

## Management and Administration
The management of Drury Lane saw various leaders and challenges over the centuries. John Bannister, regarded as the best low comedian of his era, served as manager of Drury Lane in 1802.

In 1823, Alfred Bunn was appointed stage-manager. By 1833, he undertook the joint management of Drury Lane and Covent Garden. Bunn's tenure was marked by vigorous opposition and difficulties with his company and the lord chamberlain. He faced a rivalry with the tragedian Macready, which resulted in Macready assaulting him. Despite being declared bankrupt in 1840, Bunn continued to manage Drury Lane until 1848. Artistically, his period was successful; he produced the principal works of Balfe in an attempt to establish English opera and employed nearly every leading English actor.

## Traditions and Legacy
The theatre maintained specific customs, such as the "Baddeley cake." Robert Baddeley bequeathed £3 per annum to provide wine and cake in the green-room of the Drury Lane theatre on Twelfth Night, a ceremony that remained a regular institution.
