## Early Banking and Deposit Systems
During the reign of James I, goldsmiths in the city began taking deposits, establishing the practice of "deposit-banking." This system evolved further during the Civil War, when these "new-fashioned bankers" began receiving rents from the estates of gentlemen. The goldsmiths offered interest on these funds, even if the money remained in their hands for a lesser time than a single month. 

This arrangement served as a significant allurement for depositors, as their money earned interest until the moment it was needed. Depositors enjoyed the convenience of drawing out their funds in increments of L100 or L50, as they desired. This method was noted for being infinitely less troublesome than lending money out on personal or real security. Consequently, these bankers accumulated large quantities of cash, which enabled the most prominent among them to provide advances to Cromwell based on revenues.

## Scottish Banking and Currency
In Scotland, the Bank of Scotland began issuing notes—referred to at the time as tickets or bills—in several denominations, including L100, L50, L20, L10, and L5. It was not until 1704 that L1 notes were introduced. 

The Bank of Scotland also attempted to establish a branch system. Around the year 1700, branches were opened in Montrose, Dundee, Aberdeen, and Glasgow, but these were closed because expenses exceeded profits. A second attempt to establish agencies in Dundee, Aberdeen, and Glasgow occurred in 1731, but these were discontinued after two years. The bank did not establish branches again until 1774. Other institutions included the Royal Bank of Scotland, established by a charter of incorporation in 1727, and the British Linen Company, which was incorporated in 1746 for linen manufacture before shifting its focus exclusively to banking transactions by 1763.

## Legal Duties and Bills of Sale
The archive records specific stamp duties and fees associated with bills of sale. For an absolute bill of sale, the duty is 5s. on every L50 for amounts exceeding L300; for amounts up to L300, the duty is 2s. 6d. on every L25. In cases where bills of sale are used as security, the duty is 1s. 3d. for every L50 up to L300, and 2s. 6d. for every L100 for amounts over L300.

Filing fees for these bills are determined by the consideration, including further advances. If the consideration does not exceed L100, the fee is 5s. For amounts above L100 but not exceeding L200, the fee is 10s., and for amounts above L200, the fee is L1. The registration of such bills is typically published in trade protection papers, which generally results in the destruction of the credit of the person providing the bill.
