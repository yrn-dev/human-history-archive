# Border Papers (1894-1896)

The *Border Papers*, published between 1894 and 1896 by Bain, serve as a critical historical resource for the verification of facts within the tradition of border ballads. These papers provide a factual baseline—consisting of contemporary letters and despatches—that allows historians to evaluate the accuracy of popular poetry associated with the border regions.

## Relation to Border Ballads
The *Border Papers* are used to demonstrate the discrepancy between historical reality and the narratives found in border ballads. While some authorities, such as Gaston Paris, have suggested that early popular poetry is contemporary with the facts it describes and is improvised accordingly, a comparison between the *Border Papers* and historical border ballads (particularly those from 1595-1600) suggests otherwise.

When compared to the records in Bain's work, it becomes evident that contemporary poets often possessed limited knowledge of the events they described, and the information they did have was frequently incorrect. In many ballads, facts are so distorted or confused that scholars conclude they were likely composed in later generations based on erroneous oral traditions. In other instances, such as *The Queen's Marie*, the original accounts appear to have been defaced by the fantastic interpolations of those reciting the poems.

## Chronology and Composition
Evidence suggests that the existing form of most border ballads does not date earlier than the generation of 1603-1633, following the union of the crowns. The mythopoeic nature of these works persisted well into the future; for example, ballads concerning the sons of Rob Roy remained more or less mythopoeic as late as 1750. Furthermore, the transmission of these ballads was not purely oral; reciters were sometimes inspired by printed broadsheets or "stall copies."

## The Tradition of Border Literature
Border ballads hold a distinctive position in English literature. Much of this material was preserved from oblivion by Sir Walter Scott, who collected materials from the district for his *Minstrelsy of the Scottish Border*, published in 1802 and 1803. The stirring, pathetic, and picturesque incidents of the border country's folklore and traditions also influenced other writers, including John Mackay Wilson (whose *Tales of the Borders* was published in 1835), John Wilson ("Christopher North"), and James Hogg, known as "the Ettrick Shepherd."

Other significant historical and poetic studies of the region include:
* George Ridpath's *Border History of England and Scotland* (1776)
* Professor John Veitch's *History and Poetry of the Scottish Border* (1877)
* Sir George Douglas's *History of the Border Counties* (1890)
* Sir Herbert Maxwell's *History of Dumfries and Galloway* (1896)
* W.S. Crockett's *The Scott Country* (1902)
