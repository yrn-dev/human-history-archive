# Colonel Ingalls

Colonel Ingalls (also referred to as Captain Ingalls or Captain James M. Ingalls, U.S.A.) was a military officer and author who contributed to the field of ballistics, specifically regarding the calculation of trajectories and the development of ballistic tables.

## Contributions to Ballistic Tables
Colonel Ingalls is credited with converting numerical data from kilogramme-metre units to pound-foot units. These converted numbers were utilized by him in the calculation of an extended ballistic table. This extended table serves as a point of comparison for the results found in the abridged table. 

The calculations for these tables can be performed across various regions of velocity using specific formulae to determine the change in time $T(V) - T(v)$, distance $S(V) - S(v)$, and the integral $I(V) - I(v)$. For the arithmetical operations associated with these applications, the use of a slide rule is recommended to achieve practical accuracy.

## Trajectory Calculations and High Angle Fire
In the study of trajectories, Captain Ingalls developed methods to obtain accurate results using a limited number of arcs. In one instance, while Lieutenant A. H. Wolley-Dod, R.A., employed Siacci's method with approximately twenty arcs to calculate a trajectory, Ingalls was able to achieve a very accurate result using only two arcs over the entire trajectory (covering the path up to the vertex and back down). This was achieved by assuming a mean tenuity-factor $[\tau]$ of 0.68, which corresponds to a height of approximately 2 meters, based on an estimate that the shot would reach a height of 3 meters. These findings are detailed in his work, *Handbook of Ballistic Problems*.

Captain James M. Ingalls also provided a convenient rule for approximating high angle trajectories within a single arc. This rule is based on the premise that the mean density of the air can be taken as the density at two-thirds of the estimated height of the vertex. This approach is founded on the observation that in a stream of bullets from a Maxim gun, or a jet of water—representing an unresisted parabolic trajectory—the average height of the shot is two-thirds the height of the vertex.
