# Deaths and Mortal Wounds of Military Leaders

The historical record contains numerous accounts of military commanders and officers who sustained mortal wounds during the course of their service. These events often occurred during pivotal engagements, ranging from ancient Greek battles to 19th-century naval and colonial conflicts.

## Naval Engagements and Mortal Wounds

In the naval conflicts of the late 18th and early 19th centuries, close-quarters combat frequently resulted in fatal injuries for commanding officers. On April 21, 1798, during a duel between the "Mars" and the "Hercule" near the Bec du Raz, the captain of the "Mars" was mortally wounded early in the fight. He died at the moment the French captain, L'Heritier, placed his sword in the captain's hand; L'Heritier himself also died of his wounds.

In a separate naval encounter on June 1, 1813, the British frigate "Shannon," commanded by Sir Philip Bowes Vere Broke, engaged the American frigate "Chesapeake," commanded by Lawrence. The "Shannon" had gained a reputation for excellence in shooting due to Broke's thorough training of his men in gunnery. As the "Chesapeake" rounded to on the "Shannon's" weather quarter at a distance of approximately fifty yards, the British frigate delivered a broadside that immediately struck down one hundred members of the "Chesapeake's" crew.

## Conflicts in British India

During the Mutiny in British India, Sir Henry Lawrence was a central figure in the defense of the residency of Lucknow. Lawrence concentrated a small force within entrenchments covering nearly 60 acres, which included public edifices, detached houses, and batteries welded together by stockades, parapets, and ditches. Sir Henry Lawrence died of a shell-wound at the ruins of the surgeon's house, located beyond the Baillie Guardgate and near the banqueting hall. A marble cross on a 30-foot artificial mound, known as the Lawrence Memorial, now stands close to the residency.

## Ancient Military Casualties

Ancient history records several instances of leaders falling in battle. Epaminondas, a Theban general, received a mortal wound in the breast from a spear during a battle in which his infantry and cavalry charges defeated the enemy. According to Diodorus, Epaminondas had fought on foot in the ranks, using a shield and spear. During a desperate effort to force the Lacedæmonians to give way, he slew the Lacedæmonian commander and several other warriors. While he avoided some darts and turned others away with his shield, some entered his body; he reportedly plucked these out to continue repelling the enemy before finally receiving the fatal spear wound to the breast, which broke off, leaving the point sticking in him.

Similarly, in the eighth year of a war during the thirteenth year of Euphaes’s reign, a bloody battle took place near Ithome. King Euphaes, attacking the battalions of Theopompus with precipitation, received a multitude of wounds, several of which were mortal. Although he appeared to give up the ghost, he was saved and carried off by the Messenians. Despite being mangled and bloody, Euphaes expressed joy that he had not been worsted.

## Other Notable Fatalities

In the Napoleonic era, General Geraud Christophe Michel Duroc, the duc de Frioul and first aide-de-camp to Napoleon, was mortally wounded during the battle of Bautzen in Saxony on May 20-21, 1813. He died in a farmhouse near the battlefield on May 23. In recognition of his service, Napoleon purchased the farm and erected a monument to his memory; Duroc was buried in the Invalides.

Additionally, Arabian authorities record that Abrahah, a Christian Abyssinian who built a church at Sana, attempted to revenge the defilement of his temple by attacking Mecca. After being repelled by a miracle—specifically, his elephant kneeling and refusing to advance—Abrahah fled to Sana, where he was discomfited and mortally wounded.
