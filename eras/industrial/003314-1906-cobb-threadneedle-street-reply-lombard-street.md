# Threadneedle Street and the London Banking System

Threadneedle Street serves as a central hub for British banking, most notably as the location of the Bank of England. The area is closely linked to the broader financial activities of the City of London, including the operations of the Clearing House and the historical evolution of banking houses.

## The Bank of England and Threadneedle Street
The Bank of England is located on Threadneedle Street. Its history has been documented in various works, including T. Fortune's *A Concise and Authentic History of the Bank of England* (1802), John Francis's *History of the Bank of England* (1847), and Sir F. Schuster's *The Bank of England and the State* (1906). J. E. Thorold Rogers provided an account of the institution's first nine years.

The Bank of England maintains a unique relationship with the London Clearing House. While it has never been a member of the Clearing House, it "clears on one side." This means that while the Bank's claims on clearing bankers are processed through the Clearing House, the claims that clearing bankers hold against the Bank of England are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times every day.

## The London Clearing House
The purely city banks formed a "Clearing House" by 1776, though evidence from the books of the "Grasshopper" suggests a less organized settlement system existed as early as 1773, noting a quarterly charge for the use of a clearing-room. A dedicated house for these operations was established in 1810, at which time forty-six banking houses settled their accounts there.

The membership of the Clearing House evolved over time:
* **1844:** Of the forty-nine banks in London, twenty-six were connected to the Clearing House; at this time, membership was restricted to private banks.
* **1854:** Joint-stock banks were admitted to the body.
* **1906:** The membership consisted of nineteen banks in total: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank.

Banks located at Charing Cross or Fleet Street did not belong to the Clearing House. In 1858, Sir John Lubbock (then Lord Avebury) arranged for the clearing of country cheques. This "country clearing" system significantly aided business by allowing cheques from distant English villages to be handled as conveniently as those drawn on London.

## Literature on London Banking
The financial activities of the City, particularly those centered around Lombard Street and Threadneedle Street, have been the subject of extensive analysis. Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873), with a new edition in 1906, is described as a brilliant depiction of the city during that era. Bagehot's work is noted for its scientific, dispassionate description and conversational, epigrammatic style. 

In 1891, A. S. Cobb published *Threadneedle Street, a reply to "Lombard Street"*, providing a counter-perspective to Bagehot's observations. Other significant texts on the London money market and banking practice include A. Crump's *The Key to the London Money Market* (1872) and the works of J. W. Gilbart, specifically *The History, Principles and Practice of Banking* (1882).

## Historical Context and Associated Figures
The banking environment of Threadneedle Street also intersected with intellectual and political life. In 1820, the philosopher Grote settled in a house attached to the bank in Threadneedle Street. From 1823, Grote participated in a society for reading and discussion that met twice a week in a room at the bank before business hours began.

The development of the banking system was preceded by the activities of goldsmiths who kept "running cashes." The *London Directory* of 1677 listed such firms. Some of these early houses, or their descendants, continued into the late 19th century, including Child & Co. of Temple Bar, Hoare & Co. of Fleet Street, and Martin & Co. of Lombard Street. Notably, Sir John Houblon, the first governor of the Bank of England, held an account with the eminent goldsmith and banker Edward Blackwell, though Blackwell was ruined when the exchequer closed in 1672.
