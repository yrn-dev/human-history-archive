# Backergunje

Backergunje, also known as Bakarganj, is a district of British India located in the Dacca division of Eastern Bengal and Assam. It is situated within the joint delta of the Brahmaputra and Ganges rivers and encompasses an area of 4,542 square miles.

## Geography and Landscape
The district is characterized by a flat, even terrain devoid of hills or hillocks. Its landscape is defined by a network of sluggish, dark-colored streams and wide expanses of cultivation. The vegetation is notably green and fresh, particularly during the rainy season, with villages frequently walled by groves of betel-nut palms and bamboos.

Due to its position in the great Gangetic delta, the land is low-lying. The abundance of water-courses, rivers, and natural hollows—often filled with reeds, long grasses, and aquatic plants—makes travel by boat necessary during any season of the year. In the north-west, the region is highly marshy, consisting of rice lands and unreclaimed swamps where scattered huts are built on earth mounds. The southern portion of the district, bordering the Bay of Bengal, contains the forest tracts of the Sundarbans, which serve as a habitat for leopards, tigers, and other wild beasts.

## Hydrography
The district is defined by several principal rivers and their offshoots:

*   **The Meghna:** Flowing along the eastern boundary for approximately 100 miles before entering the Bay of Bengal, this river carries the combined waters of the Ganges and Brahmaputra. Its lower course expands into a large estuary containing various islands, most notably Dakshin Shahbazpur.
*   **The Arial Khan:** A branch of the Ganges that enters from the north and flows south-easterly into the Meghna estuary. It is navigable year-round by the largest native cargo boats; its width ranges from 1,700 yards in the dry season to between 2,000 and 3,000 yards during the rains.
*   **The Haringhata (Baleswar):** Also known as the Garai or Madhumati in different sections, this Ganges offshoot forms the western boundary. It winds through its upper reaches, crosses the Sundarbans, and enters the Bay of Bengal via a deep estuary capable of receiving large ships. It is navigable by large native boats throughout its course and by large sea-going ships as far as Morrellganj in the neighboring district of Jessore.
*   **The Kacha:** An important tributary of the Baleswar, this stream is navigable by large boats throughout the year.

Other rivers of lesser importance include the Nihalganj, Khairabad, Kumar, Ghagar, Bishkhali, and Barisal. All rivers in the district are subject to tidal action from the Bay of Bengal to the south and the Meghna to the north.

## Demographics and Society
As of 1901, the population of Backergunje was 2,291,752, representing a 6% increase over the previous decade. The district includes several towns, with the headquarters station, Barisal, having a population of 18,978 in 1901, followed by Pirojpur with 14,119.

The population is composed of several distinct groups:
*   **Mussulmans:** This group includes the Faraizis, a Puritan sect that is exceedingly numerous in the district. Some sources characterize the Mussulmans of the region as being steeped in prejudice and ignorance, noting a history of litigiousness and violence that necessitated enforced disarmament due to an epidemic of murders.
*   **Buddhists:** This population consists of the Maghs, or people of Arakan, who first settled in Backergunje around 1800. They have been noted for their usefulness in clearing the Sundarbans.
*   **Bebajias:** A numerous gipsy-like tribe that professes Mahommedanism. They primarily live in boats and travel from place to place, earning a living through fishing, trading in trinkets, fortune-telling, and wood-cutting in the Sundarbans.

## Economy and Climate
The material condition of the inhabitants is described as good, as every resident is a small landholder who cultivates enough rice and necessities to support their family. Consequently, hired labor is very scarce. Rice is the primary crop, with three annual harvests: *boro* (spring), *aus* (autumn), and *aman* (winter). Trade is facilitated through small trading villages and periodical fairs.

The climate is considered one of the healthiest in Eastern Bengal, aided by a strong south-west monsoon from the Bay of Bengal that keeps the air cool. The average annual temperature ranges from 78°F to 85°F, with extremes between 62°F and 98°F. However, the use of bad water combined with heavy rainfall and high humidity serves as a source of disease.
