# Global Trade and Production of Silk and Opium

Across various regions of Asia, Europe, and Africa, the production and trade of silk and opium have served as central pillars of local economies, influencing urban development and international commerce.

## Silk Production and Industry
Silk is produced and traded extensively across several continents, ranging from raw material exports to highly specialized manufacturing centers.

### European Centers
In France, the city of Lyons has relied on the silk and silk goods trade for its prosperity for several centuries. This industry developed rapidly following a monopoly granted by Charles VII in 1450 and subsequent patronage from Francis I, Henry II, and Henry IV. The industry has evolved through the invention of various fabrics, including velvets, poplins, satinades, moires, and silk stuffs woofed with gold, silver, or wool. A significant technological advancement occurred in the early 19th century when J. M. Jacquard introduced a loom that allowed a single worker to produce elaborate fabrics by changing "cartoons." 

By the 17th century, Lyons employed between 9,000 and 12,000 looms. While these numbers fluctuated—dropping to 3,000 or 4,000 after the revocation of the edict of Nantes and rising again to 12,000 around 1801—the city remains a primary business center for dyeing and printing. In 1905, the *la Condition des soies* office examined over 4,700 tons of silk; two-thirds of this came from Japan and China, with the remainder from the Levant and Italy. Today, 300,000 workpeople depend on the silk industry in and around Lyons. Additionally, the French port of Calais imports silk goods as part of its trade with the United Kingdom.

### Asian Production
In Japan, Fukuoka is a large center of population where silk-weaving is the most important local industry, with the area of Hakata specifically noted for producing durable silk fabrics. In Korea, the district around Phyöng-yang is noted for being rich in silk, among other resources.

In East Turkestan, the town and oasis of Khotan (locally known as Ilchi) produces silk goods and silk carpets. Similarly, in the oasis of Khiva, the embroidering of silks is a notable domestic industry, and silk cocoons are exported to Russia, while silks are imported from Bokhara. In Bali, silk and gold thread weaving are considered important local activities.

## Opium Trade and Commerce
Opium appears as a significant trade commodity in several diverse geographic hubs, often paired with other agricultural exports.

### Asia Minor and Central Asia
In Asia Minor, the town of Balikisri—the capital of the Karasi sanjak in the vilayet of Brusa—serves as a center of trade for opium, cereals, and silk. Similarly, the vilayet of Konia in Asia Minor lists opium as one of its principal exports, alongside minerals, cotton, wool, livestock, and cereals.

In East Turkestan, the town of Khotan produces opium as a local product, which is then traded actively with China, West Turkestan, and India.

### International Ports
Hong-Kong operates as a free port where opium is among the principal goods dealt with, alongside other commodities such as tea, silk, sugar, ivory, and cotton. The total value of exports and imports in Hong-Kong is estimated at approximately £50,000,000 per year.

## Related Trade Networks
The movement of silk and opium often coincides with the trade of other luxury and raw materials. In Siberia, the town of Kiakhta serves as a chief center of trade between Russia and China. Russians exchange cattle, furs, leather, and woollen cloths for Chinese produce, which includes manufactured silks, porcelain, and teas (the latter accounting for 95% of all imports).

In the vilayet of Konia, the economy is primarily agricultural and pastoral, with the manufacture of silk and cotton stuffs being the only industries. In the oasis of Khotan, the trade of silk and opium is complemented by the export of jade (nephrite), a semi-precious stone highly valued by the Chinese for creating bracelets, bottles, cups, and fancy boxes.
