# Savary Model Bassoon

The Savary model is a specific design of the bassoon, a woodwind instrument characterized by a double reed mouthpiece. As a member of the oboe family, the bassoon serves as its bass. The instrument is distinguished by being the first of its kind to be doubled back upon itself, a design feature that led to its German name (*Fagott*) and Italian name (*fagotto*), both of which are derived from a perceived resemblance to a bundle of sticks. This design evolved from the bass pommer, a straight ancestor measuring 6 feet in length.

## Technical Characteristics and Evolution
The Savary model is identified in archival illustrations as a bassoon featuring 17 keys. In the development of the instrument, the Savary model served as a point of comparison for further mechanical and theoretical advancements. 

Carl Almenraeder introduced several improvements to the bassoon based on acoustic principles. One significant issue Almenraeder addressed was a phenomenon where the pitch of a note is lowered and the tone is damped when the diameter of the bell or lateral opening is smaller than that of the bore. This was particularly evident in the A (A2) of the bassoon, where the hole was considered too small in diameter and positioned too high.

To correct this, Almenraeder readjusted the A hole—which is stopped by the third finger of the right hand—by boring lower down the tube. Instead of a single large hole, he implemented two medium-diameter holes covered by an open key. One of these holes communicates with the wider bore, while the other communicates with the narrower bore in the butt joint, resulting in a tone that is full, accurate, and clear.

## Comparison with the Almenraeder Model
While the improvements made by Almenraeder resulted in an instrument that was theoretically and mechanically more perfect than the Savary model, the Almenraeder design was noted for lacking some of the specific characteristics found in the bassoon of the Savary type. Despite this, Almenraeder's improvements were generally adopted in Germany. His 16-key model was followed by most makers, including Heckel of Biebrich.

## General Bassoon Context
The bassoon typically possesses an eight-foot tone. Its standard compass extends from Bb bass (B1b) to Ab treble (A4b), though modern versions with additional mechanisms can reach C or F (C5 or F5). These highest notes are referred to as *vox humana* due to their extreme sweetness. 

The instrument's pitch is a twelfth below that of the oboe. The fundamental scale of the bassoon is F major; notes from F down to Bb are extra notes achieved via interlocking keys on the long joint, operated by the left thumb. The complex fingering system involves a combination of holes, keys, cross fingering, and overblowing.

## Musical Usage
The bassoon has been utilized by various great masters, though it was not a favorite of Handel. Notable uses include:
* **Beethoven:** Employed the instrument extensively in his symphonies for original and beautiful independent parts.
* **Bach:** Included parts for two bassoons in his mass in B min.
* **Mozart:** Composed a concerto in Bb for bassoon and orchestra (Kochel, No. 191), and used the instrument in chamber music, such as a short adagio for bassoon and two basset horns.
* **Weber:** Wrote a concerto for bassoon in F (op. 75) scored for full orchestra.

Additionally, the instrument maker W. Heckel of Biebrich-am-Rhein, acting on the instigation of Wagner, produced bassoons with an extra key to extend the downward compass to A.
