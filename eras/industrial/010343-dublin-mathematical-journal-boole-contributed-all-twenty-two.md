# George Boole and the Cambridge and Dublin Mathematical Journal

George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician whose work bridged the gap between mathematical investigation and logical reasoning. Throughout his career, Boole published approximately fifty scattered articles and several separate publications. Among these, he contributed a total of twenty-two articles to the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* and its successor, the *Cambridge and Dublin Mathematical Journal*.

## Early Life and Academic Career
Born in Lincoln on November 2, 1815, Boole was the son of a studious tradesman who provided his first lessons in mathematical science. Although he initially favored classics, Boole began studying higher mathematics at age seventeen, though his progress was slowed by a lack of efficient assistance. 

Boole spent much of his life in the scholastic profession. At sixteen, he served as an assistant-master at a private school in Doncaster. He later established his own school in Lincoln, which he eventually moved to Waddington. In 1849, he was appointed professor of mathematics at Queen's College in Cork. He married Mary Everest in 1855; she later authored educational works based on her husband's principles.

## Mathematical Contributions and Publications
Boole was recognized by the public as the author of several standard works and numerous complex papers. His first published paper, "Theory of Analytical Transformations," appeared in the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* in 1839. This publication initiated a friendship with the journal's editor, D.F. Gregory, which lasted until Gregory's death in 1844.

During his lifetime, Boole completed two systematic treatises on mathematical subjects: the *Treatise on Differential Equations* (1859) and the *Treatise on the Calculus of Finite Differences* (1860), the latter of which served as a sequel to the first. These works combined elementary exposition with profound philosophical investigation. In the *Differential Equations* treatise, Boole provided an account of a general symbolic method and a general method in analysis, the latter of which had been previously described in a famous 1844 memoir printed in the *Philosophical Transactions*.

Boole was distinguished by his ability to separate symbols of operation from symbols of quantity, treating them as distinct objects of calculation. He maintained a high degree of confidence in results derived from treating symbols according to their primary laws and conditions.

## Logical Theory and the Laws of Thought
Boole is regarded as one of the first English mathematicians since John Wallis to write on logic, alongside contemporaries such as Augustus de Morgan. Boole applied his confidence in symbolic reasoning to the field of logic, though he did not view logic as a branch of mathematics. Instead, he identified a deep analogy between algebraic symbols and those representing logical forms and syllogisms.

In 1847, he published a pamphlet titled *Mathematical Analysis of Logic*, which he later considered a hasty and imperfect exposition. He viewed his 1854 work, *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, on which are founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities*, as the mature statement of his views.

In this system, Boole used the number 1 to denote the universe of thinkable objects and literal symbols (such as x, y, z, v, u) to represent elective meanings similar to adjectives and substantives. For example, if x represents "horned" and y represents "sheep," the combination of x and y represents "horned sheep." Boole demonstrated that these elective symbols follow the same primary laws of combination as algebraical symbols, allowing them to be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided. For instance, "1 - x" represents all things that are not horned.

The *Laws of Thought* also introduced a general symbolic method of logical inference to draw conclusions from premises and an attempt to discover a general method in probabilities to determine the probability of connected events.

## Other Works and Personal Life
Beyond his technical works, Boole was well-versed in general literature. He particularly admired Dante, preferring the *Paradiso* to the *Inferno*, and studied the ethics of Spinoza, the metaphysics of Aristotle, and the works of Cicero. He also wrote and delivered four addresses on various topics: *The Social Aspect of Intellectual Culture*, *The Claims of Science*, *The Right Use of Leisure*, and *The Genius of Sir Isaac Newton*.

Boole's professional contributions were recognized through an honorary LL.D. from the University of Dublin and a medal from the Royal Society for his 1844 memoir. He was described by friends as modest and dedicated to the pursuit of truth. George Boole died of a fever that resulted in suffusion on the lungs on December 8, 1864.
