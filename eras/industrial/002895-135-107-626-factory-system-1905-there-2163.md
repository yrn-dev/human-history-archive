# Industrial Manufacturing and the Factory System (1880–1910)

The development of the "factory system" in the United States during the late 19th and early 20th centuries is characterized by significant growth in product value and the expansion of specialized industrial centers across various states, including Illinois, Virginia, Idaho, and Alabama.

## Manufacturing in Baltimore
Baltimore experienced rapid growth as a manufacturing city starting in 1880. In that year, the city had 3,683 manufacturing establishments with a total annual product value of $78,417,304. By 1900, this number grew to 6,359 establishments, producing commodities valued at $161,249,240. Of these 1900 establishments, 2,274 operated under the factory system, contributing $135,107,626 to the total value. By 1905, there were 2,163 establishments under the factory system, which produced an annual product valued at $151,546,580, representing a 12.2% increase over the previous five years.

Baltimore ranked eighth among U.S. manufacturing centers by product value in the 1880, 1890, and 1900 censuses, though it dropped to ninth in 1905. The city was a primary center for several industries:
*   **Canning and Preserving:** Baltimore was the most important U.S. center for this industry. In 1905, its output for preserving fruits and vegetables was $5,981,541 (7.7% of the U.S. total), down from 15% of the national total in 1900. The city was the center of the oyster-canning industry until 1900, when canneries began appearing in southern states like Mississippi. Thomas Kensett built what appeared to be the first American oyster-canning establishment in Baltimore in 1820, and the industry began on a permanent footing there in 1850.
*   **Clothing:** In 1905, Baltimore ranked fourth in the U.S. for clothing manufacture, with a value of $22,684,656.
*   **Other Industries:** Notable 1905 outputs included foundry and machine shop products ($6,572,925), shirts ($5,710,783), tinware ($5,705,980), and cigar and cigarette-making ($4,360,366). The city also produced fertilizers, furniture, and bricks (approximately 150,000,000 annually), and its cotton-duck output accounted for about three-fourths of the total for the United States.

## Industrial Growth in Illinois
Manufacturing in Illinois saw remarkable growth in the latter half of the 19th century, driven by exceptional transportation facilities. The state's rank in the value of its manufactures rose from fifteenth in 1850 to third in 1890 and 1900. Between 1850 and 1900, the average decade increases for products and invested capital were 152.9% and 189.26%, respectively.

In 1900, the capital invested in Illinois was $776,829,598, with $732,829,771 of that in factory system establishments. The product value in 1900 was $1,259,730,168, of which $1,120,868,308 came from the factory system. By 1905, the factory system's product value rose to $1,410,342,129 (a 25.8% increase) and invested capital rose to $975,844,799 (a 33.2% increase).

In the city of Belleville, Illinois, industrial development began in 1852 with the sinking of the first coal mine. The value of the city's factory products grew from $2,873,334 in 1900 to $4,356,615 in 1905. Its industries include rolling mills, distilleries, breweries, flour mills, glass works, copper refining, shoe factories, and factories for stoves, ranges, nails, tacks, and agricultural implements.

## Regional Centers in the South and West
### Virginia and Alabama
In Danville, Virginia, the river provided water-power for manufactories. In 1900, the total product value was $8,103,484, with $3,693,792 categorized as "factory" product; by 1905, the factory product value increased to $4,774,818. Danville ranked first in the state for the value of cotton goods produced in 1905.

In Birmingham, Alabama, the city served as the principal manufacturing center of the state and the most important center for iron production in the southern states. While the total value of manufactory products increased from $7,064,248 in 1890 to $12,581,066 in 1900, the specific output of establishments under the "factory system" decreased by 11.7%, falling from $8,599,418 in 1900 to $7,592,958 in 1905.

### Idaho
Idaho's manufacturing was primarily for local demand. The value of products from the factory system was $3,001,442 in 1900, increasing by 192.2% to $8,768,743 by 1905. The primary industries were:
*   **Lumber and Timber:** Valued at $908,670 in 1900 and $2,834,506 in 1905. In 1906, the Weyerhauser Syndicate built a lumber mill at Potlatch with a daily capacity of 750,000 ft.
*   **Flour and Grist Mills:** Valued at $1,584,473 in 1905.
*   **Steam-car Construction:** Valued at $913,670 in 1905.
*   **Beet Sugar:** Refineries were installed at Blackfoot, Idaho Falls, Nampa, and Sugar (or Sugar City). Between 57,000,000 and 64,000,000 lb. of beet sugar were refined in 1906.

## Other Major Manufacturing Hubs
### Buffalo, New York
Buffalo ranked second in New York state for manufactures. In 1900, manufactures were valued at $122,230,061, with the factory product accounting for $105,627,182. By 1905, the factory product value rose to $147,377,873. Key 1905 industries included slaughtering and meat-packing ($12,216,433 and $3,919,940 for non-packing slaughtering), foundry and machine shop products ($11,402,855), and flour and grist mill products ($9,807,906).

### Louisville, Kentucky
Louisville became a large commercial center due to its transport facilities and local resources. The value of factory-made products increased from $66,110,474 in 1900 to $83,204,125 in 1905. The most valuable manufacture in 1905 was snuff and smoking and chewing tobacco (specifically plug tobacco) at $11,635,367. Combined with cigars and cigarettes ($1,225,347), these products made up 15.5% of the city's factory product value.
