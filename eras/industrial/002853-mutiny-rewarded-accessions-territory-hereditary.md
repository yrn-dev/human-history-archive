# Rewards and Consequences of the Mutiny

In the history of British India, the Mutiny of 1857 served as a pivotal event that resulted in significant shifts in territory, status, and privilege for various local rulers and districts based on their allegiance to the British government.

## Rewards for Loyalty in the United Provinces
Several rulers in the United Provinces were granted substantial rewards for their conspicuous loyalty during the Mutiny. In Balrampur, a town near the river Rapti in the Gonda district, the raja, Sir Drigbijai Singh K.C.S.I., was rewarded with hereditary privileges and accessions of territory. The Balrampur estate, one of the largest *talukdari* estates in the province, had an estimated income of £120,000 and paid a revenue of £46,000. Following the death of Sir Drigbijai Singh in 1882, the estate became the subject of prolonged litigation and was thrown into chancery.

In the state of Bijawar, Bhan Pratap Singh rendered signal services to the British during the Mutiny. As a reward, he was granted certain privileges and a hereditary salute of eleven guns. His status continued to rise in subsequent years; he received the title of maharaja in 1866 and the prefix *sawai* in 1877. Upon his death in 1899, he was succeeded by his adopted son, Sanwant Singh, who was a son of the maharaja of Orchha.

## Loyalty and Recognition in Bengal
In lower Bengal, the maharaja of Burdwan also maintained loyalty to the government during the mutiny of 1857, as well as during the Santal rebellion of 1855. This loyalty, combined with the excellent management of Maharaja Mahtab Chand (who died in 1879), resulted in the grant of a coat of arms in 1868 and the right to a personal salute of 13 guns in 1877. The *raj* of Burdwan, founded in 1657 by Abu Ra Kapur, became the headquarters for the premier nobleman of lower Bengal, with a rent-roll exceeding £300,000.

## Equivocal Positions and Rebellion
Not all rulers maintained a clear stance during the conflict. In the state of Bundi, the ruler Ram Singh—described as a "grand specimen of the Rajput gentleman" and the most conservative prince in conservative Rajputana—maintained an equivocal attitude during the mutiny of 1857. Despite this ambiguity, he continued to enjoy the favor of the British government, receiving the titles of C.I.E. in 1878 and G.C.S.I. and counsellor of the empire in 1877.

Conversely, some regions actively joined the rebellion. The district of Banda, part of Bundelkhand, was poverty-stricken and over-taxed at the time of the Mutiny, leading the district to join the rebels. The town of Banda was eventually recovered by General Whitlock on April 20, 1858. Following this, the fiscal system was remodelled, which led to a period of greater prosperity, though this was occasionally interrupted by famine.
