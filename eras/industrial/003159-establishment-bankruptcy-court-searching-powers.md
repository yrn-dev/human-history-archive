# The Establishment and Evolution of Bankruptcy Courts

The establishment of bankruptcy courts is defended on the basis that the administration of justice affects the interests of the community at large. These courts possess searching powers of investigation and the authority to enforce penalties for misconduct. Such oversight is intended to prevent the administration of justice from becoming a mere matter of barter and arrangement between individuals, which can lead to abuses such as secret understandings between debtors and favored creditors or the creation of fictitious debts.

## Evolution of the English System
Originally, jurisdiction over bankruptcy was exercised by commissioners appointed by the lord chancellor. In 1831, a special court of bankruptcy was established, comprising six commissioners, four judges serving as a court of review, and official assignees tasked with collecting and distributing the assets of the bankrupt. At this time, non-traders were excluded from this court; instead, a "court for relief of insolvent debtors" was created for them. In that court, non-traders could avoid imprisonment by surrendering their property, though they were not discharged from their debts, leaving any subsequently acquired property liable.

Both of these courts were eventually abolished. Non-traders were later permitted to obtain the benefits of bankruptcy laws, including a discharge. In 1869, the system of official assignees was replaced by a new court of bankruptcy led by one of the vice-chancellors as chief judge, supported by subordinate registrars or inferior judges. This court was also subsequently abolished. Currently, bankruptcy business is administered by a judge of the high court appointed by the lord chancellor, with high court registrars handling judicial routines in the London district and county-court judges performing similar duties throughout the rest of the country.

## Administrative Reforms and the Board of Trade
The Act of 1883 shifted the treatment of bankruptcy from a matter of legal procedure to one of commercial legislation. The objective was to ensure the equitable distribution of assets while enforcing commercial morality for the benefit of the trading community. A primary feature of this act was the separation of judicial and administrative functions. Administrative duties were transferred to the Board of Trade, a public department of the state responsible to parliament.

Under this act, the bankruptcy department of the Board of Trade was created, headed by the inspector-general in bankruptcy. This department supervises all administrative work, audits the accounts of trustees, and possesses the power to remove trustees for unfitness, neglect, or misconduct, subject to court appeal. Additionally, the Board of Trade appoints official receivers who act as officers of the courts. These receivers manage estates interimly until creditors are consulted and oversee the conduct of bankrupts.

## International Systems of Bankruptcy

### Germany
Bankruptcy in Germany is governed by a code passed in 1877, which replaced a "wholly chaotic" system of individual state laws. German law distinguishes between commercial failure and bankruptcy, which is categorized as either simple or fraudulent. Simple bankruptcy results from offenses such as extravagance in living, disorderly book-keeping, gambling, or dealing in "futures." Fraudulent bankruptcy involves more serious offenses, including the falsifying of books, the manufacture of fictitious debts, the concealment of property, and the giving of illegal preferences. 

Fraudulent bankruptcy is punishable by penal servitude, or imprisonment for at least three months in mitigating circumstances. Accessories, such as creditors who conspire with a debtor to prejudice other debtors, are also liable to penal servitude. In Germany, assets are typically sold by auction for rapid liquidation. Creditors may accept a composition if a majority in number and three-fourths in value agree, provided the court approves the arrangement.

### Italy
Under the Commercial Code of 1883, only merchants—defined as those who engage in commercial business as a habitual profession, including merchant companies—can pass through the bankruptcy court. Proceedings may be initiated by the debtor, by a creditor for a commercial debt, or ordered by the court. Bankruptcy is declared only where there is insolvency; the amount of debt is immaterial as long as non-payment proves insolvency. 

The court's judgment deprives the bankrupt of the right to administer their affairs and appoints a trustee to realize property under the supervision of a judge and a commission of creditors. All movable and immovable property is sold by auction. Bankrupts may face criminal proceedings for offenses such as failing to keep books as prescribed by law.

## Regional Variations in the United Kingdom

### Ireland
Irish bankruptcy is regulated by statutes from 1857 and 1872, and the Irish Debtors Act 1872. While it shares features with older English enactments and modifications from the English act of 1869, Ireland never imported the system of liquidation by arrangement and composition without court control. Local bankruptcy courts were established in certain districts in 1888. The Deeds of Arrangement Act 1887 and the Irish Deeds of Arrangement Amendment Act 1890 align Irish law with English law regarding the publicity and registration of such deeds.

### Scotland
In Scotland, the Bankruptcy Frauds and Disabilities (Scotland) Act 1884 applies criminal provisions regarding the obtaining of credit of £20 or more by an undischarged bankrupt without disclosure. The Judicial Factors Act of 1889 seeks to limit administration costs by requiring that trustee remuneration be intimated to creditors and the accountant of court, who may modify the rate. Unlike England and Ireland, the Deeds of Arrangement Act 1887 does not apply to Scotland, and there is no requirement to register private deeds of assignment for validity. The office of accountant in bankruptcy was abolished in 1889, with duties merged into the office of accountant of the court of session.
