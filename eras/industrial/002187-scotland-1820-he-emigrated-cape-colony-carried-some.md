# Scotland

The archive's records regarding Scotland primarily document individuals born in the region who emigrated to other parts of the world or returned to the country after achieving prominence abroad.

## Notable Emigrants and Figures

Several individuals of Scottish origin are recorded as having moved to North America and Africa during the late 18th and early 19th centuries.

**Sir Alexander Mackenzie (c. 1755–1820)**
Probably a native of Inverness, Mackenzie emigrated to North America at a young age. He spent several years working in the fur trade at Fort Chippewyan, located at the head of Lake Athabasca. From this base, he conducted two major journeys of exploration:
* In 1789, he traveled from Fort Chippewyan along the Great Slave Lake and down the river that now bears his name to reach the Arctic Ocean.
* Between 1792 and 1793, he traveled from Fort Chippewyan across the Rocky Mountains to the Pacific coast near Cape Menzies.

Mackenzie published an account of these travels in 1801 titled *Voyages on the River St Lawrence and through the Continent of North America to the Frozen and Pacific Oceans*, which provided information on native tribes and included a dissertation on the Canadian fur trade. After amassing considerable wealth, he was knighted in 1802 and eventually settled back in Scotland. He died on March 11, 1820, at Mulnain, near Dunkeld.

**William Lyon Mackenzie (1795–1861)**
Born on March 12, 1795, near Dundee, Scotland, William Lyon Mackenzie grew up in poverty following the death of his father. After working for six years in a shop at Alyth, he emigrated to Canada with his mother in April 1820. He established himself as a general merchant in Queenston, Dundas, and York. 

Mackenzie entered politics due to the discontented state of Upper Canada. On May 18, 1824, he published the first issue of the *Colonial Advocate* at Queenston, using the paper to attack the ruling oligarchy. This caused significant anger among the political and social circles in York (Toronto), led by John Beverley Robinson. In November 1824, Mackenzie moved to Toronto. Although his office was attacked and his printing type thrown into the bay by opponents, he was awarded £625 and costs in a legal action against the rioters. This allowed him to establish a more efficient plant; the *Colonial Advocate* continued publication until November 4, 1834.

**Andrew Geddes Bain (1797–1864)**
A native of Scotland and a British geologist, Bain emigrated to Cape Colony in 1820. For several years, he operated a saddlery business at Graaf Reinet. During the Kaffir War of 1833–34, he commanded a provisional battalion raised for frontier defense. 

Bain later transitioned into engineering, constructing a military road through the Ecca Pass. His talents led to permanent employment as a surveyor of military roads under the corps of Royal Engineers. This work sparked an interest in geology, which was furthered in 1837 when he was loaned Lyell's *Elements*. Bain discovered various reptilia remains, including the *Dicynodon* from the Karroo Beds near Fort Beaufort. In 1852, he prepared the first comprehensive geological map of South Africa, which the Geological Society of London published in 1856. He died in Cape Town in 1864.
