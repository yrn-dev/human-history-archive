# Sequestration and Bankruptcy in Scotland

## Overview of the Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act 1856
The law of bankruptcy in Scotland is primarily regulated by the Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act of 1856. Under this legislation, "notour" bankruptcy—meaning bankruptcy that has been legally declared—is constituted through two primary means: sequestration (which is referred to as adjudication in Ireland and England) or insolvency. In the case of insolvency, the status is achieved when it concurs with either a duly executed charge for payment or the sale of the debtor's effects under a sequestration for rent or a poinding, or when the debtor makes an application for the benefit of *cessio bonorum*.

A notour bankruptcy continues until the insolvency ceases or, in cases of sequestration, until the debtor has obtained a discharge.

## Awarding and Recalling Sequestration
Sequestration may be awarded by either the sheriff or the court of session. The law provides specific conditions under which the estate of a person may be sequestered:

1.  **Living Debtors:** If the debtor is subject to the jurisdiction of Scottish courts, sequestration can occur via the debtor's own petition (with the concurrence of qualified creditors) or via a petition from qualified creditors. In the latter case, the debtor must be a notour bankrupt and must have maintained a place of business or a dwelling-house in Scotland within the previous year.
2.  **Deceased Debtors:** If the debtor was subject to the court's jurisdiction at the time of death, sequestration may be petitioned for by qualified creditors or by the debtor's mandatory.

A sequestration is not necessarily permanent; it may be recalled if a majority in number and four-fifths in value of the creditors prefer to wind up the estate through a private arrangement.

## Administration and Legal Obligations
When a sequestration proceeds, creditors hold a meeting to elect a trustee to administer the estate, chosen by a majority in value. Additionally, three commissioners—who are either mandatories or creditors—are elected to control the administration and assist in declaring dividends.

The bankrupt is legally required to provide all available information regarding their estate. Failure to do so may result in imprisonment. Furthermore, the bankrupt must undergo a public examination on oath before the sheriff; "conjunct and confident persons" are also subject to examination.

## Discharge and Amendments
A bankrupt may be discharged either with or without composition. If discharged without composition, the process follows specific timelines and requirements:
*   By petition with the concurrence of all creditors.
*   After six months, with the concurrence of a majority and four-fifths in value of the creditors.
*   After eighteen months, with the concurrence of a bare majority in both number and value.
*   After two years, without any concurrence. In this final instance, a judge may refuse the application if the bankrupt willfully failed to comply with the law or fraudulently concealed effects.

The 1856 Act was modified by several subsequent laws. The Bankruptcy and Real Securities Act 1857 addressed the cost of competition for trusteeships. The Bankruptcy Amendment (Scotland) Act 1860 allowed the court to recall a sequestration if winding up the estate in Ireland or England was more convenient. The Bankruptcy Amendment Act (Scotland) 1875 established preferential claims for wages; workmen's claims are preferential for up to two months, while servants, shopmen, and clerks are covered for up to four months, with amounts not exceeding L50.

## Later Legislation and Administrative Changes
The Debtors (Scotland) Act 1880 abolished imprisonment for debt, though it maintained provisions for punishing fraudulent bankrupts. Regarding fraud, the Bankruptcy Frauds and Disabilities (Scotland) Act 1884 applied criminal provisions from the English Bankruptcy Act 1883 to decrees of cessio and sequestrations. This specifically concerned undischarged bankrupts obtaining credit of L20 or more without disclosing their status, and it aligned Scottish disqualification laws with English law.

Administrative costs were addressed by the Judicial Factors Act of 1889. This act required that the rate of remuneration for a trustee under sequestration be intimated to the accountant of court and the creditors before being acted upon. The accountant of court was empowered to modify this remuneration if deemed expedient, subject to appeal. This same 1889 Act abolished the office of accountant in bankruptcy (established in 1856), merging its duties into the office of accountant of the court of session.

Finally, it is noted that Scotland does not have provisions analogous to the Deeds of Arrangement Act 1887 (which applies to Ireland and England), and there is no requirement in Scotland for the registration of private deeds of assignment for the benefit of creditors to ensure their validity.
