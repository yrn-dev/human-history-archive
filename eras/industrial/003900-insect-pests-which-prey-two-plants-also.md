# Insect Pests and Predation

In the natural world, insects act both as destructive pests to agricultural crops and as prey for various animals and plants. Their interactions with flora and other fauna range from the consumption of plant sap and tissues to being captured by insectivorous plants or hunted by aerial predators.

## Agricultural Pests

Various insects target specific crops, causing significant economic and botanical damage.

### Cereal and Vegetable Pests
Barley is susceptible to several insect pests, similar to those that attack wheat. In Great Britain, the larvae of the ribbon-footed corn-fly (*Chlorops taeniopus*) caused substantial injury to barley crops in 1893, a period when the plants had been weakened by extreme drought. In the case of asparagus, two types of beetles serve as pests: the "common asparagus beetle" (*Crioceris asparagi*), which was introduced to America around 1856, and the "twelve-spotted" or "red" asparagus beetle (*C. duodecimpunctata*), which was detected in America in 1881.

### Hop Crop Pests
The cultivation of hops is frequently aggravated by recurring attacks of aphis blight, caused by the insect *Aphis (Phorodon) humuli*. To combat this, growers spray every branch and leaf three or four times a season with insecticidal solutions. These solutions typically consist of 100 gallons of water mixed with 4 to 8 lb. of soft soap and 8 to 10 lb. of quassia chip extract. The soft soap acts as a vehicle to keep the bitter quassia on the leaves, starving out the aphides.

Another destructive pest of the hop crop is the red spider (*Tetranychus telarius*), a type of "spinning mite" that is most active during very hot summers. These spiders congregate on the undersides of leaves to exhaust the sap, causing the leaves to fall—a phenomenon known in Germany as "fire-blast." Because quassia is ineffective against red spiders, growers use a solution of 8 to 10 lb. of soft soap and three pints of paraffin per 100 gallons of water. This must be applied with great force to break through the spiders' protective webs.

## Insects as Prey

Insects serve as a primary food source for a variety of organisms, including other insects, specialized plants, and birds.

### Insectivorous Plants
The butterwort (*Pinguicula vulgaris*), a small herb found in wet, boggy lands of the north temperate and arctic zones, captures insects using its fleshy, sticky leaves. When a small insect settles on the leaf, it is caught in a viscid excretion containing a digestive enzyme that makes the insect's nitrogenous substances soluble for absorption. Upon capture, the excretion becomes strongly acid, and the leaf margins curve inward to ensure complete contact with the prey.

### Predatory Insects
The dragon-fly (known in various regions as "horse-stingers" or "devil's darning needles") is a predator that preys upon other insects. Its physical structure is adapted for this purpose; the anterior pair of legs likely assists in capturing and holding prey, while its overall agility allows it to quit a position of repose quickly to chase passing insects. In the prehistoric record, some early dragon-flies were very large, with wings spreading up to twenty-nine inches.

### Avian Predators
The ordinary kestrel of Europe (*Falco tinnunculus*), the most common bird of prey in the British Islands, hunts by hanging in the air with rapid wing beats to spot prey. While its diet consists chiefly of mice, the kestrel also consumes insects, such as cockchafers, as well as small birds, frogs, and earthworms.

## Specialized Insect Rearing
Not all interactions between insects and plants are predatory or parasitic. The cochineal insect (*Coccus Cacti*) is nurtured on species of *Opuntia* (such as *O. coccinellifera*, also called *Nopalea*, or *O. Tuna*). Specialized plantations called nopaleries, which can contain up to 50,000 plants, are established to rear these insects. Females are placed on the plants around August, and three crops of cochineal are produced annually. While native to Mexico, much of the supply is sourced from the Canary Islands and Colombia.
