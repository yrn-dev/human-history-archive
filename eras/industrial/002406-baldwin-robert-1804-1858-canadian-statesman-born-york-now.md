# BALDWIN, ROBERT (1804-1858)

Robert Baldwin was a Canadian statesman born on May 12, 1804, in York (now Toronto). He was the son of William Warren Baldwin, a man of wealth and good family who had emigrated from Ireland to Canada in 1798. Although a devoted member of the Church of England, the elder Baldwin opposed the political and religious oligarchy then leading Canadian affairs, and he raised his son according to these same principles.

## Early Career and Political Philosophy
Robert Baldwin was called to the Bar in 1825 and entered into a legal partnership with his father. His entry into politics began in 1829 when he was elected as a member of the parliament of Upper Canada for the town of York. Following a defeat in the subsequent year, Baldwin retired into private life for a period. During the following six years, he became known as "the man of one idea" due to his constant advocacy for a responsible executive, which he viewed as the sole remedy for the economic and political evils of the era.

## Executive Service and Reform
In 1836, the lieutenant-governor, Sir Francis Bond Head, called Baldwin to the executive council. However, Baldwin resigned within a month, finding himself without influence and forced to support measures he opposed. While Baldwin was a reformer, he strongly disapproved of the rebellion that took place between 1837 and 1838.

Following the union of the two Canadas, Baldwin became a member of the executive council in 1841 under Lord Sydenham. He resigned shortly thereafter over the issue of responsible government.

## Liberal Administrations
In 1842, Baldwin formed the first Liberal administration in connection with Mr. (later Sir) L. H. Lafontaine. This administration ended in resignation the following year after a quarrel with the governor-general, Sir Charles Metcalfe, regarding a question of patronage that Baldwin believed involved the principle of responsible government. Although the governor-general was sustained by a narrow majority in the ensuing general election, the Liberals returned to power in 1848.

Under Lord Elgin, Baldwin and Mr. Lafontaine formed their second administration. This government implemented several significant reforms, including the introduction of an important municipal system into Upper Canada and the removal of the Provincial University from sectarian control. The La Fontaine-Baldwin administration is noted for maintaining power after the contest of 1848, which achieved the establishment of responsible government.

## Later Years and Resignation
Internal conflicts within the Liberal party led to Baldwin's resignation in 1851. The immediate cause was an attempt to abolish the court of chancery of Upper Canada—an institution established by a measure Baldwin himself had introduced in 1849. Although the attempt to abolish the court failed, it was supported by a majority of representatives from Upper Canada, which Baldwin's "fastidious conscience" interpreted as a vote of no confidence. Additionally, Baldwin found himself unable to approve the advanced views of the Radicals, who became known as "Clear Grits."

When seeking re-election in York, Baldwin was defeated after declining to provide a pledge on the "burning question" of the Clergy Reserves. In 1858, a coalition known as the Liberal-Conservative party (formed in 1854) attempted to recruit him as a candidate for the elective upper house. Baldwin refused to stand, as he could not approve of the tactics used by the opponents of the advanced reformers, despite having broken with the reformers themselves.

## Legacy and Death
Robert Baldwin died on December 9, 1858. He was remembered for the purity and unselfishness of his motives, a sentiment shared even by those who had bitterly attacked his political measures. After the concession of responsible government, Baldwin dedicated himself to fostering a good understanding between the French-speaking and English-speaking inhabitants of Canada. Consequently, he is held in high regard in his native province of Ontario as well as among French Canadians.
