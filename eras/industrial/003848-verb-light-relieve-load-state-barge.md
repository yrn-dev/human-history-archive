# Barge

A barge is generally defined as a flat-bottomed boat used for the transport of goods on inland navigations. Historically, the term referred to a small sailing vessel. The word is derived from the Medieval Latin *barca* (possibly connected to the Latin *baris* or Greek *baris*, a boat used on the Nile), and is related to the Old French *bargaigne*.

## Types and Functions of Barges
Barges are utilized in various capacities depending on the environment and the nature of the cargo:

*   **Inland and Canal Navigation:** On canals, barges are typically towed, though some are equipped with engines. The individuals responsible for these vessels are called bargees.
*   **Tidal Rivers:** In tidal waters, "sailing barges" are provided with masts and sails. Alternatively, "dumb-barges" drift with the current and are guided by one or more long oars.
*   **Harbor Operations:** Barges used specifically for loading or unloading the cargo of ships in harbors are known as "lighters," a term derived from the verb "to light," meaning to relieve of a load.
*   **State and Ceremonial Use:** A state barge is a heavy vessel, often highly ornamented, used to carry passengers during state ceremonials.
*   **Specialized Use:** In Oxford, college barges serve as houseboats moored in the river for members of college rowing clubs. In New England, the term "barge" frequently refers to a covered vehicle with side seats used for picnic parties or transporting passengers to railway stations or piers.

## Regional Variations and Examples
Different regions have developed specific barge-like vessels to suit their geography:

**The Nile**
The *dahabeah* (also spelled *dahabiya*, *dahabiyeh*, or *dahabeeyah*) is a native passenger boat used on the Nile. It is a barge-like house-boat with sails, resembling painted galleys found on the tombs of the Pharaohs. These vessels were used by the Mahommedan rulers of Egypt as state barges and were ornamented with gilding (which is one possible derivation of the name from the Arabic *dahab*, meaning gold). While traditionally used by travelers ascending the Nile, modern versions are often made of iron, draw approximately 2 feet of water, and accommodate between two and a dozen passengers. Steam dahabeahs are also constructed for tourists.

**The Danube**
The Danube river utilizes various barge capacities based on the section of the river. Between Braila and Turnu Severin, the river is open to flat barges with capacities ranging from 1,500 to 2,000 tons. From Turnu Severin to Orsova, barges draw 6 feet of water. Barges of 600 tons register can be towed from the lower Danube as far as Regensburg. Various entities operate these fleets, including the Societe Imperiale et Royale Autrichienne of Vienna, the Rumanian State Navigation Company, the Hungarian Navigation Company, the Servian government, and private owners.

## Industrial and Military Applications
Barges are frequently employed in large-scale logistics and engineering:

*   **Agriculture:** In London, stable manure is transported by barge or rail to be used for manuring.
*   **Construction:** During the erection of the Luiz I. bridge over the Douro, wire cables were used to lift members from barges for assembly.
*   **Military Logistics:** During the Third Burmese War (1885-86), the British campaign utilized a river fleet of 55 steamers, launches, and barges (also called "flats") from the Irrawaddy Flotilla Company to convey troops and stores from Rangoon to Mandalay.
*   **Remote Navigation:** On the lower Shiré, from the Chinde mouth of the Zambezi to Port Herald, barges are used to complete voyages during the dry season (April-November) when steamers cannot ascend the river.
