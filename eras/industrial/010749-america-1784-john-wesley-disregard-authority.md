# Methodism and Early English Influence in America

## The Introduction of Methodism to the American Colonies
Methodism was introduced into the American colonies three years prior to October 1771. At that time, the number of converts to the movement was small, totaling scarcely 300. The growth of the movement accelerated significantly with the arrival of Francis Asbury, an English clergyman born in 1745 in Staffordshire. Asbury had been converted to Methodism at age thirteen and became a local preacher at sixteen. After being enrolled as a regular itinerant minister by John Wesley in 1767, Asbury volunteered for missionary work in the colonies in 1771. His efforts infused new life into the movement, resulting in the membership of several congregations more than doubling within a single year.

## Leadership and the War of Independence
In 1772, John Wesley appointed Francis Asbury as the "general assistant" in charge of American operations. Although Thomas Rankin, an older preacher, superseded him in 1773, Asbury remained practically in control of the work. 

The outbreak of the War of Independence created significant challenges for the Methodist community, which by that time numbered several thousand. The Methodists fell under suspicion of Loyalism, a charge described as unjust, primarily because they refused to take the prescribed oath. This political climate led many ministers, including Thomas Rankin, to return to England. Asbury chose to remain at his post due to his duties and sympathies toward the colonies. Despite being threatened and arrested once, he continued his itinerant preaching. He was eventually driven into exile in Delaware by the hostility of Maryland authorities, where he remained for two years.

## The Establishment of the Methodist Episcopacy
In 1782, Asbury was reappointed to supervise the affairs of the Methodist congregations in America. A pivotal shift in the church's structure occurred in 1784 when John Wesley took the radical step of appointing superintendents, or "bishops," for the church in the United States, acting in disregard of the authority of the Established Church. These appointments were given to Francis Asbury and the Rev. Thomas Coke.

The Methodist Episcopal Church of America's system of episcopacy is distinct from that of the Catholic Church (in the narrow sense of the ancient East and West churches). It is based on the denial of any special *potestas ordinis* in the degree of bishop. The orders of the bishops in this church derive from Thomas Coke, a presbyter of the Church of England, who was ordained as "superintendent" of the Methodist Society in America in 1784 by John Wesley, assisted by two other presbyters.

## Other English Literary and Political Connections
Beyond the religious movement of Methodism, other English figures engaged with the American colonies in the 17th and 18th centuries. John Dunton, an English bookseller and author born in 1659, visited New England in 1686. He stayed for eight months selling books and observing the inhabitants and the country, likely because he was concerned in the Monmouth rising. His letters from this period were later published in America in 1867.

## Colonial Tensions and British Authority
The broader historical context of the colonies during the late 18th century was marked by a turbulent stream flowing toward civil war. The British government attempted to assert its authority through various means that the colonists found intolerable. This included the quartering of soldiers and trade restrictions, which were actively defied by "free traders" (smugglers) in Rhode Island. A notable act of defiance occurred when a government schooner, the *Gaspee*, was captured and burnt by armed men off Providence.

Tensions peaked in 1773 when the British Parliament gave special advantages to the East India Company for the importation of tea into America, disregarding the existing colonial tea trade. This led to a boycott by the colonists. On December 16, 1773, in Boston, a group of men disguised as Indians boarded three tea ships and threw the tea overboard in front of a large crowd.
