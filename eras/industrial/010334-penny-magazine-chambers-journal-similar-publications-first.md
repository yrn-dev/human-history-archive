# Penny Magazine and 19th Century Periodical Publishing

## Development of the Periodical Trade
The periodical trade expanded significantly during the 19th century. In its early stages, this sector of the publishing business saw the emergence of publications such as *Chambers's Journal* and the *Penny Magazine*. The growth of this industry was further accelerated by several key factors, including the introduction of attractive illustrations and the availability of facilities that allowed the public to purchase books by instalments. Additionally, the removal of the duty upon paper and the abolition of the newspaper stamp played critical roles in promoting the expansion of the trade.

## Charles Knight and the Penny Magazine
The *Penny Magazine* was started in 1832 by Charles Knight (1791–1873), an English author and publisher who was the son of a printer and bookseller from Windsor. Knight's career in publishing spanned over forty years, beginning with his work as the publisher of *The Etonian* from 1820 to 1821. He later launched *Knight's Quarterly Magazine* in 1823, which featured contributions from Macaulay, Derwent Coleridge, and W. M. Praed, though the venture ended after its sixth number.

Following a period as the superintendent of publications for the Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge—for which he edited *The British Almanack and Companion* starting in 1828—Knight resumed independent business in 1829 with *The Library of Entertaining Knowledge*. In addition to the *Penny Magazine*, Knight launched *The Penny Cyclopaedia* in 1833. While both publications achieved large circulations, *The Penny Cyclopaedia* was not completed until 1844, a process that resulted in great pecuniary sacrifice due to heavy excise duty.

## Related Works and Contributions
Charles Knight's publishing efforts extended to various illustrated works and standard editions. In 1842, he published *The Pictorial Shakespeare*, which had previously appeared in parts between 1838 and 1841. Other illustrated titles included *The Land we Live in* and *Old England*. Knight also managed a series titled *Weekly Volumes*; he contributed the first volume, a biography of William Caxton, and published first editions of works such as G. H. Lewes's *Biographical History of Philosophy*, Mrs. Jameson's *Early Italian Painters*, and Miss Martineau's *Tales*. Later in his career, Knight edited *The English Cyclopaedia* in 1853, which served as a revision of *The Penny Cyclopaedia*, and wrote an eight-volume *Popular History of England* between 1856 and 1862.

The *Penny Cyclopaedia* also served as a venue for specialized scientific knowledge. For example, the zoological articles within the publication were written by William John Broderip (1789–1859), an English naturalist and metropolitan police magistrate. Additionally, the *Penny Cyclopaedia* is cited as an authority on the subject of the astrolabe.

## The Broader Publishing Context
The periodical and book trade of this era involved various partners, including the retail bookseller. To protect their interests, a London booksellers' society was formed in 1890 to control trade details, arrange prices, and restrict discounts to 25%. This organization later expanded its operations across the United Kingdom and Ireland, becoming "The Associated Booksellers of Great Britain and Ireland." 

Parallel to the mass-market periodicals, a higher class of business existed in the trade of second-hand or old books, which required bibliographic knowledge and focused on individual volumes rather than mass copies. Dealers in this sector occasionally acquired "remainders"—books that had ceased to sell with the original publisher—to offer as bargains to the public.
