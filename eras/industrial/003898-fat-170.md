# Fat

In the context of biological composition and food science, fat is a constituent found in various plants and animals, serving as a primary component in dairy products and artificial butter substitutes.

## Composition in Plants
In barley, fat constitutes a small portion of the grain's composition, measured at 2.170%. Other components of barley include starch (59.950%), water (15%), nitrogenous compounds (12.981%), gum (6.744%), and sugar (3.200%).

## Biological Production in Animals
The origins of fat in animals vary depending on the physiological process. In fattening animals, it has been shown that while some fat may be produced from the albuminoids of food, the greater part, or perhaps the whole, is derived from carbohydrates. However, the production of milk involves different physiological conditions. While some suggest milk fat is formed from albuminoids in the same way as fattening increase, others argue that the sources of fat in milk may be derived from albuminoids in large proportion, though the two processes are distinct.

## Butter-Fat in Dairy
Butter-fat is a critical measure of milk quality. In milking trials, such as those at the London Dairy Show in 1900, prizes are not determined by the quantity of milk alone but by a points system. Under this scheme, twenty points are awarded for every pound of butter-fat produced, while four points are given for every pound of "solids other than fat."

### Separation Processes
Butter-fat can be removed from milk through various methods:
*   **Centrifugal Cream-Separator:** This machine rotates a bowl several thousand times per minute. The heavier watery portion of the milk moves to the outer circumference, while the lighter butter-fat particles are forced into an inner zone. These are then collected in distinct vessels. This method is most effective at temperatures between 80°F and 98°F, though 150°F is sometimes used. Efficient separators can reduce the fat remaining in the milk to as low as 0.1%.
*   **Deep-setting Method:** This method leaves between 0.2% and 0.4% of fat in the skim-milk.
*   **Shallow-setting Method:** This method leaves between 0.3% and 0.5% of fat in the skim-milk.

Consequently, "separated" milk generally contains less fat than "skim" milk produced via deep or shallow vessel methods.

## Testing and Regulation of Milk Fat
The fat test for milk was developed by Dr. S. M. Babcock of the Wisconsin experiment station and later improved by Dr. N. Gerber of Zurich. The apparatus, often called a butyrometer, uses centrifugal force, warm water, and standard strength sulphuric acid to determine the percentage of fat. These devices are used to measure the fat-producing capacity of cows, determine the quality of milk delivered to factories, and check the richness of milk in the urban trade. To ensure accuracy, the National Physical Laboratory in Richmond, Surrey, verifies the measuring vessels.

### Legal Standards in Great Britain
Under the Sale of Milk Regulations 1901 and the Sale of Food and Drugs Acts 1875 to 1899, specific fat thresholds are used to determine if milk is "genuine." 
*   Milk (excluding skimmed, separated, or condensed) containing less than 3% milk-fat is presumed not genuine due to the addition of water or the abstraction of fat.
*   Other regulations specify that milk with total solids under 12% and milk-fat under 3.25% is deemed deficient in fat.
*   Seasonal variations are also noted: from July to February, the limit is 3% fat (with total solids under 11.75%), while from March to June, the limit is 2.75% fat.
*   For skimmed or separated milk, a deficiency is presumed if total milk-solids fall below 9%.

## Margarine and Artificial Fats
Margarine is an artificial substitute for butter, first named by Chevreul. It is made from beef and other animal fats and is sometimes mixed with real butter. A method developed by Mège-Mouries in Paris involved heating minced beef suet with water, carbonate of potash, and fragments of fresh sheep's stomach. The pepsine in the stomach and the heat (45°C or 113°F) separated the fat from cellular tissue. This fatty matter was then subjected to hydraulic pressure to separate stearin from oleomargarin; only the latter was used for butter-making. The process involved churning approximately 10 lb of this fat with 4 pints of milk and 3 pints of water, with annatto added for color.

To prevent the fraudulent sale of margarine as real butter, the Sale of Food and Drugs Act 1899 mandates that margarine and margarine-cheese be conspicuously marked. Furthermore, Section 8 of the Act limits the amount of butter-fat allowed in margarine to a maximum of 10%.
