# Balance of Trade

The "balance of trade" is an economic term that originated during the era of the "mercantile theory." While it remains in use, its application and meaning have evolved from its original conceptualization.

## Historical and Popular Conceptions
Under the mercantile theory, the balance of trade was identified as the total sum of precious metals a country received through trading with other nations or specific countries. This was based on the belief that the receipt of precious metals represented trading profit, leading sovereigns and princes to pursue the acquisition of these metals for political objectives. In some instances, rulers may have encouraged the export of "money's worth" specifically to acquire the desired precious metals.

This theory was later exploded by Adam Smith, who demonstrated that gold and silver are commodities with no special virtue; they enter a country based on demand and the country's ability to pay relative to other demands. Despite this, the concept persists in popular political and business discussions. In these contexts, a nation is generally viewed as prosperous if its statistics show a trade balance in its favor and unprosperous if the reverse is true. In modern popular speech, the term is often identified with the excess of exports over imports (a "favourable" balance) or imports over exports (an "unfavourable" balance), with the latter often interpreted as a sign that a nation is living on its capital.

## Application of Trader's Language
The phrase "balance of trade" is essentially an application of a trader's business language to the aggregate of individuals in a nation engaged in foreign trade. An individual trader determines his trade balance or profit by setting his sales against his purchases; the amount by which sales exceed purchases is the profit. The assumption is that what applies to the individual must apply to the nation: if collective sales exceed collective purchases, the balance is in their favor, and they have money to receive. Conversely, if purchases exceed sales, they must pay money and are presumably living on capital.

However, this argument fails in several ways. First, an individual trader does not necessarily receive profit in money. Profit may be invested as it accrues into warehouses, machinery, stock-in-trade, book debts, or stocks and shares outside the regular business. Consequently, a trader may be profitable without having an increased cash balance. Even when a cash balance exists, it may be held at a bank that has invested the amount, meaning no "hard money" or precious metals actually represent it. Similarly, a nation may invest its foreign trading profits in machinery, warehouses, or shares purchased abroad, leaving no corresponding "balance of trade" in cash.

## Statistical Limitations and Illusions
There is a significant distinction between a balance of profit and a balance of cash paid or received. The identification of the balance of trade solely with the excess of imports over exports is considered an illusion by economists. Several factors contribute to the inaccuracy of using statistical import and export returns to determine a true balance of trade:

*   **Nature of Transactions:** Statistical returns do not always correspond to actual purchases and sales. Some sales may be set off by loans rather than payments. For example, the United Kingdom may export ships, ammunition, or railway materials, but these sales may be set off by loans to foreign governments or people rather than international purchases.
*   **Remittances:** Movements of goods may occur to repay capital or pay interest on loans received in the past, which are not international sales or purchases but appear in import and export records.
*   **Excluded Services:** A proper account of trade should include the earning of commissions and freights, as an individual trader would include repairs performed for others. These services are not treated as imports or exports in national statistical returns.
*   **Valuation and Estimation:** Import and export values are estimates and not literal facts. A sale may prove abortive if a debtor fails to pay, and the "squaring" of accounts may take a long time.
*   **International Variations:** Different countries use different recording methods. The United Kingdom values imports at the port of arrival and exports at the port of dispatch. In contrast, the United States values imports at the port of shipment rather than the port of arrival (excluding freight and other charges). These variations make comparing the balance of trade between countries extremely difficult.
