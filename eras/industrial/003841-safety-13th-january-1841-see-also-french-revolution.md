# Safety and the French Revolution

The archive's records concerning "Safety" in the context of the French Revolution primarily relate to the Committee of Public Safety and the individuals whose lives and careers were intersected by its authority and the broader revolutionary upheaval.

## The Committee of Public Safety
During the French Revolution, the Committee of Public Safety functioned as a powerful governing body capable of exercising significant control over the liberty of citizens. The records indicate that the Committee possessed the authority to order the immediate release of prisoners. For example, Jean Jacques Barthelemy, a French writer and numismatist, was arrested during the Revolution on the grounds of being an aristocrat. However, after the duchess of Choiseul informed the Committee of Public Safety of his arrest, the Committee issued orders for his immediate release.

## Revolutionary Figures and the Convention
The French National Convention included members whose conduct was marked by the volatility of the era. Bertrand Barere de Vieuzac (1755–1841) was a notorious member of the Convention. Born in Gascony, Barere was an orator and journalist who wrote for the paper *Point du Jour*. His political trajectory shifted from the constitutional party to the republican party following the king's flight to Varennes. Archive notes suggest that Barere lacked guiding principles or a conscience, regulating his conduct solely by a determination to align himself with the strongest faction.

Other figures of the Convention, such as Edmond Louis Alexis Dubois-Crance (1747–1814), focused on military restructuring. Dubois-Crance, a former musketeer and lieutenant of the *marechaux*, proposed the idea of conscription on December 12, 1789, as an alternative to the old regime's recruiting system. He also advocated for the replacement of the mercenary system with national guards open to all citizens. While Dubois-Crance served on the two committees of general defence that preceded the Committee of Public Safety, he was not a member of the Committee of Public Safety at the time of its creation.

## Legal and Political Transitions
The revolutionary period saw the dissolution of old feudal structures and the creation of new legal entities. On August 4, feudal rights were renounced, though some individuals, such as Barere de Vieuzac, continued to use names derived from ancestral fiefs. In October 1791, a court of cassation was instituted, and Barere was nominated as one of its judges.

The period was also characterized by extreme political shifts. Dubois-Crance, for instance, sat among the *Montagnards* in the Convention and voted for the death of Louis XVI without appeal or delay. Despite his contributions to the army—specifically the fusion of veteran troops with volunteers and the rapid advancement of lower officers—he faced political instability, eventually being excluded from the Jacobin club at the instance of Robespierre.

## Later Reflections and Historiography
The events of the French Revolution continued to be analyzed and rehabilitated in later centuries. Laurence Gronlund (1846–1899), an American socialist, authored a work in 1888 titled *Ca Ira, or Danton in the French Revolution*, which served as a rehabilitation of the figure of Danton. Additionally, the archive notes the existence of various memoirs and studies on the Revolution, including those by Brissot and Helena Williams, as well as the works of F. A. Aulard regarding the orators of the Convention and literary portraits of the era.
