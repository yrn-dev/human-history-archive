# Communications in Ashanti

The communications infrastructure of Ashanti, centered around the capital of Kumasi, has evolved from a reliance on forest paths and caravan routes to the establishment of modern rail and telegraph systems.

## Land and Water Transport
Historically, the primary means of communication in the region have been tortuous paths through the forest, which are too narrow to accommodate wheeled vehicles. A wide road measuring 141 miles in length has been cut through the bush to connect Cape Coast to Kumasi. From Kumasi, ancient caravan routes extend to the chief trading centers located further inland. In areas where swamps and rivers must be crossed, ferries are maintained for transport. A preferred method of travel within the bush is the use of a palanquin, which is carried on the heads of four carriers.

## The Railway to Kumasi
A railway line from Sekondi to Kumasi was completed in September 1903. This line, which covers a distance of 168 miles, was cut through one of the densest forest regions and passes through the auriferous (gold-bearing) regions, specifically the Obuassi and Tarkwa gold-fields. The arrival of the railway led to a marked revival in trade and the rapid expansion of Kumasi, as merchants from the Gold Coast ports opened branches in the town. By 1906, Kumasi had supplanted the coast towns to become the distributing center for the hinterland and the whole of Ashanti.

## Postal and Telegraphic Services
The region is served by a well-organized postal service. Telegraph lines have been established to connect Kumasi with the towns in the Northern Territories as well as with the coast towns.

## Trade and Distribution
Kumasi serves as the central distributing hub for Ashanti and its hinterland. While some Ashanti trade was previously diverted to the French port of Assini due to wars between England and the Ashanti, measures were implemented to improve trade between Cape Coast and Kumasi following the suppression of the 1900 revolt. Ashanti maintains considerable trade with its neighbors in Germany and France, as well as with the Niger countries to the north. Revenue for the region is derived principally from liquor licences, rents from government land, contributions from gold-mining companies, and caravan taxes.
