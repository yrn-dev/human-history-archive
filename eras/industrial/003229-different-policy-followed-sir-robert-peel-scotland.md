# Banking Policy of Sir Robert Peel in Scotland and Ireland

During the mid-19th century, Sir Robert Peel implemented a banking policy in Scotland and Ireland that differed from the system he established in England. This approach was codified through the acts of 1844-1845, which granted specific liberties to the banks in these two countries regarding the issuance of notes and the structure of their organizations.

## Note Issuance and Specie Requirements
Under the acts of 1844-1845, banks in Scotland and Ireland were permitted to exceed their authorized issues of notes, provided they held specie (gold) equal to the amount of that excess. Sir Robert Peel explained in a speech delivered on April 25, 1845, that the amount of specie to be held was determined by the average note-issue of the preceding four weeks. This gold was held at the offices of the issuing banks.

The primary objective of this requirement was to ensure that the country's circulating medium—which consisted of both notes and specie—would fluctuate in the same manner as if the circulation were entirely metallic. In a speech on May 6, 1844, Peel stated that this legislation was intended to "ensure the uniform equivalency of bank notes to coin." The specie held in these banks was not intended as a special security for the note circulation but was placed there specifically for this purpose.

## Operational Differences and Bank Structure
The policy in Scotland and Ireland allowed for several operational freedoms not found in the English system:
* **Note Denominations:** Notes for amounts less than £5 continued to be permitted.
* **Amalgamations:** No restrictions were placed on the amalgamation of banks within these countries.

As a result of these policies, the power to issue notes was held by ten large banks in Scotland and six of the nine banks in Ireland. This capacity to issue notes greatly assisted these banks in maintaining a large proportion of local branches.

## Long-term Impact on Scottish Banking
The limitation of note-issuing power to those Scottish banks that already possessed it in 1845 had a significant effect on the development of the industry; consequently, no important bank has been established in Scotland since that time. Because notes are so extensively used in ordinary business in Scotland, any bank lacking the power to issue them is practically unable to supply the needs of its customers or carry on business.

Despite this limitation on the number of banks, there was no resulting deficiency in the supply of banking accommodation for the public. In fact, Scotland maintained a larger number of banking offices in proportion to its population than was found in Ireland, England, or Wales.

## Evolution of the Circulating Medium
The legislation designed by Sir Robert Peel was influenced by the workings of the note circulation during the earlier part of the 19th century. However, the archive notes that these policies are not applicable to the later position of the United Kingdom's circulating medium, which shifted to consist mainly of a circulation of cheques. This shift differed absolutely from what Peel had contemplated, as no attempt can be made to cause a paper circulation of cheques to fluctuate as if it were a circulation of specie only.
