# The Bank Charter Acts of 1844-1845 and Specie Requirements

The legislation of 1844-1845, primarily associated with Sir Robert Peel, established a regulatory framework for the issuance of bank notes in the United Kingdom. A central feature of these acts was the management of the circulating medium to ensure that bank notes remained uniformly equivalent to coin.

## Specie Requirements in Scotland and Ireland
Under the acts of 1844-1845, a specific policy was applied to banks in Scotland and Ireland that differed from the policy established in England. Banks in these two countries were permitted to exceed their authorized note issues, provided they held specie (gold) at their offices to cover the amount of the excess. 

Sir Robert Peel explained in a speech on April 25, 1845, that the amount of specie to be held was determined by the average amount of the note-issue for the preceding four weeks. The primary objective of this requirement was to ensure that the country's circulating medium—which consisted of both notes and specie—would fluctuate in the same manner as if the circulation were entirely metallic.

Despite this requirement, the specie held by Scottish and Irish banks against their note-issue was not classified as a special security for the note circulation; rather, it was placed in the banks for the specific purpose of meeting the legal requirements of the acts.

## Impact on the Scottish Banking System
The limitation of note-issuing power to those Scottish banks that possessed it in 1845 had a significant long-term effect on the country's financial structure. Because notes are used so extensively in ordinary business in Scotland, banks lacking the power to issue them found it practically impossible to carry on business or meet the needs of their customers. Consequently, no important bank has been established in Scotland since 1845.

However, this restriction on the number of banks did not result in a deficiency of banking accommodation for the public. In fact, Scotland maintained a larger number of banking offices in proportion to its population than Ireland, England, or Wales. By 1844, there were 368 banking offices in Scotland.

## Note Issuance and Monopoly in England
In England, the legislation culminated in the Bank Charter Acts of 1844 and 1845, which granted the Bank of England an absolute monopoly on the issuance of notes within the city of London and a radius of three miles. Outside this area, but within a 65-mile radius of the city, a concurrent right to issue notes was maintained by banks established before 1844 that were issuing notes at that time and consisted of six or fewer persons. Beyond the 65-mile radius, the privilege was extended to all banks established and issuing notes before 1844, provided they had not lost the right through abandonment of business, bankruptcy, or temporary suspension of issue.

The act of 1844 also influenced the overall constitution of the banking system. While it favored small banks for several years, it eventually led to the formation of large, powerful banks in England and Wales. The legislation prohibited the establishment of any new banks of issue. While private issuing banks with no more than six partners could remain or amalgamate with other private issuing banks to retain joint issues, the issue lapsed if a private issuing bank joined or was formed into a joint-stock bank.

## Comparison with Other Systems
The English system under the act of 1844 differed from the German system governed by the law of 1875. The German Reichsbank possessed an automatic statutory power to overpass its limit of issue upon the payment of a 5% surplus fee. In contrast, the English act allowed no such automatic power. 

While there were discussions regarding granting the Bank of England a similar power to augment note issue on securities to prevent panic, these negotiations failed. A primary hindrance was the government's requirement for a high rate of interest on such advances, which critics argued would intensify panic rather than relieve it.
