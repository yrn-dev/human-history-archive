# Victorian London: Life in the Industrial Capital

## Overview
During the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), London evolved into the undisputed center of the global economy, serving as the heart of the British Empire and the vanguard of the Industrial Revolution. This era was defined by a stark dichotomy: unprecedented wealth and technological progress juxtaposed against systemic poverty and urban decay.

The city experienced an explosive population boom, growing from roughly 2 million in 1840 to over 6 million by the century's end. This rapid urbanization outpaced infrastructure, leading to the creation of notorious slums in areas like East London. While the West End flourished with grand architecture and luxury, the East End struggled with overcrowding and disease. The industrialization of the Thames waterfront turned the river into a highway for global trade, but also into an open sewer, culminating in the "Great Stink" of 1858. This crisis eventually forced the government to commission Joseph Bazalgette’s massive intercepting sewer system, a feat of engineering that fundamentally changed urban public health.

Historians continue to debate the "Standard of Living" during this period. Some argue that real wages rose and that the industrial era eventually improved the quality of life for the working class. Others, however, point to the psychological toll of factory labor, the rise of child exploitation, and the plummeting life expectancy in urban centers as evidence of a net decline in well-being for the proletariat.

## Key Facts
*   **The Class Divide:** Society was strictly stratified into the aristocracy, a burgeoning middle class (bourgeoisie), and the working class. The "working poor" often relied on precarious day labor or the dreaded workhouse.
*   **Public Health:** Cholera outbreaks were frequent until John Snow identified the Broad Street pump as a source of infection in 1854, challenging the prevailing "miasma theory" (the belief that diseases were spread by "bad air").
*   **Transportation:** The transition from horse-drawn omnibuses to the "Tube" revolutionized the city's geography, allowing the middle class to move to suburbs while commuting to the city center.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Fog was Chemical:** The famous "pea-soupers" were not natural fog but smog—a mixture of water vapor and coal smoke from millions of domestic fires and industrial chimneys.
2. **The Mudlarks:** Impoverished children and adults, known as "mudlarks," would scavenge the banks of the Thames at low tide for scraps of coal, iron, or copper to sell.
3. **The Penny Post:** The introduction of the Uniform Penny Post in 1840 democratized communication, making it affordable for the working class to send letters for the first time.
