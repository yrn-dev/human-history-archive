# France, Germany, Holland, and Belgium

The nations of France, Germany, Holland, and Belgium are closely linked by geography, commerce, and political history. Situated in West Europe, Belgium serves as a natural meeting-ground for these nations, possessing water communications with each. It is connected to France via the upper courses of the Meuse, Sambre, Scheldt, and Lys, with the Meuse being navigable by deep-draught vessels far into Lorraine. While its connection to Germany is less direct—as the outlet of the Rhine runs through Holland—Belgium's position fronting the mouth of the Thames also facilitates trade with England.

## Political and Historical Development
Historically, the Belgian principalities achieved political autonomy by rejecting German, English, and French influences. In the fifteenth century, the dukes of Burgundy aided in the union of these principalities into a solid monarchic federation. This consolidation saved Belgium from absorption or conquest by France, which was exhausted following the Hundred Years’ War, while Germany had lost the strength and prestige of its monarchic power. Consequently, Belgium and Holland became buffer states situated between France and Germany.

In earlier eras, such as the time of Charles the Great, Belgium's geography contributed to its prosperity. The imperial palace at Aix attracted merchants, priests, and soldiers from across the Carolingian empire, who traveled through Belgium to reach the Emperor.

## Commerce and Trade
Belgium ranks sixth globally in the annual value of its commerce (excluding goods in transit), following Holland, France, Germany, the United States, and Great Britain. Since 1870, Belgian trade in both imports and exports has more than trebled. 

The nation's primary imports consist of raw materials and food supplies, including jute, hemp, flax, silk, wool, and cotton. Mineral imports include diamonds, lead, tin, coal, copper, sulphur, and iron ore. Its most valuable exports include firearms, railway material, machinery, glass, briquettes, coke, coal, lace, and textiles.

Trade data indicates significant exchange between Belgium and its neighbors:
*   **France:** Imports of 465,684,000 francs; exports of 346,670,000 francs.
*   **Germany:** Imports of 351,025,000 francs; exports of 505,473,000 francs.
*   **England:** Imports of 335,404,000 francs; exports of 392,324,000 francs.
*   **Holland:** Imports of 240,873,000 francs; exports of 268,781,000 francs.

Additionally, these nations produce basket-ware for the English market. While France produces light basket-work in Verdun (department of the Aisne), Germany produces work in the Black Forest, Sonnefeld in Saxony, and Lichtenfels in Bavaria. Between 1900 and 1905, the value of basket-ware imports to the United Kingdom from these nations shifted: Belgium's exports increased from £72,031 to £77,766, while Holland, France, and Germany all saw decreases.

## Economic and Demographic Data
Financial records from 1876 to 1906 show varying frequencies of changes in bank rates. During this period, England had 183 changes, followed by Germany (110), Belgium (77), Holland (55), and France (27).

Regarding taxation on beer, the nations employ different methods:
*   **France:** Taxes on wort at 4s. 1d. per English barrel.
*   **Belgium:** A malt tax of 2s. 9d.
*   **Holland:** A tax on cubic contents of Mash-Tun or quality, ranging from about 1s. 9d. to 3s. 3d.
*   **Germany:** The Union has no listed tax, but the N. German Customs apply a malt tax of 1s. 6d., and Bavaria applies a malt tax between 3s. 5d. and 4s. 8d. depending on production quantity.

Population estimates vary by source. One account suggests 20 million in France, 22 million in Germany, and 4 million in the Low Countries. Another source (Almanach de Gotha) provides more specific figures: Germany (including Prussian and Austrian Poland and Hungary) at 56,136,213; France at 32,897,521; Belgium at 3,533,538; and Holland at 2,444,550.

## Military and Diplomatic Relations
In military contexts, estimates of available divisions upon mobilization have placed the German force at 110 and the French force at 85. Strategic concerns have previously focused on the "Maestricht Appendix"—a projection of Holland between Germany and Belgium—which was viewed as a potential route for German troops moving west of the Belgian Meuse.

In diplomatic proposals for a league of nations, France and Belgium were listed as original members. Germany was among the powers proposed for exclusion from the original league, though it could be included later with the assent of two-thirds of the Assembly. Holland was listed as a power to be added by invitation due to its neutrality in the war.
