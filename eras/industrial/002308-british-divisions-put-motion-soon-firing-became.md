# British Divisions in Military Engagements

The deployment and movement of British divisions have been central to various military actions across different conflicts, ranging from the Crimean War to the First World War. These movements were often dictated by specific orders, the urgency of the tactical situation, or the coordination between allied forces.

## The Action of Balaklava
During the Crimean War, a significant engagement took place on October 25, 1854, at Balaklava in the Crimea, east of Sevastopol. This action was triggered by the advance of a Russian field army led by General Liprandi, who sought to attack the allied forces of the English, French, and Turkish armies besieging Sevastopol. The engagement occurred in the valleys and on the Vorontsov ridge.

Initially, the ridge was weakly occupied by Turkish battalions. As the Russian advanced guard moved southward and the firing became serious, the two nearest British divisions were put into motion. However, their orders prevented them from descending into the plain immediately. Consequently, the Turkish forces faced superior numbers and, despite a gallant resistance, were cleared from the ridge by the Russians, who captured several guns.

Following this, a heavy mass of Russian cavalry descended into the Balaklava plain. At this time, the British cavalry division, commanded by the Earl of Lucan, was present in the plain, but the commander was prevented from engaging the enemy due to the tenor of his orders. The British cavalry was split into two brigades: the Light Brigade under Lord Cardigan, located in the valley north of the Vorontsov ridge, and the Heavy Brigade under Brigadier-General J. Y. Scarlett, located in the plain. Both brigades were very weak in numbers. When the Russian cavalry moved toward Balaklava, the Heavy Brigade—consisting of the 1st, 2nd, and 6th Dragoons and the 4th and 5th Dragoon Guards—charged. The 4th Dragoon Guards delivered a decisive charge against the flank of the Russian mass, causing the enemy cavalry to break and flee back to the ridge in an action that lasted less than five minutes.

## Operations in the First World War
In the First World War, British divisions were involved in various strategic maneuvers across different theaters. In September, during the struggle for Antwerp, Lord Kitchener declined to use British Territorial Divisions in the manner proposed, refusing to send any Territorials into the city.

In Flanders, during an autumn engagement between Hooge and Gheluvelt, the 1st (Guards) Brigade of the 1st Division faced a desperate position after Brigadier-General the Hon. Charles FitzClarence, V.C., had exhausted his last reserves. While riding to the 1st Division headquarters, FitzClarence encountered the 2nd Worcesters, a battalion from the right brigade of the 2nd Division. Despite belonging to a different division, the Worcesters were placed under FitzClarence's orders. Under heavy artillery fire, they advanced 1,000 yards in short rushes to a position between the northern edge of Gheluvelt and the right of the South Wales Borderers. By digging in and opening a heavy flank fire, they halted the German advance, which enabled the 6th Cavalry Brigade to fill the gap between the 1st and 7th Divisions and allowed the 7th Division to return to its old line.

## Large-Scale Engagements and Allied Cooperation
During the Second Battle of the Somme in 1918, the British faced a massive German onslaught. While the British expected the primary attack to fall between the Bapaume-Cambrai road and the river Sensée (the right of Byng's Army), the sector south of St. Quentin in front of the Oise was held by only four divisions. This area was the target of von Hutier's army. In total, 1,150,000 Germans were engaged against 160,000 British troops under Byng and Gough. The German force included 64 attacking divisions, a number exceeding the total of British divisions present in France.

Additionally, British commanders oversaw Allied divisions in other sectors. The Second Corps, comprising the Twenty-seventh and Thirtieth Divisions, cooperated with the Australian Corps on September 29 and October 1 in an assault on the Hindenburg line near a tunnel under a ridge where the St. Quentin Canal passes. The Thirtieth Division successfully broke through the main line of defense to reach its objectives, while the Twenty-seventh Division pushed through to Gouy. Between October 6 and October 19, the Second Corps advanced over 13 miles and captured more than 6,000 prisoners.
