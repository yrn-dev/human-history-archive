# Wiring in Periodicals and Other Applications

## Wiring in Periodicals
Wiring is utilized as a cheap method for keeping together tracts or thin parts of periodicals. The process is executed by a machine that is simple in its use and construction. This machine drives a short wire pin through the entire thickness or through the folds of the book's sections sideways, a process described as being "after the manner of stabbing." The wire pin is bent at right angles at each end; once it has passed through the paper, the projecting ends are flattened and bent over to ensure a firm grip.

Historically, the metal used for these pins was highly susceptible to rust, which caused significant damage to the paper in the immediate vicinity of the wire. While this specific defect has been largely remedied, the use of hard metal wire instead of flexible hempen thread is considered essentially vicious. Consequently, wiring should only be employed as a temporary expedient for publications that are of little value.

## Medical Applications of Wiring
In the field of surgery, wiring is used to treat fractures when ordinary methods cannot ensure the close adjustment of fractured surfaces. In such cases, a surgeon may cut down to the broken ends and fix them together using a strong wire suture that remains permanently within the tissues. This immediate wiring of fragments is also considered a necessary part of primary treatment for compound fractures—fractures associated with an open wound—where the broken ends cannot be easily adjusted.

Wiring is also applied to "ununited fractures," where nature has failed to consolidate the parts after many weeks or months. This condition can be caused by constitutional defects, the interposition of tendon or muscle between broken ends, the constant disturbance of the fracture site, or the failure to bring the ends close enough together. Unless the cause is constitutional, treatment involves cutting down to the broken ends, sawing off a thin slice to freshen them, and fixing them in place with a screw or wire.

## Agricultural and Industrial Uses of Wire
Wire is employed in various agricultural capacities, ranging from crop training to livestock containment.

### Hop Training
Modern systems for training hops utilize wires and strings fastened to permanent upright poles, a method that is cheaper in the long run than the traditional practice of removing poles every winter. One such arrangement involves stout posts at the end of every row of hop-hills, with thick poles fixed at intervals. Three wires are stretched between these posts: one at 1/2 ft. from the ground, one at approximately 6 ft., and one along the tops of the posts. Coconut-fibre strings are threaded onto hooks clipped to these wires—using a machine called a stringer for the top wire—to receive the hop bines. 

An alternative, simpler, and less expensive method used in Sussex and Kent involves fastening wires only from the tops of the posts. In this system, coconut-fibre strings are fixed by pegs to the ground near each hop-stock and radiate upwards to the top wires. Because these strings are light and prone to being blown by the wind, the plants must be well sheltered, or "lewed."

### Fencing
Galvanized barbed wire is used for fencing due to its economy and durability. It is described as almost imperishable and effective as a barrier against wild beasts, dogs, and all kinds of stock. Cattle generally avoid pressing against it once they discover its nature. Because it does not oppose the wind with a large surface, it does not rack the posts or contribute to the formation of snow drifts, making it a preferred choice for highways and railroads. Barbed wire consisting of two twisted wires is particularly durable, as the twist allows the wire to adapt to varying temperatures and remain taut for many seasons without repair.

## Other Specialized Uses
Wire is utilized in several other distinct applications:
*   **Aviaries:** The outer portions of aviaries are constructed from wire-netting stretched over a framework of iron or wood. To prevent vermin such as weasels, stoats, and rats from entering, the wire-netting is sunk 2 ft. into the ground and turned outwards for an additional foot.
*   **Performance Art:** In *Ombres Chinoises* (shadow plays), cardboard figures are projected onto a gauze scene or thin calico. The performer supports each figure with a long wire in one hand, while wires controlling movable parts terminate in rings inserted into the fingers of the other hand.
