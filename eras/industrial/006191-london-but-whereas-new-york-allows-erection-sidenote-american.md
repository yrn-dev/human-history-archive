# London

## Building Regulations and Construction
The construction of buildings in London is governed by numerous building acts that dictate the materials and methods used in their erection. These regulations specify requirements for the thickness of walls, the rates of inclination for roofs, drainage, and the means of escape from fire. A primary restriction within these laws is the absolute prohibition of timber-framed buildings for use as dwellings.

In the case of premises that serve a combined purpose as both a shop and a dwelling-house, specific fire-safety rules apply. If such a building exceeds 10 squares or 1,000 square feet in area, a large portion of the structure and the stairs must be constructed from fire-resisting materials. Similarly, under certain conditions, the corridors and stairs of London flats must be made of fire-resisting materials.

## Urban Planning and Space Requirements
London's building acts include specific provisions regarding the space required at the rear of domestic buildings. While the minimum depth for this space is 10 feet—a standard shared with New York City—London employs a different system for determining how this space relates to the height of the building. The height must be constructed to remain within an angle of 63½ degrees, which inclines from the rear boundary toward the building, measured from the level of the pavement in front of the building. The specific position from which this angle is measured may vary under special circumstances.

## Public and Specialized Architecture
The planning and construction of various building types in England are overseen by different controlling authorities. Public schools are governed by the board of education, while the erection of churches is governed by various financing societies, with the Incorporated Church Building Society exercising the strongest control. Factories must be planned and erected to comply with separate acts. Additionally, fire insurance companies establish requirements based on the building's size and the specific trade for which it is intended, adjusting their premium rates accordingly.

In London, as in Paris and New York, public buildings and theatres are subject to regulations that govern not only the general construction but also the seating accommodation, the exits, and the stage, including the flies and the scenery dock.

## Commercial and Industrial History
London has served as a hub for international trade and industry. In the late 18th century, the city hosted specialized manufacturing, such as the piano and flute factory of the firm Astor & Broadwood, where John Jacob Astor was employed for four years starting around 1777. Later, London remained a primary market for the global fur trade, where merchants sold furs obtained from the Americas for great profit.
