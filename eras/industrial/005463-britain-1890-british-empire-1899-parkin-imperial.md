# The British Empire (Late 19th and Early 20th Centuries)

The British Empire during the late 19th and early 20th centuries was characterized by a complex system of territorial acquisitions, evolving trade policies, and a growing body of intellectual discourse regarding imperialism. While the empire spanned the globe, its administration and economic structures varied significantly across its different regions.

## Territorial Acquisitions and Administration

The expansion of the empire occurred through various methods, including treaties, royal charters, and military conquest. In India, the empire was developed between the 17th and 19th centuries via a royal charter granted to the East India Company by Queen Elizabeth in 1600. Key centers of the Indian empire include Madras, founded in 1639 through a treaty with its Indian ruler (and later recovered in 1748 after being ceded to France), and Bombay, where trade was established in 1608 before it was ceded to the British crown by Portugal in 1661 and transferred to the East India Company in 1668. The Indian territories were transferred to the imperial government in 1858, and Queen Victoria was proclaimed empress under the Royal Titles Act in 1877.

Other regions were acquired through different means. Egypt came under British military occupation in 1882, though it did not form part of the British empire. Following a claim of "right of conquest," Great Britain established joint sovereignty with Egypt over the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan via an agreement on January 19, 1899. In Africa, the "hinterland doctrine" was used to define spheres of influence. Following an Anglo-German agreement on July 1, 1890, Uganda was assigned to the British sphere. To consolidate this position against French intrigues, Captain F.D. Lugard was sent to the capital, Mengo, in December 1890 to establish the authority of the company. This move also served the goal of suppressing the slave trade, as agreed upon by powers in Brussels in July 1890.

## Imperial Trade and Economic Policy

British trade policy underwent a significant transition from a restrictive mercantile system to free trade, and later toward "imperial preference." For nearly two hundred years, the Navigation Act of 1660 and similar laws restricted colonial trade to British ships to benefit British merchants. These acts were repealed in 1849, following the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846.

By the late 19th century, a movement for "imperial preference" emerged. At the 1887 Colonial Conference in London, a South African delegate proposed a 2% duty on foreign goods to maintain the imperial navy. By 1897, Britain denounced treaties with Belgium and Germany that hindered domestic trade arrangements between the mother-country and its colonies. Canada led this trend in 1897 by introducing an imperial tariff that granted a 25% deduction on customs duties for British goods, a preference also extended to other British colonies. This sentiment was echoed by Australia, New Zealand, and the South African states.

## Intellectual and Political Currents

The period saw the rise of a "new British imperialism," which some observers noted was influenced by the nationalist imperialism of Germany. This movement found a poet in Mr. Kipling and support from business and financial interests seeking monopolies. While Germany operated as a compact economic system (the Zollverein), the British Empire was described as "sprawling like an open hand," with members possessing diverse needs and no common interest beyond a guarantee of safety.

Literature on the subject grew during this era. Notable works include Sir Charles Dilke's *The British Empire* (1899) and *Greater Britain* (1869), G.R. Parkin's *Imperial Federation* (1892), and J.A. Hobson's *Imperialism* (1902), which provided an anti-imperialist perspective. Politically, a "Unionist" (Imperialist) government held power in Britain in 1895, though the Unionist party was defeated by Liberals in 1906 over the issue of tariffs.

## International Relations and Conflict

Britain's imperial standing was frequently challenged by other powers, particularly Germany. In 1890, Britain ceded the island of Heligoland to Germany, which the Kaiser converted into a naval fortress. The rapid extension of the German colonial empire in the Pacific and Africa, combined with the growth of the German navy, caused alarm among British liberals.

The empire also faced internal and external conflicts. The war against the Boer Republics in South Africa began in 1899, generating general hostility toward Britain across Europe. Other notable events included the Fashoda quarrel with France in 1898 and the British invasion of Tibet in 1904.

## Domestic Agriculture

During this period, Great Britain saw a steady increase in the area dedicated to fruit and flower farming. The extent of orchards grew from 202,234 acres in 1887 to 234,660 acres by 1901. Similarly, the area used for small fruit production increased from 46,234 acres in 1890 to 74,999 acres in 1901. By 1908, the total area for small fruit in Great Britain reached 84,880 acres, with the vast majority located in England.
