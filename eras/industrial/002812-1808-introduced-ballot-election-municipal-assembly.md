# The History and Implementation of the Ballot

The ballot is the modern method of secret-voting utilized within judicial, legislative, and political assemblies, as well as in the proceedings of corporations and private clubs. The term is derived from the Italian *ballotta* (a diminutive of *balla*, meaning ball), referring to the historical practice of dropping a small ball into a specific receptacle to indicate a choice. While the term now applies to any system of secret-voting, the use of the ball persists in some contexts, where a "black ball" denotes a hostile vote.

## Ancient and Early Origins
In ancient Athens, dicasts used balls of metal (*sponduli*) or stone (*psephi*) to deliver verdicts. Unpierced or white balls signified acquittal, while black or pierced balls signified condemnation. These were placed in brass or wooden boxes. Secret-voting was also used in the Athenian assembly for *privilegia*, including the release of state-debtors, the naturalization of foreigners, and ostracism. Additionally, Syracuse practiced "petalism," which was voting via words written on olive-leaves.

The Roman Republic introduced the ballot to the *comitia* through the *Leges Tabellariae*. The *Lex Gabiana* (139 B.C.) applied to the election of magistrates, the *Lex Cassia* (137 B.C.) to *judicia populi*, and the *Lex Papiria* (131 B.C.) to the repeal and enactment of laws. Romans used wooden *tabellae* placed in wicker boxes (*cista*). For laws, they marked A. (*antiquo*) or U. R. (*uti rogas*); for public trials, they used D. (*damno*) or L. (*libero*); and for elections, they made *puncta* opposite the initials or names of candidates. Roman *judices* also used *tabellae* to express judgments: N. L. (*non liquet*), C. (*condemno*), or A. (*absolvo*).

## Development in Europe
In Prussia, the ballot was introduced to the election of the municipal assembly (*Stadtverordnetenversammlung*) in 1808 by Stein through the *Staedteordnung*, or municipal corporation act. Later, under the new constitution of January 1, 1871, and the German constitution of 1867, Reichstag elections were conducted via universal suffrage under the ballot, following the electoral law of May 31, 1869.

In France, the *scrutin* (ballot) was used for deliberative voting in the chamber of deputies from 1840 to 1845. During the Second Empire, the Organic Decree of February 2, 1852, regulated its use for elections to the Corps Legislatif. Voters presented a polling-card to a bureau consisting of a secretary, four unpaid assessors, and a deputy returning-officer. Votes were recorded via a "bulletin," which was generally printed with a candidate's name and provided by an agent outside the assembly. To be valid, the bulletin had to be on white paper without external signs.

Hungary's history with the ballot is noted as remarkable. Secret voting was unknown there before 1848. The electoral law of 1848 left regulations to town and county councils, few of which used the ballot. The primitive method involved candidates having large, color-coded boxes with their names; voters entered alone and placed a rod (4 to 6 feet long) through a slit in the lid. While the 1874 electoral law abolished the ballot for parliamentary elections, it made the ballot obligatory for county and town council elections.

## The Ballot in Great Britain
The ballot was suggested for the British parliament during the time of Charles II. In 1662, the Scots parliament used it for the Billeting Act to ostracize political opponents, including Lauderdale. Members wrote names of suspected persons in a disguised hand on paper, which were placed in a sealed bag, opened in the exchequer chamber, and then burned. In 1705, Fletcher of Saltoun proposed secret-voting to protect members from court influence.

Demand for the ballot in parliamentary elections grew during the early 19th-century reform agitations. The Benthamites advocated for it in 1817, and banners mentioning the ballot appeared at the 1819 Peterloo Massacre. O'Connell introduced a bill in 1830, and Lord John Russell's Reform Bill originally provided for its introduction. Between 1833 and 1839, the historian Grote became a primary supporter in the House of Commons.

In 1872, W. E. Forster's Ballot Act introduced the ballot for all municipal and parliamentary elections, excluding university parliamentary elections. This act abolished public nominations and addressed election expenses and the offense of personation. The procedure was adopted by the Scottish Education Board for the first School Board election in 1873. The Act also established penalties for forging papers, fraudulently interfering with boxes, or supplying unauthorized papers; officers and clerks could face two years' imprisonment, while others faced six months.

## The Ballot in the Americas
Early American elections used *viva voce* voting, though some colonies adopted written or printed ballots early on. By 1775, ballots were used in Pennsylvania, Delaware, North Carolina, South Carolina, and the New England states. New Jersey adopted them in 1776 and New York in 1778. At the time of the U.S. Constitution's adoption, only Georgia, Virginia, and Maryland still used *viva voce*. Maryland, Georgia, Arkansas (1846), and Illinois (1848) adopted the ballot in the first half of the 19th century. Missouri generally adopted it in 1863, and Virginia in 1869.

Initially, ballots were written by voters or provided by candidates. Later, political committees prepared "ballot-tickets" for party-lists. Because these were distributed near the polls, secrecy was difficult, facilitating bribery and intimidation.

The "Australian ballot" was introduced in Canada and later in the U.S. Massachusetts enacted the first complete law in 1888, requiring the state to print and distribute alphabetical ballots. This system gained momentum after the 1888 presidential election, where allegations of large-scale vote purchasing created alarm. In 1889, nine states enacted new laws, including Montana, Missouri, Tennessee, Indiana, Michigan, Wisconsin, Rhode Island, and Connecticut.

By 1891, seventeen more states and two territories adopted the Australian system. Some, like Colorado, Pennsylvania, West Virginia, Ohio, Maine, Illinois, and Delaware, used party-groups. Others, such as Texas, Kentucky, Oregon, South Dakota, North Dakota, New Hampshire, Nebraska, and Arkansas, followed the Massachusetts arrangement of names by office. California used the Massachusetts arrangement but added a list of party names to allow a voter to cast a vote for all candidates of a single party.
