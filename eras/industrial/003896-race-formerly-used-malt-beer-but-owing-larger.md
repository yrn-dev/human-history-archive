# Barley and the Brewing Industry

## Barley Characteristics and Varieties
Barley (_Hordeum sativum_) is a member of the grass family and a cereal primarily found in temperate regions. It originated from a wild species native to western Asia, _H. spontaneum_, and has been cultivated since the earliest times. There are three recognized subspecies or races of barley:

1. **Two-rowed barley** (_H. sativum_, subsp. _distichum_): This race is most common in central Europe and the British Isles. It is characterized by spikes that are strongly compressed laterally and ears containing only two longitudinal rows of grain, as only the middle spikelet of each triplet is fertile. This race includes the finest malting-barleys. Notable sub-races include the "peacock, fan or battledore barley" (with short, erect ears resembling a peacock's tail), "erect-eared barleys" (with plump grains and broad ears), and "nodding barleys" (where ripe ears hang almost parallel to the stem). The Chevalier variety of nodding barley is noted as one of the best for malting.
2. **Six-rowed barley** (_H. sativum_, subsp. _hexastichum_): In this race, all flowers of each triplet of spikelets on both sides of the rachis are fertile, resulting in six longitudinal rows of grain. The straw and ears are short, and the grain is coarse and thin. While hardy, it is rarely found in Great Britain due to the poor quality of the grain.
3. **Four-rowed barley** (_H. sativum_, subsp. _vulgare_): Also known as bere or bigg, this race also has fertile flowers in each triplet, but the rows are not arranged at equal distances.

## History of Beer
Beer is a beverage produced through alcoholic fermentation, primarily using water, hops, and cereals—chiefly malted barley. Its history spans several thousand years. Egyptian writings from as early as the fourth dynasty mention beer made from red barley or malt, calling it _heqa_. Papyri from the time of Seti I (1300 B.C.) describe individuals inebriated from beer. While Herodotus (450 B.C.) claimed Egyptians made wine from barley because they lacked vines, other records and Scripture suggest vines were abundant there. Pliny referred to Egyptian corn-wine as _sythum_.

The Greeks learned the art of brewing from the Egyptians; the poet Archilochus provided evidence of Greek brewing knowledge around 650 B.C. Greek writers such as Sophocles called the drink _bryton_, while Dioscorides mentioned types called _zythos_ and _kourmi_. Although the Romans and Greeks often viewed beer as a "barbarian drink," it was the usual beverage of the Germans in the time of Tacitus (1st century after Christ). Pliny also noted its use in Gaul (as _cerevisia_) and Spain (as _celia_ and _ceria_).

## Brewing Materials and Processes
The primary materials for brewing are water, malt, and hops, though preservatives and various substitutes are also used. In the United Kingdom, brewers sometimes use substitutes like sugar, flaked maize, rice, or glucose to simplify operations, accelerate turnover, or reduce costs. While the diastatic enzyme in malted barley can convert the starch of unmalted grains into sugar, excessive use of substitutes (beyond 15-20%) can diminish the flavor and quality of the beer.

Different types of beer require different malts and mashing temperatures:
* **Pale ales:** Made from pale malt (often a mix of English and foreign malts like California or Smyrna) or pale malt with glucose, rice, or flaked maize.
* **Running beers (mild ale):** Made from a mixture of amber and pale malts, flaked goods, and sugar.
* **Stout:** Produced from roasted (black), amber, and pale malts, sometimes with flaked maize or sugar.

The mashing temperature significantly affects the final product. High temperatures (150° to 154° F) are used for stock or pale ales to produce a "dry" beer with high dextrin and low maltose. Lower temperatures (149° to 154° F) are used for mild running ales to create a sweeter beer. Stouts and black beers typically use temperatures between 148° and 150° F.

## Taxation and Regulation in England
The English brewing industry has been closely tied to taxation. The first beer duty was imposed under Charles II in 1660, charging 2s. 6d. per barrel for strong beer and 6d. for weak beer. By 1800, this rose to 8s. to 10s. before being repealed in 1830. A malt tax was introduced under William III in 1697, starting under 7d. per bushel and reaching 4s. 6d. after 1830. A hop duty existed from 1711 to 1862 but was abolished due to revenue fluctuations caused by crop uncertainty.

The Free Mash-Tun Act of 1880 shifted the duty from malt to the wort (beer), setting a rate of 6s. 3d. per 36-gallon barrel at a specific gravity of 1.057. This allowed brewers more freedom in choosing materials, though "saccharin" was prohibited in 1888 because it artificially inflated the apparent gravity of the beer. By 1909, the duty had risen to 7s. 9d. per barrel.

## Global Consumption and Composition
Beer generally contains 3% to 6% alcohol and 4% to 7% solids, including maltose, dextrins, and nitrogenous bodies like peptones. It provides nutritional value; for an average British worker consuming two pints daily, beer may provide roughly 3% of their total proteid and 11% of their total carbohydrate intake.

Consumption varies widely by region. Bavaria has the highest per capita consumption at over 50 gallons (exceeding 70 gallons in Munich), followed by Belgium at 47.7 gallons. While Germany is often seen as the primary beer-drinking nation, its per capita consumption is slightly lower than in England. In British colonies, consumption is lower: Western Australia consumes about 25 gallons per head, while Canada consumes 5 gallons.
