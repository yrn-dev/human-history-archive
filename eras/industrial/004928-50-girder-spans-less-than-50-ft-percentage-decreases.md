# Girder Bridges: Design, Materials, and Impact Stresses

Girder bridges are a category of bridge where half of the principal members are in tension and the other half are in compression. The selection of a girder bridge over other types, such as arched bridges (where principal members are in compression) or suspension bridges (where they are in tension), depends on complex factors including available materials, cost, maintenance requirements, and aesthetics. While masonry and concrete are more durable than metal, and metal more durable than timber, steel is more economical for longer spans and becomes imperative for very long spans.

## Materials and Evolution
Historically, bridges were constructed from masonry, timber, brick, or concrete. In the late 18th century, wrought iron began to be used, often in combination with cast iron or timber. Early railway bridges utilized cast iron girders, though cast iron is now limited to arched bridges of moderate spans. Wrought iron was used extensively in early 19th-century suspension road bridges and in major mid-19th-century girder bridges, such as those at Saltash and over the Menai Strait.

From 1880 onwards, mild steel—which possesses greater toughness and tenacity than wrought iron—largely superseded wrought iron, except for girders with spans of less than 100 feet. More recently, concrete strengthened with steel bars (ferro-concrete or armoured concrete) has been introduced for arched bridges. The use of metal has enabled the construction of massive spans ranging from 500 to 1,800 feet.

## Structural Design and Economy
The design of a girder involves a balance between depth and span. In the Britannia bridge, the proportion of depth to span was 1/16. While increasing the depth of a girder reduces the stress resisted by the flanges, it requires a heavier web. For plate web girders, the economic ratio of depth to span is limited to between 1/12 and 1/15. Plate web girders are currently only used for spans under 100 feet. In contrast, braced, lattice, or open web girders are more economical for larger spans, a design influenced by earlier American timber bridges.

The "economic span" is the length that minimizes the total cost of a multi-span bridge. This occurs when the cost of one pier is equal to the cost of the erected main girders of one span. Sir Guilford Molesworth provided a less exact formula for this: $l = 100\sqrt{P}/\sqrt{G}$, where $P$ is the cost of one pier and $G$ is the cost of a 100-ft. span superstructure.

## Support Systems and Configurations
Girder bridges are categorized by their mode of support:
*   **Discontinuous (Independent):** Main girders are supported only at the ends.
*   **Continuous:** Girders are supported at two or more points. This allows for weight economy; in a three-span bridge, continuous girders can be approximately 45% lighter for dead load and 15% lighter for live load than discontinuous ones. However, continuity is risky because small settlements in pier levels can greatly alter stress distribution.
*   **Cantilever and Suspended Girders:** These use hinges at points of contrary flexure to eliminate ambiguity regarding stresses. Cantilevers can be built out from piers without temporary scaffolding, which is advantageous for deep water or active navigation.

Regarding boom configuration, most bridges with modest spans use parallel booms. For spans exceeding 200 feet, it is more economical to use one horizontal boom and one polygonal (approximately parabolic) boom.

## Load and Impact Stresses
The total load on a bridge consists of the live load and the dead load. The dead load includes wind-bracing, flooring, and main girders. In railway bridges, flooring (rails, sleepers, etc.) weighs 0.2 to 0.25 tons per foot run per line, while cross girders and rail girders weigh 0.15 to 0.2 tons. A footway adds approximately 0.4 tons per foot run.

Impact stresses caused by moving trains create vibrations and deflections. Research by S.W. Robinson and F.E. Turneaure indicates that locomotive balance weights significantly cause vibration, which can be cumulative. General observations include:
*   Vibration is minimal at speeds below 25 m. per hour.
*   At speeds of 40 to 50 m. per hour, the increase in deflection due to impact can reach 40% to 50% for girder spans of less than 50 feet.
*   This percentage decreases rapidly for longer spans, dropping to about 25% for 75-ft. spans.
*   Impact increases boom stresses similarly to deflection, though the increase in web bracing bars is somewhat greater.
*   Train speed affects the magnitude of vibrations but not the mean deflection.

Empirical allowances for impact stresses are added to live load stresses: 20% for floor stringers, 15% for floor cross girders, 10% for 40-ft. main girder spans, and 5% for 100-ft. main girder spans.

## Notable Examples
The Ohio bridge on the Cincinnati & Covington railway is cited as perhaps the largest girder span constructed. Built in 1888 by the Phoenix Bridge Company, it features a center span of 550 feet and side spans of 490 feet. The center girder is 545 feet long with a depth of 84 feet. It utilizes mild steel for the main structure and wrought iron for the wind-bracing, stringers, and cross girders, with a total weight of approximately 5,000 tons of iron and steel across the three spans.
