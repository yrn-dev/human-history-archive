# Administrative Regulations and Legal Systems

Across various historical and regional contexts, the functioning of government and the delivery of justice have often relied on administrative regulations, customs, and the specific structures of executive power rather than solely on codified law.

## Language and Administration in Austria
In the Austrian empire, the "external service"—the communication between government offices and private individuals—was historically conducted using the language of the district. This practice allowed evidence to be presented in law courts in the language generally spoken by the population. This arrangement was not the result of any law, but instead depended on administrative regulations of the government service. Such regulations were practically necessary in remote districts like Bukovina and Galicia, where few people understood German. In certain instances, a Slav-speaking individual was required to provide their own interpreter to approach the government in German.

The Austrian government demonstrated liberality regarding local authorities, such as diets and town councils, which were free to use any language they wished. This custom was later established as a right under the constitution of 1867. Article 19 of this constitution recognized the equality of all customary (*landesueblich*) languages in public life, office, and school, asserting that every race had an inviolable right to the use and preservation of its own language and nationality. However, because the government retained the power to determine which languages were considered "customary," the application of this law granted the government significant power. This led to disputes, such as Germans demanding the recognition of their language in Galicia and Dalmatia, and Czechs claiming their language be recognized as customary in districts like Reichenberg, which were almost entirely German.

## Judicial Administration in Tokugawa Japan
During the Tokugawa period, the administration of justice was based on ethical principles rather than laws promulgated for prospective application. Laws were compiled only as occasions arose, with the goal of ensuring provisions remained consonant with the dictates of humanity. 

The second shogun, Hidetada, rejected a proposal to record the judgments of the expert municipal administrator Shimada Yuya for the guidance of future judges. Hidetada argued that because human affairs change radically, establishing universally recognizable precedents was impossible and would lead to the "slavish sacrifice of ethical principles on the altar of stereotyped practice."

Initially, the shogun served as the court of appeal for important cases, which were first brought before a *bugyo*. In 1631, under the third shogun, Iemitsu, a public courthouse known as the *Hyojo-sho* was established in Yedo. Later, in 1742, the *roju* Matsudaira Norimura and three administrators were commissioned to compile a fifteen-volume body of laws called the *Hatto-gaki* (Prohibitory Writings). This project involved the shogun's personal interest and consultation with veteran officials, including Ooka Tadasuke, Kada Arimaro, and Ogyu Sorai, and drew upon the ancient Japanese *Daiho-ritsu* and the Ming laws of China.

## Communication with the Japanese Throne
The Bakufu maintained influence over the Throne through strict administrative controls regarding communication. All reports to the Emperor had to be submitted via an administrator (*bugyo*), the *denso* (an official appointed by the Bakufu for this purpose), or a regent. Any person who attempted to address the Throne directly, regardless of rank, was sent into exile. Because the Bakufu controlled the appointment and continuance of ministers and regents, and the *bugyo* were nominees of Yedo, the Throne was only approachable through the channel of the Bakufu.

## British Colonial and Legal Administration
The British colonial empire utilized over fifty distinct governments, categorized into three classes of colonies and various dependencies. These were sometimes associated in federated groups for administrative purposes.
*   **Crown Colonies:** The crown maintained entire control of legislation, and the home government controlled public officers. Laws were generally made by the governor with a crown-nominated council, though in Gibraltar and St Helena, the governor could make laws alone.
*   **Representative Colonies:** The crown held a veto on legislation, and the home government retained control of public officers.
*   **Responsible Colonies:** The crown held a veto on legislation, but the home government had no control over public officers except for the governor.

In British India, various states were placed under different government agencies (such as the Madras, Bombay, Central Provinces, Bengal, Punjab, and Central Indian governments). Nepal remained independent regarding its internal administration but was bound to receive a British resident and had its political relations with other states controlled by the government of India following the campaign of 1814-15.

In England, the latter half of the 19th century saw legislative changes, including the 1875 merger of old courts into a new Supreme Court of Judicature. While some branches of common law (such as partnership, sale of goods, and bills of exchange) underwent "partial codification," other areas, such as the law of real property, remained uncodified. Consequently, the fate of English law became increasingly dependent on the ministry or the will of parliament.
