# Baby-Farming

Baby-farming is a general term used to describe the practice of taking in infants to nurse in exchange for payment. While the term covers the basic act of nursing for hire, it typically carries an implication of improper treatment of the children involved.

## Practices and Abuses in England
Previous to the year 1871, the abuse of baby-farming in England grew to an alarming extent. This practice was largely connected to the issue of illegitimacy, as baby-farms existed where children were received without question upon the payment of a lump sum. Because these children were nearly all illegitimate, it was to the pecuniary advantage of the baby-farmer to hasten the child's death. 

The trials of Mary Hall and Margaret Waters brought public attention to the "infamous relations" existing between baby-farming houses and lying-in houses in London. Additionally, it became a common practice for mill-hands and factory operatives to place their children out by the day. In many of these instances, parents viewed the children as a drain on their resources or a burden, leading to a lack of particular inquiry into how the children were being cared for. In some cases, parents paid a sum for maintenance that was ridiculously insufficient.

## Findings of the 1871 Select Committee
In 1871, the House of Commons appointed a select committee to investigate the best methods for preventing the destruction of the lives of infants put out to nurse for hire. The committee identified several factors that led to the wasting away, convulsions, and diarrhoea of these infants within a few months, including:
* Wilful neglect
* Bad air and crowded rooms
* Want of cleanliness
* Drugs, opiates, and improper or insufficient food

The committee noted that nearly all these unfortunate children were illegitimate. Experience from the Magdalene home and the Foundling hospital suggested that the great mortality among these children was partly explained by the fact that they were hand-nursed rather than breast-nursed. Conversely, children nursed by their mothers in the workhouse generally lived.

## Legislative Responses in England
The practical outcome of the 1871 committee's findings was the act of 1872. This legislation mandated the compulsory registration of all houses that received more than one child under the age of one year for a period longer than twenty-four hours.

## Regulations in the United States
In most United States states, the law regarding baby-farming is noticeably strict. 

In New York, the penal code requires a license for baby-farming to be issued by the board of health of the town or city where the children are kept. Licensed individuals must maintain a register that includes:
* The names and ages of all children boarded or kept, and children born on the premises.
* The residences and names of the parents, as far as they are known.
* The time of discharge and reception of the children, including the reasons for these actions.
* A correct register of any child under five years of age who is indentured, taken away, adopted, or given out, including the residence and name of the person adopting.

In Massachusetts, a law from 1891 requires any person receiving an infant under three years of age for board or for the purpose of procuring adoption to use diligence to determine if the infant is illegitimate. If the person knows or has reason to believe the infant is illegitimate, they must notify the State Board of Charity. The board's agents and officers may then inspect any building where they believe such an infant is boarded and remove the child if they judge it necessary to preserve the infant's life due to abuse, neglect, or other causes. Once removed, the infant is placed in the custody of the Board of Charity.

## Regulations in Australia and New Zealand
Most Australian states have implemented specific care to prevent the evils associated with baby-farming. 

In South Australia, the State Children Act of 1895 established a State Children's Council with broad powers to oversee infants under two years who are boarded out by their mother. Women who take in infants as boarders, known as "foster-mothers," must be licensed. The license fixes the number of children the foster-mother is authorized to keep. These licensed foster-mothers are required to keep a register detailing:
* The age, name, and place of birth of every child received.
* The description, addresses, and names of the parents, or any other person from whom the child was delivered or to whom they were delivered.
* The date of delivery or receipt.
* The name of any medical practitioner who attended to the child's illness or any accident.

New South Wales implemented similar state supervision and licensing of foster-mothers through the Children's Protection Act of 1892 and its 1902 amendments. In Victoria, an act was passed in 1890 to provide better protection for infant life. Similarly, New Zealand enacted the "Infant Life Protection Act 1896" and the "Adoption of Children Act 1895" to achieve the same effects.
