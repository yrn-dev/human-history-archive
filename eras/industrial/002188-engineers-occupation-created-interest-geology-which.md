# Engineers and the Study of Geology

The professional practice of engineering has frequently served as a catalyst for the study of geology, providing practitioners with the field experience and technical access necessary to investigate the earth's composition. This intersection is evident in the careers of several prominent geologists who began as mining engineers, military surveyors, or civil engineers.

## Engineering as a Path to Geological Study

For some, the transition from engineering to geology was a direct result of their professional duties. Andrew Geddes Bain, a native of Scotland who emigrated to the Cape Colony in 1820, initially worked as a saddler. However, his later engagement to construct a military road through the Ecca Pass revealed engineering talents that led to his permanent employment as a surveyor of military roads under the corps of Royal Engineers. This specific occupation created an interest in geology, which was further fostered in 1837 when he was loaned Lyell's *Elements*. Bain eventually devoted his spare energy to geological studies, discovering various reptilia—including the *Dicynodon* from the Karroo Beds near Fort Beaufort—and preparing the first comprehensive geological map of South Africa in 1852.

Similarly, Sir Richard John Griffith's career illustrates the overlap between civil engineering and geological mapping. After retiring from the Royal Irish Artillery in 1800, Griffith devoted himself to mining and civil engineering. He studied mineralogy and chemistry in London and examined mining districts across Scotland, Wales, and England, where he discovered cobalt and nickel ores in materials previously rejected as worthless. In 1812, he was elected as a mining engineer to the Royal Dublin Society and a professor of geology. His later work as an engineer of public works in Limerick, Kerry, and Cork, where he spent years repairing and laying out roads, as well as his role in the boundary survey of Ireland (1825–1844), provided the extensive investigations necessary to improve his geological map of the country.

## Mining Engineering and Academic Geology

Mining engineering, in particular, provided a rigorous academic and practical foundation for geological research. Gabriel Auguste Daubree, a French geologist born in 1814, qualified as a mining engineer at the age of twenty after being educated at the Ecole Polytechnique in Paris. His engineering background led to his appointment in 1838 to charge the mines in the Bas-Rhin (Alsace). Daubree's career progressed from mining engineer to engineer in chief of mines in 1859, and he eventually became a professor of geology at the museum of natural history in Paris and director of the Ecole des Mines in 1872. His engineering work between 1857 and 1861 regarding the springs of Plombieres allowed him to make observations on thermal waters and their effects on Roman masonry.

Joseph Marie Elisabeth Durocher also followed this trajectory. Educated at the Ecole Polytechnique and Ecole des Mines in Paris, Durocher qualified as a mining engineer. Early in his career, he traveled through northern Europe to study metalliferous deposits. This practical experience informed his later academic work as a professor of geology and mineralogy at Rennes, where he focused on the genesis of igneous rocks and the artificial production of minerals.

## Educational and Institutional Links

The relationship between engineering and geology extended into institutional teaching. Alexander Henry Green, an English geologist and former fellow of Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge, held a lectureship on geology at the school of military engineering at Chatham for many years. This demonstrates the integration of geological science into the formal training of military engineers.

Beyond these professional links, the broader interest in geology was often shared by individuals from various backgrounds. For example, a prolific writer and clergyman—whose eldest son was settled in America as an engineer—maintained an enthusiastic study of geology and natural history despite his primary vocation in the church.
