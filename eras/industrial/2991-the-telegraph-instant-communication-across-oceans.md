# The Electric Nerve: The Telegraph and the Shrinking World

## Overview
During the Industrial Revolution, the speed of information was tethered to the speed of physical transport—be it a galloping horse or a steamship. The advent of the electrical telegraph in the 1830s and 1840s fundamentally decoupled communication from transportation, creating what historians often call the "annihilation of space and time." 

While Samuel Morse is the most recognized figure in the English-speaking world, the telegraph was a global effort involving pioneers like William Cooke and Charles Wheatstone in Britain. The technology evolved from simple needle indicators to the sophisticated Morse code system, allowing complex messages to be sent via pulses of electricity. The ultimate triumph of this era was the laying of the Transatlantic Cable, which reduced the communication time between Europe and North America from ten days (by ship) to mere minutes.

## Key Facts
*   **Economic Impact:** The telegraph revolutionized global markets. For the first time, commodity prices (such as cotton or wheat) could be synchronized between London and New York, reducing arbitrage and creating the first truly global financial markets.
*   **Political Control:** Imperial powers, particularly Great Britain, used the telegraph to manage distant colonies. It allowed for more centralized control, though some historians debate whether this actually increased efficiency or simply allowed for more micromanagement from the metropole.
*   **The Cable Struggle:** Laying the undersea cables was a feat of industrial endurance. Early attempts failed due to insulation breakdowns (gutta-percha failure) and the sheer difficulty of deploying thousands of miles of cable from ships like the *Great Eastern*.

## Did You Know?
1. **The "Victorian Internet":** Many historians draw parallels between the telegraph and the modern internet, as both triggered a massive shift in how humans perceive distance and social interaction.
2. **The Morse Code Nuance:** Samuel Morse did not invent the telegraph alone; he collaborated heavily with Alfred Vail, who is credited with refining the code to match the frequency of letters in the English language.
3. **The 1858 Failure:** The first transatlantic cable of 1858 worked for only three weeks. The chief electrician, Wildman Whitehouse, attempted to increase the voltage to boost the signal, which inadvertently fried the cable's insulation.
