# Mashing and Brewing Processes

Mashing is a fundamental process in brewing that involves using water at an adequate temperature to extract soluble materials already present in malt. Additionally, it converts insoluble nitrogenous compounds and starch into products that are soluble and partly fermentable. Because the character of the beer is largely determined in the mash-tun, mashing is considered the most important of the brewing processes.

## The Mashing Process and Equipment
In modern brewing, the crushed malt, known as the grist, and the mashing liquor (water) are introduced into the mash-tun simultaneously via a mashing machine. This machine consists of a cylindrical metal vessel with a central shaft and screw that rotates rapidly to ensure a thorough mixture. 

The mash-tun itself is a large wooden or metal vessel featuring a false bottom made of plates with small holes or slits, which allows the liquid portion of the finished mash—called the wort—to be separated from the spent grains. The tun is equipped with rakes for stirring and a sparger, an automatic sprinkler with rotating hollow arms that sprays water to wash out the remaining wort from the grains. After the grist and liquor are mixed and the rakes are stopped, the mash typically rests for about two hours. The wort is then separated from the grains using a quantity of water totaling two to three barrels per quarter (336 lb) of malt.

## Temperature and Quality Control
The quality and type of beer produced depend almost entirely on the mashing temperature and the kind of malt used. Control can be exerted in the mash-tun or during the kiln process. The science of modern brewing utilizes various combinations of kiln and mashing heats.

Mashing temperatures vary by beer type:
*   **Stouts and black beers:** Usually 148° to 150° F.
*   **Pale or stock ales:** 150° to 154° F.
*   **Mild running beers:** 154° to 149° F.

English brewing generally operates within a limited range of 140° to 160° F. Higher temperatures serve to "cripple" the enzyme diastase, which converts insoluble starch into soluble sugar and dextrin. Consequently, higher temperatures produce more non-fermentable dextrinous matter and less fermentable maltose. This is essential for "dry" beers like stock ales that must keep. Conversely, lower temperatures produce the sweeter profile required for mild running ales.

## Regional Mashing Systems
### The Decoction System
The decoction method begins by mashing grist with cold water, then adding hot water to reach 85-100° F. About one-third of the mash is transferred to a mash copper (_Maisch Kessel_) and heated to approximately 165° F. until the mash becomes transparent. This portion, called the _Dickmaische_, is boiled for fifteen to forty-five minutes and then returned to the mash-tun to raise the overall temperature to 111-125° F. This process is repeated for a second "thick mash." Finally, another third, the _Läutermaische_ (clear mash), is withdrawn, boiled to precipitate coagulable albuminoids, and returned to the tun, with the final heat exceeding 160° F.

### The American System
American brewing typically combines infusion mashing with bottom fermentation. It begins with a very low initial heat of about 100° F. for one hour. The temperature is then rapidly raised to 153-156° F. using boiling "cooker mash" (raw grain wort from a converter) and is eventually increased to about 165° F. This method, along with the use of transformable malt adjuncts, allows American brewers to use malt that would be considered unfit for English breweries.

## Boiling and Fermentation
After mashing, the wort passes to a copper—either directly or via an underback—where it is boiled for one to three hours. Boiling serves to sterilize the wort, concentrate it, precipitate nitrogenous matter to prevent cloudiness, and extract flavor and aroma from hops. Hops provide hop tannin (which precipitates proteids), hop resin (a bitter and preservative), and volatile hop oil.

Following boiling and cooling, some systems employ "bottom fermentation." In this process, fermentation occurs in small vessels at low temperatures; cellars are kept at 37-38° F., and the wort does not exceed 50° F. The yeast settles at the bottom of the tun. Primary fermentation lasts eleven to twelve days, after which the beer is moved to lager casks at temperatures near the freezing point of water.

## Raw Grain Integration
When raw grains like rice or maize are used, they are typically gelatinized in a separate converter or cooker using high-temperature steam before being mixed with the main mash shortly before the taps are set. Alternatively, a limited-decoction system can allow raw grain to be used directly in the mash-tun, eliminating the need for a converter.
