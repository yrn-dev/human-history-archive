# Trial Holes and Site Investigation in Building

In the practice of building—defined as the mechanical operations required to carry an architect's designs into effect—the investigation of a site's physical composition is a critical preliminary step. When a building site is large or the nature of the soil is uncertain, the architect is advised to have trial holes sunk immediately following the approval of the sketch plans.

## The Role of the Architect in Soil Analysis
An accomplished architect must possess a scientific knowledge of the various types of soil they may encounter, including rock, gravel, silt, earth, clay, and chalk. This expertise is essential when trial holes are dug on a site, as it allows the architect to determine the nature of the soil. Through this observation, the architect can immediately identify the appropriate kind of foundation for the building and determine the necessary depth to reach a "good bottom."

Beyond soil science, the architect's technical competency must extend to several other areas to ensure the structural integrity of the work. This includes a knowledge of chemistry to understand the objections to, and effects of, various gases and acids found in materials. They must also be proficient in calculating the strengths and strains placed on any part of the structure and be acquainted with principles such as shoring, excavations, brickwork, carpentry, plumbing, ventilation, and timbering in trenches.

## Site Preparation and Legal Considerations
The process of preparing a site involves several administrative and legal hurdles. Once an architect has obtained the approval of the client and the consent of building authorities, they may face disputes with adjoining owners regarding party walls, air space, or "ancient lights." In the city of London, these specific difficulties frequently result in long suspensions of work and significant financial loss for the client.

If the property is held as a leasehold, negotiations must take place to obtain approval from freeholders and senior lessors. Once these approvals are secured, the architect can serve the required notices regarding party walls. 

Furthermore, the architect must ensure the following documentation is meticulously prepared:
* **Contract Plans:** These must be carefully developed, including elevations, plans, and sections of all building parts, with levels provided from a datum line.
* **Detailed Drawings:** In addition to general drawings, larger scale details of the building's principal portions are required.
* **Site Plans:** These must show the various levels of the ground.
* **Existing Surveys:** If there are existing buildings on the site, accurate detail plans must be made for reference, particularly concerning the rights and easements of adjoining owners.

## Archaeological Application of Trial Trenches
The use of trial excavations is not limited to modern construction but is also employed in archaeological efforts to locate historical sites. For example, in the effort to locate the agora (public square) of a city, twenty-one trial trenches were dug during the first year of work. These excavations led to the discovery of a ruined Roman theatre—built upon a Greek theatre—under 20 feet of soil. Further trial trenches revealed a broad limestone pavement, which allowed researchers to identify the intersection of roads and subsequently locate the agora.
