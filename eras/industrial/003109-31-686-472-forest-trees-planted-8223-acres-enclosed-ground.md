# The Seafield Plantations and Forest Tree Management

## The Seafield Plantations
In the counties of Nairn, Moray, and Banff, extensive agricultural enterprise was encouraged by landlords such as the earls of Fife and the earls of Findlater (who later became the earls of Seafield). A member of the Seafield family was recognized for his thriving and immense plantations of useful timber-trees, receiving an honorary gold medal from the Highland and Agricultural Society of Scotland in 1846.

Between the years 1811 and 1845, this individual planted a total of 31,686,472 forest trees across 8,223 acres of enclosed ground. This aggregate consisted of the following species:
*   Scots firs: 18,938,224
*   Larches: 11,904,798
*   Hardwoods: 843,450

## Characteristics of Forest Trees
Different species of forest trees exhibit distinct physical and ecological properties. The beech, for example, is noted for its smooth olive-grey bark and a sturdy, pillar-like stem that often reaches 15 to 20 feet in girth. Its main branches grow vertically, while subsidiary branches spread to create a rounded outline. In April, the tree produces clear green leaves with delicate silky hairs, followed by inconspicuous male and female flowers in May. The resulting fruit, or mast, consists of two or three chestnut-brown fruits enclosed in a tough, brown, four-sided cupule.

Ecologically, the beech has a strong ability to hold ground in congenial soil, and its deep shade prevents other trees from growing. It is considered more injurious to plants beneath its drip than other trees, causing shade-bearing species like thuja, yew, and holly to suffer, although its leaves enrich the soil. The beech is often mixed with Scotch fir and oak. While its timber is not known for durability or strength, it is used for charcoal, fuel, and the manufacture of bedsteads, chairs, and other minor articles.

## Global Forest and Flora Distributions
Forest distributions vary significantly by region. In British Central Africa, the landscape is primarily savannah or park-like, and actual forests of lofty West African-type trees are few. These are mainly limited to the Shiré Highlands, Angoniland, and Nyika plateaus, as well as some valleys near the south end of Tanganyika. Tropical luxuriance can be found on the slopes of Mounts Chiradzulu and Mlanje. The upper plateaus of Mount Mlanje feature forests of *Widdringtonia whytei*, a yew-like conifer related to the cypress but resembling the cedar.

In Japan, historical accounts under the Yamato describe the guardian of forests, Susanoo, fixing the specific uses for various trees. These included the use of hinoki (ground-cypress) and pine for building houses, camphor-wood for boats, and maki (*Podocarpus chinensis*) for making coffins.

## Orchard and Fruit Tree Management
The planting and maintenance of fruit trees involve specific spatial and environmental considerations. In the United States, apple trees in New York State are planted between 40 and 50 feet apart, though some orchards use 60-foot spacing (approximately 28 trees per acre) to allow for maximum development of air and light. Experience indicates that trees planted at 60 feet produce 43 bushels more fruit than those planted 30 feet apart (approximately 48 trees per acre). In the middle western states, the distance is typically 25 to 30 feet.

In Kent, England, various methods are used:
*   **Standard trees:** Apple and pear trees on arable land are planted 20 to 24 feet apart.
*   **Bush/Dwarf trees:** These are grafted on Paradise stock to ensure they bear fruit within two or three years. They may be planted alone at 8 to 10 feet apart (485 to 680 trees per acre) or placed between standard trees.
*   **Spacing for other fruits:** Cherry and apple trees on grass are generally spaced 30 feet by 30 feet.

The impact of ground cover is significant; the growth of grass around young apple trees is highly deleterious, causing a much greater injury than weeds. Grass-grown trees may show an increase in weight eighteen times smaller than those in tilled ground over five years, likely due to increased soil water evaporation and subsequent drought.
