# Palazzo Poggi and the University of Bologna

The Palazzo Poggi is a 16th-century building that has served as the established site of the University of Bologna since 1803. 

## History of the University of Bologna
The University of Bologna was founded in the 11th century, although legends suggest it was founded by Theodosius the Great in A.D. 425. The institution gained a European reputation as a school of jurisprudence through the work of the glossators, including Irnerius and Pepo, the first known teacher of Roman law at Bologna (approximately 1076). 

During the 12th through the 15th centuries, student populations typically ranged between three and five thousand, though it is said that by 1262 the number reached nearly ten thousand; notable students from this era included Petrarch and Dante. The university also provided instruction in anatomy during the 14th century.

## Institutional Development and Facilities
Despite its early fame, the university functioned as an autonomous corporation without a fixed residence for a significant period. Professors conducted lectures in their own homes or in rooms provided or hired by civic authorities. This changed in 1520 when professors of law were granted apartments in a building belonging to the church of S. Petronio. 

In 1562, Cardinal legate Carlo Borromeo constructed the university building nearby by order of Pius IV. This move was likely motivated in part by disciplinary needs, as Bologna had come under the dominion of the papacy in 1506. This building later served as the communal library starting in 1838. Its courtyard features the arms of students who were elected as representatives of their faculties or nations.

## Academic Life and Enrollment
The university has a history of diverse scholarship and faculty, including the appointment of women as professors on more than one occasion. In the 17th century, the university counted Marcello Malpighi, a great anatomist, among its most famous professors. Earlier, in 1564, the student Tasso was tried for the authorship of a satirical poem.

Enrollment numbers fluctuated significantly in the 19th century. Between 1815 and 1848, the number of students sank to approximately one hundred in some years, a decline attributed primarily to political persecutions carried out by the government. By 1859, the student population had risen to 355. In its current state, the university is attended by some 1,700 students across four faculties.
