# Marine Insurance

Marine insurance is a specialized field of insurance governing risks associated with shipping and maritime commerce. The subject is detailed in several legal and professional texts, including the *Handbook of Marine Insurance* (London, 1900) by Gow and *On Marine Insurance* by Arnould, the latter of which was edited by Messrs Hart & Simey in 1901 and published in a 7th edition in London in 1901.

## General Average and Insurer Liability

A significant area of marine insurance concerns "General Average" (G.A.) and the resulting liability of insurers. Legal precedents have historically debated whether an insurer's liability depends on the character of the loss or the possibility of contribution from other parties.

In the case of the *Brigella* (1893, P. 189), a shipowner incurred expenses that would typically be subject to G.A. contributions. However, because the shipowner was the sole party interested in the voyage, there were no other contributories. The claim against the ship's insurers for what would have been the ship's G.A. contribution was disallowed. The ruling established that there could be no G.A. under those specific circumstances, meaning the insurer's liability depended on the fact or possibility of contribution rather than the nature of the loss itself.

This precedent was not followed in the case of *Montgomery v. Indemnity Mutual M. I. Co.* (1901, 1 K.B. 147). In this instance, the ship, cargo, and freight all belonged to a single individual. While the ship was not insured, the cargo was. The cargo underwriters were held liable to pay a contribution for damage caused to the ship when masts were cut away for general safety. The court reasoned that the loss was theoretically spread across all interests at risk, and the underwriters had agreed to bear the cargo's share of such losses, regardless of whether all interests belonged to one person. This perspective aligns with the view held in the United States.

## Impact of War on Contracts

The outbreak of war has specific legal effects on the status of marine insurance contracts. Unlike fire insurance contracts, which are not considered dissolved by war, marine insurance contracts are dissolved by the outbreak of war. 

An exception to this dissolution exists if the risk being insured against had already been incurred prior to the outbreak. In cases where the risk had not yet attached, any premiums paid are recoverable. If the risk had attached, both the premiums due and the sums due on losses are recoverable.
