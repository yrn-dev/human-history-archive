# Porcelain Industry and Production

The manufacture of porcelain is a global industrial activity appearing in various forms, ranging from large-scale regional concentrations to specialized urban production and medical applications.

## European Production Centres

In Europe, porcelain production is found across several countries, with notable concentrations in Austria, Germany, and Portugal.

### Bohemia
Bohemia is one of Europe's greatest manufacturing centres and ranks first among the Austrian provinces from an industrial perspective. The region's industry grew significantly during the second half of the 19th century, aided by abundant water supplies, coal, and iron. Within Bohemia, the porcelain industry is described as very extensive and is concentrated in and around Carlsbad. Additionally, the town of Dux (Czech *Duchcov*), located 86 miles north-north-west of Prague, manufactures porcelain along with earthenware and glass, benefiting from its location in the centre of a well-worked lignite deposit.

### Other European Regions
In Germany, the city of Bonn lists the manufacture of porcelain as one of its principal industries, alongside beer, machinery, flags, and jute spinning and weaving. In southern Portugal, the town of Loulé—situated in the district of Faro—manufactures porcelain in addition to leather, basket-making, and products derived from esparto grass, agave, and palm.

## Japanese Porcelain Origins

The manufacture of porcelain in Japan is distinguished from the production of earthenware. This innovation is attributed to Shonzui, who travelled to China in 1510. Upon his return, Shonzui established a kiln at Arita, located in Hizen. During this early period, he produced small quantities of porcelain using materials sourced from China, as Japanese supplies of the necessary materials were not yet known at that time.

## Specialized Applications in Dentistry

Beyond decorative or household wares, porcelain is utilized in the construction of dental dentures. In standard construction, porcelain teeth are attached to a gold base-plate using gold stay-pieces and platinum pins baked into the tooth's body.

A more advanced technique, known as the continuous gum method, originated in France during the early 19th century and was perfected by Dr. John Allen of New York around 1846-1847. In this method, porcelain teeth are attached to a pure platinum base-plate. The interstices between the teeth are filled, and the plate is covered with a porcelain paste called the "body," which is modelled to imitate the contour of the gums and baked in a muffle furnace until vitrified. This is then finished with a vitreous enamel to imitate the natural colour of the gum.
