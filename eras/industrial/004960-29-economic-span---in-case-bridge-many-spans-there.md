# Economic Span in Bridge Engineering

In the field of bridge engineering, the determination of the economic span is a critical component of design. Engineers must balance the requirement for adequate strength with the goal of securing the greatest economy. While local conditions—such as navigation requirements, waterway needs, or convenient sites for piers—may fix the span, the question of economy remains a primary consideration.

## Principles of the Economic Span
For a bridge consisting of many spans, there exists a specific length of span that minimizes the total cost. The costs associated with bridge flooring and abutments are generally independent of the span length adopted. The total cost that varies with the span is primarily the sum of the cost of the piers and the cost of the main girders.

The cost of a pier is largely determined by the height of the bridge and the character of the foundations, and does not vary materially based on the span length. Conversely, the cost of the main girders for one span varies nearly as the square of the span for any given intensity of live load and girder type. Broadly, the most economical arrangement is achieved when the cost of the superstructure of a single span is equal to the cost of one pier and its foundation.

Mathematically, if $P$ represents the cost of one pier and $G$ represents the cost of the main girders for one span erected, the cost is least when $P = G$. Sir Guilford Molesworth provided a less exact but convenient formula for the economic span ($l$): $l = 100\sqrt{P}/\sqrt{G}$, where $G$ is the cost of the superstructure of a 100-ft. span erected and $P$ is the cost of one pier with its protection.

## Material and Type Selection
The choice of bridge type—whether arched (compression), suspension (tension), or girder (both compression and tension)—depends on complex factors including available materials and maintenance costs. 

For moderate spans, concrete, masonry, or brick can be used without excessive cost. However, steel becomes more economical for longer spans and is imperative for very long spans. While masonry and concrete are more durable than metal, and metal more durable than timber, aesthetic considerations may also influence the choice; masonry is often preferred for appearance, and metal arch bridges are generally less objectionable than most girder forms.

## Girder Design and Economy
In girder bridges, the ratio of depth to span is a key factor in economy. While increasing the depth of a girder reduces the stress resisted by the flanges, it requires a heavier web. For plate web girders, the economic ratio of depth to span cannot be increased beyond 1/12 to 1/15, partly due to the need for extra thickness to reduce corrosion and stiffen against buckling. Consequently, plate web girders are now only used for spans of less than 100 ft. Open web, lattice, or braced girders are more economical of material than solid web girders, except in the case of small spans.

Furthermore, the shape of the booms affects economy in longer bridges. For spans under 200 ft., parallel booms are most common due to ease of workmanship. In spans exceeding 200 ft., it is more economical to utilize one horizontal boom and one polygonal (approximately parabolic) boom.

## Continuity and the Cantilever System
The mode of support significantly impacts the weight and cost of a bridge. Continuous girders—those supported at two or more points—permit an economy of weight compared to discontinuous or independent spans. In a three-span bridge, the theoretical advantage of continuity is approximately 49% for dead load and 16% for live load (or 45% and 15% respectively in other calculations). However, continuity introduces the risk that small settlements in the piers may alter stress distribution and render the bridge unsafe.

To mitigate this, the cantilever system can be used. By introducing hinges or joints at points of contrary flexure (where the bending moment is zero), ambiguity regarding stresses is removed. The cantilever system is often the simplest and most economical method for very large spans. It allows for "building out" from the piers member by member, which eliminates the need for temporary scaffolding below, reduces costs, and avoids interrupting navigation or building in deep water.
