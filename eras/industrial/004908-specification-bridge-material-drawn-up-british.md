# Specification for Bridge Material by the British Engineering Standards Committee

The British Engineering Standards Committee has established specific requirements for the materials used in the construction of bridges, focusing primarily on the composition and strength of the steel employed.

## Steel Composition and Quality
According to the specifications, the steel used for bridge material must be either basic open-hearth steel or acid open-hearth steel. To ensure the quality and integrity of the metal, the committee provides that the steel must contain no more than 0.06% of phosphorus or sulphur.

## Tensile Strength and Elongation
The specifications define different strength requirements based on the form of the steel component:

*   **Plates, Angles, and Bars:** For these components (excluding rivet bars), the required tensile strength is between 28 and 32 tons per square inch. These materials must also demonstrate an elevation of 20% in 8 inches.
*   **Rivet Bars:** These are tested using a gauge length that is eight times the diameter. For rivet bars, the specified tensile strength is between 26 and 30 tons per square inch, with a required elongation of 25%.

## Riveting Practices and Material Stress
In the broader context of bridge and girder work, the method of creating holes for rivets impacts the material's condition. While high-class boiler work now requires drilling for rivet-holes, average bridge and girder work still utilizes the practice of punching and reamering. This is permissible because the conditions of service for bridges are not as severe as those found in steam boilers.

Historically, the use of a "drift"—a tapered rod driven by hammer blows to align holes in adjacent plates—was common. However, this practice caused violent treatment of the metal, resulting in permanent strains and the enlargement of incipient cracks. Additionally, the process of reamering holes after plates were in place sometimes failed to provide a safeguard, as the metal was occasionally removed from only one side of the hole, leaving the other side torn by the punch.

## General Bridge Material Considerations
The choice of materials for bridges is influenced by several complex considerations, including cost, permanence, and aesthetics. While brick, concrete, or masonry are viable for moderate spans, steel is more economical for longer spans and becomes imperative for very long spans. In terms of durability, masonry and concrete are more durable than metal, which in turn is more durable than timber.

In the construction of railway bridges in England, wrought iron and steel plate web girders were used extensively following the construction of the Menai and Conway bridges. It was determined that for plate webs, the ratio of depth to span could not be economically increased beyond 1/12 to 1/15. This is due to the need for excess material to reduce the effects of corrosion and to stiffen the web against buckling. Consequently, open web, braced, or lattice girders are generally more economical than solid web girders, except in the case of small spans. Currently, plate web girders are only utilized for spans of less than 100 feet.
