# Franz Xaver von Baader

Franz Xaver von Baader (1765–1841) was a German theologian and philosopher. Born on March 27, 1765, in Munich, he was the third son of F. P. Baader, who served as the court physician to the elector of Bavaria. Baader had two distinguished brothers: the eldest, Clemens, was an author, and the second, Joseph (1763–1835), was an engineer.

## Early Education and Professional Career
Baader initially studied medicine in Vienna and Ingolstadt, and spent a brief period assisting his father in medical practice. Finding this path uncongenial, he transitioned to the field of mining engineering. He pursued studies under Abraham Gottlob Werner at Freiberg and traveled through various mining districts in northern Germany. Between 1792 and 1796, Baader resided in England. During this time, he encountered the ideas of Godwin, Hartley, and Hume, which he found extremely distasteful. Conversely, he found the mystical speculations of Saint Martin, Meister Eckhart, and particularly Jakob Boehme to be in harmony with his own thinking.

Upon returning from England in 1796, Baader settled in Hamburg, where he formed a long-term friendship with F. H. Jacobi. During this period, he became acquainted with the works of Schelling; while his publications from this era show a manifest influence from Schelling, Baader was not a disciple of the philosopher. Their friendship lasted until approximately 1822, when Baader wrote a letter to Emperor Alexander I of Russia denouncing modern philosophy, an act that alienated Schelling.

Baader continued to apply himself to his profession as an engineer for a significant period. He received a prize of 12,000 gulden (approximately L1000) for developing a new method of using Glauber's salts in glassmaking as a substitute for potash. From 1817 to 1820, he served as the superintendent of mines and was raised to the rank of nobility in recognition of his services.

## Academic Career and Later Life
Baader retired from his engineering post in 1820. Shortly thereafter, between 1822 and 1825, he published *Fermenta Cognitionis* in six parts, a work in which he recommended the study of Boehme and combated modern philosophy. In 1826, he was appointed as a professor of speculative theology and philosophy at the newly opened university in Munich. He later published some of these lectures in a four-part work titled *Spekulative Dogmatik* (1827–1836).

A member of the Roman Catholic Church, Baader opposed the church's interference in civil matters in 1838. As a result, he was interdicted from lecturing on the philosophy of religion during the final three years of his life. He died on May 23, 1841.

## Philosophical and Theological Thought
Baader is regarded as one of the most significant speculative theologians of modern Catholicism, influencing figures such as Hans L. Markensen, Julius Mueller, and R. Rothe. His philosophy is essentially a theosophy, though it is difficult to summarize because he often expressed his deepest thoughts through mystical symbols, analogies, or obscure aphorisms rather than systematic works. Much of his doctrine is found in journals, extensive correspondence, and short detached essays.

Baader's thought is characterized by several core tenets:
* **Faith and Reason:** He maintained that human reason alone cannot reach its intended end and that the presuppositions of tradition, church, and faith cannot be discarded. His approach is described as Scholasticism, as he believed reason should be used to clarify truths provided by revelation and authority.
* **God-Consciousness:** He posited that existence depends on being cognized by God (*cogitor ergo cogito et sum*). In his view, all self-consciousness is simultaneously God-consciousness, and knowledge is always *con-scientia*—a participation in or consciousness of God.
* **The Nature of God:** Baader conceived of God not as an abstract Being (*substantia*), but as an everlasting activity or process (*actus*). He distinguished between the emanent (exoteric) and immanent (esoteric) aspects of this self-generation. He argued that God has reality as absolute spirit, and that the primitive will becomes spirit only when it is conscious of itself.

## Collected Works
Following his death, several adherents—including Schlueter, von Osten-Sacken, Lutterbeck, E. v. Schaden, J. Hamberger, and F. Hoffman—collected and published his writings in *Baader's saemmtliche Werke* (16 volumes, 1851–1860). This collection includes a full biography in volume xv and a sketch of his system by Lutterbeck in volume xvi. Other notable scholarly surveys of his work have been provided by J. Claassen, Otto Pfleiderer, R. Falckenberg, Kuno Fischer, and Reichel.
