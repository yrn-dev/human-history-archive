# Ratio of Live to Dead

In the context of structural engineering and bridge design, the ratio of live to dead load is a critical factor used to determine working stress, deflection, and the impact of moving loads on a structure.

## Structural Engineering and Bridge Design

In bridge engineering, "dead load" refers to the weight of the structure itself, while "live load" refers to the moving loads it must carry. The ratio of live to dead load, denoted as $\rho = B/A$ (where $B$ is the live load and $A$ is the dead load), is used to establish safe working stress limits.

As early as 1869, Unwin suggested in *Wrought Iron Bridges and Roofs* that a rational method for fixing working stress would be to make it depend on this ratio. Specifically, the method proposes that the factor of safety for live load stresses should be double that for dead load stresses. Using this rule, the safe working stress $f$ can be computed based on the safe working limit of stress for a bar subjected to dead load only ($f_1$). The formula used is:
$f = f_1(1+\rho)/(1+2\rho)$

For iron, where $f_1$ is assumed to be 7½ tons per sq. in., and for mild steel, where $f_1$ is 9 tons per sq. in., the working stress varies according to the ratio:
*   **All dead load ($\rho = 0$):** Iron = 7.5; Mild Steel = 9.0 tons per sq. in.
*   **Live load = Dead load ($\rho = 1.00$):** Iron = 4.9; Mild Steel = 5.9 tons per sq. in.
*   **All live load ($\rho = \infty$):** Iron = 3.7; Mild Steel = 4.5 tons per sq. in.

This design rule has been utilized in America and is noted to differ little from designs based on Wöhler's experiments.

## Deflection and Impact Stresses

The ratio of live to dead load also influences the "excess of deflection" and stress caused by moving loads compared to resting loads. Data indicates that as the percentage of dead load increases, the ratio of live to dead load and the resulting excess of deflection decrease:
*   At 10% dead load (90% live load), the ratio is 9 and the excess of deflection/stress is 23%.
*   At 50% dead load (50% live load), the ratio is 1.0 and the excess is 4.0%.
*   At 90% dead load (10% live load), the ratio is 0.10 and the excess is 0.3%.

Furthermore, E.H. Stone found that the increment of deflection due to impact depends on the ratio of dead to live load. Impact stresses occur because vertical loads imposed suddenly, or those that increase deflection with speed, create greater stress than loads applied gradually. These stresses are more pronounced on short main girders than on long ones, and on flooring girders than on main ones.

## Material Fatigue and Breaking Strength

The breaking stress of a bar is not a fixed quantity but depends on the range of variation of stress ($\Delta$) to which it is subjected over many repetitions. This is referred to as "fatigue," where the breaking stress diminishes as the range of variation increases. For a statical load, the range of stress is nil ($\Delta = 0$). For a bar subjected to alternate tension and compression of equal amount, $\Delta = 2 f_{max.}$.
