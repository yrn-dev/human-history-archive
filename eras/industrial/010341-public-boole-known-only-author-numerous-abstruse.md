# George Boole

George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician. Born in Lincoln on November 2, 1815, he was the son of a studious tradesman of limited means who provided his first lessons in mathematical science. Although Boole's extraordinary mathematical abilities did not appear early in life—his initial favorite subject being classics—he began studying higher mathematics at age seventeen. His progress was hindered by a lack of efficient help. Professionally, Boole remained in the scholastic profession throughout his life, starting as an assistant-master in a private school at Doncaster at age sixteen. He later established a school at Lincoln, which he moved to Waddington, and was appointed professor of mathematics at Queen's College in Cork in 1849. In 1855, he married Miss Mary Everest, who later wrote educational works based on her husband's principles.

## Mathematical Publications and Research
To the general public, Boole was primarily known as the author of several standard publications and numerous abstruse papers on mathematical topics. His published output consisted of approximately fifty scattered articles and a few separate publications. His earliest paper, "Theory of Analytical Transformations," appeared in the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* in 1839, leading to a friendship with the journal's editor, D.F. Gregory, which lasted until Gregory's death in 1844.

Boole contributed twenty-two articles to the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* and its successor, the *Cambridge and Dublin Mathematical Journal*, and sixteen papers to the third and fourth series of the *Philosophical Magazine*. His work also appeared in the *Philosophical Transactions* of the Royal Society (six important memoirs), the *Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh*, the *Transactions of the Royal Irish Academy*, *Crelle's Journal*, and the *Bulletin de l'Academie de St-Petersbourg* for 1862 (where he published under the name G. Boldt). Additionally, he published a paper on the mathematical basis of logic in the *Mechanic's Magazine* in 1848.

During his lifetime, Boole completed two systematic treatises: the *Treatise on Differential Equations* (1859) and the *Treatise on the Calculus of Finite Differences* (1860), the latter serving as a sequel to the first. These works combined elementary exposition with profound philosophical investigation. In the *Differential Equations* treatise, Boole provided an account of the general symbolic method and a general method in analysis originally described in his 1844 memoir in the *Philosophical Transactions*.

## Symbolic Reasoning and Logic
Boole was one of the most eminent mathematicians to recognize that symbols of operation could be treated as distinct objects of calculation, separate from symbols of quantity. He possessed an unrivalled power in tracing results obtained by treating symbols according to their primary laws and conditions.

Aside from Augustus de Morgan, Boole was likely the first English mathematician since John Wallis to write on logic. He applied his confidence in symbolic reasoning to logical method. While he first presented these ideas in the 1847 pamphlet *Mathematical Analysis of Logic*, he later viewed this as an imperfect exposition. He considered his 1854 work, *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, on which are founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities*, to be the mature statement of his views.

Boole did not view logic as a branch of mathematics, but he identified a deep analogy between algebraic symbols and those representing logical forms and syllogisms. He proposed that logic is essentially mathematics restricted to the quantities 0 and 1. In this system, unity (1) denoted the universe of thinkable objects, while literal symbols (such as x, y, z, v, u) represented elective meanings similar to adjectives and substantives. For example, if x represents "horned" and y represents "sheep," the combination of x and y performed on unity represents the class of "horned sheep." Boole demonstrated that these elective symbols follow the same primary laws of combination as algebraical symbols, allowing them to be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided. Consequently, "1 - x" represents all things in the world except horned things.

In the *Laws of Thought*, Boole detailed a general symbolic method of logical inference to draw conclusions from premises through purely symbolic treatment. The second part of the work attempted to discover a general method in probabilities to determine the probability of an event logically connected to a system of given events.

## Later Life and Interests
In his final years, Boole worked toward a more complete second edition of his *Differential Equations*, spending part of his last vacation in the British Museum and the libraries of the Royal Society. However, he died before completing it. The manuscripts he left were so incomplete that Isaac Todhunter, who received them, could not use them for a second edition and instead printed them in a supplementary volume in 1865.

Beyond his professional work, Boole had a deep acquaintance with general literature. He studied the ethics of Spinoza, the metaphysics of Aristotle, and the philosophical works of Cicero. His favorite poet was Dante, specifically preferring the *Paradiso* over the *Inferno*. Boole also wrote and delivered four addresses on scientific, religious, and philosophical topics: *The Genius of Sir Isaac Newton*, *The Right Use of Leisure*, *The Claims of Science*, and *The Social Aspect of Intellectual Culture*.
