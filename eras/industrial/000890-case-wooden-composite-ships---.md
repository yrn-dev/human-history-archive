# Wooden and Composite Ships: Regulations and Construction

The archive contains diverse records regarding ships constructed from wood or composite materials, ranging from maritime insurance and repair regulations to historical accounts of construction techniques and ancient interpretations.

## Insurance and Repair Regulations
For wooden or composite ships, specific rules govern deductions made during the assessment of accidents. These deductions are generally regulated by the age of the ship rather than the age of the specific part being addressed. 

When a ship is less than one year old from the date of its original register at the time of an accident, no "new for old" deduction is applied. Once a ship exceeds one year of age, a deduction of one-third is typically made, though there are several notable exceptions:
* **Anchors:** These are allowed in full.
* **Chain cables:** These are subject to a smaller deduction of one-sixth.
* **Provisions and stores:** No deduction is made for those that have not been in use.
* **Metal sheathing:** The cost is allowed in full for a weight equal to the gross weight of the stripped metal sheathing, minus the proceeds gained from the old metal.
* **Nails, felt, and labour metalling:** These items are subject to a one-third deduction.

Additionally, no deduction is permitted for old material that is repaired without being replaced by new material. Regarding maintenance, painting of the bottom is only allowed if the bottom had been painted within the six months preceding the date of the accident.

For all ships generally, the archive notes that graving dock dues—including expenses for removals, cartages, use of stages, shears, and graving dock materials—are allowed in full. Similarly, the expense of replacing and taking out bent ironwork, including the labour for straightening it, is allowed in full.

## Historical Construction and Materials
Historical accounts describe varied methods of constructing wooden vessels, particularly in the Sea of India. Some ships in this region were described as "wretched affairs" due to a lack of iron fastenings. Instead of iron nails, builders used wooden trenails and stitched planks together using twine made from the husk of the Indian nut. This husk was beaten until it resembled horse-hair before being spun into twine. While this method resisted corrosion from sea-water, it was noted as being unable to withstand storms, making voyages perilous.

These vessels lacked decks, utilizing instead a cover made of hides spread over the cargo; horses intended for sale in India were placed on top of these hides. Furthermore, these ships were not pitched but were instead rubbed with fish-oil. They typically featured one rudder, one sail, and one mast.

## Ancient and Literary Interpretations
In the study of ancient texts, different interpretations of wooden structures have emerged. For instance, Euphorion construed the "wooden horse" not as an animal, but as a Grecian ship named *Hippos* ("The Horse").
