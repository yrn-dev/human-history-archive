# The Austrian Empire and the Legacy of 1815

The political landscape of the Austrian Empire during the mid-19th century was defined by a struggle between autocratic centralization and the demands for constitutional rights and national identity among its diverse populations. Central to this era was the influence of the Congress of Vienna of 1815 and the subsequent attempts by the House of Habsburg to maintain dominion over its territories.

## The Restoration of Autocracy
By 1851, Austria appeared to have overcome its immediate difficulties. Revolutionary movements had been suppressed, and the attempt by Prussia to lead Germany had been defeated. During this period, the old Federal Diet of 1815 was restored. Vienna once again served as the center of a despotic government. The primary objectives of this regime were to suppress attempts to secure a free press, check all agitation for a constitution, and Germanize the Slavs and Magyars. For approximately ten years, the Austrian dominion existed under one of the worst possible forms of autocratic government.

The failure of the Habsburg emperor to sustain this despotic regime was the result of three primary factors: the successful assertion of Prussia's claim to leadership in Germany, the establishment of Italian unity, and the Crimean War.

## The Crimean War and International Relations
The Crimean War highlighted the limited role of "gratitude" in international affairs. While Emperor Nicholas of Russia had relied on a sense of obligation from Austria, Austrian statesmen prioritized the free navigation of the Danube, which was threatened by Russian occupation of the Principalities. 

Austria initially avoided active participation in the war due to the fear of a Sardinian attack on its Italian provinces and the refusal of Prussia to support her. However, after Russian forces withdrew from the Principalities, Austrian troops occupied them. On December 2, 1854, Austria signed a treaty of alliance in Vienna with France and Great Britain, undertaking to defend the frontier of Moldavia and Walachia against returning Russian forces. As Russian arms suffered progressive disasters, Austria delivered an ultimatum to Emperor Alexander II in December 1855, which forced Russia to come to terms.

## Constitutional Transitions and the Bohemian Conflict
The events of the 1859 Italian campaign made the continuation of absolutism impossible. The empire attempted to transition to a constitutional system that would recognize the ancient constitutional rights of some united countries while maintaining imperial unity. A decree on October 20, 1860, established diets with limited powers, modeled partly on the ancient diets of Bohemia.

However, a subsequent decree on February 26, 1861, established a central parliament in Vienna with extensive powers and an electoral system that was grossly partial to Germans. While Bohemians initially sent representatives to Vienna, they left the parliament in 1863, claiming the assembly had encroached upon the constitutional power of the diet of Prague.

The constitutional position of Bohemia was further altered by the Austro-Prussian war of 1866. The Congress of Vienna in 1815 had previously declared that Bohemia should be part of the Germanic Confederation, a decision made without consulting the country's estates. Following the treaty signed at Prague on August 23, 1866, all lands ruled by the House of Habsburg were excluded from Germany, leading to a decline in German influence in Bohemia and the wider empire.

## The Constitution of 1867 and Nationalist Movements
In 1867, a new constitution was introduced. While Hungary obtained complete independence, the new constitution applied only to the Slavic and German parts of the empire, attempting to maintain centralization and waning German influence. This was energetically opposed by the Bohemians.

By 1868, nationalist movements gained ground in Galicia and Bohemia. In Galicia, the Poles demanded that the powers of the Galician diet be increased and that they cease attending the Reichsrath for matters the Galician diet was qualified to handle. In Bohemia, Czech leaders presented a "declaration" to the diet at Prague in 1868, demanding the full restoration of the Bohemian kingdom and asserting that no foreign assembly could impose taxes in Bohemia. This declaration was signed by eighty-one members, including bishops and feudal nobles.

## Political Instability and the Reichsrath
The late 1860s were marked by ministerial instability. In January 1870, Counts Taaffe and Alfred Potocki resigned after disagreeing with others who wished to cripple the opposition by removing Reichsrath elections from the diets. When new Galician demands were rejected in March, the Polish club, along with Slovenes and the Tirolese, left the House. This left the parliament consisting of only 110 members—Germans and German representatives from Moravia and Bohemia—rendering the government nearly impossible.

A subsequent attempt by Potocki to reconcile with the Czechs in Prague failed, as the Czechs and Feudals remained committed to the declaration of 1868. During the outbreak of war between France and Germany in July, the emperor dissolved the Reichsrath and all diets. In Bohemia, the Czechs won a large majority but refused to recognize the diet as the legal representative of the Bohemian kingdom, viewing it instead as an informal assembly.
