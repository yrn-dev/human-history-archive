# Industrial, y Estadistica de Bolivia (La Paz, 1900); Breves

The *Noticia Politica, Geografica, Industrial, y Estadistica de Bolivia* (La Paz, 1900) and *Breves Indicaciones para el Inmigrante y el Viajero a Bolivia* (La Paz, 1898) are listed among the primary authorities for the study of Bolivia's geography, industry, and statistics. These works, alongside other regional monographs and dictionaries, provide a framework for understanding the nation's resources, infrastructure, and administrative history during the turn of the 20th century.

## Geography and Climate
Bolivia is characterized by a wide range of temperatures across different latitudes, with natives identifying five distinct climatic zones. The *yungas* comprise lowlands and mountain valleys up to 5,000 ft., featuring a humid, tropical atmosphere with a mean temperature of 70 deg. F. Above this is the *valle* zone (5,000 to 9,500 ft.), a sub-tropical region of "perpetual summer." The *cabezera de valle* (9,500 to 11,000 ft.) is a temperate region suitable for vegetables and cereals. 

The great central plateau is known as the *puna* (11,000 to 12,500 ft.), where the air is cold and dry, limiting production primarily to barley and potatoes. Finally, the *puna brava* extends from 12,500 ft. to the snow limit at approximately 17,500 ft., a bleak territory inhabited by miners and shepherds. Above this lies the Arctic zone of eternal snow.

## Industrial and Agricultural Resources
The development of Bolivia's agricultural resources has been described as slow and hesitating. In the temperate and warm valleys, the cultivation of fruits, vegetables, and cereals followed the establishment of mining settlements. While sugar-cane was introduced early, it is primarily used today for the manufacture of rum due to limited sugar demand. Other crops include rice and fair-quality tobacco in the east and the *yungas* valleys of Cochabamba and La Paz. Superior grade cacao is grown in the department of Beni and the warm Andean valleys of Cochabamba and La Paz, while high-flavor coffee is produced in the eastern Andes.

Two indigenous products are of particular importance: quinoa, a staple food for natives, and coca, which is used medicinally for export and masticated by the native population. Coca is extensively grown in La Paz and Cochabamba, especially in the province of Yungas.

Mining remains a central industry. The Huanchaca mining company operates a great silver mining center at Pulacayo, employing 3,200 men in surface works and mines. In the north-east, the department of Beni exports forest products, including rubber and coca, as well as cattle.

## Infrastructure and Communications
Bolivia has historically been an isolated region, leading to several international treaties to improve communication. Under a 1903 treaty, Brazil agreed to build a 180 m. railway around the falls of the Madeira to provide north-eastern Bolivia access to the Amazon, and provided L2,000,000 in cash for internal railway construction. A 1904 treaty with Chile (ratified 1905) involved the construction of a 295 m. railway from Arica to La Paz.

Other key transport links include:
*   **Argentina:** An extension of the northern railway from Jujuy to the Bolivian town of Tupiza.
*   **Peru:** A 54 m. line from Guaqui on Lake Titicaca to Alto de La Paz, connected to the city of La Paz by a 5 m. electric line. This provides access to the port of Mollendo.
*   **Internal Lines:** Up to 1903, the only railways were the Antofagasta and Oruro line (350 m. within Bolivia), a 26 m. branch to the Pulacayo mines, and the Guaqui to Alto de La Paz line.

By 1907, further lines were projected to connect La Paz to Cochabamba and the navigable waters of the Beni, as well as routes from Viacha to Oruro and Uyuni to Potosi, Sucre, and Tupiza.

## Administration and Demographics
The total area of Bolivia, adjusted for territory lost to Brazil in 1903, is 515,156 sq. m. The national capital has shifted between Sucre (Chuquisaca), La Paz, Cochabamba, and Oruro. Following a revolution in 1898 and a battle near La Paz on January 17, 1899, the capital was established at La Paz, which serves as the commercial metropolis.

The 1900 census recorded a population of 1,609,365 Roman Catholics and 24,245 people of other creeds. The state recognizes and supports the Roman Apostolic Catholic religion, though toleration is extended to resident foreigners. The department of Beni, in the north-east, had an estimated population of 32,180 in 1900, including 6,000 wild Indians.

## Political History and Boundaries
Under the presidency of Colonel Pando, who took office on October 26, 1899, Bolivia addressed several boundary disputes. A conflict arose over the Acre rubber-producing territory, where a majority of workers were Brazilians. This led to a declaration of independence by the state of Acre and the dispatch of Bolivian troops in 1900. The dispute was settled amicably via a 1903 treaty, in which Bolivia ceded a portion of the Acre province to Brazil in exchange for a cash indemnity of $10,000,000.
