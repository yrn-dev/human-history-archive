# Golden Brown Morocco, Gold Tooled (Binding by Miss E.M. MacColl)

The binding described as golden brown morocco, gold tooled, is a specific example of bookbinding craftsmanship executed by Miss E.M. MacColl. This particular work was used for a 1772 edition of *Walton's Compleat Angler*.

## The Art Binder and the Modern School
Miss E.M. MacColl belongs to a small class of art binders found in large cities such as New York, Paris, and London. Unlike large trade binders who utilize machinery to produce high quantities of work not intended for longevity, art binders operate on principles of first-class work that have been in continuous use since approximately the 5th century.

The impetus for this school of binding can be traced to William Morris, who created designs for bindings to be executed in blind and gold. The development of the art further progressed under T.J. Cobden-Sanderson, who is considered the founder of the modern school of design for gold-tooling on book-covers. Other notable practitioners of this school include Douglas Cockerell, whose work is characterized by scholarly appreciation of ancient methods and originality, and Mr. Alfred de Sauty, who developed a style combining delicate pointille work with inlaid leathers.

## Contributions of Miss E.M. MacColl
Bookbinding has served as a lucrative and fitting field for a number of women artists in America and England. Among these is Miss Sarah Prideaux, who is noted for her originality and her contributions to literature on the subject. 

Miss E.M. MacColl is specifically recognized for her technical innovation in the field. She may claim to be the inventor of the small curved gold line, which is produced using a tiny wheel. While the ability to produce such a line in blind had been known for a long time, it was rarely utilized. The graceful lines and curves appearing on Miss MacColl's work, including the binding of *Walton's Compleat Angler*, were designed for her by her brother, Mr. D.S. MacColl.

## Context of Gold Tooling and Morocco
The use of morocco leather and gold tooling represents a significant tradition in the history of bookbinding. Gold tooling on leather is an art likely brought to Venice from the East, with the finest early examples appearing in Italian work from the late 15th century. In England, Thomas Berthelet, the Royal Binder to Henry VIII, is believed to be the first to practice this art, working until approximately 1558 with a style strongly influenced by Italian masters.

Throughout history, various binders have utilized morocco for high-quality work:
*   **Jean Grolier:** Used pale brown morocco, gold tooled.
*   **James I:** Had bindings made in dark blue morocco, gold tooled, with red morocco inlays in the coat-of-arms.
*   **Samuel Mearne:** Bound a 1678 *Common Prayer* in smooth red morocco, gold tooled with black fillets.
*   **Nicholas Eve:** Bound *Le Livre des Statuts et Ordonnances de l'Ordre du Benvist Sainct Esprit* (1578) in brown morocco, gold tooled.
*   **Douglas Cockerell:** Bound the *Catalogue of the Pictures at Hagley Hall* in red niger morocco, gold tooled.

Other historical styles include the "Harleian style" used by Eliot and Chapman for the library of Robert Harley, which featured morocco with diamond-shaped centre-pieces and richly gold-tooled borders. Additionally, Thomas Hollis utilized fine red morocco ornamented with stamps engraved by the medallist Thomas Pingo.
