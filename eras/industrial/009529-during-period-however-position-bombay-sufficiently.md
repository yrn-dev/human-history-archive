# Bombay

Bombay is the capital of the Bombay Presidency and the primary seaport of western India, located at 18 deg. 55' N. and 72 deg. 54' E. The city is situated on an island of the same name, which is part of a group now linked to the mainland via causeways. Covering an area of 22 square miles, the town and island had an estimated population of 977,822 in 1906, having reached 776,006 in 1901. While it is the second most populous city in the Indian empire, trailing Calcutta, it competes closely with Calcutta for commercial prosperity and population. Its advantages include its position nearest to Europe, its status as a railway center, and its monopoly of the cotton industry, though it lacks a great waterway like the Brahmaputra or Ganges and serves a region less fertile than the valley of the Ganges.

## Early History and Colonial Acquisition
The name Bombay is derived from Mumba, a form of Parvati, the goddess of the Kolis, who were the earliest known inhabitants (husbandmen and fishermen) and likely occupied the island around the beginning of the Christian era. Originally consisting of seven islands, the area was part of the dominions of the Rashtrakutas, Chalukyas, Mauryas, and Satavahanas. Between 450 and 750, during the Chalukya and Maurya periods, Puri on Elephanta Island served as the principal location in the harbor. The first town on Bombay Island, Mahikavati (Mahim), was founded by King Bhima—likely of the house of the Yadavas of Deogiri—following a 1294 raid into the Deccan by Ala-ud-din Khilji. Mahikavati remained under Hindu rule until 1348, when it was captured by a Mahommedan force from Gujarat. The islands remained part of the kingdom or province of Gujarat until 1534, when Sultan Bahadur ceded them to the Portuguese.

English presence in the presidency began in 1618 with a factory at Surat established by the East India Company under a charter from Emperor Jahangir. After an unsuccessful joint attempt with the Dutch to take Bombay in 1626, the island was ceded to the English crown in 1661 as part of the dower for the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II. Due to poor administration by crown officers and low esteem for the acquisition in England, the crown transferred Bombay to the East India Company in 1668 for an annual payment of £10.

## Administrative and Political Struggles
The Company was granted powers for justice and defense, enrolling a European regiment and erecting fortifications that deterred a Dutch attack in 1673. By 1687, Bombay became the head of all Company possessions in India, though it later became subordinate to Calcutta in 1753.

During the late 17th century, Bombay's position was precarious. Trade was constantly threatened by Malabar pirates, and the settlement was isolated between the Mogul empire and the Mahrattas. Under the leadership of Aungier, the settlement was maintained, though the Siddi admirals of the Moguls used the harbor as winter quarters. Following Aungier's death on June 30, 1677, the situation worsened under his successor, Rolt (1677-1682), as the waters became a battleground for rival navies.

Internal instability also plagued the Company's rule. A mutiny occurred in 1674, and in 1683, Richard Keigwin—a naval officer and former governor of St Helena—led a formidable movement. Elected governor by popular vote, Keigwin declared his government under the authority of the crown, citing "exactions, oppressions and intolerable extortions" by the Company. Keigwin reformed taxation, obtained concessions from the Mahrattas, and increased trade by admitting "interlopers." However, he surrendered the fort to Sir John Child after receiving a royal order.

## Development and Expansion
Development was initially hindered by cholera and plague caused by the silting of creeks. The governor's seat moved from Surat to Bombay after the 1708 amalgamation of the old and new companies. The city wall was finished in 1718, and the population grew from 16,000 in 1718 to 70,000 by 1744, aided by settlers from Gujarat.

Strategic security improved through a 1733 alliance with the Siddis and the Mahratta conquest of Salsette and Bassein (1737-1739), which ended Portuguese hostility. Fortifications were strengthened during the French wars (1744-1748 and 1756-1763), and the acquisition of Bankot in 1755 secured supplies. Naval capabilities grew with the construction of docks in 1750 and 1762. The cotton trade with China began in 1770 following a famine in China. By 1780, the population reached 113,000, further increasing after the famine of 1803 drove people from the Deccan and Konkan to the city.

Following the annexation of the Deccan and victory over the Mahrattas, Bombay entered a period of unrestricted development. Under Mountstuart Elphinstone in 1819, the city's fortunes were fostered, leading to a population of 236,000 by 1838. This grew to 821,000 by 1891, driven by the foundation of cotton factories, the opening of the Suez Canal, and the completion of the first railway line in 1853.

## Industry and Economy
The modern industrial life of Bombay is defined by the growth and manufacture of cotton. The city and island contain nearly two-thirds of the presidency's mills. By 1905, there were 432 factories in the presidency, most focused on cotton. While the industry faced depression between 1891 and 1901 due to famine and plague, it generally expanded in trade and quality. Other industries include silk manufacturing (though declining due to European competition) and gold and silver ornament work.

The city is also known for "Bombay blackwood furniture" made from Shisham (blackwood). While some pieces are considered clumsy due to original Dutch models, others are highly prized for their graceful contours and lace-like carving. "Bombay boxes" are also produced, featuring geometrical inlays of ivory, ebony, sapan wood, sandal wood, wire, and stags' horns.

## Geography and Presidency
The Bombay Presidency spans a long strip of land along the Indian Ocean from north of Mysore to the south of the Punjab. The coast is rock-bound and difficult to access. Only a few harbors—including Bombay, Karwar, Marmagoa, and Karachi-in-Sind—are sufficiently land-locked to protect shipping during the south-west monsoon. The coastline is generally regular, except for the peninsula of Kathiawar located between the Gulfs of Cutch and Cambay.
