# Sequestration in Scottish Bankruptcy Law

Sequestration is a legal process used to constitute "notour" (legally declared) bankruptcy under the Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act of 1856, which serves as the primary regulation for bankruptcy law in Scotland. Along with insolvency—which may concur with the sale of a debtor's effects under a poinding, a sequestration for rent, a duly executed charge for payment, or an application for the benefit of *cessio bonorum*—sequestration is one of the primary methods by which bankruptcy is established.

## Awarding and Jurisdiction
Sequestration may be awarded by either the sheriff or the court of session. The law provides specific conditions under which the estate of a person may be sequestered:

*   **Living Debtors:** For those subject to the jurisdiction of Scottish courts, sequestration may be awarded upon the debtor's own petition (provided there is concurrence from qualified creditors) or upon a petition from qualified creditors. In the latter case, the debtor must be a notour bankrupt and must have maintained a place of business or a dwelling-house in Scotland within the preceding year.
*   **Deceased Debtors:** For those who were subject to the court's jurisdiction at the time of their death, sequestration may be awarded upon the petition of qualified creditors or the petition of the deceased's mandatory.

Additionally, under the Bankruptcy and Cessio Act 1881, the court is granted discretionary power to award sequestration under the bankruptcy act in any case where the debtor's liabilities exceed £200, following a petition for a decree of *cessio* presented by either the debtor or any creditor of a notour bankrupt debtor, regardless of the debt amount.

## Administration of the Estate
Once a sequestration proceeds, the creditors hold a meeting to elect a trustee by a majority in value to administer the estate. To assist and control this administration and to declare dividends, three commissioners—who must be creditors or their mandatories—are also elected.

The bankrupt is required to provide all available information regarding their estate under pain of imprisonment and must undergo a public examination on oath before the sheriff. The law also allows for the examination of "conjunct and confident persons."

To prevent excessive administration costs, the Judicial Factors Act of 1889 requires that if commissioners are to fix a trustee's remuneration, the rate must be intimated to the creditors and the accountant of court. The accountant of court has the power, subject to appeal, to modify this remuneration if deemed expedient.

## Recall and Discharge
A sequestration may be recalled if a majority in number and four-fifths in value of the creditors prefer to wind up the estate through a private arrangement. Furthermore, the Bankruptcy Amendment (Scotland) Act 1860 allows the court to recall a sequestration if winding up the estate in Ireland or England is more convenient.

A bankrupt may be discharged from their status either with or without composition. In cases without composition, discharge can be obtained via:
1. A petition with the concurrence of all creditors.
2. After six months, with the concurrence of a majority and four-fifths in value of the creditors.
3. After eighteen months, with the concurrence of a bare majority in both number and value.
4. After two years, without any concurrence.

In the final instance, a judge may refuse the application if the bankrupt willfully failed to comply with the law or fraudulently concealed effects. Under the Bankruptcy and Cessio Act 1881, discharge also requires the fulfillment of specific conditions, such as the payment of a dividend of five shillings in the pound from the debtor's estate or the provision of satisfactory security for such payment.

## Legislative Amendments and Related Law
The framework of sequestration has been modified by several acts:
*   **Bankruptcy and Real Securities Act 1857:** Addressed the costs of competition for trusteeships.
*   **Debtors (Scotland) Act 1880:** Abolished imprisonment for debt (excluding public rates, assessments, and crown debts) and introduced punishments for fraudulent bankrupts.
*   **Bankruptcy Frauds and Disabilities (Scotland) Act 1884:** Applied criminal provisions regarding the obtaining of credit of £20 or more by an undischarged bankrupt without disclosing their position, aligning Scottish disqualifications with the English Bankruptcy Act 1883.
*   **Judicial Factors Act 1889:** Abolished the office of accountant in bankruptcy, merging its duties into the office of accountant of the court of session.

Notably, the Deeds of Arrangement Act 1887, which applies to Ireland and England, does not apply to Scotland; consequently, Scotland has no analogous requirement for the registration of private deeds of assignment for the benefit of creditors to ensure their validity.
