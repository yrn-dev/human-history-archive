# Buckinghamshire

## Economic History and Industry
Buckinghamshire has historically been characterized as an agricultural county rather than a manufacturing one, gaining a long-standing reputation for its cattle and corn. Local proverbs have referenced "Buckinghamshire bread and beef," and the vale of Aylesbury was noted by Fuller for producing the largest bodied sheep in England.

Despite its agricultural focus, the county developed several profitable industries. Lace-making was introduced by Fleming refugees fleeing the Alva persecution. This trade became highly lucrative, particularly in Olney and Newport Pagnell; the parish of Hanslape reportedly earned an annual profit of £8,000 to £9,000 from the manufacture of lace. However, the industry suffered due to the monopolies of James I, leading to a 1623 petition to the high sheriff of Buckinghamshire regarding the distress caused by the decay of bone lace-making. Additionally, the straw-plait industry was introduced during the reign of George I and provided employment for a significant portion of the population.

## Political Representation
The county's parliamentary representation began in 1290 with two members. As various towns acquired representative rights, this number grew, reaching a total of fourteen members for the county and its boroughs by 1603. This representation was later reduced to eleven members by the Reform Act of 1832. Following the Redistribution of Seats Act of 1885, the boroughs lost their representation, and the county returned three members representing three divisions.

## Historical Events and Conflict
Due to its proximity to London, Buckinghamshire was frequently involved in major national events. During the Norman invasion, the county was likely part of the earldom of Leofwine, son of Godwin; consequently, the sweeping confiscations following the Conquest punished the county for the support it gave Leofwine at the battle of Hastings.

In 1216, during the conflict between King John and his barons, Falkes de Bréauté captured and demolished Hanslape Castle, which had been held against the king by William Mauduit. The county also suffered severe depopulation in certain districts, such as Winslow, during the Black Death.

During the English Civil War, Buckinghamshire was among the first counties to join a mutual defence association supporting the parliament, maintaining important garrisons at Brill and Aylesbury. Conversely, royalist troops briefly garrisoned Newport Pagnell, and King Charles II established his headquarters at Buckingham in 1644.

## Architecture and Antiquities
Buckinghamshire's ecclesiastical buildings are generally not considered to be of the first rank, and monastic remains are limited. Notable sites include:
* **Medmenham:** Fragments of a Cistercian abbey founded in 1201. In the mid-18th century, it became the meeting place for the "Hell-Fire Club" (also called the "Franciscans"), founded by Sir Francis Dashwood (Lord le Despencer). Members included Bubb Dodington and John Wilkes. The club's motto, *fay ce que voudras* ("do what you will"), is inscribed on a doorway.
* **Notley Abbey:** An Augustinian abbey from 1162, now incorporated into a farm-house.

Regarding churches, Wing church contains pre-Norman workmanship, including a crypt and polygonal apse. Stewkley church is noted for the finest Norman work in the county. Early English style is evident in Haddenham and Chetwode churches, the latter being the eastern part of an Augustinian priory church from 1244. Perpendicular architecture is represented by the churches of Hillesden and Maids Moreton, as well as Eton College chapel.

In terms of domestic architecture, Chequers Court is a notable country house dating from the end of the 16th century, situated in the Chiltern Hills between Wendover and Prince's Risborough.

## The Earldom of Buckinghamshire
The titles and estates of the earldom have seen various successions. The 4th earl had no sons, and the title passed to his nephew, George Robert Hobart (1789-1849), the 5th earl. In 1824, the 5th earl inherited the Hampden family estates and adopted the name Hampden. He was succeeded in February 1849 by his brother, Augustus Edward Hobart (1793-1884), the 6th earl, who took the name Hobart-Hampden in 1878. The 6th earl was succeeded by his grandson, Sidney Carr Hobart-Hampden (b. 1860), the 7th earl, who also added the name Mercer-Henderson. Another son of the 6th earl was Augustus Charles Hobart-Hampden, known as Hobart Pasha.

Historically, some confusion exists regarding the 2nd earl of Buckinghamshire, who signed himself "Buckingham" and was known by that name until 1784, when George Grenville, Earl Temple, was created marquess of Buckingham.
