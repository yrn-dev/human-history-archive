# Instrumental

The term "instrumental" encompasses a wide array of devices designed for specific technical, scientific, or musical purposes. These range from precision tools used in navigation and astronomy to a diverse variety of musical apparatuses used for solo, ensemble, and religious performances.

## Scientific and Navigational Instruments

Instruments used for observation and measurement often require precise construction and specific operational procedures to ensure accuracy.

### Binocular Instruments
The development of binocular instruments spans several centuries. In 1608, Johann Lipperhey, the inventor of the Dutch or ordinary telescope, constructed the first binocular telescope by placing two telescopes side by side. In 1645, the Capuchin Antonius Maria Schyrlaeus (Schyrl) de Rheita described double terrestrial telescopes. Further advancements were made by the Capuchin Cherubin d'Orleans, who created large double telescopes of the Dutch type with high magnification for military use, as well as smaller, lower-magnification instruments. These later devices included mechanisms to adjust for the distance between the observer's eyes.

### Navigational and Astronomical Tools
The dip circle is used to determine dip, a process requiring multiple separate readings to eliminate instrumental defects. The "face of the needle" is the side with the engraved number, and the "face of the instrument" is the side of the protecting box next to the vertical circle. To obtain readings, the instrument is adjusted in azimuth so the needle's axle points magnetic east and west, and the needle and instrument faces are observed in four relative positions (east/next to, west/next to, west/away from, and east/away from).

In Peking, Father Matteo observed astronomical instruments that were exactly alike, suggesting they were cast by the same artist during the period of Tartar dominance in China. Other records indicate that instruments represented by Lecomte remained on an observatory terrace, though their positions were altered to accommodate two additions made in the last century.

## Brass Wind Instruments

Brass instruments utilize various mechanisms to alter pitch and expand their musical range.

### Valves, Crooks, and Slides
The French horn utilizes valves, crooks, and slides to provide additional tubing length, which deepens the pitch and allows for transposition into different keys. The slide is the oldest of these devices and likely inspired the crook. While crooks allow a performer to play in many keys on one instrument, valves and slides operate instantaneously, providing a chromatic compass. In a French horn with three valves:
* The first valve lowers the pitch a tone (changing a horn in F to E$\flat$).
* The second valve lowers the pitch a semitone (changing it to E).
* The third valve lowers the pitch a tone and a half.

### Tubas and Bombardons
The bombardon and euphonium resulted from applying valves to the bugle family. The bombardon exists in an upright model for stationary band music and a circular model called the helicon, which is worn around the body with the bell on the left shoulder, similar to the Roman *cornu*. 

A distinction exists between "half" and "whole" instruments (*Halbe* and *Ganze Instrumente*). Older instruments with narrow bores, such as saxhorns, are "half instruments" because their tubes must theoretically be twice as long as an organ pipe of the same note (e.g., a French horn sounding 8 ft. C requires a 16 ft. tube). Conversely, "whole instruments" like tubas have a wide conical bore that allows for the production of fundamental sounds. Consequently, a euphonium sounding 8 ft. C only requires an 8 ft. tube.

## String and Percussion Instruments

Musical instruments vary significantly by construction, purpose, and social application.

### Ancient and Classical Instruments
In ancient contexts, David composed hymns to God and created instruments for the Levites to use on festivals and the sabbath. These included the viol (a ten-stringed instrument played with a bow), the psaltery (featuring twelve musical notes played with fingers), and broad, large brass cymbals.

In Greek tradition, a distinction is made between the lyre and the cithara. The lyre, often featuring a tortoise-shell body, was used by amateurs and for the musical training of young men and maidens; it is frequently depicted in scenes of domestic life and banquets. The cithara was the instrument of professional performers (*citharista* or *citharoedus*) at festivals and the Pythian Games, used for purely instrumental music and accompanied by rich, distinct costumes.

### Other Stringed Instruments
The banjo is a stringed instrument plucked by a plectrum or fingers. Popular among American negroes who introduced it to Europe, its name may be a corruption of "pandura" or "bandore," or derived from "bania," a primitive Senegambian instrument.

## Instrumental Compositions

In music, a duet is a composition for two performers, who may be either vocal or instrumental. For a work to be classified as a duet, both parts must be of equal importance; if one part is subordinated, it becomes an accompaniment. Instrumental duets may feature two different instruments (such as Mozart's violin and piano duets) or two similar instruments. Duets for the pianoforte may involve two separate instruments or two performers on one instrument, the latter known as *duets a quatre mains*.
