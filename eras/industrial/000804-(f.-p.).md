# F.P.

In the context of materials science and chemistry, F.P. refers to the freezing point of a substance or alloy. The determination of F.P. curves is a critical, though often laborious and complicated, process used to extract valuable information regarding the composition and behavior of alloys.

## Freezing Point Curves in Binary Alloys
To obtain a complete F.P. curve for a binary alloy, researchers must operate with several different alloys of suitably chosen compositions and observe the freezing points of each individually. Because there is a risk that oxidation or other factors may alter the composition of the alloy during the process, each alloy should be analyzed after the procedure.

The most effective method for determining freezing points is the observation of the gradual cooling of a considerable mass of the material, ensuring it is well stirred as long as it remains in a liquid state. These cooling curves can be recorded using two primary methods:
* **Automatic Recording:** Utilizing a thermocouple and galvanometer, a method attributed to Sir W. Roberts-Austen, which allows for photographic recording.
* **Manual Recording:** Using pen and ink with a platinum thermometer, a method implemented by F.H. Neville and C.T. Heycock.

Automatic recording is considered highly advantageous because the primary arrest in temperature is often very slight and difficult to detect through other means.

## Thermal Behavior During Freezing
The behavior of a cooling substance varies based on its purity and composition:
* **Pure Metals:** When a pure metal (referred to as metal A) reaches its F.P., the temperature becomes stationary and remains constant for a significant period. In some instances, the temperature may fall slightly below the F.P. due to super-fusion, but it will rise back to the F.P. and remain constant once freezing begins.
* **Alloys:** In an alloy where metal A is mixed with another metal (metal B), freezing begins at a lower temperature with the separation of pure A. Unlike pure metals, the temperature does not remain constant during this phase; instead, it falls more rapidly as the proportion of metal B in the liquid increases.
* **Eutectic Point:** Once the eutectic temperature is reached, a second F.P. or "arrest" occurs, at which point all remaining liquid solidifies. As the percentage of metal B increases (for example, from 10% to 20%), the first F.P. is lowered further and the temperature falls more quickly, though the eutectic F.P. remains at the same temperature, albeit for a longer duration.
* **Eutectic Composition:** For an alloy that possesses the exact composition of the eutectic itself, no temperature arrest occurs until the eutectic temperature is reached, at which point the entire mass solidifies without a change in temperature.

## Molecular Depression of the F.P.
The depression of the freezing point can be analyzed through the relationship between the weight of water (W) and the weight of salt (w) in a given volume of solution. For instance, if M grammes of salt are dissolved in 100 grammes of water, w equals M and W equals 100.
