# Mining and Mineral Extraction

Mining has historically served as a primary industry for various regions, encompassing the extraction of precious metals, industrial minerals, and fuel sources. The practice ranges from ancient slave-driven operations to modern industrial complexes utilizing advanced mechanical engineering.

## Historical Extraction and Labor
In antiquity, the extraction of gold and silver often required immense labor due to metal veins rarely appearing on the surface. Miners were forced to trace these veins through "frightful depths," where they frequently encountered floods of water. To combat this, the Romans utilized pumps invented by Archimedes in Egypt to drain the pits. These operations were characterized by extreme brutality; numberless multitudes of slaves perished under the direction of masters who forced them to labor day and night through the use of heavy stripes. According to Polybius, as quoted by Strabo, over forty thousand men were employed in the mines near Nova Carthago, providing the Romans with twenty-five thousand drachmas daily.

## Industrialization and Technological Advancement
The development of the steam engine in the eighteenth century significantly altered mining operations. The earliest steam engines were pumping engines designed specifically to remove water from newly opened coal mines. These mines provided the coke necessary for iron smelting, replacing the previously used wood-charcoal. James Watt later improved these pumping engines, making them viable for driving machinery.

Further mechanical advancements include the use of revolving furnaces for processing ores. These furnaces, which may feature a rotating circular disk or a horizontal hollow cylinder, are used as calciners in tin mines and for the chlorination of silver ores. In more modern contexts, such as in British Columbia, the industry employs the most modern types of mining machinery.

## Regional Mining Operations
Mining outputs and focuses vary by geography:

*   **British Columbia:** Mining is the principal industry, with the country rich in coal, lead, copper, silver, and gold. Between 1894 and 1904, output rose from $4,225,717 to $18,977,359, reaching $22,460,295 by 1905. While placer discoveries on the Fraser river and in the Cariboo district were dominant between 1858 and 1882, lode mining has provided the greater part of returns since 1899. Key vein-mining centers include the Kootenay, Boundary, and Rossland districts.
*   **Bolivia:** In the department of Potosi, the Huanchaca mining company operates a great silver mining center at Pulacayo. This center has a population of 6,512, with 3,200 men employed in surface works and mines. Tupiza also serves as a commercial and mining center near the Argentine frontier.
*   **Durham:** The region's Coal Measures have been a source of enormous wealth, with seams extending horizontally for many miles at depths of 20 to 100 fathoms. Other extracted materials include ironstone, fine millstones from Wolsingham, Newcastle grindstones from Gateshead Fell, and Frosterley marble used for decorative purposes in Durham cathedral.
*   **South Africa:** Diamond mining employs Kaffir workers who are housed in large compounds enclosed with wire netting to prevent the illicit disposal of diamonds. These workers earn between 17s. and 24s. a week. White workmen often reside in "model villages" such as Kenilworth, established by the De Beers company.

## Labor Regulations and Social Conflict
The conditions of mine workers have historically been a point of severe social tension. In the oil-mines of Galicia and factories of Moravia, conditions were described as peculiarly unfortunate, characterized by long hours, health hazards, and a lack of accident precautions. This led to legislative interference in 1883, introducing factory inspections. Subsequent laws in 1884 and 1885 established a maximum working day of ten hours in mines and forbade Sunday labor. By 1887-1888, compulsory insurance against sickness and accidents was introduced.

These stresses between "selfish employers and reluctant workers" contributed to the global spread of Marxism. Karl Marx theorized that a necessary conflict of interests exists between the employing classes and the employed mass, prophesying that a class-conscious majority of workers would eventually seize power.

## Other Applications of "Mines"
The term "mine" also refers to explosive devices. In naval contexts, self-igniting mines have been used to create massive water columns—some estimated at thirty metres in height—upon explosion.
