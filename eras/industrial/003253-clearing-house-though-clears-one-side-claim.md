# Clearing House

A clearing house is a financial institution or association used by banks to settle accounts and clear cheques. This system has been developed extensively in Great Britain and the United States to facilitate the movement of funds and the settlement of debts between banking houses.

## The London Clearing House
Purely city banks had associated themselves into a Clearing House by 1776, although records from the books of the Grasshopper indicate a potentially less organized system of settlement as early as 1773, noting a quarterly charge for the use of a "Clearing-room." By 1810, a dedicated house was acquired for this purpose, and forty-six banking houses settled their accounts there that year.

The membership of the London Clearing House has evolved over time. In 1844, twenty-six of the forty-nine London banks were connected to the house, though at that time membership was restricted to private banks. Joint-stock banks gained entry in 1854. By 1906, the membership consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank. Notably, banks located at Charing Cross or in Fleet Street did not belong to the association.

The Bank of England has never been a member of the Clearing House. However, it "clears on one side," meaning that while its claims on clearing bankers are processed through the Clearing House, the claims that clearing bankers hold against the Bank of England are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times a day.

## Expansion and Volume of Clearings
The scope of clearing expanded in 1858 when arrangements made by Sir John Lubbock (Lord Avebury) added the clearing of country cheques. This "country clearing" provided significant assistance to business by allowing a cheque from a distant English village to be handled as conveniently as one from London.

The volume of turnover in cheques is vast. While the London Clearing House provides specific data, the total yearly turnover in cheques—including exchanges in large towns across Scotland, Ireland, and England, as well as settlements between bank branches and customers—is estimated to be at least twice the amount registered by the London Clearing House. Historical figures for London clearings show a significant increase over time:
*   1839: £954,401,600 (29 banks)
*   1840: £978,496,800 (29 banks)
*   1899: £9,150,269,000 (19 banks)
*   1900: £8,960,170,000 (19 banks)
*   1906: £12,711,334,000 (18 banks)

## Clearing Houses in the United States
The United States has developed a system of clearing cheques that is among the most advanced in the world, perhaps surpassed only by Great Britain. Clearing houses operate in approximately 112 leading cities. For the year ending September 30, 1907, the aggregate clearings reached $154,662,515,258.

The New York Clearing-House handles a substantial portion of this activity; in 1906, its clearings accounted for 67.2% of the total clearings across 55 of the larger cities. The volume of these clearings is subject to great fluctuation based on the prosperity of the country's business and the volume of transactions on the stock exchange.

## Influence and Regulation in New York
The Clearing-House Committee of the New York Clearing-House possesses significant influence over the banking environment. It can refuse emergency aid to banks that are conducted unwisely. This authority was utilized during the panic of 1907 to remove several speculative financial interests from the control of national banks.

During this period, membership in the New York Clearing-House was limited to state banks, national banks, and the sub-Treasury. The money-market and exchanges relied on the weekly reports of condition issued by these members every Saturday. However, because trust companies did not provide regular weekly reports, this index was sometimes misleading or incomplete. To rectify this, the state superintendent of banking announced in early 1908 that he would use legal powers to require trust companies to submit weekly reports, thereby creating a more complete mirror of New York banking conditions.
