# Brick Clays and Brickmaking

The production of bricks relies on various types of clay, which are categorized by their geological composition and distribution. The process involves the selection of raw materials, tempering, shaping, and firing to achieve specific physical properties and colors.

## Classification of Brick Clays

Brick clays are primarily divided into two classes based on their composition and geological range.

### Class (1): Ordinary Brick Clays
Ordinary brick clays are widely distributed and possess a more extensive geological range than marls. Their composition varies considerably; many are unsuitable for brickmaking in their natural state and require the addition of sand or other clays. The strongest clays—those with the highest tensile strength and plasticity—typically contain the highest percentage of hydrated aluminium silicates. 

All such clays contain some amount of free silica (sand) and usually a small percentage of undecomposed felspar. Oxide of iron is a critical ingredient, ranging from 2% to 10%, as it determines the hardness, durability, and color of the burnt brick. Clays with 5% to 8% oxide of iron generally produce red bricks. However, if the clay contains 3% to 4% alkalis or is fired too hard, the color becomes darker or more purple. Conversely, an excess of alumina tends to make the color brighter and lighter. The clay substance itself can vary from 15% to 80%.

### Class (2): Lime Clays (Marls)
Lime clays, or marls, contain a high percentage of chalk or limestone (usually 15% to 30% calcium carbonate) and are found only in connection with limestone or chalk formations. Because natural deposits of these clays have been largely exhausted in England, artificial "malm" is sometimes created by mixing ordinary brick-clay with ground chalk. These clays typically burn to a distinctive yellow color, though a limestone content exceeding 40% can alter this.

## Processing and Manufacturing Methods

### Preparation of Clay
The preparation of clay depends on the material's consistency. For marls, the clay is often mixed with water in a wash-pan to create a creamy fluid, which is then passed through sieves onto a drying-bed. To prevent the bricks from shattering during weather exposure, limestone pebbles must be removed; this is achieved by squeezing the plastic clay through iron gratings or using a grinding-mill with heavy iron rollers to crush the pebbles.

For machine-made plastic bricks, grinding-mills and pug-mills are used. Grinding-mills crush hard clays or shales using rollers. The pug-mill uses a central shaft with knives and cutters to knead the clay and force it through a delivery nose. This produces a continuous mass of clay that is cut into bricks using a wire cutting frame, resulting in "wire-cuts."

Semi-plastic or "dust-made" bricks are produced from ground shale moistened with water. The shale is crushed in an iron grinding-pan, sifted to remove coarse particles, and then stamped into moulds using heavy-duty presses.

### Hand-Moulding
Hand-made bricks are created by pressing tempered clay into wooden or metal moulds. To prevent sticking, the moulder dips the case in water or a mixture of water and sand. The clay is dashed into the mould, pressed into the corners, and leveled with a wooden strip called a "strike." A "stock-board" with an elevated tongue may be used to create a hollow "frog" in the bottom of the brick.

## Firing and Specialized Bricks

Firing is the most critical stage of production, as heat causes chemical re-combinations that alter the clay's physical character. For ordinary bricks, an oxidizing atmosphere is used with finishing temperatures between 900°C and 1250°C (typically around 1050°C). Lime-clays require higher temperatures, usually 1150°C to 1200°C, to ensure the lime chemically combines with other substances.

Various specialized bricks are produced for specific uses:
*   **Blue Bricks:** Made primarily in North Wales and South Staffordshire, these are vitrified, heavy, and impervious, making them ideal for engineering work and areas requiring high compressional resistance.
*   **Fire-bricks:** Designed to withstand great heat for use in furnaces; they are laid with fire-clay mortar.
*   **Glazed Bricks:** These include salt-glazed bricks (brown) and enamelled bricks, the latter of which are dipped in colored slip for sanitary or decorative purposes.
*   **Moulded Bricks:** Used for plinths, cornices, and copings, as well as voussoirs for arches and sewer work.

## Historical Development

The art of brickmaking was practiced by all civilized nations of antiquity. The earliest known burnt bricks were found in Babylonia, where the method of burning dried clay blocks was likely discovered. Evidence shows the Babylonians produced well-burnt bricks more than 6,000 years ago, and they were used extensively during the time of Sargon of Akkad (c. 3800 B.C.). Babylonians and Assyrians also developed proficiency in colored glazes and enamels for wall decoration.

It is believed that this knowledge traveled eastwards from Babylonia through Persia and northern India to China. While the Great Wall of China (c. 210 B.C.) utilized both burnt and unburnt bricks, there is no evidence that the Chinese possessed knowledge of burnt bricks while the art was flourishing in Babylonia.
