# Joint-Stock Banks in the United Kingdom

Joint-stock banks are financial institutions established as companies or partnerships. Their development in the United Kingdom was shaped by a series of legislative acts that transitioned the banking system from a landscape dominated by the Bank of England and small private firms to one featuring large, powerful joint-stock organizations.

## Legal Evolution and Establishment
The Bank of England, incorporated by the act of 1694, originally held a monopoly as the only joint-stock bank permitted to exist in England and Wales. This position was reinforced by the act of 1697, which prohibited the establishment of any joint-stock bank within England during the continuance of the Bank of England, and the act of 1708, which limited companies or partnerships to six persons if they were to borrow or take up money on bills or notes payable on demand or within six months. These restrictions were confirmed by the act of 1800.

Significant changes began with the acts of 1826, which allowed the establishment of joint-stock co-partnerships of more than six persons provided they were located more than 65 miles from London. The act of 1833 further expanded this by permitting joint-stock banks within the 65-mile radius, although these banks were not granted the power to issue notes.

## Liability and Corporate Structure
Until 1862, the privilege of limited liability was exclusive to the Bank of England. Shareholders in other joint-stock banks were liable for the full extent of their means. This lack of limited liability discouraged many wealthy individuals from becoming shareholders. The risks of unlimited liability were highlighted by the failures of the Western Bank of Scotland in 1858 and the City of Glasgow Bank in 1878. The latter event led to the Companies Act of 1879, which allowed unlimited companies to adopt limited liability. Under this act, shareholders who paid the nominal amount of their holding were no longer liable for further amounts, unless the company issued bank notes, in which case the unlimited liability remained.

## Note Issuance and the Bank Act of 1844
Prior to the Bank Act of 1844, many provincial banks in England and Wales possessed the privilege of issuing notes. Following the announcement of the act, one joint-stock bank and eight private banks ceased issuing notes before October 12, 1844; the Western District Joint-Stock Banking Co. was dissolved during this process.

Under the act of 1844, the maximum circulation for English issuing banks was fixed based on the average circulation of the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. At that time, 72 joint-stock banks in England were allowed a permitted issue of £3,478,230, with an actual circulation of £3,331,516. In Scotland, chartered, private, and joint-stock banks had a circulation of £2,987,665.

The act of 1844 also influenced the long-term constitution of the system. While it initially favored small banks, it eventually necessitated the formation of larger, more powerful banks. The act stipulated that when two joint-stock banks amalgamated, only the continuing bank retained its issue, and if a private issuing bank joined a joint-stock bank, the issue lapsed.

## Growth and Distribution
The number of joint-stock banks grew steadily in the early 19th century. Records show that between 1826 and 1842, registered joint-stock banks increased from 6 in 1827 to 35 in 1833, and 118 by 1842. By 1844, there were 640 joint-stock offices in England and Wales. By 1906, the banking business in England was primarily conducted by approximately sixty joint-stock banks and ten private banks. Although the number of individual banks decreased, the number of offices increased significantly; in England and Wales, offices grew from 976 in 1844 to over 5,880 in 1906.

In Ireland, by May 1907, there were 17 joint-stock banks with an authorized issue of £1,084,836 and an actual issue of £437,693. The actual circulation remained low because these banks' circulation was limited to very small areas, and their notes were often difficult to cash outside the immediate neighborhood of issuance.

## The Clearing House and Legal Precedents
Joint-stock banks entered the London Clearing House in 1854, following a period where only private banks were permitted membership. By 1906, the Clearing House consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank.

The operations of these banks have also been defined by legal rulings. In *London Joint Stock Bank v. Simmons* (1892), the House of Lords determined that negotiable securities could be taken in pledge for advances if taken honestly, even if the tenderer was acting as an agent. Similarly, in *Lloyd's Bank v. Cooke* (1907), the principle of estoppel was vindicated regarding bills, notes, and cheques.
