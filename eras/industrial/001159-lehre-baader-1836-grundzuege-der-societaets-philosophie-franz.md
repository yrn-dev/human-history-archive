# Franz Xaver von Baader

Franz Xaver von Baader (1765–1841) was a German theologian and philosopher born in Munich on March 27, 1765. He was the third son of F. P. Baader, who served as the court physician to the elector of Bavaria. His brothers were also distinguished figures: the eldest, Clemens, was an author, and the second, Joseph (1763–1835), was an engineer.

## Early Life and Education
Baader's early academic pursuits were in medicine, which he studied in Vienna and Ingolstadt. Although he briefly assisted his father in medical practice, he found this career uncongenial. Consequently, he transitioned to the study of mining engineering under Abraham Gottlob Werner at Freiberg. 

Between 1792 and 1796, Baader resided in England, where he traveled through various mining districts in north Germany. During this period, he encountered the ideas of Godwin, Hartley, and Hume, all of which he found extremely distasteful. Instead, he found harmony in his thinking through the mystical speculations of Saint Martin, Meister Eckhart, and particularly Jakob Boehme.

## Intellectual Development and Influences
Upon returning from England in 1796, Baader settled in Hamburg and formed a long-term friendship with F. H. Jacobi. During this time, he became acquainted with the works of Schelling. While the publications Baader produced during this era show a manifest influence from Schelling, the archive notes that Baader was not a disciple of Schelling and likely contributed more to the philosopher than he received. This friendship ended around 1822 after Baader wrote a letter to Emperor Alexander I of Russia denouncing modern philosophy, an act that entirely alienated Schelling.

## Professional Career and Academic Work
Baader maintained his professional practice as an engineer throughout much of his life. He achieved notable success in this field, earning a prize of 12,000 gulden (approximately L1000) for developing a new method of using Glauber's salts in glassmaking as a substitute for potash. His services led to his appointment as superintendent of mines from 1817 to 1820, and he was raised to the rank of nobility for his contributions.

After retiring in 1820, Baader turned his focus toward philosophical publication. Between 1822 and 1825, he published *Fermenta Cognitionis* in six parts, a work in which he advocated for the study of Boehme and combated modern philosophy. In 1826, he was appointed as a professor of speculative theology and philosophy at the newly opened university in Munich. He later published some of these lectures as *Spekulative Dogmatik* (1827–1836).

## Later Years and Conflict
Baader was a member of the Roman Catholic Church. In 1838, he opposed the church's interference in civil matters. As a result of this opposition, he was interdicted from lecturing on the philosophy of religion during the final three years of his life. He died on May 23, 1841.

## Legacy and Bibliography
Baader's system and works were preserved and published by several adherents, including Schlueter, von Osten-Sacken, Lutterbeck, E. v. Schaden, J. Hamberger, and F. Hoffman. Their collective effort resulted in *Baader's saemmtliche Werke* (16 volumes, 1851–1860), which includes a full biography in volume xv and a sketch of his system by Lutterbeck in volume xvi.

Other significant works and surveys related to his philosophy include:
* **By F. Hoffmann:** *Vorhalle zur spekulativen Lehre Baader's* (1836) and *Grundzuege der Societaets-Philosophie Franz Baader's* (1837).
* **By J. Hamberger:** *Cardinalpunkte der Baaderschen Philosophie* (1855) and *Fundamentalbegriffe von F. B.'s Ethik, Politik, u. Religions-Philosophie* (1858).
* **By J. A. B. Lutterbeck:** *Philosophische Standpunkte Baaders* (1854) and *Baaders Lehre vom Weltgebaeude* (1866).
* **Surveys:** Works by Erdmann, Otto Pfleiderer (*Philosophy of Religion*), R. Falckenberg, Kuno Fischer, and J. Claassen, the latter of whom wrote *Franz von Baaders Leben und theosophische Werke* (1886–1887) and *Franz von Baaders Gedanken ueber Staat und Gesellschaft* (1890). Reichel also published *Die Sozietaetsphilosophie Franz v. Baaders* in 1901.

Baader's influence extended to other thinkers; for example, the philosopher Karl Christian Friedrich Krause only obtained a position in Munich through the influence of Franz von Baader.
