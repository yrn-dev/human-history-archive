# Rhyming Elegy on Brooke

## Overview and Publication
A rhyming elegy written on Brooke was published within a collection titled *Huth's Inedited Poetical Miscellanies*. This specific work is noted for bringing charges of extreme penuriousness against Brooke. However, these claims are contrasted by abundant testimony regarding his generous treatment of writers who were his contemporaries.

## Literary Works of Brooke
During his lifetime, Brooke published only four poems and the *Tragedy of Mustapha*. One of these poems was an elegy on Sidney, which appeared in *The Phoenix Nest* in 1593. Following his death, several volumes of his work were released, including a volume of his works in 1633, a biography of Sidney in 1652, and a volume of *Remains* in 1670.

Brooke authored two tragedies based on the Senecan model: *Alaham* and *Mustapha*. He stated himself that these tragedies were not intended for the stage. The play *Alaham* is set in Ormuz; its prologue features the ghost of a former king of Ormuz who reveals a curse destined for a doomed family, a gloom that is maintained throughout the development of the piece. The theme of *Mustapha* was borrowed from Madeleine de Scudéry's *Ibrahim ou l'illustre Bassa* and focuses on the ambition of the sultana Rossa.

## Style and Critical Reception
The tragedies written by Brooke feature choruses that function as philosophical dissertations, though their connection to the rest of the drama is often slight. In *Mustapha*, the third chorus is a dialogue between Eternity and Time, while the fifth chorus contains an invective against the evils of superstition, followed by a chorus of priests that fails to dispel the initial impression of scepticism.

Charles Lamb characterized Brooke's writing as being "nine parts Machiavel and Tacitus, for one of Sophocles and Seneca," suggesting that his work—including his most passionate love-poems—is "frozen and made rigid with intellect." Lamb also noted a consistent obscurity of expression in Brooke's poetry, attributing this not to a lack of verbal lucidity, but rather to the subtlety and intensity of the thought involved. Furthermore, Lamb suggested that Brooke's tragedies should be classified as political treatises rather than plays.
