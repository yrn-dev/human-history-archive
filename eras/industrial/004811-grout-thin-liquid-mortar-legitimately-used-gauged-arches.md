# Grout and Gauged Arches

## Definition and General Use of Grout
Grout is defined as a thin liquid mortar. Its legitimate application is found in gauged arches and other masonry work where the objective is to achieve fine joints. In ordinary brickwork, grout is occasionally employed every four or five courses to fill spaces that may have been inadvertently left between bricks. However, this practice is considered a sign of inefficient workmanship and is not countenanced in high-quality work, as such spaces should be filled or flushed up with mortar during the initial laying of the bricks. Furthermore, grout is prone to oozing out and staining the face of the brickwork.

## Gauged Arches
Arches in brickwork are categorized into three types: plain, rough-cut, and gauged. Gauged arches are constructed using specially made soft bricks. These bricks are cut and rubbed using templates or gauges to create perfectly fitting voussoirs. The process of gauging involves bringing every brick to an exact form through cutting and rubbing.

Gauged brickwork is set in lime putty rather than common mortar, and the finished joints are required to be no more than 1/32 in. wide. To ensure stability, the sides of the voussoirs are gauged out hollow and then grouted in Portland cement. This technique connects each brick to the next via a joggle joint.

## Construction and Technical Challenges
Gauged arches typically possess a soffit thickness of only a half-brick and are not tied by a bond to the structure behind them; instead, they are supported by a lintel with a rough discharging arch over it, which supports the remaining wall width. Due to this structure, they must be executed with great nicety and care.

A frequent error made by workmen is rubbing the bricks thinner at the back than at the front to reduce the effort needed to achieve a fine face joint. This practice can cause the work to bulge outwards. The highest quality work maintains an identical thickness at the back and front of the brick. This same bulging issue occurs when a gauged arch is inserted into an existing old wall because of the difficulty involved in filling the space behind the bricks with cement.

## Comparison with Other Arch Types
In contrast to gauged arches, rough-cut arches utilize bricks that are roughly cut into a wedge form using an axe. These are used for openings like windows and doors where a strong arch with a neat appearance is desired, with joints usually equal in width to ordinary brickwork. Plain arches are built from uncut bricks; because ordinary bricks are not wedge-formed, the necessary difference in the arch's periphery is made up in mortar, resulting in wedge-shaped joints. To maximize strength in plain arches, it is advisable to build them in independent rings of half-brick thickness, with the undermost rings having thin joints and succeeding rings being slightly thickened to prevent fissures.

## Centering and Support
The construction of arches requires centering. For gauged arches, the laggings (small strips of wood nailed to the ribs to form the building surface) are nailed close together and the joints are planed off. This differs from ordinary arches, where laggings are spaced 1 in. apart. 

For arches with a soffit of 4-1/2 in., a "turning piece" is used for turning the arch over. This piece is generally cut from 3 or 4 in. stuff with a circular top edge. For spans up to 15 ft., thin ribs (made of two thicknesses of 1 in. stuff nailed together to lap the joints) are used; for spans exceeding 15 ft., ribs of 4 in. stuff or larger are required.
