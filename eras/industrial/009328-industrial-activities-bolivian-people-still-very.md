# Industrial Activities of Bolivia

The industrial activities of the Bolivian people are characterized as being of a very primitive nature. While the government attempted to stimulate the development of manufacturing industries—specifically sugar production—through an act passed in 1894 that offered premiums and advantageous concessions, the results were disappointing due to unfavorable conditions.

## Textile and Handicraft Industries
Spinning and weaving are common household occupations. Native populations often use rude spindles while traveling with pack animals over mountain roads, later weaving the yarn into cloth at home using rough Spanish looms of colonial patterns. These activities produce a variety of fabrics:

*   **Llama Wool:** Used to create coarse cloth for native garments.
*   **Alpaca and Vicuna Wool:** Used for finer fabrics, sometimes mixed with lamb's wool or silk. The vicuna is specifically used for beautiful and costly *ponchos* (blanket cloaks) and other apparel.
*   **Cotton and Wool:** Serviceable goods are manufactured from these materials. In the Chiquitos and Mojos districts, an industry producing excellent cotton cloth persists, having been taught to the natives by Jesuit missionaries.
*   **Specialized Fabrics:** Indians in the Andean departments produce baize, cashmere, and waterproof *ponchos* made of silk and fine wool.

Indian women are noted as expert weavers whose handiwork often commands high prices, and they are particularly skilled in using dyes to create brilliantly-colored, finely-woven petticoats.

## Forest and Natural Resource Industries
The exploitation of forest products provides Bolivia with its greatest immediate profits.

*   **Rubber:** This became a prominent industry between 1880 and 1890. Annual output reached nearly 3,500 metric tons before Bolivia's best rubber forests were transferred to Brazil in 1903. Rubber-producing *Hevea* is still found in extensive forest areas on the Madre de Dios and the Beni.
*   **Cinchona Bark:** One of the oldest forest industries, it is valued for yielding a high percentage of quinine sulphate. However, the industry uses destructive methods, leading to a steady decrease in the area of cinchona forests.

## Animal Husbandry and Stock-Raising
Stock-raising was one of the country's earliest industries following mining. 

*   **Cattle:** Introduced to the Mojos and Chiquitos llanos by Jesuit missionaries, cattle provide a steady supply of beef for inland markets. Large herds are also sustained in the Chaco and the llanos of Tarija and Chuquisaca, with smaller districts in Cochabamba, Potosi, and La Paz. *Saladeros* are used to salt and sun-dry beef for home markets, while hides are exported.
*   **Camelidae:** The rearing of llamas and alpacas in the highlands is an industry managed entirely by Indians. The llama serves as a pack animal, while alpaca wool is a staple export, though the animal is difficult to rear. The guanaco is hunted for its skin to make robes and rugs.
*   **Other Livestock:** Goats are raised in temperate and warm regions, and sheep are kept for wool in temperate areas. The government also encourages the production of the native fur-bearing chinchilla and has prohibited the exportation of live animals.

## Local Manufacturing and Infrastructure
Native industry supplies home markets with a variety of essential goods, including:
*   Pottery, soap, and candles.
*   Hats, gloves, and starch.
*   Cheese, cigars, and cigarettes.
*   Tanning and saddlery (performed with primitive methods).
*   Lap robes and rugs made from vicuna and alpaca skins.

Sugar continues to be produced using old methods, and certain districts maintain small productions of silk and wine. Regarding energy and infrastructure, electric power plants and electric lighting have been established at La Paz, utilizing the country's abundant water power. Additionally, efforts were made under President Pando to improve communications to revive mining and other industries.
