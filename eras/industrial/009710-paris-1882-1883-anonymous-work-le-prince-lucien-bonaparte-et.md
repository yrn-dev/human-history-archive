# Lucien Bonaparte

Lucien Bonaparte was a member of the Bonaparte family whose life was marked by political volatility, a strained relationship with his brother Napoleon, and a later devotion to literature and family. He died in Rome on June 29, 1840, leaving behind a family consisting of six daughters and four sons.

## Political Career and Conflict with Napoleon
During the period of the consulate (1799–1804), the relationship between Lucien and Napoleon became strained. Lucien had expressed suspicions that Napoleon possessed an ambition that surpassed his love for the general weal. Although Lucien served as the minister of the interior, he was soon removed from the position due to personal and political differences with the First Consul.

In November 1800, Napoleon appointed Lucien as ambassador to the court of Madrid. However, Lucien again displeased his brother during the planned partition of Portugal. On June 6, 1801, at Badajoz, Lucien and the Spanish minister Godoy signed preliminaries of peace after the court of Lisbon successfully used bribes. This action frustrated Napoleon's plans to seize Lisbon, leading Lucien to resign and return to Paris, where he joined the Tribunate in opposing some of Napoleon's schemes.

The final breach between the brothers occurred following the death of Lucien's first wife in 1800. In the early summer of 1802, Lucien became enamored with Mme Jouberthou. Despite an express prohibition from the First Consul—who wanted Lucien to marry the widow of the king of Etruria for reasons of state—Lucien secretly married Mme Jouberthou on October 23, 1803, at Plessis. Upon discovering this, Napoleon ordered Lucien to leave French territory. Lucien departed for Italy with his wife and infant son, publicly bestowing the name of Bonaparte upon his wife.

## Exile and Return
Lucien lived in Italy for several years, primarily in Rome, where he displayed marked hostility toward the emperor. In December 1807, Napoleon attempted to reach an arrangement where Lucien would be recognized as a French prince on the condition that he annul his marriage. Lucien refused. After spending time at his estate of Canino, from which he took the papal title of prince of Canino, he departed for America.

During this journey, a British ship captured him. He was taken first to Malta and then to England, where he remained under some measure of surveillance until the peace of 1814. Following this, he returned to Rome. During the Hundred Days of 1815, he offered his help to Napoleon, stood by him at the "Champ de Mai" in Paris, and was the final person to defend Napoleon's prerogatives during his second abdication. He spent the remainder of his life in Italy.

## Literary Works and Legacy
Lucien Bonaparte authored several works, including a two-volume epic titled *Charlemagne, ou l'Eglise delivree*, published in 1814. He also wrote *La Verite sur les Cent Jours* and a set of *Memoirs*, though the latter were not completed.

His descendants continued to be prominent in various fields. His son, Charles Lucien Jules Laurent (1803–1857), the prince of Canino, became a renowned scientist specializing in zoology and ornithology, publishing *American Ornithology* and several catalogs of European birds, fish, and mammals. Another son, Louis Lucien (1813–1891), served as a deputy for Corsica and the Seine and later became a philologist who published works on the Basque language.
