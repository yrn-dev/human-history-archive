# Brewing Processes and Wort Treatment

Brewing is a complex industrial process consisting of seven primary stages: grinding, mashing, boiling, cooling, fermenting, cleansing, and racking and storing. The process begins with malt, which is cleaned, graded, and crushed in a malt mill to create "grist." This grist is mixed with hot water from a hot liquor vessel in a mashing machine before passing into a mash-tun, where the starch of the malt is rendered soluble.

## The Boiling Process
From the mash-tun, the clear wort is transferred to the copper. In breweries using the gravitation system, the coppers are situated beneath the mash-tuns; otherwise, an underback collecting vessel is used to pump the wort into the copper. The copper is a large vessel heated by steam or direct fire. While pale-ale brewers often prefer open coppers, modern closed coppers with dome-shaped heads are common and may boil the wort under slight pressure.

The wort is boiled for generally one to three hours, depending on the beer type. This stage serves four main purposes:
1. Sterilization of the wort.
2. Extraction of flavor and aroma substances from hops.
3. Concentration of the wort.
4. Coagulation and precipitation of coagulable albuminoids (nitrogenous matter) to prevent cloudiness and fret in the finished beer.

Hops are added during this stage, typically ranging from 3 to 15 lb per quarter (336 lb) of malt. Mild ales and porters use 3 to 4 lb, light stouts and pale ales use 6 to 10 lb, and strong stouts and ales use 9 to 15 lb. Because hop oil is volatile, brewers often reserve about one-third of the hops until near the end of the copper stage. Boiling extracts three distinct substances: hop resin (a bitter and preservative), hop tannin (which precipitates proteids from the malt), and hop oil (providing aroma).

## Cooling and Aeration
After boiling, the wort is turned into a hop back—a wooden or metal vessel with a perforated false bottom—to separate insoluble hop constituents. The bright wort is then moved to coolers. A cooler is a shallow vessel with a large area that exposes hot wort to a large volume of air, causing certain substances and hop constituents to precipitate. 

Due to the risk of wild yeast and bacterial infection associated with open coolers, some breweries now run wort directly from the hop back to the refrigerator. Other alternatives include placing the cooler in an air-tight chamber with sterilized air or using a cooling tank, an enclosed vessel capable of artificial aeration, which is frequently used in America.

Because coolers cannot bring wort to the proper temperature for fermentation, a refrigerator is employed. These may be vertical or horizontal, operating by allowing a thin stream of wort to trickle over pipes circulating cold water.

## Specialized Brewing Systems
### The Decoction System
In the decoction system, grist is mashed with cold water and then heated to 85-100° F. About one-third of the mash is transferred to a *Maisch Kessel* (mash copper), heated to approximately 165° F until transparent, and then raised to a boil for fifteen to forty-five minutes. This "thick mash" (*Dickmaische*) is returned to the mash-tun to raise the overall temperature to 111-125° F. This process is repeated with a second thick mash. Finally, a third portion, the *Läutermaische* (clear mash), is withdrawn and boiled to precipitate coagulable albuminoids before being returned to the mash-tun. The final heat of mashing in this system is slightly over 160° F.

### Bottom Fermentation and Lager
Wort produced via the decoction system often undergoes bottom fermentation. In this system, fermentation occurs in small vessels at low temperatures; fermenting cellars are kept at 37-38° F, and the wort does not exceed 50° F. The yeast remains at the bottom of the tun. Primary fermentation lasts eleven to twelve days, after which the beer is moved to lager casks at temperatures near the freezing point of water.

Modern lager brewing, such as that used by Messrs Allsopp, utilizes refrigerated brine for cooling and huge glass-enamel sheet-steel cylindrical vessels for fermentation. In this system, wort ferments under reduced pressure while a vacuum pump removes carbonic acid and introduces cool sterilized air. This process is faster, finishing in about six days. The beer then undergoes secondary fermentation for three weeks in store tanks.

## Final Stages: Cleansing, Racking, and Storing
Following fermentation and cleansing, beer is racked into trade casks or storage vessels. "Running" beers, such as mild ales, may be sent out within ten days of mashing. Higher quality "stock" and "pale" ales are stored for six weeks to three months. 

Some beers undergo "dry hopping," where hops are added directly to the cask. Others are "primed" by adding a small quantity of sugar solution to rapidly produce "condition." In some northern English breweries, the "Stone Square System" is used, involving two superimposed stone vessels kept very cool, between which the fermenting wort is pumped.
