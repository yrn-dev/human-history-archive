# Cross Tubes in Vertical Boilers

In the construction of cylindrical boilers designed for small installations or environments where floor space is limited, vertical boilers are frequently employed. One common variety is the simple vertical boiler, which features a plain vertical fire-box and an internal smoke stack that traverses the steam space. In these boilers, the space between the shell and the slightly tapering fire-box is filled with water.

## Design and Specifications of Cross Tubes
Except in the smallest sizes, simple vertical boilers are generally fitted with cross tubes. These tubes are constructed from 3/8-in. plate and have a diameter of approximately 9 in. To facilitate their installation, they are flanged at each end, which enables them to be riveted to the fire-box plates. 

To ensure efficient operation, these tubes are typically installed with a slight inclination, a design choice intended to facilitate the circulation of water. For maintenance and cleaning purposes, the outer shell of the boiler is provided with a hand-hole opposite each tube, which is closed by a suitable door. This specific boiler configuration is often utilized for auxiliary purposes on board ships.

## The Process and Purpose of Flanging
Flanging is a critical operation in boilermaking, involving the bending or turning over of the edges of a plate to provide a means of union to other plates. This technique is used in various components, including the ends of conical cross-tubes, the crowns of vertical boilers, the fire-boxes of locomotive boilers, and the back end-plates of Cornish and Lancashire boilers.

The adoption of flanging superseded an older system that used angle iron rings to form rectangular sections. Those older rings were a frequent source of explosions and grooving because their sharp angular form lacked elasticity. In contrast, a flange turned with a large radius provides the necessary elasticity to counteract the effects of temperature changes.

## Manufacturing Methods
The methods used to create flanges have evolved from manual labor to industrial machinery:

*   **Hand-Flanging:** In small establishments, a skilled workman known as an angle ironsmith performs the task. This involves heating a length of the edge (approximately 10 inches to a foot) and hammering it around a block of iron of a suitable shape. This process is repeated in "heats" until the flange is complete.
*   **Machine-Flanging:** Modern boiler shops utilize hydraulic presses to bend entire flanges in a single squeeze between massive dies for plates ranging from 4 to 8 ft. in diameter. For marine boiler ends that are too large for this method, a special press bends the edges over in successive heats. Additionally, flanges for Adamson seams are produced using a special machine where the flue is rotated on a table and the flange is turned over by revolving rollers within a minute.

Machine-flanging is preferred not only for the enormous saving of time but also because it causes far less injury to the material than hand-flanging.

## Material and Structural Requirements
Boilers are primarily constructed from steel and iron. Because they are subject to high pressures and temperatures, they must be made of materials that combine strength when heated with high ductility. To prevent fractures or grooving, boilers require provisions for freedom of movement and elasticity. This is achieved through the selection of steel or iron with a high percentage of elongation, the imposition of bending tests (both hot and cold), the limitation of tube lengths, judicious staying, and the use of flanging.
