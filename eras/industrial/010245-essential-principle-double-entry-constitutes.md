# Double-Entry Book-keeping

Double-entry is a system of accounting evolved to address the inherent weaknesses of single-entry records. While single-entry is still frequently utilized by retail traders and small business houses, it is considered manifestly incomplete because it lacks an automatic check on clerical accuracy. In a single-entry system, any mistake made during the keeping of books or the extraction of lists regarding liabilities and assets results in an equally incorrect statement of profit, loss, and the balance of assets and liabilities.

## Essential Principles and Mechanics
The essential principle of double-entry is that it provides a complete record of every business transaction. Because business transactions are invariably cross-dealings—meaning they simultaneously involve the imparting of a benefit by one party and the receipt of a benefit by another—a complete record requires an entry of an equal amount on both the credit and debit sides of the ledger.

If the clerical work is performed correctly, the aggregate amount entered on the debit side of the ledger must always equal the aggregate amount entered on the credit side. This allows for the creation of a trial balance, which is a complete list of all ledger balances showing that the total credit balances agree with the total debit balances.

## Limitations of the Trial Balance
Although the trial balance provides a mechanical test of the ledger, it does not prove absolute accuracy. It is not an adequate safeguard against fraud, nor does it reveal mistakes in the monetary values attached to transactions. Specifically, the trial balance cannot detect:
* Transactions that were omitted from the books entirely.
* Transactions recorded to the wrong accounts.
* Incorrect money values attached to records.
* Fictitious records of transactions that never occurred.
* Inaccuracies caused by altered circumstances, which effectively constitute omitted additional transactions.

## Account Classifications and Financial Statements
To complete the record of transactions, double-entry introduces a third class of accounts known as nominal or impersonal accounts. Unlike the reshuffling of assets and liabilities, these accounts record the transferences of money (or money's worth) that result in a reduction or increase of the amount invested in the business, representing a loss or a profit.

Under this system, transactions representing losses (including expenses) are recorded on the debit (Dr.) side, while those representing profits are recorded on the credit (Cr.) side. Consequently, all credit balances that are not liabilities are profits, and all debit balances that are not assets are losses.

By aggregating these results, a business can deduce its net profit or loss through a method distinct from single-entry, providing a valuable additional check. This process involves several documents:
* **Trading Account:** This records the movements of goods representing stock-in-trade and serves as a subdivision of, or preliminary to, the profit and loss account.
* **Profit and Loss Account:** A statement of all profits and losses.
* **Balance Sheet:** A statement of assets and liabilities. By transferring the balance of the profit and loss account to the capital account, the balance sheet can show the credit balance representing the surplus of assets over liabilities to date.

## Historical Development
Modern book-keeping records generally utilize three columns for the date, nature, and money value of a transaction. A ledger from 1697 in the Advocates' library at Edinburgh, which was ruled by hand, is likely the earliest extant example of this format. However, double-entry was in general use long before this.

While some have claimed that Lucas de Bergo invented the system around 1494, this is inaccurate. 1494 is actually the date of the first Venice publication of *Everything about Arithmetic, Geometry and Proportion* by Luca Paciolo, which explained double-entry as it was understood at the time. It is probable that the system had already been in use for approximately 200 years prior to Paciolo's book.

The archive notes that accounting literature from 1494 until recently was largely written by students and theorists rather than practical business men, leading to doubts about how accurately these texts describe contemporary procedures. Additionally, a 1796 publication by E.T. Jones titled *Jones's English System of Book-keeping by Single or Double Entry* is regarded as being without merit, despite Jones's claims of a patented, improved system used to secure thousands of subscribers.
