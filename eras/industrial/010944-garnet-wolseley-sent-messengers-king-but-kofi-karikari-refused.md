# The Anglo-Ashanti War of 1873-74

The conflict between the British and the Ashanti in 1873-74 was precipitated by two primary events. The most significant cause was the transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti viewed the Elmina as their subjects and considered the fort to be Ashanti territory. Historically, the king of Ashanti held the "custom-note," receiving an annual payment from the Dutch that the Dutch viewed as a trade present, but which the Ashanti regarded as ground rent. Consequently, the Ashanti deeply resented the British occupation of the fort.

A secondary cause involved the captivity of four Europeans. In June 1869, an Ashanti force invaded Krepi, a territory located beyond the Volta. During this incursion, they captured Mr. J. Kuhne and Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer along with his wife and infant son (the child died shortly thereafter), all of whom were members of the Basel mission. Additionally, a French trader named Monsieur M.J. Bonnat was captured elsewhere. These prisoners were taken to Kumasi. Although negotiations for their release began, the Europeans remained prisoners at the time of the Elmina fort sale.

## The Reign of Kofi Karikari
The political climate in Ashanti shifted following the death of King Kwaka Dua I in April 1867. Kwaka Dua I had been a peace-loving monarch who encouraged trade, though his reign had seen conflict in 1852 and 1863. After a period of civil war following his death, Kofi Karikari was enstooled. Upon taking power, Kofi Karikari declared, "My business shall be war." Under his leadership, preparations were made throughout Ashanti to attack the Fanti tribes, leading to the war of 1873-74.

## The British Expedition and Sir Garnet Wolseley
The Ashanti initiated hostilities on January 22, 1873, by crossing the Prah and invading the British protectorate. The Ashanti forces defeated the Fanti, caused disputes at Elmina, and encamped at Mampon near Cape Coast. In response, the British parliament voted £800,000 for an expedition to punish the assailants and defend the territory. Sir Garnet Wolseley was dispatched as the British administrator.

Wolseley landed at Cape Coast on October 2. Upon arrival, he found that the Ashanti forces had been decimated by fever and smallpox and were preparing to return to their own lands. Despite this, Wolseley determined to march on Kumasi. He was supported by Captain John Glover, the administrator of Lagos, who led a force of native levies to cooperate from the east and attack the Ashanti from the rear.

## The Fall of Kumasi
The campaign culminated in the destruction of the Ashanti capital. Before its fall in 1874, Kumasi was a city of high culture with broad, regular streets and a main avenue 70 yards wide. The king's palace was constructed of red sandstone and was believed to be modeled after Dutch buildings at Elmina. On February 6, 1874, the forces of Sir Garnet Wolseley blew up the royal palace. Following the British withdrawal, the city was only partially rebuilt, and the new royal palace was constructed of clay rather than brick.

## Aftermath and Successors
The war resulted in several states dependent on the Ashanti declaring their independence and seeking British protection, though the British colonial office refused these requests. Shortly after the war, the Ashanti deposed Kofi Karikari and placed his brother, Mensa, on the golden stool, which served as the symbol of sovereignty. Mensa's rule was characterized as tyrannous, involving repeated human sacrifices and the violation of nearly every article of the Fomana treaty. Of the 50,000 oz. of gold stipulated as an indemnity, only 4,000 oz. were paid.

Mensa was displaced by a revolution in 1883 and succeeded by Kwaka Dua II, who died in June 1884. The former king, Kofi Karikari, also died in June 1884. Following a desolating civil war, Prince Prempeh (taking the name Kwaka Dua III) was chosen as king on March 26, 1888, with the sanction of a Gold Coast government officer.
