# BOOKSELLING

The trade in books is of a very ancient date, evolving from the early practices of poets and orators who recited their works in public to encourage hearers to acquire written copies. In these early stages, transcripts were frequently taken down *viva voce* and sold to wealthy purchasers. Scribes served as the earliest booksellers, supplying copies as demanded. Examples of this practice include the prophet Jeremiah dictating to the scribe Baruch. In antiquity, Plato is recorded to have paid one hundred minae for three small treatises by Philolaus the Pythagorean, and Aristotle is noted to have possessed an extensive library.

## Classical and Early Trade
The founding of the Alexandrian library around 300 B.C. stimulated Athenian booksellers, known as *biblion kapaeloi*. By the end of the Roman republic, libraries became common household furniture, leading to a flourishing trade managed by *librarii* or *bibliopolae*. Their shops, or *taberna librarii*, were primarily located in the Vicus Sandalarius and the Argiletum. These sellers listed available books on their doors or side posts; for instance, Martial noted that a copy of his First Book of Epigrams cost five denarii. During the era of Augustus the great, the Sosii were prominent booksellers. Additionally, a law passed by Justinian secured the property of materials used by scribes, which may represent the first germ of modern copyright law.

## Medieval and Pre-Printing Developments
The trade appears to have been established in the 12th century in Bologna and Paris, driven by universities and lawyers. Dealers known as *stationarii* (possibly named for their open stalls) sold books on commission and provided parchment and writing materials—the origin of the term "stationery." They also engaged in binding, decorating, and the employment of transcribers. While *librarii* originally referred to copyists, the term later expanded to include those who traded in books.

In England, the spread of Christianity increased demand for sacred books, such as the Gospels, and devotional volumes like missals. In 671, Benedict Biscop brought a cargo of books from France to the abbey at Wearmouth. By 1403, text writers or "stacyoneres" in the neighborhood of St Paul's, London, formed a gild. Some operated stalls against the cathedral walls. At Oxford, "sworn" university stationers held a near-monopoly on supplying pupils. A 1373 statute attempted to restrict non-sworn booksellers from selling books exceeding half a mark in value to prevent the cheating of owners and the loss of business for sworn stationers.

## The Impact of Printing
The modern system of bookselling began shortly after the introduction of printing. Early printers often acted as editors and booksellers, utilizing agents at seats of learning to sell their stock. Antony Koburger, who brought printing to Nuremberg in 1470, operated sixteen shops and had agents throughout Christendom. In Westminster, Wynkyn de Worde, successor to Caxton's press, maintained a shop in Fleet Street.

During the 16th and 17th centuries, the Low Countries became the world's chief bookselling center. Notable names included Plantin, Blauw, and Jansen, with imprints from Antwerp, Leiden, Utrecht, and Amsterdam. The Elzevirs produced pocket classics, while the towns of St Omer and Douai produced English polemical works.

## Evolution of the Industry
Over time, a distinction emerged between the publisher and the bookseller. The publisher produces books from manuscripts and acts as an intermediary between the author and the bookseller. The bookseller, in a restricted sense, acts as the intermediary between the publisher and the public. By the 19th century, this distinction between the "literary promoter" (publisher) and "shopkeeper" (bookseller) became fundamental.

Booksellers divided into several categories:
*   **Wholesale Booksellers:** These agencies act as critical distributors. Retailers rely on them for miscellaneous supplies because they cannot stock every published work. In London, Paternoster Row served as the industry center for over a century. Publishers "subscribe" new books to these wholesalers, a process that can determine a book's fate.
*   **Retail Booksellers:** To protect their interests, a London society was established in 1890 to control trade details and restrict discounts to 25%. This later became "The Associated Booksellers of Great Britain and Ireland."
*   **Second-hand Booksellers:** This trade requires bibliographic knowledge and deals with individual books rather than mass copies. These dealers sometimes purchase "remainders" from publishers to sell as bargains.
*   **Periodical Trade:** Growing in the 19th century with publications like *Chambers's Journal* and the *Penny Magazine*, this sector was boosted by the abolition of the paper duty and newspaper stamp.

## Bookselling in America
Printing was introduced to Boston (1676), Philadelphia (1685), and New York (1693). While Benjamin Franklin made the trade illustrious, few were engaged in it by the start of the 19th century, and scholarly works were mostly imported from Europe. Following the second war, printing presses multiplied. Because there was no international copyright, American publishers reprinted the works of British authors—including Wordsworth, Byron, and Scott—without payment. Between 1820 and 1852, half the authors in the "American" catalogue were British. After the Civil War, high labor costs and restrictive duties led publishers to make liberal offers for early sheets of new publications. New York, Philadelphia, and Boston remained the primary centers until American business conditions assimilated to those of Europe.
