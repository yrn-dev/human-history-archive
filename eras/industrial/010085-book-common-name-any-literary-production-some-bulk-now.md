# Book

A book is the common name used for any literary production of some bulk. In modern application, the term refers particularly to a printed composition that forms a volume; if a single organic literary work consists of more than one volume, it is still referred to as a book. Additionally, the word is used descriptively to identify the internal sections or divisions of a comprehensive work.

## Printing and Production
The art of printing has been advanced by figures such as the English printer John Baskerville (1706–1775). Baskerville, who began experimenting with type-founding around 1750, produced types noted for their elegance and distinctness. His press issued several significant works, including a *Virgil* in royal quarto (1757), an edition of Milton (1758), and editions of the Bible and the Book of Common Prayer. He is also noted for his *Horace* (1762), praised for its type and paper quality, as well as a series of quarto editions of Latin authors such as Lucretius, Terence, Sallust, Florus, Catullus, Tibullus, and Propertius.

The physical quality of a book's production can reflect its intended audience. For example, early editions of *The Pilgrim's Progress* utilized plates, printing, and paper of the "meanest description," as they were intended for servants' halls and cottages.

## Bibliography and Arrangement
Bibliography involves the recording and arrangement of books. While 18th and early 19th-century French writers believed bibliographers should appraise the value of every recorded work and fit them into a logical classification of all knowledge, modern bibliographers are more modest. They view the classification of knowledge as a task for scientists and philosophers, focusing instead on general principles of arrangement.

Books may be arranged for various purposes:
* **Printing History:** Organized by the printing-houses or places of production.
* **Regional Pride:** Grouped by the author's place of residence or birth.
* **Special Purposes:** Arranged by dialect or language.
* **General Methods:** Organized alphabetically by author and title, chronologically by publication date, or logically by subject.

An alphabetical arrangement by author and title is considered most useful for students seeking detailed information about individual books.

## Collecting and Value
Books that appeal to collectors often possess associations with famous events or persons, such as kings, queens, poets, soldiers, or statesmen. A book's value increases if it bears marks of being valued by such a person or if its pedigree can be traced through a series of book-loving owners.

Collectors also prize first editions, partly for their superiority in study and partly for the associations they create. While the high prices paid during the era of George III for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics are difficult to justify—as they are of little value as texts—the prestige of first editions of French and English literary masterpieces has increased. For instance, a first folio Shakespeare from 1623 was sold in 1906. Other imaginative interests for collectors include books that caused the persecution of their authors or those condemned to be burnt.

## Literary Forms and Examples
Books take various forms, ranging from legal text-books—which have steadily improved in arrangement and statement—to novels and satirical works. 

* **The Novel:** Honoré de Balzac began his real literary career in 1829 with the publication of *Les Chouans* (initially *Le Dernier Chouan*) and the analytic-satirical sketches of *Physiologie du mariage*.
* **Satire:** Sebastian Brant's *Narrenschiff* (1494), which featured 103 types of fools and woodcut illustrations, served as a starting point for new satirical literature. It was translated into Latin as *Stultifera Navis* (1497) and into English by Barclay as *The Shyp of Folys of the Worlde* (1509).
* **Epistles:** While a letter is a spontaneous, private substitute for conversation, an epistle is a literary form and artistic effort aiming at permanence, often acting as a substitute for a public speech.

Some books have faced scrutiny regarding their authorship. Professor E.G. Bourne proved in 1906 that the bulk of the narrative attributed to Carver was actually a close paraphrase of works by James Adair, La Hontan, and Charlevoix, suggesting the book was likely the work of Dr. John Coakley Lettsom.
