# Industrial Applications of Bones

The industrial utilization of animal bones has been developed through human inventiveness to a degree where nearly every constituent of the bone is recovered for commercial use. Bones are composed of mineral matter (such as phosphates), fat, and gelatinous substances, all of which serve as raw materials for various secondary enterprises.

## Chemical and Material Extraction
The different components of the animal frame are processed to create a variety of commercial products:
*   **Phosphates:** These mineral constituents are utilized as artificial manures.
*   **Fats:** The fat extracted from bones is processed by chandlers and soap-makers. In the meat-packing industry, fats are also used to produce lard and butterine.
*   **Gelatinous Matter:** This substance forms the basis for commercial glue and gelatin. In industrial slaughtering, small bones and hoofs specifically provide the raw material for glue manufacture.

## Carbonization and Distillation
A specific industrial process involves the partial extraction of gelatinous matter followed by the carbonization of the remaining residue. Bones are heated in horizontal cast-iron retorts (holding approximately 5 cwt.) for twelve to thirteen hours. The resulting residue is removed while red-hot into air-tight vessels, cooled, passed through grinding mills, and riddled using revolving cylindrical sieves.

This process yields several by-products:
*   **Animal Charcoal:** Comprising 55% to 60% of the carbonized bones, this deep black material contains approximately 75% calcium phosphate and 10% carbon. It is widely used as a clarifying and filtering material.
*   **Bone Oil and Bone Pitch:** Vapors from carbonization are condensed into a liquid that separates into two layers. The upper tarry layer is redistilled to produce "bone oil"—which consists mainly of pyridine derivatives and fatty amines and is noted for a disgusting odor—and "bone pitch," which is used in the production of black varnishes and similar compositions.
*   **Ammonium Sulphate:** The lower ammoniacal liquor layer is distilled with steam, and the ammonia is received in sulphuric acid to produce ammonium sulphate, which is primarily used as a manure.
*   **Inflammable Gases:** Gases evolved during the distillation of tar and carbonization of bones are purified and used for illuminating or motive purposes.

## Direct Manufacturing and Historical Uses
Beyond chemical extraction, bones are used directly as a raw material for carving and crafting. Large bones are carved into items such as knife-handles and buttons.

Historically, the use of bone for implements dates back to early human history. In the era when humans hunted the open steppes of Europe and later transitioned to forests, fine bone implements increased. Specifically, bone needles from this epoch are described as being superior to those of later historical times, including those used by the Romans.

In other cultural contexts, such as in Scythia, bones served a functional role in food preparation. Due to a lack of wood, the Scythians would strip flesh from the bones of victims (especially horses and other cattle) and light the bones underneath caldrons or the victims' own stomachs to boil the meat.

## Integration with the Meat Industry
The industrial use of bones is closely linked to the wholesale slaughtering and packing of meats. In the United States, Illinois surpassed other states in this industry between 1870 and 1905. In 1900 and 1905, Illinois produced more than one-third of the total product of the United States, with the industry yielding 22.9% of the state's total manufactured product in 1900 and 22.5% in 1905. This industry fostered secondary enterprises that ensured nearly all portions of the slaughtered animal were utilized, including the conversion of bones into the aforementioned glues and carved handles.
