# The Bubble Act

The Bubble Act (6 Geo. I. c. 18) was a piece of English legislation designed to check speculative banking. It was passed in England following the bursting of the South Sea Bubble, a period of intense speculative fever.

## Origins and the South Sea Bubble
The speculative fever known as the South Sea Bubble was based on the perceived value of privileges granted to the South Sea Company. This company had been conveyed the rights of the Asiento Treaty of 1713, which had been signed on March 16. Under the terms of this treaty, British subjects were authorized to introduce 4,800 slaves per annum into Spanish colonies in America over a period of thirty years, totaling 144,000 slaves. Additionally, the treaty allowed for one ship of 500 tons per year to be sent to the fairs of La Vera Cruz and Porto Bello with manufactured goods, subject to import duties.

## Provisions and Impact on Banking
The Bubble Act forbade the formation of banking companies unless they possessed a special charter. By restricting the creation of such companies, the act served as a mechanism to limit speculative banking activities.

The influence of this legislation extended beyond England. In 1740, the Bubble Act was extended to the American colonies. During the early history of the American colonies, many crude experiments in currency issues and banking took place; however, most of these were projects focused on the issue of paper money rather than the establishment of commercial banks. The Bubble Act acted as a check on speculative banking within these colonies.

## Related Banking Restrictions in England and Ireland
The Bubble Act existed alongside other restrictive banking laws in the United Kingdom. In England, the act of 1697 declared that no joint-stock bank was to be established during the continuance of the Bank of England. Furthermore, an act of 1708 (confirmed in 1800) stipulated that no partnership or company exceeding six persons in England could borrow or take up money on notes or bills payable on demand, or at any time less than six months from the borrowing.

Similar restrictive legislation existed in Ireland. In 1695, Dublin merchants attempted to form a public bank based on the model of the Bank of England, but the proposal lacked favor for many years. When the Bank of Ireland was eventually established in 1783, its forming act decreed that no society or company exceeding six in number—with the exception of the Bank of Ireland—could take up or borrow money on their notes or bills payable on demand. This restriction was modified in 1821 to allow companies exceeding six people to be established if they were located more than 50 miles from Dublin, a limit that was not withdrawn until 1845.

## Evolution of Banking Legislation
The restrictive environment created by acts like the Bubble Act and the limitations on partnership size gradually evolved during the 19th century. In England, the act of 1826 allowed for the establishment of co-partnerships of more than six persons beyond 65 miles from London. By 1833, joint-stock banks were permitted within the 65-mile limit, although they were not allowed to issue notes within that radius.

The restriction on the number of partners in private banks remained at six until 1862, when it was increased to ten, though this did not apply to issuing private banks. The transition toward modern banking was further shaped by the Bank Charter Acts of 1844 and 1845, and the Companies Act of 1879, the latter of which allowed unlimited companies to adopt limited liability following the failure of the City of Glasgow Bank in 1878.
