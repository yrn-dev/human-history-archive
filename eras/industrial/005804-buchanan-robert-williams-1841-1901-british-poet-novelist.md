# BUCHANAN, ROBERT WILLIAMS (1841-1901)

Robert Williams Buchanan was a British dramatist, novelist, and poet. He was born on August 18, 1841, at Caverswall, Staffordshire. He was the son of Robert Buchanan (1813-1866), who served as a journalist and Owenite lecturer. Although his father was a native of Ayr, the family moved to Glasgow after spending several years in Manchester. In Glasgow, Buchanan received his education at the university and the high school. During this time, he formed a close friendship with the poet David Gray, a fellow student.

## Early Career and Poetry
In 1860, Buchanan and David Gray traveled to London in search of fame, a period marked by disappointment and struggle. Buchanan's first publication, a "tentative" volume titled *Undertones*, appeared in 1863. This was followed by a series of works that demonstrated his sympathetic insight into the humbler conditions of life and a faculty for poetic narrative, including *Idyls and Legends of Inverburn* (1865), *London Poems* (1866), and *North Coast and other Poems* (1868).

While narrative poems are considered his strongest work, Buchanan attempted a more ambitious study in mysticism with *The Book of Orm: A Prelude to the Epic* in 1870. Other notable poems include "The Drama of Kings" (1871), "Balder the Beautiful" (1877), "The City of Dream" (1888), "The Outcast: a Rhyme for the Time" (1891), and "The Wandering Jew" (1893). He also published a lively tale of Salt Lake City in 1872 titled "St Abe and his Seven Wives," which was released anonymously. His poetical works were collected into three volumes in 1874, a single volume in 1884, and finally as *Complete Poetical Works* in two volumes in 1901.

## Literary Controversies
Buchanan was a frequent contributor to periodical literature. He gained notoriety in October 1871 when he wrote an article for the *Contemporary Review* titled "The Fleshly School of Poetry," using the pseudonym Thomas Maitland. Although he expanded this critique into a pamphlet in 1872, he later withdrew from the criticisms and expressed regret regarding the violence of the attack. 

The article provoked significant responses, most notably from Mr. Swinburne in *Under the Microscope* (1872) and from D.G. Rossetti, who wrote a letter to the *Athenaeum* on December 16, 1871, titled "The Stealthy School of Criticism." Rossetti is identified as the "old enemy" to whom Buchanan dedicated his work *God and the Man*.

## Novels and Drama
Buchanan began writing novels in 1876 with *The Shadow of the Sword*, which is regarded as one of the best in a long series of novels. His early novels, including *The Shadow of the Sword* and the 1881 tale of a family feud *God and the Man*, are noted for their simplicity and breadth of treatment. Later novels include *The Martyrdom of Madeline* (1882), *Foxglove Manor* (1885), *Effie Hetherington* (1896), and *Father Anthony* (1898).

As a dramatist, Buchanan authored several successful plays, including:
* *Lady Clare* (1883)
* *Sophia* (1886), which was an adaptation of *Tom Jones*
* *A Man's Shadow* (1890)
* *The Charlatan* (1894)

## Other Writings and Essays
Beyond his poetry and fiction, Buchanan produced several significant prose works. *The Land of Lorne* (1871), published in two volumes, provides a vivid record of his yachting experiences along the west coast of Scotland. His contributions to periodical literature are further contained in *David Gray and other Essays, chiefly on Poetry* (1868), *Master Spirits* (1873), *A Look round Literature* (1887), and *A Poet's Sketch Book* (1883). The latter work reprints his essay on David Gray, which was originally contributed to the *Cornhill Magazine* and details the friendship between the two men.
