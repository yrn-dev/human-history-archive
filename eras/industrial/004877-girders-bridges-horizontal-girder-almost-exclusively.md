# Horizontal Girders in Bridge Construction

In the construction of bridges, the horizontal girder is a primary supporting member. These structures are designed to manage specific loading forces and internal stresses to ensure the stability of the bridge superstructure.

## Structural Composition and Forces
A main girder typically consists of three primary components: an upper flange (also referred to as a boom or chord), a lower flange (or chord), and a vertical web. In bridge girders, the horizontal girder is almost exclusively subjected to vertical loading forces. 

Investigation into the internal stresses used to balance these external forces indicates that the material is most effectively arranged in the top flange, which is subjected to compression, and the bottom flange, which is subjected to tension. The function of these flanges is to resist horizontal tension and compression, which are distributed practically uniformly across their cross sections. These horizontal stresses reach their maximum intensity at the center of a span.

Connecting the two flanges is the vertical web, which may be constructed as a system of bracing bars or a solid plate. The web's role is to resist forces equivalent to a shear on horizontal and vertical planes. In the case of a braced web, the inclined compressions and tensions in the bars provide this resistance. Stresses within the web are greatest at the ends of the span.

While the exact cross-section of girders can vary, they are virtually an I section. For practical calculations, it is considered accurate to view the web as resisting the entirety of the vertical forces and, in the case of plate webs, the equal horizontal shearing forces, while the booms or chords carry the horizontal compression and tension exclusively.

## Types of Girder Configurations
The form of the girder's chords often depends on the span of the bridge:
*   **Parallel Booms:** The majority of bridges that do not have a great span utilize girders with parallel booms. This configuration is preferred because it involves the fewest difficulties in workmanship and allows for the closest approximation between theoretical and actual dimensions.
*   **Polygonal Booms:** In spans exceeding 200 feet, it is considered economical to utilize one horizontal boom and one polygonal boom (which is approximately parabolic).
*   **Hog-backed Girders:** This type serves as a compromise between parallel and polygonal designs, helping to avoid certain construction difficulties near the ends of the girder. Curved chords generally result in diminished stresses within the web.

## Bridge Classifications and Support Systems
Girder bridges are categorized by how they carry their platforms and how they are supported.

### Platform Placement
Girders may carry a floor or platform either on top, in which case they are called "deck" bridges, or near the bottom, known as "through" bridges. In railway bridges, the platform commonly consists of cross girders attached to the main girders, with longitudinal rail girders (stringers) supporting the rails and sleepers.

### Modes of Support
The method of support creates fundamental differences in bridge design:
*   **Discontinuous or Independent:** The main girders are supported only at the ends.
*   **Continuous:** A main girder is supported at two or more points across several spans. This allows for an economy of weight; for example, in a three-span bridge, continuous girders can be lighter than discontinuous ones by approximately 45% for dead load and 15% for live load. However, continuity can be risky, as small changes in the level of supports due to pier settlement can greatly alter stress distribution.
*   **Cantilever and Suspended Girders:** This system is as economical as continuous girders but avoids uncertainty regarding stresses. In long-span bridges, the cantilever system is particularly useful as it permits erection by building out.

## Material and Mechanical Properties
The widespread expansion of railways since 1830 led to the construction of many girder bridges, utilizing wrought iron and later mild steel for economy and convenience. In these structures, approximately half of the superstructure is in tension and half is in compression.

Compared to arch or chain bridges, girders require more material because they act as a combination of both. However, they offer the advantage of imposing only a vertical load on abutments and piers, avoiding the horizontal thrust associated with suspension chains or arches.
