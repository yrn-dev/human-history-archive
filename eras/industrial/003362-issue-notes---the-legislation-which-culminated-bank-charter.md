# Issue of Notes

The issuance of banknotes has been governed by various legislative frameworks across different nations, often evolving from widespread provincial or private privileges toward centralized control by national banks.

## The Bank Charter Acts in England
The Bank Charter Acts of 1844 and 1845 established a structured monopoly for the Bank of England regarding the issuance of notes. The Bank of England was granted an absolute monopoly within the city of London and a radius of 3 miles. Between that 3-mile limit and a 65-mile radius from the city, a concurrent right to issue notes was maintained by banks established before 1844 that were issuing notes at that time and consisted of six or fewer persons. Beyond the 65-mile radius, the privilege was extended to all banks established before 1844 that issued notes, provided they had not lost the right through bankruptcy, abandonment of business, or temporary suspension of issue.

While some authorities suggest that the Companies Consolidation Act 1908 (re-enacting 20 and 21 Vict. cap. 49, sec. 12) allowed banks within the 3-to-65-mile radius to increase their constitution from six to ten persons without losing note-issuing powers, the rule formulated by Bowen J. in *Capital and Counties Bank v. Bank of England* (1889) maintains the original restrictions.

## Evolution of English Note Circulation
Prior to the 1844 Act, nearly all provincial banks in England and Wales held the privilege of issue. However, as the Act's arrangements became public, nine banks (eight private and one joint-stock) ceased issuing notes before October 12, 1844. Of these, the Western District Joint-Stock Banking Co. was dissolved, one private bank closed, and seven transitioned to issuing Bank of England notes.

The 1844 Act fixed the maximum circulation for English issuing banks based on the average circulation of the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. At that time, 207 private banks had an authorized issue of £5,153,417, and 72 joint-stock banks had an authorized issue of £3,478,230. By October 1844, the actual circulation in England consisted of £20,228,800 from the Bank of England, £4,674,162 from private banks, and £3,331,516 from joint-stock banks. In Scotland, chartered, private, and joint-stock banks had a circulation of £2,987,665.

Over time, the growth of joint-stock banks and the absorption of smaller concerns minimized the output of notes other than those from the Bank of England. Legal precedents, such as *The Attorney-General v. Birkbeck*, indicate that the note-issuing power of an absorbed bank cannot be transferred to an amalgamated body, though a bank with the right does not necessarily lose it by absorbing others. Foreign banks operating branches in Great Britain under the Companies Consolidation Act 1908 are apparently unable to issue notes, even if payable abroad.

## The Bank of England's Central Role
The 1844 Act positioned the Bank of England as the sole source of legal tender notes. This role is particularly significant during times of financial pressure. Between 1844 and 1906, the government granted the bank authority to exceed the fixed limit of notes on only three occasions via a "treasury letter," granting the bank substantial power during crises.

Regarding security, no security was ever required for local issues in the United Kingdom. While the 1844-1845 acts required Scottish and Irish banks to hold specie against notes issued beyond legal limits, this coin was not considered a security. The only state recognition of security for note-holders occurred in 1879, when note issues became a first charge with unlimited liability on the total assets of joint-stock banks that accepted limited liability for other business.

## Note Issuance in Austria-Hungary
The Austro-Hungarian Bank, founded in 1816 as the Austrian National Bank, held the legal exclusive right to issue banknotes. Originally entirely Austrian-directed with branches in Hungary, it was converted into a joint Austro-Hungarian bank in 1877, with management divided between the two countries and head offices in both Vienna and Pest. This arrangement was renewed in 1887.

The currency was historically a mix of silver (gulden) and paper notes. Due to the government authorizing the suspension of cash payments in 1848 and subsequent wars in 1859 and 1866, the notes became an inconvertible paper currency. Although the bank had the sole right of issue, the government violated this charter during the 1866 war by issuing *Staatsnoten* valued at 312 million gulden. An operation begun in 1892 sought to redeem these *Staatsnoten*, resume cash payments, and substitute a gold standard for the silver standard.

Under a 1899 statute, the bank became a joint-stock company with a stock of £8,780,000. Its notes must be covered by two-fifths legal specie (gold and current silver) in reserve. As of 1900, the bank's note circulation was £62,251,000; by 1907, this had risen to £84,501,000.

## Note Issuance in the United States
In the United States, the national banking system emerged from Civil War financial necessities. Secretary of the Treasury Mr. Chase created a system where banks organized under Federal charters could issue circulating notes only if secured by the deposit of government bonds. To encourage this, a 10% tax was imposed on the face value of state bank notes in circulation after July 1, 1866, driving state banks out of the note-issuing business. This led to rapid growth: national banks grew from 453 in 1864 (aggregate capital $79,366,950) to 1,014 in 1865 (aggregate capital $242,542,982).

In Kentucky, the first banking currency was issued in 1802 by a co-operative insurance company. The Bank of Kentucky (est. 1806) held a monopoly for several years. Later, in 1818-1819, the legislature chartered 46 banks, most of which failed during the panic of 1819. The Bank of the Commonwealth (est. 1820) failed in 1829 after a court denied the legal tender quality of its notes. By 1834, the Bank of Kentucky, the Bank of Louisville, and the Northern Bank of Kentucky were chartered and survived the panic of 1837.
