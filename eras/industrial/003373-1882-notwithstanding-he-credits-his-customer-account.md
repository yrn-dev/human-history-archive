# Banking Relations and the Law of Cheques

## The Banker-Customer Relationship
The legal relationship between a banker and a customer is defined as that of debtor and creditor. This was established by the House of Lords in the 1848 case of *Foley v. Hill*, which overturned previous contentions that the relationship was one of depositee and depositor or trustee and *cestui que trust*. Under this framework, when an individual pays money into a bank account, they are legally lending that money to the banker. Consequently, the banker is not bound by a fiduciary relation and holds no responsibility to the customer regarding how the deposited funds are used.

Because this relationship is a mere debt, the Statute of Limitations bars a customer's right to recover paid-in money after six years, provided there has been no acknowledgment or payment on the principal or interest during that time. In practice, such scenarios are rare because current accounts typically attribute earlier drawings to earlier payments, allowing credit and debit items to cancel each other. An exception occurs if a person wrongfully deposits money held in a fiduciary capacity into their own account; in such cases, the account balance is attributed to the trust money, and it is presumed the individual drew out their own funds first.

## Obligations and Rights of the Banker
A banker is obligated to maintain secrecy regarding a customer's account unless there is good cause for disclosure, a duty that persists even if the account becomes overdrawn. Additionally, the banker is obligated to honor a customer's cheques, provided there is a sufficient and available balance. If a banker dishonors a cheque despite having sufficient funds, they are liable for substantial damages to the customer without the need for proof of actual injury.

When multiple cheques are presented simultaneously and funds are insufficient to cover all of them, the banker is not required to discriminate between them and should pay as many as the available funds allow. However, banks may refuse to pay cheques drawn to "petty cash or order" or "wages or order," as some banks do not consider these payable to the bearer. Furthermore, funds paid in too shortly before a cheque's presentation to be processed through the bank's books are not treated as available for drawing.

## The Law of Cheques and the Bills of Exchange Act 1882
Under section 53 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, a cheque in England is not considered an assignment of funds held by the banker. Therefore, if payment is refused, the holder of the cheque has no claim against the banker and must instead seek remedy from the endorser or the drawer. This legal standing supports the English banking custom of refusing to pay a portion of a cheque's amount if the available funds are insufficient to cover the total sum. This specific section of the Act does not apply in Scotland, where banks appear bound to pay whatever funds are available toward the satisfaction of a cheque.

## Collection of Crossed Cheques
Bankers are granted qualified immunity under section 82 when collecting crossed cheques for a customer. To qualify for this protection, the banker must meet several conditions:
*   The cheque and the money must be received from a customer with whom the banker has existing and definite business relations.
*   The cheque must already be specially or generally crossed to the banker; a crossing made by the banker under section 77 is ineffective for this purpose.
*   The banker must act in good faith and without negligence.

Negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care for the true owner of the cheque. To avoid this, the banker must ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct and verify authority for endorsements made *per procuration*. The banker must not take a cheque for a private account if the holder is an agent, in an official capacity, or acting for partnership purposes. Additionally, "account payee" cheques must not be taken for any account other than the one indicated.

The case of *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* (1903) clarified that this protection only applies while the banker acts as an agent or "conduit pipe" for the customer. If a banker credits cheques as cash in the bank's books before the money is actually received, they are viewed as the owner or transferee of the cheque, thereby forfeiting the protection of section 82.

## Account Records and Guarantees
Entries in a pass-book serve as *prima facie* evidence against the banker. If a customer returns a pass-book without comment, it is also considered evidence against them, though the idea that this constitutes a final settlement of account is disputed. In cases of forgery, a customer may still object to being debited regardless of their dealings with the pass-book. However, credit entries cannot be disputed by the customer if they have altered their position based on those entries.

Regarding guarantees, the existence of a guarantee does not force a banker to use a specific accounting system. The banker is not obligated to apply unappropriated payments to a guaranteed account over an unguaranteed one, provided the account is not unfairly manipulated to the guarantor's detriment. Upon the termination of a guarantee, a banker may close the account and open a new one for the customer. While a guarantee does not compel a banker to advance up to a specific limit, the banker generally cannot shut off all facilities immediately upon revocation without notice.
