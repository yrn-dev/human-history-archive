# Welding in Boiler Work

Welding is a significant operation in the construction of boilers, where it is more common than in bridge and girder work. In the context of boilermaking, welding is primarily utilized for the edges of plates, a practice that predominates over the welding of rods and bars typically found in the smithy.

## Technical Application and Process
The plates requiring union in boiler work are characterized as being long and thin. Because of these dimensions, the welding process is carried out in succession, with short lengths completed at successive "heats." While much of this process is performed by hand, large shops employ steam-driven power hammers to close the welds. In these operations, "gluts" or insertion pieces are generally preferred over the use of overlapping joints.

Welding is categorized as an operation that must be performed at a red or white heat. It is one of several specialized tasks—alongside flanging, rolling, planing, punching, shearing, bending, forging, drilling, riveting, caulking, and tubing—that require the labor of machine attendants and gangs of helpers.

## Common Welded Components
Several specific parts of a boiler are commonly welded:
*   **Furnace Flues:** These are always welded. In "Lancashire" and "Galloway" tank boilers, furnace flues are made in sections of 3 to 3-1/2 ft. length, consisting of a plate bent into a cylindrical form with a welded longitudinal joint.
*   **Conical Cross-Tubes and Angle Rings:** These components are frequently welded.
*   **Stand Pipes:** These are frequently welded in boiler construction.
*   **Water-Chambers:** In the "Durr" boiler, the large water-chamber is formed from steel plates with welded joints.
*   **Specialized Furnaces:** In cylindrical multitubular return tube boilers, certain furnace types—specifically the Morison, Fox, and Deighton types—are made from plates rolled to a uniform thickness, formed into a cylinder with a welded longitudinal joint, and then corrugated.

## Materials and Construction Context
Boilers are constructed primarily from iron and steel. In ordinary land and marine boilers, plates and stays are almost invariably made of mild steel. Specifically, the tubes of ordinary marine and land boilers are usually composed of lap-welded wrought iron.

The necessity for precise construction methods, including welding, arises from the fact that boilers are subject to high pressures and temperatures. To prevent grooving or fractures, they must be made of materials that combine strength with high ductility when heated to furnace temperatures. This is ensured through the selection of iron or steel with a high percentage of elongation and the application of hot and cold bending or temper tests.

## Comparison with Related Trades
While boiler, bridge, and girder shops employ similar materials and methods, their practices diverge. In boilermaking, plates are bent to cylindrical shapes for furnaces and shells, and flanging is extensively used for tube-plates and furnace flues—practices scarcely represented in girder work. Furthermore, while holes in boiler work are generally drilled through the seams of adjacent plates, bridge work typically involves drilling or punching each bar or plate separately.
