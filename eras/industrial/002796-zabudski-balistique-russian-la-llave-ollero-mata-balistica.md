# Zabudski, Balistique (Russian); La Llave, Ollero, Mata, &c., Balistica

The study of ballistics has been documented by various international authorities across several centuries. Among the recognized Russian contributions to this field is the work *Balistique*, authored by Mayevski and Zabudski. Similarly, the Spanish tradition of ballistic study is represented by the work *Balistica*, authored by La Llave, Ollero, Mata, and others.

## Contributions of Colonel Zabudski
Colonel Zabudski conducted specific analyses based on the Krupp experiments to determine the most appropriate index, denoted as *m*, within a region of velocity. This work is applied in instances where it is possible to represent *p* with sufficient accuracy using an empirical formula consisting of a single power of *v* (expressed as $v^m$). 

Through this analysis, Zabudski identified the corresponding value of *gp*, which is denoted as $f(v)$, $v^m/k$, or its equivalent $Cr$, where $r$ represents the retardation. These findings are detailed in an Abridged Ballistic Table.

## Context of Ballistic Authorities
The works of Zabudski and the Spanish authors (La Llave, Ollero, and Mata) exist within a broader historical lineage of ballistic research. Other notable authorities in the field include:

*   **Early Works:** Tartaglia's *Nova Scientia* (1537) and the writings of Galileo (1638).
*   **18th Century:** Robins' *New Principles of Gunnery* (1743) and Euler's *The True Principles of Gunnery* (1777), the latter translated by Hugh Brown.
*   **National Traditions:** French contributions include works by Didion, Helie, Hugoniot, Vallier, and Baills; Italian contributions include Siacci's *Balistica*.
*   **19th and 20th Century:** Bashforth's *The Motion of Projectiles* (1872) and *The Bashforth Chronograph* (1890); Cranz's *Compendium der Ballistik* (1898); *The Official Text-Book of Gunnery* (1902); and Charbonnier's *Balistique* (1905).
*   **Technical Handbooks:** Ingalls' *Exterior and Interior Ballistics, Handbook of Problems in Direct and Indirect Fire*; Bruff's *Ordnance and Gunnery*; and Lissak's *Ordnance and Gunnery* (1907).

## Related Ordnance Concepts
In the broader context of gunnery and ballistics, the British service adopted explosive cordite. This material is named for its appearance as short lengths of cord, created by squeezing a viscous composition through a die. The size of the cordite is designated by the diameter of the hole in hundredths of an inch; for example, cordite size 30 is produced using a hole with a 0.30-inch diameter. 

The combustion of explosive constituents produces gas, the volume, temperature, and pressure of which are assigned an upper limit by thermochemical properties. Further experimental results regarding these properties were published by Sir Andrew Noble in the *Phil. Trans.* from 1905-1906 and subsequent years.
