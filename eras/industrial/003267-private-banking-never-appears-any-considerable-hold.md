# Private Banking in Europe

Private banking has exhibited varying levels of influence and prevalence across different European nations, ranging from minimal hold in Scotland to significant roles in Germany and England.

## Private Banking in Scotland
Private banking never appears to have had any considerable hold in Scotland. While eight private banks existed in 1819, they had all disappeared by 1844. By 1906, the vast majority of the country's banking business was carried out by ten banks of issue, which operated 1,180 branches. Two other small banks had been established more recently.

In contrast to the limited role of private banks, note circulation was a vital component of Scottish banking. For example, the Bank of Dundee operated from 1764, but did not begin receiving money from the public until 1792; for twenty-eight years, its entire business consisted of issuing notes, with values ranging from £23,000 to £56,000. By the time it amalgamated with the Royal Bank of Scotland in 1864, its deposits reached approximately £700,000 and its note circulation was £41,000. Despite the rapid increase in bank deposits after 1792, the practice of hoarding savings in chests in amounts of £10 or £20 persisted for a significant period.

## Private Banking in England and Ireland
In England, the Bank of England maintained a practical, though perhaps not absolutely legal, monopoly on issuing notes in London from its inception. It was gradually surrounded by a circle of private banks, some of which possessed considerable power. By 1906, the banking business in England was conducted primarily by approximately sixty joint-stock banks and ten private banks, though more than one of these was a private firm organized under a joint-stock form.

In Ireland, private banks continued to operate into the early 20th century. In May 1907, there were 12 private banks with an authorized issue of £482,744 and an actual issue of £122,536. The actual circulation of these banks remained far below the authorized amount because their circulation was limited to very small areas. Notes from country banks were almost unknown outside the immediate vicinity of their issuance, and while they were payable in London, they were often difficult to cash.

## Private Banking in Germany and France
In Germany, there are many private banking firms that conduct a considerable amount of business. Beyond these, there are approximately 140 banks operating within the states of the German empire. These industrial and credit banks have had an immense influence on the country's industrial development. Five specific banks hold the right of uncovered note-issue: the Imperial Bank of Germany (Reichsbank), and the banks of Saxony, Bavaria, Wuerttemberg, and Baden. 

A significant portion of German bank resources is employed in industrial concerns, sometimes beyond the empire's boundaries, to obtain higher interest rates than those available from home loans or discounting bills. This practice has been criticized by Dr. R. Koch, president of the Reichsbank, and Caesar Strauss, as it contradicts banking traditions that avoid making large fixed investments of a bank's resources.

In France, outside of six important joint-stock banks, a large number of banks operate primarily in the provinces. In 1898, 182 of these firms belonged to the French Country Bankers' Association, operating in 66 of France's 86 departments. These banks often employ a system of small branches similar to the English method; one such bank possessed 18 offices, including its head office.

## Regulatory Framework and Note Issues
In the United Kingdom, no security was ever required for local issues. While the acts of 1844-1845 required Scottish and Irish banks to hold specie against notes issued beyond the legal limit, this coin was not considered a security. The 1879 legislation provided the only state recognition of the duty to secure note-holders by making note issues a first charge with unlimited liability on the total assets of joint-stock banks that accepted limited liability for other business.

Regarding liability, the privilege of limited liability was exclusive to the Bank of England until the act of 1862. Prior to this, shareholders in joint-stock banks were liable to the full extent of their means. This led to significant hardship during the failures of the City of Glasgow Bank (1878) and the Western Bank of Scotland (1858). The failure of the City of Glasgow Bank prompted the Companies Act of 1879, allowing unlimited companies to adopt limited liability. However, shareholders remain liable as if the company were unlimited if the company issues bank notes.
