# Committee on Banks of Issue, House of Commons, 1840

The Committee on Banks of Issue was a parliamentary body of the House of Commons that conducted investigations into the banking systems and the issuance of notes. The committee's activities during the early 1840s provided significant data regarding the financial operations of the era, particularly concerning the movement of funds through clearing houses.

## Reports and Documentation
The House of Commons established the Select Committee on Banks of Issue in 1840. The committee's findings were subsequently detailed in the First and Second Reports published in 1841. These documents serve as official parliamentary records and are listed among the authorities for the study of banking history, alongside other related inquiries such as the 1832 Committee of Secrecy on the Bank of England Charter and the 1875 Report of the Select Committee on Banks of Issue.

## Clearing House Data
The Second Report of the Committee on Banks of Issue (1841) is noted for containing the earliest authentic statement regarding the clearing of cheques. The appendix to this report provided specific figures for the London clearings, illustrating the volume of turnover and the number of participating banks during that period.

According to the committee's data, the figures for London clearings were as follows:
*   **1839:** L954,401,600 involving 29 banks.
*   **1840:** L978,496,800 involving 29 banks.

These figures provide a baseline for understanding the vast amount of yearly turnover in cheques, which is estimated to be at least twice as great as the figures registered by the London Clearing House alone when accounting for exchanges in large towns across Scotland, Ireland, and England, as well as settlements between bank branches and customers.

## Context of Bank Issues
The work of the 1840 committee preceded further legislative changes regarding the issuance of notes. Until 1844, the majority of provincial banks in Wales and England functioned as banks of issue. However, the Act of 1844 restricted their power to issue notes. 

Later testimony provided to a 1875 House of Commons banking inquiry by Sir R. H. Inglis Palgrave indicated that the 1844 restrictions left these banks with a property in their issues that they could utilize but were unable to sell. Subsequent statistics from the 1875 committee report showed that the proportion of notes in circulation relative to banking deposits had diminished greatly in the years following 1844 across various agricultural, industrial, and commercial districts.
