# Cheddar Cheese Production and Characteristics

Cheddar cheese is described as a cosmopolitan variety of cheese characterized by a firm touch and a flaky texture that is neither hard nor crumbly. It possesses a distinct "nutty" aroma and flavour, and it is designed to melt in the mouth without tasting sweet or acid. While some Cheddar cheeses are "plain," maintaining the natural paleness of the curd, many are coloured with annatto. A typical Cheddar cheese has an average weight of approximately 70 lb. If the development of acid during production is not excessive, the cheese is long-keeping and early-ripening.

## Production in Ayrshire
In the county of Ayrshire, dairying has received significant attention, supported by the Ayrshire breed of cows, which are noted for the excellence and quantity of their milk. Historically, the region produced Dunlop cheese; however, this product was unequal in character and lacked commercial value in English markets. To remedy this, the Ayrshire Agricultural Association brought a Somerset farmer and his wife to the region in 1855 to teach the Cheddar method. This effort was successful, and first-rate quality Cheddar cheese is now produced in Ayrshire. The annual cheese show at Kilmarnock has become the most important such event in Scotland.

The methods of production in Ayrshire have evolved over time. Previously, the county utilized the Joseph Harding or English Cheddar system. This "old method" involved adding sour whey to the milk daily before adding rennet, which sometimes resulted in spoiled cheese if the milk was already sufficiently acid. Furthermore, the old method involved heating the curd by dipping whey into a can, heating it to 140 deg. F., and returning it to the curd—a process described as laborious and unsatisfactory. Cheese made via this old method also lacked uniformity and failed to "toast," as the butter-fat and casein would separate.

In 1885, the Ayrshire Dairy Association engaged Mr. R. J. Drummond to teach the Canadian system of cheese-making. This system differed from the English method in several details, particularly in how acidity was produced and the use of a "curd-cooler" with a sparred bottom to allow whey to separate from the curd during ripening, rather than letting the curd mature in the bottom of a tub.

## Scientific Principles and Acidity
The production of first-rate Cheddar cheese depends heavily on the domain of ferments. It is established that lactic acid bacilli are essential for the creation of high-quality cheese. A "starter"—consisting of buttermilk, whey, or sour milk in an advanced stage of fermentation—is added to accelerate the development of acidity. Professor J. R. Campbell's experiments indicate that first-class Cheddar can be made using pure cultures of a lactic organism, and that whey starters produce results equal to milk-starters.

To manage acidity more accurately than the traditional "hot-iron test," some makers use an acidimeter. This chemical method employs a standard solution of alkali (soda) and an indicator called phenolphthalein. The acidimeter helps the cheese-maker determine exactly when to grind the curd and when to draw off the whey. For optimal results, the acidity of the liquid from the press should be kept near 1.00%, and should not fall below 0.80% or rise above 1.20%.

## Global Production and Yields
The factory system of cheese-making, prevalent in Australasia, Canada, and the United States, produces cheese of the Cheddar type. In the United States, production grew from 100,000,000 lb. in the mid-19th century (all made in farm dairies) to approximately 300,000,000 lb. at the start of the 20th century, with 96% to 97% produced in factories. New York and Wisconsin are the primary producers, together accounting for three-fourths of the U.S. output. More than 90% of U.S. cheese is a standard type copied after the English Cheddar.

Regarding yield, data from the Bath and West of England Society for 1899 and 1900 shows that 53,028 gallons of milk yielded 50,168 lb. of saleable cheese. This equates to approximately 1.05 gallons of milk per 1 lb. of cheese, or roughly 10 lb. of milk per 1 lb. of pressed cheese.

## Ripening and Transformation
The ripening of cheese involves a physical transformation of the casein, where the firm elastic curd becomes plastic and insoluble casein is converted into soluble decomposition products. Two primary theories explain this: a European theory attributing the change to living bacteria (specifically liquefying bacteria or lactic-acid type bacteria), and a United States theory attributing it to digestive enzymes inherent in the milk.
