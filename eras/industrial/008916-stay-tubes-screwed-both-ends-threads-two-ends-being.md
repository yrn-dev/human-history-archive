# Stay Tubes

Stay tubes are specialized components used to strengthen the tube plates of boilers. They are characterized by being screwed at both ends, with continuous threads that allow them to be screwed into both tube plates. In some instances, nuts are fitted to the front ends of these tubes. Once installed, the stay tubes are expanded into the plates and subsequently beaded over.

## Boiler Applications and Specifications

In various boiler designs, stay tubes serve as critical structural reinforcements for tube plates. The dimensions and specifications of the tubes often depend on the type of draught employed:

*   **Natural or Chimney Draught:** Tubes generally have an outside diameter of 3 or 3-1/4 inches and are rarely longer than 7 feet.
*   **Forced Draught:** Tubes are typically 2-1/2 inches in diameter and range from 8 to 8-1/2 feet in length.

Regardless of the tube size, a clear space of 1-1/4 inches is almost always maintained between the tubes.

## Related Boiler Staying Mechanisms

Stay tubes are part of a broader system of stays used to maintain the integrity of boiler shells and chambers:

*   **Combustion Chambers:** The backs and flat sides of combustion chambers are stayed to each other or to the boiler shell using numerous screw stays. These are screwed through the two connected plates and are nearly always fitted with nuts inside the combustion chambers. The tops of these chambers are typically stayed by strong girders resting on the chamber back plates and tube plates, though some use vertical stays attached to riveted T bars or heavy transverse girder plates.
*   **End Plates:** In the steam space and below combustion chambers, end plates are stayed by longitudinal stays that pass through the entire length of the boiler and are secured with double nuts at each end.
*   **Locomotive Boilers:** These boilers utilize a cylindrical barrel and a nearly rectangular fire-box. The fire-box sides are stayed to the shell by stays approximately 1 inch in diameter, usually pitched 4 inches apart both horizontally and vertically. Depending on the boiler size, the top of the fire-box is supported either by longitudinal girder stays (supported by the opposite fire-box plate and tube plate, and sometimes by slings from the boiler crown) or by vertical stays connecting the flat fire-box crown to the shell crown.

## Materials Used in Boiler Construction

The materials for plates and stays vary based on the boiler type and the required strength:

*   **General Land and Marine Boilers:** Plates and stays are almost always made of mild steel. Shell plates and long stays typically use steel with a tensile strength of 28 to 32 tons per sq. in. Short-screwed stays, furnaces, flues, and plates requiring flanging use a softer steel with a strength of 26 to 30 tons per sq. in.
*   **Locomotive Boilers:** Shells are generally mild steel. The fire-box plates are made of copper (though steel is used in America), and the fire-box side stays are composed of special bronze or copper. Other stays are made of steel.
*   **Tubes:** Ordinary land and marine boiler tubes are usually lap-welded wrought iron. Naval water-tube boilers invariably use seamless steel. Locomotive tubes are typically brass (composed of 70% copper and 30% zinc), though steel or copper is occasionally used. Water tubes in these systems are made of seamless steel.

## Alternative Tube Connections

While stay tubes use continuous threads and beading, other boiler designs employ different connection methods. In the Reed boiler, for example, tubes are not expanded into the tube plate. Instead, they are fitted at each end with specially designed nut and screw connections, which allows them to be replaced or removed quickly.
