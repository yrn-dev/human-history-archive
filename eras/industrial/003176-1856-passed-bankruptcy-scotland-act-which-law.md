# Bankruptcy Law in Scotland

The law of bankruptcy in Scotland originated as a legal remedy to address the frauds of insolvent debtors. Early legislation, such as an act of the Scottish parliament in 1621, established that debtors could not fraudulently diminish funds belonging to creditors after insolvency; specifically, any deed of assignment executed gratuitously in favor of a confidential friend or near relation after contracting debt was presumed to be fraudulent dealing.

## The Concept of Notour Bankruptcy
The definition of a "notour" (or notorious) bankrupt was settled by the act of 1696. Under this statute, a notour bankrupt was defined as any debtor who, while under diligence by caption or horning at the instance of creditors, either fled or absconded for personal security, defended their person by force, retired to an abbey or other privileged place, or was imprisoned, and was subsequently found insolvent by a sentence of the lords of session. While initially intended to remedy fraudulent preference rather than serve as a general distribution process, later statutes made this definition a necessary requisite for such processes. The 1696 act also rendered null and void all assignations, voluntary dispositions, and other deeds made within sixty days before bankruptcy or after it occurred.

## The Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act 1856
The Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act 1856 serves as the primary regulation for bankruptcy law in Scotland. This act established that notour bankruptcy is constituted through two primary means:
1. Sequestration.
2. Insolvency occurring in conjunction with the sale of the debtor's effects under a sequestration for rent or poinding, a duly executed charge for payment, or an application for the benefit of *cessio bonorum*.

Notour bankruptcy persists until insolvency ceases in the latter cases, or until the debtor obtains a discharge in cases of sequestration.

## Sequestration Procedures
Sequestration may be awarded by the sheriff or the court of session. It applies to living debtors subject to the jurisdiction of Scottish courts—either via their own petition with the concurrence of qualified creditors, or via a petition from qualified creditors provided the debtor has had a place of business or dwelling-house in Scotland within the previous year and is a notour bankrupt. In the case of deceased debtors subject to the court's jurisdiction at death, sequestration may be petitioned for by qualified creditors or the debtor's mandatory.

A sequestration can be recalled if a majority in number and four-fifths in value of the creditors prefer to wind up the estate through private arrangement. If the process continues, creditors meet to elect a trustee to administer the estate (by a majority in value) and three commissioners to control the administration and declare dividends. The bankrupt is required to provide all available information regarding their estate and undergo a public examination on oath before the sheriff under pain of imprisonment. "Conjunct and confident persons" may also be examined.

## Discharge and Amendments
A bankrupt may be discharged without composition through several paths:
* By petition with the concurrence of all creditors.
* After six months with the concurrence of a majority and four-fifths in value of creditors.
* After eighteen months with a bare majority in number and value.
* After two years without concurrence, though a judge may refuse this if the bankrupt wilfully failed to comply with the law or fraudulently concealed effects. Discharge may also be obtained by composition.

The 1856 Act was modified by several subsequent laws:
* **Bankruptcy and Real Securities Act 1857:** Addressed the cost of competition for trusteeships.
* **Bankruptcy Amendment (Scotland) Act 1860:** Allowed the court to recall sequestration if winding up the estate in Ireland or England was more convenient.
* **Bankruptcy Amendment Act (Scotland) 1875:** Established preferential claims for wages of servants, shopmen, and clerks (up to L50 for a period not exceeding four months) and workmen (for a period not exceeding two months).
* **Debtors (Scotland) Act 1880:** Abolished imprisonment for debt while introducing punishments for fraudulent bankrupts.

## Later Legislation and Administration
The Bankruptcy Frauds and Disabilities (Scotland) Act 1884 aligned Scottish law with the English Bankruptcy Act 1883 regarding the disqualifications of bankrupts and the criminal provisions for undischarged bankrupts obtaining credit of L20 or more without disclosing their position. 

The Judicial Factors Act of 1889 sought to limit excessive administration costs by requiring that the accountant of court be notified of the rate of remuneration for a trustee before it is acted upon; the accountant is empowered to modify this rate, subject to appeal. This 1889 Act also abolished the office of accountant in bankruptcy (established in 1856), merging its duties into the office of accountant of the court of session.

Unlike the law in England and Ireland, the Deeds of Arrangement Act 1887 does not apply to Scotland, and Scotland has no analogous requirement for the registration of private deeds of assignment for creditors as a condition of validity. Additionally, Scottish law does not recognize the English distinction between non-traders and traders.
