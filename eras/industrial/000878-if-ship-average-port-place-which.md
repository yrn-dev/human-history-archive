# Maritime Average and Port Expenses

In maritime commerce, the term "average" refers to damages or expenses that result from accidents of navigation. Under modern law, this is categorized into two distinct types: particular average and general average.

## General and Particular Average
Particular average refers to a partial loss or damage happening to the freight, goods, or ship due to an unavoidable or fortuitous accident. This loss is borne by the insurers or the parties to whose property the misfortune occurred.

General average, conversely, occurs when expenditures are incurred or sacrifices are made to preserve the cargo, ship, and freight from the effects or perils of the sea. This implies a rateable contribution from all concerned parties, based on the values of their respective interests, to compensate for the loss. While the term "average" originally referred only to what is now called general average, the term "particular average" was later adopted for convenience to distinguish losses for which no general contribution could be claimed.

## Expenses at Ports of Refuge
Under Rule X, when a ship returns to its place of loading or enters a port or place of refuge due to sacrifice, accident, or other extraordinary circumstances necessary for common safety, the expenses of entering that port are admitted as general average (G.A.). If the ship subsequently sails with some or all of its original cargo, the corresponding expenses of leaving the port are also admitted as G.A.

The treatment of subsequent expenses varies by legal jurisdiction and circumstance:
*   **English Law:** Generally, expenses incurred after the ship and cargo have reached safety at a port of refuge are not treated as G.A., even if the entry into port was a G.A. act. If the entry was necessitated by a G.A. sacrifice (such as cutting away masts), the expenses for repairing that damage, as well as the incidental costs of storing, unloading, and reloading cargo, are treated as consequences of the original sacrifice and are subject to contribution. However, if the entry was to avoid a storm or hostile cruiser, or to repair accidental damage, the G.A. sacrifice is considered ended once physical safety is reached. In such cases, further expenditure is viewed as the shipowner's contractual obligation to complete the voyage.
*   **Cargo Protection:** Under English practice, expenditures incurred to protect cargo, such as warehousing, are treated as charges to be borne by the cargo for whose benefit the expense was incurred.
*   **Unloading:** While English law is strict, the practice of English average adjusters has modified this by treating the expense of unloading as G.A., though the legal support for this practice is questioned.

## Cargo Handling and Transhipment
The cost of discharging cargo is admitted as G.A. if it was necessary for the common safety or to allow for repairs to damage caused by accident or sacrifice during the voyage, provided those repairs were necessary for the safe prosecution of the voyage. When discharging costs are admissible as G.A., the costs of storing and reloading that cargo on the ship, including storage charges, are also admitted. However, if the voyage is abandoned or the ship is condemned, no storage expenses incurred after the date of abandonment or condemnation are admitted as G.A.

If a ship under average is in a place where it is practicable to repair her so she can carry the whole cargo, but is instead towed to another place of repair or destination to save expenses—or if the cargo is forwarded or transhipped by another ship to save expenses—the extra costs of forwarding, transhipment, or towage are payable by the parties to the adventure. This payment is proportional to the extraordinary expense saved, up to the amount of that saving.

## Crew Wages and Maintenance
Rule XI provides that the cost of maintenance and wages for the officers, master, and crew are allowed in G.A. during the detention of a ship for the purpose of repairs mentioned in Rule X or under similar circumstances. This rule is considered a significant concession to the shipowner and aligns with practices in foreign countries. 

Under common law and English law, it is questionable whether these wages and maintenance are allowed at a port of refuge; they are clearly not allowed where the detention is to repair accidental damage. Furthermore, these rules do not provide equal protection to shippers. For example, a shipper of cattle cannot claim G.A. for the extra fodder consumed by the cattle or the extra provisions and wages for cattlemen on board during a stay at a repairing port.

## Other General Average Admissions
General average also admits the following:
*   **Lightening:** When a ship is ashore, the loss or damage sustained and the extra costs of reshipping, lighter hire, and lightening incurred to float the ship by discharging ship's stores, bunker coals, or cargo are admitted as G.A.
*   **Fuel:** Cargo, stores, and ship's materials necessarily burnt for fuel for common safety during a time of peril are admitted as G.A., provided an ample supply of fuel had been provided. In these cases, the shipowner is charged for the estimated quantity of coals that would have been consumed (calculated at the price current at the last port of departure) and credited to the G.A.
