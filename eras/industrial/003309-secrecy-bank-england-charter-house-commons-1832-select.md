# Secrecy on Bank of England Charter, House of Commons, 1832

## Overview of Parliamentary Inquiries
The House of Commons has conducted various inquiries into banking and financial matters through the use of select and secret committees. Among the parliamentary papers documenting these activities are the reports from the Committee of Secrecy on Bank of England Charter, which took place in 1832. This committee is part of a broader history of official investigations into the British banking system, which include reports from the Select Committee on the High Price of Gold Bullion in 1810, and subsequent inquiries into banks of issue in 1840, 1841, and 1875.

## Context of Financial Regulation
The 1832 Committee of Secrecy on the Bank of England Charter existed within a period of evolving banking legislation. Later regulatory efforts included the Bank Charter Act of 1844, which restricted the power of provincial banks in England and Wales to issue notes. Under the 1844 Act, the Bank of England became the sole source for obtaining legal tender notes. This granted the institution significant power, particularly during times of financial pressure when the government might grant a "treasury letter" to allow the note issue to exceed the limits fixed by the act.

## Banking Inquiries and Evidence
Official inquiries have frequently relied on evidence from banking officials and experts. For example, the 1857 and 1858 reports from the Select Committee on the Bank Acts featured evidence from the governor, Mr. Sheffield Neave, and the deputy-governor, Mr. Bonamy Dobree. Their testimony described a period of "wildest alarm" where the bank sold £3,000,000 in government securities at a loss and exchequer bills were barely saleable. Similarly, the 1875 banking inquiry included evidence from Sir R. H. Inglis Palgrave regarding the property rights of provincial banks over their note issues.
