# Experimental Measurement of Pressure in the Bore of a Gun

The measurement of pressure within the bore of a gun is a central component of interior ballistics. This branch of science investigates the dynamics of the movement of a shot up the bore, the work realized by the expansion of powder, the stress placed upon the material of the gun, and the relations between the temperature, volume, and pressure of the powder-gas.

## The Crusher-Gauge and Associated Methods
To experimentally measure pressure in the bore of a gun, the crusher-gauge is employed. This device records maximum pressure through the compression of an internal copper cylinder. Depending on the requirements of the experiment, the gauge may be fastened in the base of the shot or placed within the powder-chamber.

In the researches conducted by Sir Andrew Noble, crusher-gauges were utilized in conjunction with chronographic appliances. These appliances were carried by plugs inserted into the side of the experimental gun, reaching the bore, and registered the passage of the shot. This method allowed for the inference of energy and velocity, providing an independent control for the records produced by the crusher-gauge.

## Preliminary and Comparative Testing
As a preliminary step to determining bore pressure, it is considered desirable to measure the pressure created by exploding a charge of powder within a closed vessel. By varying the weight of the charge, researchers can vary the density of the powder-gas. This method was pioneered in 1743 by Benjamin Robins and in 1792 by Count Rumford. 

This approach was later revived by Dr. Kellner, a chemist for the War Department. Kellner used steel spheres from bicycle ball-bearings as safety-valves. These were loaded to register the specific pressure at which the powder-gas would blow off, serving as a check for the indications provided by the crusher-gauge.

## Historical Applications and the "Soda Water Bottle"
Experimental determinations of pressure at various points along the bore were also conducted through tests by Colonel Bomford. These tests involved boring holes into the walls of the gun, allowing the pressure to act upon bodies such as pistons or pistol balls.

These experimental results found practical application in the work of Admiral John Adolf Dahlgren of the U.S. Navy. Dahlgren invented a smooth bore gun, colloquially known as the "soda water bottle" due to its shape. These guns were first mounted on a vessel named the "Experiment," which cruised under Dahlgren's command between 1857 and 1859, and were subsequently used during the Civil War.

## Pressure Analysis and Ballistic Calculations
In interior ballistics, a gun is viewed as a thermodynamic machine or heat-engine that performs its work in a single stroke. The mean effective pressure (M.E.P.) can be calculated by multiplying the cross-section of the bore by the M.E.P. and then by the length of the shot's travel up the bore, which equals the work realized in inch-tons. 

The maximum pressure in the bore can significantly exceed the mean pressure, sometimes in a ratio of 3 to 1 or 2 to 1. For example, in experiments by Sir Andrew Noble using a 6-inch gun capable of being lengthened to 50 feet (100 calibres), the M.E.P. was approximately 6.4 tons per square inch.

The determination of the size of the grain and the time of burning under varying density and pressure is of great practical importance. This data allows designers to estimate the limits of maximum pressure in the bore and design the chamber so that the acceleration and weight of the shot are suitable for the charge's G.D. While empirical formulas based on experience provide approximations, the area of a pressure-curve—in the absence of an observable law—must be calculated using Simpson's rule or read via a planimeter.

## Evolution of Propellants
The field of interior ballistics has undergone significant change with the abandonment of black gunpowder, which was used in artillery for six hundred years. It has been replaced by modern explosives that are nearly smokeless and capable of producing a much larger volume of gas at higher temperatures and pressures—more than threefold the previous capacity. This advancement allows for a proportional reduction in the size of the charge.
