# L. W. K.

The designations L, W, and K appear across various technical, mathematical, and linguistic contexts within the archive, primarily relating to structural engineering, mechanical instrumentation, accounting systems, and ancient alphabets.

## Structural Engineering and Bridge Design
In the study of bridge construction, specifically regarding the weight and span of girders, these variables are used to determine load capacities and material requirements.

### Girder Weight and Load Formulas
The weight of main girders ($W_g$) is calculated based on the total live load ($W_l$) and the total flooring load ($W_f$). According to the archive, the weight of main girders designed to carry these loads—excluding their own weight—is expressed as $k(W_l+W_f)$. The total weight is then derived from the formula $W_g = (W_l+W_f)k/(1-k)$. In a provisional design where the weight of the girder is neglected, the actual bridge members must be increased in section by the ratio $k/(1-k)$.

A partially rational approximate formula for the weight of main girders per foot run ($w_3$) is given as:
$w_3 = (w_1+w_2)lr/(K-lr)$
In this formula:
*   $w_1$ and $w_2$ represent the total live load and the weight of the platform per foot run (in tons).
*   $l$ is the span in feet.
*   $r$ is the ratio of the span to the depth of the girder at the center ($r = l/d$).
*   $K$ is a constant deduced from previously designed bridges with the same working stresses.

### Material Constants and Limiting Spans
The value of $K$ varies depending on the material used for the bridge. For iron bridges, $K$ ranges from 6000 to 7200, while for steel bridges, it ranges from 7200 to 9000. 

There is a "limiting span" beyond which dead load stresses exceed the assigned limit of working stress. This occurs when $k-lr = 0$, or $l = K/r$. For steel girders, using a ratio ($r$) of 12, the limiting span ($l$) is 600 to 750 feet. At a ratio of 10, the span is 720 to 900 feet, and at a ratio of 8, it is 900 to 1120 feet.

## Linguistics and Paleography
The letters K and L appear in the study of the Umbrian and Latin alphabets. The normal Umbrian alphabet includes the signs $K$ and $k$. In the latest form of the Umbrian alphabet, an "angular and undivided form of $k$" is noted as a characteristic feature. 

In the Latin alphabet, the sound represented by $k$ in Umbrian is represented by $C$. The archive also notes the use of $L$ as a sign in the Umbrian alphabet.
