# Bolivian Departmental and National Finance (1902–1905)

## Departmental Revenues and Expenditures
In 1903, the estimated departmental revenues in Bolivia were 2,296,172 bolivianos, while expenditures for the same period were estimated at 2,295,791 bolivianos. These departmental funds were primarily spent on public instruction, the Church, police, justice, and public works. The revenue for these departments was derived from a variety of sources, including land taxes, excise taxes, tithes, tolls, stamp taxes, inheritance taxes, mining grants, and subsidies provided by the national treasury, along with other small taxes.

Municipal finances showed different figures for 1902, where revenues aggregated to 2,317,670 bolivianos. However, expenditures for that year exceeded the revenue by 61,510 bolivianos. Municipal revenue sources included ground rents, leases, a lighting tax, cemetery fees, market and consumption taxes, licences, tolls, taxes on skins and hides, and various minor and personal taxes. Trade was further impacted by a multiplication of taxes that functioned similarly to the old colonial *alcabala* tax, serving to increase the cost of goods and restrict commerce.

## National Finance and Taxation
National financial information is limited because itemized returns of expenditures and receipts are not published; information is instead drawn from estimates presented to congress by cabinet ministers. Taxation is not regarded as heavy, and expenditures are not considered large. 

For the years 1904 and 1905, estimated revenues and expenditures (calculated at 21 pence per boliviano) were as follows:
*   **1904:** Revenue of L632,773:15s. and expenditure of L748,571:10s.
*   **1905:** Revenue of L693,763:17:6 and expenditure of L828,937:19:9.

National revenues are principally derived from export duties on coca and rubber, and on minerals (excluding silver). Other sources include general and mining taxes, excise taxes on sugar, tobacco, wines, and spirits, duties and fees on imports, taxes on the profits of stock companies, postal and telegraph revenues, and fees for patents and licences. 

While Bolivia is sometimes referred to as a "free-trade country" due to moderate nominal import duties, this is considered a misnomer. In addition to *ad valorem* import rates of 10% to 40%, the system includes a 1-1/2% consular fee for the registration of invoices over 200 bolivianos, a consumption tax of 10 centavos per quintal (46 kilogrammes), fees for viseing certificates for merchandise in transit, and "octroi" taxes on monopoly-controlled merchandise such as tobacco and spirits. Additionally, municipalities and departments levy their own consumption and import taxes.

## National Expenditures and Appropriations
National expenditures are primarily allocated to interest on the public debt, military expenses, justice, public instruction, official salaries, church and mission support, public works, and subsidies. 

The appropriations for 1905 were distributed as follows:
*   **War:** 2,081,119 bolivianos
*   **Government and fomento:** 2,021,428 bolivianos
*   **Justice and public instruction:** 1,878,941 bolivianos
*   **Finance and industry:** 1,462,259 bolivianos

## Public Debt and Currency
Bolivia's acknowledged public debt is relatively small. Following the war with Chile, the country held an indemnity debt to Chilean citizens totaling 6,550,830 bolivianos; this was Bolivia's only foreign debt and was nearly liquidated in 1904 when Chile assumed the unpaid balance. By 1905, the internal debt reached 6,243,270 bolivianos (L546,286), which included 1,998,500 bolivianos in treasury bills. In 1903, the government authorized the issue of one million bolivianos (L87,500) in treasury notes for the National Territory and the department of Beni, with 10% of customs receipts from those two districts set aside for redemption.

The republic's paper currency is issued by four private banks and is not part of the public debt. As of June 30, 1903, the official estimate of currency in circulation was 9,144,254 bolivianos (L800,122), issued on par with silver. 

The national coinage consists of copper, nickel, and silver. Copper coins are available in 1 and 2 centavos, and nickel coins in 5 and 10 centavos. Silver coins—issued from the Potosi mint at a rate of approximately 1,500,000 bolivianos per year—come in denominations of 1 boliviano (100 centavos), 50, 20, 10, and 5 centavos. By law, silver mining companies must send 20% of their product to the mint. Because paper currency is cheaper, silver bolivianos are rarely seen in circulation. To prevent the exportation of silver coins, fractional denominations have been slightly debased.
