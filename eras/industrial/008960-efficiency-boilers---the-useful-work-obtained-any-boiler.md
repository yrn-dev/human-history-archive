# Efficiency of Boilers

A boiler is a vessel designed to heat water or other liquids to the boiling point. Specifically, it serves as the apparatus for producing steam, acting as a step in the process of converting the potential energy of fuel, such as coal, into mechanical work via a steam engine. To function, every boiler must possess a furnace for burning fuel, a receptacle strong enough to safely contain water and steam at the highest intended pressure, and a sufficient heating surface to transmit heat from combustion to the water.

## Principles of Evaporative Efficiency

The efficiency of a boiler is defined as the amount of steam produced in proportion to the amount of fuel consumed. Achieving high efficiency requires different conditions than those required for a plant where a large output of steam is more important than fuel economy. High efficiency depends on combining the completeness of fuel combustion with a heating surface sufficient to absorb enough heat to reduce the temperature of funnel gases to nearly that of the steam.

Completeness of combustion is achieved by providing sufficient time and opportunity for furnace gases and air to mix before the temperature drops below a critical point. This requires admitting more air to the fire than is theoretically necessary for oxidation. While it is generally considered that the required excess air is nearly equal to the theoretical amount, experience suggests that less is needed if proper means are provided for early complete mixture. The methods for achieving this mixture vary by fuel; for example, the means used for Welsh coal are unsuitable for Scottish or North Country coal.

There is a balance between air intake and heat transmission. Because excess air must be raised to the temperature of the burnt gases, too much air lowers the temperature in the flues and fire, which lessens heat transmission and carries heat away through the chimney. Consequently, reducing the amount of air can increase efficiency by increasing fire temperature and reducing chimney waste, even if it results in slight incompleteness of combustion.

## Classes and Designs of Boilers

Boilers are generally divided into two distinct classes, though some types combine properties of both.

### Tank Boilers
Tank boilers contain relatively large quantities of water and typically feature a wholly or partially cylindrical outer shell to provide strength without requiring many stays. These are generally internally fired, with furnace plates surrounded by water to form the most efficient heating surfaces. After leaving the furnace, combustion products pass through a chamber and then through flues or small tubes to transmit heat to the water. 

Common stationary forms include the Lancashire boiler and its modification, the Galloway boiler. These typically range from 26 to 30 feet in length with diameters between 6-1/2 and 8 feet. They utilize cylindrical furnace flues and may employ "Galloway tubes"—conical tubes that increase the heating surface, stiffen the flue, promote water circulation, and improve evaporative efficiency by mixing flue gases. Working pressures vary by engine type: older boilers operate at about 60 lb. per sq. in., compound engines at 100 to 120 lb., and triple expansion engines at 150 to 170 lb., with some constructed for 200 lb. per sq. in.

### Water-Tube Boilers
In water-tube boilers, water is mainly contained in numerous small tubes, which form the major portion of the heating surface. The fire is usually placed under these tubes, and both are enclosed in a fire-resisting casing of brickwork or other substances. In some designs, the fire is entirely surrounded by water-tubes. Unlike tank boilers, water-tube designs require special provisions to maintain water circulation through the tubes.

A prominent example is the Yarrow boiler, used in various navies. It consists of two lower water-chambers and a large cylindrical steam chest connected by straight tubes arranged in equilateral triangles. In large vessels, tubes have an external diameter of 1-3/4 inches. "Express boilers," used in cruisers and torpedo-boat destroyers to maximize power relative to weight, use smaller diameter tubes and a high rate of combustion achieved through "forced draught."

## Maintenance and Operational Factors

The efficiency of a boiler can be compromised by the impurities in feed-water, such as magnesia or lime salts. These impurities concentrate as water evaporates, forming mud or scale on heating surfaces. Scale obstructs heat transmission, reducing efficiency, and can cause plates to overheat and weaken. To combat this, some boilers are designed for easy cleaning (such as the Cornish, Galloway, and Lancashire types), while others use "softeners" to precipitate impurities before the water is pumped into the boiler.

Operational accessories are essential for maintaining safety and efficiency. The water level must be kept above the highest part of the heating surfaces, monitored via glass water-gauges and try-cocks. Feeding is managed by pumps or injectors, with a check-valve regulating the admission of water.

In certain specialized systems, such as the Meldrum "Simplex" destructor, fuel efficiency is enhanced by utilizing the heat of gases after they pass the boilers to heat the incoming air to approximately 300° F via an air heater or continuous regenerator. High fuel efficiency in these systems is indicated by a high percentage (15 to 18%) of $\text{CO}_2$ in the furnaces.
