# George Boole

George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician known for his contributions to symbolic reasoning and the mathematical basis of logic. Born in Lincoln on November 2, 1815, he was the son of a tradesman who, despite limited means, possessed an active mind and a studious character. Boole's father provided his first lessons in mathematical science, although Boole's own extraordinary mathematical abilities did not manifest early in his life; his initial favorite subject was classics.

## Early Life and Professional Career
Boole began studying higher mathematics at the age of seventeen, though his progress was hindered by a lack of efficient help. He entered the scholastic profession early, becoming an assistant-master at a private school in Doncaster around the age of sixteen. He remained in the teaching profession throughout his life. His career was marked by few major events, the most notable being the establishment of a school at Lincoln, the subsequent removal of that school to Waddington, and his 1849 appointment as the professor of mathematics at Queen's College in Cork. In 1855, he married Miss Mary Everest, who later authored several educational works based on her husband's principles.

## Mathematical and Logical Works
To the general public, Boole was recognized as the author of several standard publications and numerous abstruse mathematical papers. His published output consisted of approximately fifty scattered articles and a few separate publications. His first paper, "Theory of Analytical Transformations," appeared in the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* in 1839, initiating a friendship with the journal's editor, D.F. Gregory, which lasted until Gregory's death in 1844. Boole contributed twenty-two articles to the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* and its successor, the *Cambridge and Dublin Mathematical Journal*, and sixteen papers to the *Philosophical Magazine*. He also published six important memoirs in the *Philosophical Transactions* of the Royal Society, as well as works in the *Transactions* of the Royal Irish Academy, the *Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh*, *Crelle's Journal*, and the *Bulletin de l'Academie de St-Petersbourg* (where he appeared under the name G. Boldt in 1862). Additionally, he published a paper on the mathematical basis of logic in the *Mechanic's Magazine* in 1848.

During his lifetime, Boole completed two systematic treatises: the *Treatise on Differential Equations* (1859) and the *Treatise on the Calculus of Finite Differences* (1860). These works are noted for combining elementary exposition with profound philosophical investigation. In the *Differential Equations* treatise, Boole provided a lucid account of a general symbolic method and a general method in analysis, the latter of which had been described in his 1844 memoir.

## Contributions to Logic
Boole was one of the first English mathematicians since John Wallis to write on logic, following Augustus de Morgan. His approach was characterized by a profound confidence in symbolic reasoning. He first presented his ideas in the 1847 pamphlet *Mathematical Analysis of Logic*, though he later considered this an imperfect and hasty exposition. He regarded his 1854 work, *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, on which are founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities*, as the mature statement of his views.

Boole did not view logic as a branch of mathematics but identified a deep analogy between algebraic symbols and those representing logical forms and syllogisms. He proposed that logic could be viewed as mathematics restricted to the quantities 0 and 1, where unity (1) denoted the universe of thinkable objects. He used literal symbols (such as x, y, z) to represent elective meanings, similar to adjectives or substantives. For example, if x represents "horned" and y represents "sheep," the combination of x and y performed on unity represents "horned sheep." He demonstrated that these elective symbols follow the same primary laws of combination as algebraical symbols, allowing them to be added, subtracted, multiplied, and divided.

In the *Laws of Thought*, Boole detailed a general symbolic method of logical inference, showing how to draw conclusions logically contained within premises through purely symbolic treatment. The second part of this work attempted to discover a general method in probabilities to determine the probability of an event logically connected to a system of given events.

## Personal Character and Interests
Boole was characterized by the modesty of true genius and a single-minded pursuit of truth, which inspired the deepest esteem among his friends. Despite receiving an honorary LL.D. from the University of Dublin and a medal from the Royal Society for his 1844 memoir, he did not seek or receive the ordinary rewards typically associated with his discoveries.

Beyond his professional work, Boole possessed a wide and deep knowledge of general literature. He was a student of the ethics of Spinoza, the metaphysics of Aristotle, and the philosophical works of Cicero. His favorite poet was Dante, specifically preferring the *Paradiso* over the *Inferno*. He also wrote and delivered four addresses on various topics: *The Claims of Science*, *The Right Use of Leisure*, *The Social Aspect of Intellectual Culture*, and *The Genius of Sir Isaac Newton*.

## Death
George Boole died on December 8, 1864, while still in the full vigour of his intellectual powers. His death was caused by an attack of fever that resulted in suffusion on the lungs.
