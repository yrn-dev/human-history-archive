# Charles Bonnet

Charles Bonnet was a Swiss philosopher and naturalist whose life was characterized by a lack of public eventfulness. He spent the majority of his life in Switzerland and did not engage in public affairs, with the exception of a period between 1752 and 1768 during which he served as a member of the council of the republic. He spent the final twenty-five years of his life residing quietly in the country at Genthod, near Geneva. He was married to a woman from the De la Rive family; although the couple had no children, they raised Madame Bonnet's nephew, the celebrated H.B. de Saussure, as their son. Bonnet died on May 20, 1793, following a long and painful illness.

## Philosophical System

Bonnet's philosophical system posits that man is composed of two distinct substances: a material body and an immaterial mind. He taught that all knowledge begins with sensations, which are the result of vibrations in the nerves triggered by external physical stimuli. According to Bonnet, a nerve that has been set in motion by a specific object tends to reproduce that motion; consequently, when the nerve receives a second impression from the same object, it vibrates with less resistance. This increased flexibility in the nerve is what Bonnet identifies as the condition of memory.

Abstract ideas are formed when reflection—the active element of the mind—is used to acquire and combine these sensations. In this system, pleasure and pain are the drivers of mental activity, and the ultimate end of human existence is happiness.

## Metaphysical Theories

Bonnet's metaphysics are based on two principles derived from Leibnitz:
1. The universe was completed by a single original act of the divine will, rather than through successive acts of creation, and it subsequently moves by its own inherent force.
2. There is no break in the continuity of existence.

Bonnet proposed that the divine Being created a graduated scale of germs, each possessing an inherent power for self-development. As the universe progresses, these germs are progressively modified and move closer to perfection. Bonnet argued that if some germs failed to advance, it would create a gap in the continuity of the chain. 

Based on this, Bonnet concluded that all forms of existence, not just humans, are immortal. He further asserted that man's body is immortal, though not in its current form; instead, the body will pass into a higher stage via a finer body whose germ currently exists within the person. However, Bonnet noted that absolute perfection is impossible to reach because the distance to it is infinite. This final claim violates his own principle of continuity by postulating an interval between the high and the absolute.

## Published Works

The complete works of Bonnet were published at Neuchatel between 1779 and 1783, and were partly revised by the author himself. One of his works, the *Palingenesie philosophique*, had certain portions translated into English and published in 1787 under the title *Philosophical and Critical Inquiries concerning Christianity*.
