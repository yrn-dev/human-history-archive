# Political and National Movements in Bohemia

## National Aspirations and the 1848 Revolution
Following the re-establishment of European peace in 1815, national aspirations in Bohemia began to revive after a period of suppression. Initially, this movement was expressed through the revival of Bohemian literature, as the absolutist and arbitrary government of Prince Metternich made political action impossible within the lands ruled by the house of Habsburg.

By 1845, the estates of Bohemia began opposing the government in Vienna, asserting their right to vote on the country's taxes based on the constitution of 1627. In 1847, the estates planned to propose several reforms at their following session, including more extensive representation for towns at the diet, more stringent control over national finances, and the introduction of the Bohemian language into all higher schools. However, the revolutionary outbreak of 1848 prevented this meeting.

Following the February revolution in Paris, excitement grew in Prague. On March 11, a large public meeting voted for a petition to the Vienna government. This petition demanded liberal reforms, a general diet for all Bohemian lands elected via extensive suffrage, and equal rights for the Bohemian language alongside German in government offices. Emperor Ferdinand responded on April 8, declaring that equality of rights for both nationalities would be secured and that a new meeting of the estates, including representatives from principal towns, would be convoked.

## The 1848 Transition and the Reichsrath
The idea of an independent Bohemia and Pan-Slav dreams were ended on June 16. Facing perceived peril from the German parliament, Czechs joined the Austrian government to struggle against German liberalism. The Bohemian diet, which had been summoned for the 19th, never met. Instead, writs were issued in Bohemia for elections to the Austrian Reichsrath.

When the Reichsrath assembled on July 10, Slav deputies held a majority. While this would lead to violent trouble later, the immediate priority was the emancipation of peasants. On August 31, a law abolishing feudal services was carried, receiving imperial consent on September 7. This outcome satisfied the peasants, who subsequently supported the government against the revolution.

## Religious and Legal Conflicts (1609–1742)
Earlier in Bohemian history, the diet that met on January 20, 1609, served as a critical juncture for religious liberty. The estates had previously drafted twenty-five "Articles" demanding that the *Confessio Bohemica* be recognized as a fundamental law and that complete religious liberty be granted to all classes. They sought equal rights for Roman Catholics and Protestants (a term then encompassing Lutherans, Bohemian Brethren, and Utraquists) to hold state offices and requested limits on the Jesuits' ability to acquire land. King Rudolph, however, demanded the destruction of this document and ultimately stated on March 31 that he would grant no concessions regarding religion.

In the 18th century, Bohemia became a site of conflict between the French and Austrians. French forces, joined by the elector's forces and later the Saxons, advanced on Prague. The French stormed the city on November 26. On December 19, 1741, the elector of Bavaria, styling himself the archduke of Austria, was crowned king of Bohemia.

## Later Language Disputes and Governance
In the late 19th century, Count Badeni attempted to conciliate Bohemia by addressing grievances, though he did not consider reopening the question of autonomy feasible. In early 1897, Badeni issued a decree requiring government officials employed in Bohemia to prove knowledge of both German and Bohemian. This caused violent opposition from German inhabitants of Austria and led to the fall of Badeni's cabinet at the end of 1897.

Badeni was succeeded by Count Thun and then Count Clary. The government of Count Clary repealed the decrees that had granted equal rights to the Bohemian language, resulting in troubles in Prague that were severely repressed by Austrian authorities. Under the subsequent ministries of Gautsch and Korber, Bohemians continued to assert their national rights and oppose the central government in Vienna.
