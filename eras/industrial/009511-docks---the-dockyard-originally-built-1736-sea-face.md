# Docks

Docks and dockyards serve as critical infrastructure for the maintenance, construction, and commercial handling of maritime vessels. These facilities vary from ancient fortified harbors to modern industrial complexes incorporating advanced engineering for ship repair and cargo logistics.

## Engineering and Technical Components
The closing of graving-docks is typically achieved through the use of caissons. Ship-caissons are closed iron structures resembling vessels that are floated into and out of position. Sliding or rolling caissons are also employed; these may replace lock-gates and require a side chamber for storage when drawn back. In naval dockyards, sliding caissons are particularly advantageous as they provide a strong movable bridge for transporting heavy weights, removing the need for a swing-bridge.

Additionally, the term caisson may refer to flat, air-tight constructions used to raise vessels for repairs by sinking them underneath and then floating them. These are more commonly known as pontoons or, if equipped with side air-chambers, as floating dry-docks.

## Industrial and Commercial Dock Systems
Various global ports demonstrate the scale and specialization of dock systems:

**Bombay**
The dockyard in Bombay, established in 1736, covers approximately 200 acres with a sea-face of nearly 700 yards. It features four building slips opposite the Apollo Bandar on the south-east side and is illuminated by electricity to allow for 24-hour operations. The facility includes five graving docks: three combined into one 648 ft. dock and two combined into one 582 ft. dock. Bombay is noted as the only significant location in India where the tide rise—averaging 14 ft. and reaching 17 ft. during highest spring tides—is sufficient for large-scale docks. Notable additions include the Prince's dock (opened 1879, 1460 ft. by 1000 ft.) and the Victoria dock (opened 1887-1888). The Alexandra dock, the largest in India, had its foundation-stone laid in 1905.

**Birkenhead**
Bounded by docks extending over 2 miles from the Wallasey Bridge to the Woodside Ferry landing-stage, Birkenhead's system is managed by the Mersey Docks and Harbour Board. The Alfred Dock serves as an entrance-lock, featuring 8 acres of water, 19 pairs of lock-gates, and 460 linear yards of quay-space. The "great Float" is a massive 120-acre dock with 5 miles of quay-space. Other principal docks include the Egerton, Morpeth, and Morpeth Branch. The area also supports extensive warehouses, including one opened in 1847 capable of holding 80,000 tons of goods, as well as cold storage for meat and lairages for livestock.

**Barry**
Established by colliery owners seeking an alternative to Cardiff, the docks at Barry cover 114 acres. Dock No. 1 (opened 1889) spans 73 acres, while Dock No. 2 (opened 1898) spans 34 acres. The facility includes three large graving-docks, 41 acres of timber-ponds, and 37 coal-hoists (30 fixed and 7 movable). In 1906, exports totaled 9,757,380 tons, the vast majority of which was coal.

## Naval Bases and Strategic Dockyards
Dockyards often play a central role in military strategy and national defense. In the Mediterranean, the squadron at Alexandria was reported to have moved to Malta. In Italy, Castellammare di Stabia possesses a large royal dockyard alongside a small-arms factory.

In the context of the sea front against Germany, the availability of dock facilities is a strategic factor. Chatham is the only existing naval base or military harbor in certain sectors, yet it lacks graving docks for later Dreadnoughts, and the Medway's depth limits the use of great vessels. Other locations such as Harwich, the Humber, and the Tyne are considered unsuitable for large battle fleets. Rosyth was not expected to be ready as a war repairing-base until 1916, while Cromarty utilized a temporary floating base.

During periods of high activity, dockyards may implement special precautions, such as putting magazines and oil tanks under guard and increasing night work to speed up ship repairs.

## Historical Dockyards
Ancient naval stations also utilized protective dock structures. In the Great Harbor of Syracuse, the Athenians established a naval station protected by a row of posts or stakes driven into the seabed. Similarly, the Syracusans utilized a stockade to protect their docks, ship-houses, and vessels within the interior port of Ortygia. Athenian attempts to breach these outworks involved using divers to saw through the posts at the bottom or using boat-crews to pluck them out using ropes, all under the cover of a large vessel equipped with wooden towers.
