# Legislative History of the Deceased Wife's Sister Marriage Act

The legal status of marriage with a deceased wife's sister in England was the subject of extensive legislative debate and repeated attempts at reform throughout the 19th and early 20th centuries. The process was characterized by a recurring pattern of acceptance by the House of Commons and rejection by the House of Lords.

## Early Legislative Attempts
The effort to render marriage with a deceased wife's sister valid began in 1850, when the bill was first adopted by the House of Commons. However, it was rejected by the House of Lords in 1851. Over the following decades, the legislature considered the matter in a vast number of sessions, including 1855, 1856, 1858, 1859, 1861, 1862, 1866, 1869, 1870, 1871, 1872, 1873, 1875, 1877, 1880, 1882, 1883, 1884, 1886, 1888, 1889, 1890, 1891, 1896, and 1898. 

In 1878 and 1900, the legislature dealt with "Colonial bills" regarding the matter. Specifically, in 1879, the prince of Wales and the duke of Edinburgh voted in favor of the bill while it was in the House of Lords.

## The Bill of 1896
A significant shift occurred with the bill of 1896. This version was judiciously drafted to ensure that clergymen were not compelled to celebrate marriages that conflicted with their conscientious scruples. This drafting allowed the bill to be carried in the House of Lords, with the duke of York and the prince of Wales among those who were "contents." 

Despite this progress, the bill faced strong opposition from the prime minister and eighteen bishops, including both archbishops. Other notable figures, such as Lord Kimberley and the earl of Rosebery, voted in favor. Although the bill passed its third reading with a vote of 142 to 104, its promoters failed to secure an opportunity to bring the measure before the House of Commons.

## Colonial Marriages and Final Adoption
Between 1896 and 1901, no direct steps were taken toward the general law, but the issue was raised in the House of Lords in 1898 and 1900 by Lord Strathcona. He proposed a bill that would deem marriages with a deceased wife's sister contracted in any British colony to be valid for all purposes within the United Kingdom. On both occasions, the bill passed the third reading without a dissentient vote, though the House of Commons took no action.

An imperial bill reached a second reading in the House of Commons in 1901 and again in 1902. However, High Church opponents blocked the measure during attempts to move it to the committee stage on June 6 and February 5.

The reform was eventually adopted in 1906 as the Colonial Marriages (Deceased Wife's Sister) Act. This act legalized such marriages in all respects, including honors and dignities and the right of succession to real property within the United Kingdom.

## The Deceased Wife's Sister Marriage Act, 1907
The passage of the 1906 act led to the reintroduction of the bill relating to England in 1907. Introduced by a private member and subsequently adopted by the government, the bill passed the House of Commons and then the House of Lords, where it passed the second reading by 111 votes to 79.

The resulting Deceased Wife's Sister Marriage Act, 1907, included a proviso that allowed clergymen to refuse to solemnize these marriages. Additionally, the act preserved the status of the wife's sister under the Matrimonial Causes Act 1857, which maintains that adultery committed by a husband with his deceased wife's sister is considered incestuous adultery.
