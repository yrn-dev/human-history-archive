# Maximum Axle Load and Bridge Loading

In the engineering of road and railway bridges, the maximum axle load is a critical metric used to determine the necessary strength of a structure to resist straining action and bending moments. The concentration of loading at the axles is recognized to produce greater straining action, particularly in short bridges, than the same total load distributed uniformly along a span.

## Railway Locomotive Loads
The weights and axle loads of locomotives vary significantly by type and region. Data for English railways, provided by W.B. Farr, distinguishes between several categories of engines:

*   **Passenger Engines:** These exhibit maximum axle loads of 19.00, 16.00, 18.70, and 18.50 tons across different examples. Their total weights range from 84.35 to 98.90 tons.
*   **Goods Engines:** These have maximum axle loads of 15.90, 16.00, 13.65, and 15.50 tons, with total weights between 75.65 and 78.80 tons.
*   **Tank Engines:** These record maximum axle loads of 17.54, 15.29, 17.10, and 15.77 tons, with total weights ranging from 47.00 to 60.80 tons.

In the United States, locomotives can be even heavier. A consolidation engine may weigh 126 tons with a length of 57 ft. over the buffers, resulting in an average load of 2.55 tons per ft. run. J.A.L. Waddell proposed a seven-class system for railways based on expected live loads. In this system, the lightest class utilizes a locomotive and tender of 93.5 tons (1.8 tons per ft. run), while the heaviest class utilizes a locomotive and tender of 144.5 tons (2.77 tons per ft. run). Waddell's measurements use short tons of 2000 lb.

## Road Bridge Live Loads
Live loads on road bridges include both pedestrian crowds and vehicular traffic. A dense crowd is typically calculated as a uniform load of 80 to 120 lb per sq. ft. However, the increase in weight of wagons and traction engines often produces greater straining action than crowds. In large towns and manufacturing districts, road bridges may be required to support loads of 30 tons.

In Switzerland, road load requirements are categorized by road importance:
*   **Main Roads:** Prescribed for a crowd of 92 lb per sq. ft. or a wagon with 10 tons per axle and a 13 ft. wheel base.
*   **Secondary Roads:** Prescribed for a crowd of 72 lb per sq. ft. or a wagon with 6 tons per axle and a 10 ft. wheel base.
*   **Other Roads:** Prescribed for a crowd of 51 lb per sq. ft. or a wagon with 3 tons per axle and an 8 ft. wheel base.

## Bending Moments and Equivalent Loads
To simplify the design of girders, engineers often determine an "equivalent uniform load" ($w_e$) that would produce a bending moment as great as that produced by actual concentrated wheel loads. 

W.B. Farr determined equivalent uniform loads for forty heavy locomotives across various spans. For example, at a span of 5.0 ft., the equivalent load is 7.6 tons per ft. run, which decreases to 1.97 tons per ft. run at a span of 100.0 ft. Similarly, J.C. Inglis analyzed traction engines and boiler trolleys (Case a) and traction engines with granite-loaded trucks (Case b). For Case b, the equivalent load is 3.25 tons per ft. run at a 10 ft. span, decreasing to 1.15 tons per ft. run at a 50 ft. span.

## Structural Analysis and Design
The maximum bending moment at a specific section of a bridge occurs when the average loads per ft. on either side of that section are nearly equal, with one of the largest loads positioned at the section itself. For short bridges, designers typically draw maximum bending moment curves for a typical set of loads. For longer bridges, a funicular polygon is often used.

The web of a girder must be designed to resist maximum shear. In the case of travelling loads, such as railway trains, the greatest shear often occurs during partial rather than complete loading of the girder. Designers must account for both dead loads (the weight of the structure) and live loads (the weight of the traffic).
