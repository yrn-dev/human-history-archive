# Richard Broun and the Baronetcy Movement

## The Movement for Baronet Privileges
In 1834, Mr. Richard Broun initiated a movement intended to secure certain privileges for the order of baronets. Mr. Broun's father had assumed a Nova Scotia baronetcy several years prior to this effort. The request for these privileges was refused following the advice provided by the Heralds' College. 

In 1836, a subsequent petition was submitted seeking permission for all baronets to wear a badge, similar to the practice of those of Nova Scotia. This request was also refused.

## Claims to Knighthood
During this period, the rights regarding the eldest sons of baronets were altered. On December 19, 1827, George IV revoked the right of eldest sons of baronets to claim knighthood for all future creations. Despite this revocation, Mr. Broun claimed the honor in 1836 in his capacity as an heir apparent. 

After his claim was finally met with refusal, Mr. Broun publicly assumed the honor in 1842, an action described as futile and foolish.

## Subsequent Applications for Knighthood
Following the events involving Mr. Broun, other sons of baronets were knighted. Sir J. Kingston James received a knighthood as a baronet's son in 1854, and Sir Ludlow Cotter was similarly knighted in 1874 upon reaching his age of majority.

However, a later application for the honor was denied. On May 17, 1895, the son of Sir Claude de Crespigny applied for knighthood, but the application was refused. The refusal was based on the lord chancellor's determination that a clause in the patent from 1805 was not valid. The specific reason for this decision remains unknown.
