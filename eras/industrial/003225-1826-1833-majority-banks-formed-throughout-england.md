# Banking Evolution in England and Wales (1826–1833)

The period between 1826 and 1833 marked a significant transition in the banking structure of England and Wales, shifting from a system dominated by small, private firms toward the establishment of joint-stock companies. This evolution was driven by a series of legislative acts that dismantled long-standing restrictions on bank partnerships and the issuance of notes.

## The Era of Small Private Banks
From 1694 until the legislative changes of 1826 and 1833, banking in England and Wales was characterized by small and usually isolated firms. This was due to legal restrictions—established by the acts of 1694, 1697, 1708, and confirmed in 1800—which prohibited any bank or partnership exceeding six persons from borrowing, owing, or taking up money on bills or notes payable on demand (or in less than six months). Consequently, no joint-stock bank was permitted to be established within England during the continuance of the Bank of England.

These private banks faced significant stability challenges. Because they were limited to six partners, the death of a partner often resulted in their capital leaving the business. This necessitated the search for new partners with sufficient means, leading to constant change. Such instability hindered the growth of confidence, which is described as "a plant of slow growth," unless a vacancy was filled by a relative or son. While some of these banks were powerful and well-managed—including a considerable number founded by members of the Society of Friends—they remained limited in scale. Banks in country districts often maintained branches in nearby small market-towns, whereas London banks never operated more than one office.

## Legislative Reforms of 1826 and 1833
The monopoly of the Bank of England as the sole joint-stock bank in England and Wales began to erode with the acts of 1826 and 1833. 

The first act of 1826 prohibited the use of bank notes under £5. A second Banking Act passed in 1826 allowed for the establishment of co-partnerships (joint-stock companies) of more than six persons, provided they were located more than 65 miles from London. 

The act of 1833 further expanded these permissions by allowing joint-stock banks to be established within the 65-mile limit of London. This act also removed various restrictions regarding the amounts of notes for less than £50. However, joint-stock banks located within the 65-mile radius were not permitted to issue notes. This was a critical distinction, as the power to issue notes was considered the essential introduction to the system of deposit-banking; without this power, a bank could scarcely exist outside of London.

## Growth and Composition of Banks
The shift in legislation is reflected in the registered numbers of banks. In 1792, it is believed there were 350 banks in England and Wales. Following the 1826 reforms, the number of private banks was 554 in 1826, decreasing to 465 in 1827 and 416 by 1833. Conversely, joint-stock banks grew from zero in 1826 to 6 in 1827, and then to 35 by 1833. By 1842, the number of joint-stock banks had risen to 118, while private banks fell to 311.

Despite these changes, the restriction of six partners for private banks persisted until 1862, when the limit was raised to ten (though this did not apply to issuing private banks).

## Liability and the Bank of England
Throughout this transition, the Bank of England maintained a position of pre-eminence. Founded to provide the government a loan of £1,200,000, it served as the government's banker and managed the national debt. Until 1862, the Bank of England was the only bank whose shareholders enjoyed limited liability. 

In contrast, shareholders in the newly formed joint-stock banks remained liable to the full extent of their means until the act of 1862 permitted "limited liability." This lack of protection deterred many wealthy individuals from becoming shareholders. The risks of unlimited liability were highlighted by the failures of the Western Bank of Scotland in 1858 and the City of Glasgow Bank in 1878, the latter of which prompted the Companies Act of 1879 to allow unlimited companies to adopt limited liability.
