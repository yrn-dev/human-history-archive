# Louis Gabriel Ambroise Bonald

## Early Life and Education
Louis Gabriel Ambroise Bonald, the Vicomte de Bonald, was a French politician and philosopher. He was born on October 2, 1754, at Le Monna, located near Millau in the department of Aveyron.

## Political Exile and Early Works
Bonald emigrated from France in 1791 because he disliked the principles of the Revolution. During this period of exile, he joined the army of the prince of Conde and eventually settled in Heidelberg. While in Heidelberg, he authored his first significant work, *Theorie du pouvoir politique et religieux*. Published in three volumes in 1796 (with a two-volume new edition appearing in Paris in 1854), this highly conservative text was condemned by the Directory.

## Career in France and Imperial Service
Upon returning to France, Bonald was viewed as an object of suspicion, which forced him to live in retirement. In 1806, he became involved in the conduct of the *Mercure de France* alongside Fievee and Chateaubriand. Two years later, he was appointed as a councillor of the Imperial University, an institution that he had frequently attacked.

## Restoration and Public Office
Following the restoration, Bonald served as a member of the council of public instruction. Between 1815 and 1822, he sat in the chamber as a deputy. His speeches during this time were characterized by extreme conservatism, and he went so far as to advocate for literary censorship. 

In 1822, Bonald was appointed minister of state and presided over the censorship commission. He was made a peer in 1823, though he lost this dignity in 1830 after refusing to take the oath. He had also been a member of the Academy since 1816.

## Final Years
After 1830, Bonald ceased taking part in public affairs. He retired to his seat at Le Monna, where he died on November 23, 1840.

## Geography of Aveyron
Bonald's birthplace, Aveyron, is a department in southern France with an area of 3,386 square miles and a population of 377,299 as of 1906. It is bounded by Cantal to the north, Lozere and Gard to the east, Tarn to the southwest, and Lot and Tarn-et-Garonne to the west. The region corresponds nearly to the old district of Rouergue, which was the name of a countship established in the early 9th century and united with Toulouse toward the end of the 11th century.

The earliest known inhabitants of the region were the Celtic Rutheni, who are credited with the area's numerous megalithic monuments. Geographically, Aveyron is situated on the southern border of the central plateau of France. It is divided into four zones by three chief rivers—all tributaries of the Garonne—which flow from east to west: the Lot in the north, the Aveyron in the center, and the Tarn in the south.

The north-east is characterized by the volcanic mountains of Aubrac, which contain the department's highest summit at 4,760 feet, and the pastoral plateau of the Viadene. The region between the Lot and the Aveyron consists of limestone table-lands known as *causses*, featuring profound gorges. Between the Tarn and the Aveyron lies the plateau of the Segala, bordered by the heights of Palanges and Levezou and traversed by the valley of the Viaur. The area south of the Tarn is largely occupied by the huge plateau of Larzac, situated between the Causse St Affrique and the Causse Noir; the latter contains a chaos of rocks and precipices called Montpellier-le-Vieux.
