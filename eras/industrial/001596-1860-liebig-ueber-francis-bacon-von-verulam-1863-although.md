# Francis Bacon

Francis Bacon (1561–1626), also known as Baron Verulam and Viscount St Albans, was an English statesman, essayist, and philosopher. Born on January 22, 1560/1, at York House in the Strand, London, he was the youngest son of Sir Nicholas Bacon. His mother, a daughter of Sir Anthony Cooke and a former tutor to Edward VI, was a cultured woman skilled in classical studies and a warm adherent of the Puritan or Reformed Church.

## Early Life and Education
Due to an extremely delicate state of health, Bacon likely received much of his early instruction at home. In April 1573, he entered Trinity College, Cambridge, where he resided for three years with his brother Anthony. During his time at the university, Bacon studied the sciences as they were taught at the time, but he concluded that both the results attained and the methods employed were erroneous. While he maintained a reverence for Aristotle, he grew to despise the prevailing Aristotelian philosophy, viewing it as fruitless and useful only for disputation. He believed that philosophy required a new method and a redefined true purpose, a conception he carried with him upon leaving the university.

## Scientific Contributions and Philosophy
Bacon has been described as the "Father of Experimental Philosophy," though some perspectives suggest he was the "apostle" rather than the founder of the scientific method. He is credited as a leader in the development of systematic scientific publication. His ideas were influenced in part by Dr. Gilbert of Colchester (1540–1603), an experimental philosopher who practiced the observation and experiment that Bacon preached.

One of Bacon's most significant contributions was the work *The New Atlantis*. Written as a Utopian story, it describes a "palace of invention" and a "great temple of science" where the pursuit of knowledge across all branches would be organized according to principles of highest efficiency.

## Political Career and Death
Bacon served as the Lord Chancellor to James I of England. He died in 1626.

## Critical Reception and Academic Debate
Bacon's methods and works have been the subject of extensive academic analysis and criticism. In the 19th century, J. v. Liebig published *Ueber Francis Bacon von Verulam* (1863), in which he attacked Baconian methods and their results. Liebig argued that very little science actually proceeds according to Bacon's rules. He specifically criticized the *Sylva Sylvarum*, claiming that Bacon used imagination, relied on undetermined perceptions of the senses, and applied arbitrary interpretations to phenomena. 

However, other scholars have challenged Liebig's critiques. Sigwart and Fischer commented on Liebig's "offensive and quite unjustifiable tone." Fischer argued that the "true scientific method" expounded by Liebig was, in fact, simply the Baconian doctrine that Liebig sought to attack. Furthermore, critics noted that Liebig ignored Bacon's own descriptions of the nature of the *Sylva Sylvarum* and failed to grasp the true nature of the method he was criticizing.
