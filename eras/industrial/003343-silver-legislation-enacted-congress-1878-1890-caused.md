# Silver Legislation of 1878 and 1890

The silver legislation enacted by the United States Congress in 1878 and 1890 had significant repercussions for the American banking system and the national treasury, contributing to financial instability and a subsequent period of economic decline.

## Impact on Banking and Currency
The legislation passed in 1878 and 1890 created a state of uneasiness within banking circles. This apprehension led banks to discriminate against silver certificates and silver dollars when managing their cash. 

This instability became more acute in 1893 when the treasury began to experience heavy losses of gold. In response to this crisis, leading bankers from several major cities—including Chicago, Baltimore, Philadelphia, Boston, and New York—formed a combination to replenish government reserves by turning over a large portion of their own holdings.

## The Panic of 1893 and Subsequent Decline
The financial strain culminated in the panic of 1893. During this crisis, approximately 150 national banks suspended their operations, although 84 of these institutions were later able to resume business. 

Following the initial panic, the banking system entered a period of liquidation and depression. The most severe decline occurred during the years following the actual panic. Between June 1, 1893, and June 1, 1899, the number of banks decreased from 3,856 to 3,585. During this same timeframe, the aggregate capital of these institutions fell from $698,454,665 to $610,028,895.

## Context of American Monetary Policy
The legislation of 1878 and 1890 existed within a broader shift in international and domestic monetary standards. The United States had formally established the gold dollar as its standard coin in 1873 and arranged for a return to specie payments in 1878. 

During the mid-to-late 19th century, several factors influenced the value of silver:
* **Gold Production:** The high volume of gold production seen between 1850 and 1870 diminished.
* **Silver Discovery:** Highly productive silver mines were discovered in the Pacific states of America.
* **International Trends:** The Franco-German War (1870-71) led Germany to establish a gold currency and demonetize its silver currencies.

These combined influences caused the gold price of silver to fall rapidly. This depreciation affected the German government and American silver producers, who held large amounts of demonetized silver. In response to these pressures, the United States passed the Bland-Allison Act in February 1878, which provided for the coinage of a specific amount of silver per month.

## International Efforts
The instability caused by the depreciation of silver led to various official inquiries, including an American silver commission and an English parliamentary committee in 1876. Many works produced during this time advocated for a double standard. 

In 1878, the United States initiated an international monetary conference in Paris. While the participants recognized the necessity of maintaining a place for silver in global currency, the European states rejected the proposal to adopt the double standard for general use.
