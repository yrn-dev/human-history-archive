# Ordinary Bridge

The term "ordinary bridge" appears in the archive's records in three distinct contexts: as a form of card game, as a structural engineering entity, and as a component of industrial heating apparatus.

## Ordinary Bridge (Card Game)
Ordinary bridge is a game for four players that differs from whist in several key respects. While there are four players, the partner of the dealer does not participate in the play of a particular hand. After the first lead, this partner's cards are exposed on the table and played by the dealer, similar to dummy whist; however, the partner remains equally interested in the result of the hand.

The trump suit is not determined by the last card dealt. Instead, the dealer or their partner selects the trump suit without consultation, with the dealer holding the first option. The players also have the option to play without a trump suit. The value of honours and tricks varies based on the declared trump suit. Honours are calculated on a somewhat involved scale that differs from whist. The score for honours is added to the trick score after the rubber to determine the rubber's value, rather than counting toward winning or losing the rubber itself. Additional scores are awarded for winning all tricks or all but one (a "slam"), and for holding no trumps (a "chicane").

## Bridge Engineering and Construction
In the context of civil engineering, bridges are subject to various stresses and construction methods.

### Stress and Design
Engineers have historically debated the safe limits of stress for iron structures. For a period, it was commonly held that a total dead and live load stress not exceeding 5 tons per sq. in. provided ample safety. However, Sir B. Baker noted in 1885 that opinions on safe limits were chaotic, and that the strength of existing bridges varied significantly. He observed that girders of ordinary strength had fractured under five-minutes' train services, suggesting that bridges carrying twenty trains a day safely might fail if subjected to twenty trains an hour.

There is also discussion regarding the Weyrauch method and whether an additional allowance for impact should be made if fatigue is already allowed. Some engineers argue that Wöhler's results do not apply to bridge work, rejecting the fatigue allowance (the effect of repetition) in favor of designing for total dead and live loads plus a large empirical allowance for impact. Others suggest that the allowance for fatigue in certain tables is sufficient to cover ordinary impact effects.

### Erection Methods
The erection of bridges is influenced by local conditions and can be categorized into four methods:
1. Erection on staging or falsework.
2. Floating to the site and raising.
3. Rolling out from one abutment.
4. Building out member by member, where the completed section serves as the stage for further additions.

Staging may utilize earth banks, timber stacks, piles and trestles, or iron staging. A common type of staging uses timber piles spaced 20 to 30 feet apart to support a timber platform. In cases of deep rivers or great headway, timber-braced piers or clusters of piles spaced 50 to 100 feet apart may support temporary steel or timber trusses. For example, the Kuilenburg bridge in Holland, with a span of 492 feet, utilized a timber staging containing 5 tons of bolts and 81,000 cubic feet of timber to support a superstructure weighing 2,150 tons.

### Materials and History
Timber bridges were used for early railways in England, such as the Manchester, Sheffield & Lincolnshire and the Great Western, though most were replaced due to joint decay. In America, large-span timber bridges were common between the late 18th and mid-19th centuries. Examples include the Bellows Falls bridge (spans of 184 feet), the Amoskeag bridge (six spans of 92 feet), and the Colossus bridge (a flat arched truss with a 340-foot span). 

From 1840, American designs adopted trusses of timber with cast-iron shoes and wrought-iron tension-rods, such as the Pratt truss (vertical struts, inclined ties) and the Howe truss (timber chords, timber struts, and vertical iron ties). By 1850, these were generally limited to 150-foot spans. Later, the Howe truss was stiffened with timber arches, though this composite structure was considered fundamentally defective. Timber trestle piers reached significant heights, such as the 190-foot piers of the 800-foot long Genesee viaduct.

## Other Uses of the Term
In industrial gas-firing, an "ordinary fire-place" can be adapted for gas-firing by lowering the fire-grate to at least 4 feet below the fire-bridge.
