# Bridge Foundations and Piling Methods

The construction of bridge foundations involves a variety of techniques designed to secure structures in diverse soil conditions, ranging from loose or compressible soil to running sands and deep river beds. These methods have evolved from classical timber and masonry constructions to the use of iron, steel, and reinforced concrete.

## Piling Techniques
Piles are primarily utilized as foundations when the soil is loose or compressible. Once installed, the heads of these piles are sawn off to support a platform made of concrete or timber. Modern applications include the use of concrete reinforced piles and cast iron piles.

Specific types of piles are employed based on the ground conditions:
*   **Screw Piles:** These are cast iron piles that are screwed into the soil rather than driven. They feature a cast iron blade at the end, which ranges from two to eight times the diameter of the pile shaft. The pitch of the screw is typically between one-fourth and one-half of the blade's external diameter. Screw piles are also used for mooring, such as the mooring chains for the floating pontoon bridge at Riga.
*   **Disk Piles:** Used specifically in sand, these piles feature a flat flange at the bottom. To install them, the pile is weighted to prevent it from rising while water is pumped in at the top. This process blows or pumps sand from beneath the pile, allowing it to be lowered in ground where driving piles by blows is ineffective. This is particularly useful in quicksand, where piles may otherwise slowly rise back to their original position after each blow.

## Concrete and Cylinder Foundations
Hydraulic concrete is highly valued for its ability to set into a solid mass underwater. One method, known as "concrete in a shell," involves enclosing a space with an iron or wooden shell. After the soil is removed via mechanical excavator or dredging to reach a supporting formation, concrete is shot into the space from a height of approximately 10 feet and rammed in layers of about 1 foot thickness to create a permanent artificial stone.

Cylinder foundations provide an alternative when firm bearing strata are located at considerable depths. While timber cofferdams were used historically, iron, brick, or well cylinders are now adopted. These can be sunk to almost any depth and filled with Portland cement concrete. In silty river beds, they may be sunk 100 feet or more to protect against deep scouring during floods. Examples include:
*   **Empress Bridge (Sutlej):** Each pier consisted of three brick wells, 19 feet in diameter, sunk 110 feet.
*   **Benares Bridge:** Single iron caissons (65 ft. by 28 ft.) sunk about 100 feet, lined with brick and filled with concrete.
*   **Forth Bridge:** Iron caissons 70 feet in diameter sunk approximately 40 feet using the compressed air process.

## Caissons and Wells
In civil engineering, a caisson is a wood or iron enclosure used to keep out water during the execution of foundations in water-bearing strata. There are two primary forms:
1.  **Open-top caissons:** These emerge above the water level and are either provided with a water-tight bottom or carried down into a water-tight stratum using a cutting edge and weights.
2.  **Bottomless caissons:** These function as diving bells. Compressed air is introduced to keep out water, allowing men to excavate the bottom while a pier or quay-wall is built on the roof as the structure descends.

Similarly, "wells" are used in running sands. A masonry casing is built like a well, and as the soil inside is excavated, the casing sinks. Once the required depth is reached, the interior is typically filled with brick or concrete.

## Materials and Structural Components
Bridge materials have shifted over time. Classical and medieval bridges used timber, masonry, brick, or concrete. Wrought iron became common in the late 18th century, followed by cast iron for arches and early railway girders. Since 1880, mild steel has largely superseded wrought iron for girders with spans exceeding 100 feet. The most recent advancement is the introduction of ferro-concrete (concrete strengthened with steel bars) for arched bridges.

Piers and abutments may be constructed from masonry, brickwork, or wrought and cast iron. Iron piers often consist of hollow cylindrical pillars—either vertical or raking—united by flanges, bolts, or sockets. These are strengthened against lateral yielding through diagonal and horizontal bracing. In some instances, cast iron cylinders with diameters of 10 feet or more are filled with concrete.
