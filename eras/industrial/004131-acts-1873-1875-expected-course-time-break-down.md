# Judicature Acts 1873 and 1875

The Judicature Acts of 1873 and 1875 were legislative measures in English law that significantly altered the administration of the legal system, specifically regarding the relationship between law and equity and the procedures for initiating legal actions.

## Fusion of Law and Equity
Historically, the English legal profession was marked by a distinct separation between barristers practicing in the courts of common law and those practicing in chancery. The Judicature Acts 1873 and 1875 brought about the fusion of law and equity. It was expected that this fusion would, over time, break down the distinction between these two branches of the profession. However, the archive notes that to a large extent, the separation between these two great branches of the profession remains.

## Procedural Changes and Abolitions
The Acts introduced several specific changes to legal documentation and the methods by which cases were brought before the court:

*   **Bills in Chancery:** In previous eras, a Bill in Chancery served as the written statement of a plaintiff's case, detailing the wrong upon which the suit was based and praying for relief. Following the Judicature Acts 1873 and 1875, the Bill in Chancery was replaced by a statement of claim and a writ.
*   **Bills of Exceptions:** These were written statements of objections regarding a judge's ruling during a trial—specifically when a judge was believed to have mistaken the law in admitting or refusing evidence or in directing the jury. These were limited to civil cases and were abolished by the Judicature Act 1875, which substituted a "motion for a new trial" in their place.
*   **Assumpsit:** The form of action known as *assumpsit*—an action for the recovery of damages resulting from the non-performance or breach of a simple contract (whether express or implied, oral or written)—became obsolete after the passing of the Judicature Acts 1873 and 1875.

## Impact on the Legal Profession
The broader context of the English bar involves a division of labor where barristers handle the advocacy of causes in open court, as well as conveyancing and the drafting of pleadings (counter statements of parties previous to joining issue). While the Judicature Acts aimed to merge the spheres of law and equity, the profession remains divided into several special classes of business. 

For example, counsel at common law generally attach themselves to specific circuits within England and cannot practice elsewhere except under special conditions. In contrast, king's counsel in chancery typically restrict their practice to one of the courts of the chancery division.
