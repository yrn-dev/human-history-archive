# The Indian Ocean and the Indian Peninsula

## Geography of the Indian Peninsula
The Indian peninsula, extending southwards from 25 deg. N., is characterized as a table-land. Its elevation varies, with the general level lying between a maximum of 3000 ft. and 1000 ft. The highest points are located in the west, where elevations rise to over 8000 ft., although the higher hills typically do not exceed 4000 ft. 

To the north of this peninsula lies the great plain of northern India, situated between the foot of the Himalayan slope of the Tibetan plateau and the peninsula's table-land. This plain extends from the Indus delta in the west to the deltas of the Brahmaputra and Ganges in the east, including a prolongation up the valley of Assam. Covering an area of approximately 500,000 sq. m., this region is noted as the most civilized, populous, and richest districts of India. The plain is composed of deposits so finely comminuted that a traveler could potentially journey over 2000 m. from the Bay of Bengal, through the Punjab, and down the Indus to the sea without encountering a single pebble.

## The Indian Ocean and Coastal Features
The Indian Ocean borders the southern and western reaches of the Asian continent. The base of Asia, viewed as a great isosceles spherical triangle, is deeply indented by the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea on either side of the Indian peninsula. The coast-line of the Arabian Sea and the Bay of Bengal reaches approximately to the northern tropic at the mouths of the Ganges and the Indus.

Along the Indian Ocean, the Bombay Presidency occupies a long strip of land from the north of Mysore to the south of the Punjab. This coast is rock-bound and difficult to access. While it possesses several bays that serve as fairweather ports for coasting trade, only a few harbours—specifically Karwar, Marmagoa, Karachi-in-Sind, and Bombay—are sufficiently land-locked to protect shipping during the south-west monsoon. The coast-line here is generally regular, with the exception of the peninsula of Kathiawar, which lies between the Gulfs of Cutch and Cambay.

## Coral Formations and Islands
The Indian Ocean contains several significant coral formations. Off the south-west and west of India are the Laccadive and Maldive islands, which are large groups of atolls. These small coral islands are situated along the 73rd meridian and maintain a political dependency on the Indian peninsula. Further south lies the Chagos group of atolls. Other numerous reefs are found at the Seychelles, Bourbon, Mauritius, and off the north coast of Madagascar. In the eastern part of the Indian Ocean, the Cocos-Keeling Islands (located at 96 deg. E. long. and 12 deg. S. lat.) serve as typical atolls.

In the Bay of Bengal, the Andaman and Nicobar groups are notable. These islands are positioned on a line connecting Cape Negrais (the south-western extremity of Burma) and the north end of Sumatra. Their existence is possibly due to volcanic agencies that extend across this portion of the Indian Ocean.

## Regional Connections and Dispersal
The Indian Ocean serves as a link between various landmasses and cultures. The Malay people, described as a seafaring race with exceptional powers of dispersal, are distributed across the ocean from an island centre, possibly Java, to areas including Madagascar, Borneo, Sumatra, the Philippines, and the Malay Peninsula. 

Historically, Hindu civilization reached the Malay Peninsula and islands such as Sumatra and Java, leaving behind Indian customs, words, and ruins. This influence was later superseded by Islam before the 16th century. While the Arabic alphabet is used on the mainland today, Indian alphabets persist in Sumatra and Java.

Beyond Asia, the Indian Ocean reaches the coast of South Africa. The city of Durban, the largest city of Natal and its principal seaport, is situated along the coast of the Indian Ocean. Durban's character is influenced by a large number of British Indians engaged in various types of work, which provides the city with an oriental aspect.
