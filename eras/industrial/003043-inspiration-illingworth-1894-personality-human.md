# Inspiration and Personality in Historical and Religious Contexts

The concept of inspiration and the nature of human and divine personality are addressed across various historical, religious, and academic disciplines, ranging from the delivery of theological lectures at Oxford to the study of psychology and sacred texts.

## The Bampton Lectures
The Bampton lectures are a series of annual lectures delivered at the University Church in Oxford. Established by the will of John Bampton (c. 1690–1751), an English divine and former canon of Salisbury who graduated from Trinity College, Oxford, in 1712, the lectures are intended to establish the Christian faith and confute heretics and schismatics. Other approved subjects include the divinity of the Holy Ghost, the divinity of Jesus Christ, the authority of the Holy Scriptures, and the faith and practice of the primitive Church as found in the writings of the primitive fathers.

The lecturer must be a Master of Arts from either Cambridge or Oxford and cannot be chosen for the role a second time. While the series began in 1780, elections shifted to alternate years after 1895 due to a depreciation of the fund's revenue. Each lecturer is provided an endowment of L120 and is required to publish the lectures within two months of delivery. Notable lecturers have included:
*   Heber (1815), who spoke on *The Personality and Office of the Christian Comforter*.
*   W. Sanday (1893), who lectured on *Inspiration*.
*   J. R. Illingworth (1894), who presented *Personality, Human and Divine*.
*   W. R. Inge (1899), who discussed *Christian Mysticism*.

## Divine Inspiration in the Qur'an
In the faith of Moslems, the Qur'an is regarded as the word of God, a claim the text itself advances. Throughout the book, the speaker is God, typically utilizing the first person singular or the plural of majesty ("we"). There are few exceptions to this, such as in surah i., which is a prayer for men, or specific passages where angels or Mahomet speak in the first person without the imperative "say."

This mode of address is compared to that of the prophets of the Old Testament, where the human personality disappears during the moment of inspiration, filled by God. However, while Hebrew prophets often returned quickly to the use of the human "I," the divine "I" remains the stereotyped form of address in the Qur'an. Mahomet maintained a consciousness of being the instrument of God throughout his mission, remaining zealous for the salvation of humanity and his God.

## Inspiration in Classical Antiquity
In ancient Greece, the term *bakis* (meaning "speaker") referred to inspired prophets and dispensers of oracles between the 8th and 6th centuries B.C. Suidas identifies three such figures: an Athenian, an Arcadian, and a Boeotian. The Boeotian was the most famous and was said to be inspired by the nymphs of the Corycian cave, producing oracles in hexameter verse. The Arcadian was credited with curing Spartan women of a fit of madness, though some of the oracles attributed to him were later attributed to Onomacritus.

## Psychological Perspectives on Personality
The study of personality and consciousness has been explored through various psychological and philosophical lenses. In the field of psychology, researchers have examined the "subconscious strata of the personality." For instance, Pierre Janet used automatic writing to communicate with these strata in cases of dissociation of consciousness or hystero-epilepsy, allowing patients in hysterical convulsions to reveal the secrets of their malady in writing when oral appeals failed.

Other academic works on the human mind and personality include:
*   F. W. H. Myers' *Human Personality* (1903).
*   Morton Prince's *The Dissociation of a Personality* (1906).
*   W. Wundt's *Human and Animal Psych.* (1894).

## The Influence of Belief and Emotion
Inspiration is also described in terms of human emotion and social influence. In the Greek city-states, patriotism was viewed as a personal passion of "inspiring and dangerous intensity," which could turn into hatred for those in exile. In a historical context, the "humane spirit" and "merciful maxims" of Christianity are credited with softening the barbarous art of war, replacing the horror inspired by the threat of perpetual slavery with a sense of personal liberty.

Furthermore, different philosophical views present varying visions of immortality. Positivism suggests a corporate immortality where a person lives on as a "beneficent influence" in the human race. In contrast, the hope of a conscious communion in an eternal life with the Father of all is presented as a more inspiring anticipation than unconsciously contributing to progress as a mere influence.
