# Bridge Loading and Stress Analysis

In the engineering of bridges, the primary objective is to determine the loads the structure must carry and proportion its components so that resulting stresses remain within safe limits. As spans increase, the dead weight of the superstructure becomes a larger fraction of the total load, eventually imposing a limit on further increases in span.

## Live and Temporary Loads

The live or temporary load refers to the weight of moving objects crossing a bridge. The nature of this load varies depending on the type of bridge:

*   **Road Bridges:** The live load is defined as the weight of a heavy wagon, a traction engine, or a dense crowd of people distributed uniformly. A dense crowd is typically calculated as a uniform load of 80 to 120 lb per sq. ft. However, modern traction engines and wagons often produce greater straining action than crowds. In areas near large towns or manufacturing districts, authorities may require provisions for loads of up to 30 tons.
*   **Railway Bridges:** The live load is the weight of the heaviest train likely to traverse the bridge. Historically, this was taken as a uniform load of 1 ton per foot run for each line of way, based on locomotives weighing 35 to 45 tons. Modern engines and wagons are heavier, and engineers now recognize that the concentration of loading at the axles creates greater straining action—particularly in short bridges—than a uniformly distributed load.

In Switzerland, road loads are categorized by road importance:
*   **Main Roads:** 92 lb per sq. ft. for crowds; 10 tons per axle (13 ft. wheel base) for wagons.
*   **Secondary Roads:** 72 lb per sq. ft. for crowds; 6 tons per axle (10 ft. wheel base) for wagons.
*   **Other Roads:** 51 lb per sq. ft. for crowds; 3 tons per axle (8 ft. wheel base) for wagons.

## Dead Load and Structural Weight

The dead load comprises the total weight of the superstructure, excluding parts carried directly by the piers. This includes the flooring, wind bracing, and main girders. While usually treated as uniformly distributed over the span, the distribution of weight in main girders must be specifically calculated for large spans.

For railway bridges, the weight of rails and sleepers is estimated at 0.2 to 0.25 tons per ft. run per line, with rail and cross girders adding 0.15 to 0.2 tons. An additional 0.4 ton per ft. run is allowed if a footway is included.

## Additional Forces and Stresses

Beyond basic live and dead loads, engineers must account for several other dynamical and environmental pressures:

*   **Impact:** This is the dynamical action of the live load caused by irregularities in the permanent way, yielding of the structure, or a lack of vertical balance in locomotive moving parts. Empirical allowances for impact stresses are added to live load stresses: 20% for floor stringers, 15% for floor cross girders, and for main girders, 10% for 40-ft. spans and 5% for 100-ft. spans.
*   **Horizontal and Longitudinal Pressures:** Horizontal pressure is caused by wind blowing transversely to the span, which is critical for high and long bridges. Longitudinal drag is caused by the friction of a braking train, estimated at approximately one-seventh of the train's weight.
*   **Centrifugal Load:** On curved bridges, the radial acceleration of a train exerts a horizontal force calculated as $wv^2/gr$ tons (where $w$ is locomotive weight in tons, $r$ is the radius of curvature, and $v$ is velocity in feet per sec.).
*   **Temperature Changes:** In suspension and arch bridges, temperature variations create stresses. In Europe, a variation of 70° C (126° F) is commonly assumed, resulting in an expansion of about 1 in. per 100 ft. In steel structures, preventing this expansion can set up a stress of one ton per sq. in. for every 12° change in temperature.

## Structural Considerations and Safety

The mode of support significantly affects stress distribution. Independent or discontinuous girders are supported only at the ends. Continuous girders, supported at two or more points, offer economy in weight—providing a theoretical advantage of about 49% for dead loads and 16% for live loads in three-span bridges. However, continuity can be risky, as small settlements in piers can greatly alter stress distribution.

In suspension bridges, flexibility is a known defect that becomes less serious as the dead weight of the structure increases relative to the live load. This makes them suited for great spans. However, oscillations from men marching in step can be dangerous; in 1850, a bridge at Angers collapsed under 487 marching soldiers, resulting in 226 deaths.

Regarding material safety, experience shows that while 5 tons per sq. in. for iron or 6½ tons per sq. in. for steel is safe for long bridges with high dead-to-live load ratios, it is unsafe for short bridges where stresses are primarily due to live loads. Research by A. Wöhler and others indicates that breaking stress is not fixed but depends on the range of stress variation when repeated many times.
