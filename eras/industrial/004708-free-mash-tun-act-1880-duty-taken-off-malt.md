# The Free Mash-Tun Act of 1880 and the Brewing Process

The Free Mash-Tun Act of 1880 introduced significant changes to the taxation and regulation of beer production. Under this legislation, the duty previously levied on malt was removed and instead placed upon the beer, or more accurately, the wort. This act also repealed the licenses previously required for brewers and maltsters. In their place, an annual license duty of £1 was imposed on every brewer for sale. Other brewers were required to pay licenses of 9s. or 6s. (the latter of which was later modified to 4s.).

## Taxation and Revenue Regulations
Beginning October 1, 1880, a beer duty was established at a rate of 6s. 3d. per barrel of 36 gallons, based on a specific gravity of 1.057. This system granted brewers substantial freedom regarding the materials used for coloring or manufacturing beer, including the choice of corn, sugar, or other saccharine substitutes, provided they were not drugs or deleterious articles. However, the use of "saccharin" (a coal-tar derivative) was prohibited in 1888 because it created an apparent palate-fulness that exceeded real gravity by roughly 4°, resulting in a loss of revenue.

The duty was adjusted several times in the following decades:
*   **1889:** The duty increased via a reduction in the standard gravity from 1.057 to 1.055.
*   **1894:** An additional 6d. per barrel was added, bringing the duty to 6s. 9d. at a gravity of 1.055.
*   **1900:** The war budget increased the duty to 7s. 9d. per barrel, a figure that remained in 1909.

Regarding material equivalents, the 1880 act deemed 28 lb of sugar or 42 lb of corn as equivalent to one bushel of malt. Brewers were generally expected to produce at least one barrel of worts (minus a 4% allowance for wastage) for every two bushels of malt or its equivalents. If a brewer's machinery or skill failed to meet this standard, the charge was applied directly to the material rather than the wort.

## Mashing and Production Regulations
The Inland Revenue enforced stringent regulations on mashing operations. Brewers were required to maintain a brewing book and provide notice at least twenty-four hours before mashing began, specifying the day and hour of commencement. Two hours before that notice hour, the brewer had to record the quantity of sugar, corn, and malt to be used, as well as the time when worts would be drawn from the mash-tun.

Further rules required that worts be collected within twelve hours of the start of collection. The brewer had to record the gravity and quantity of the worts before fermentation, the date of entry, and the name and number of the vessel. Worts had to remain undisturbed in the vessel for twelve hours unless an officer had already taken account of them. Additionally, regulations prohibited the alteration of gauged vessels without notice and the mixing of worts from different brewings unless each was accounted for separately.

## The Brewing Process
Brewing is categorized into seven main processes: grinding, mashing, boiling, cooling, fermenting, cleansing, and racking and storing.

**Grinding:** Malt is cleaned, graded, and passed through an automatic measuring machine before being crushed in smooth roller mills. Proper crushing is critical; if the malt is too fine, drainage in the mash-tun and clarification are hindered, but if it is too coarse, the extraction of soluble matter is incomplete.

**Mashing:** This is considered the most important process, as it determines the beer's character. It involves extracting soluble matters from malt and converting insoluble nitrogenous compounds and starch into soluble, partly fermentable products. In modern practice, a mashing machine—a cylindrical metal vessel with a screw—mixes the grist (crushed malt) and liquor (water) before they enter the mash-tun. The mash-tun is a wooden or metal vessel with a perforated false bottom to separate the wort from spent grains. It utilizes "rakes" for stirring and a "sparger"—a rotating sprinkler system—to wash out remaining wort.

When raw grains like rice or maize are used, they are typically gelatinized in a separate "converter" or "cooker" using high-temperature steam before being added to the main mash. Alternatively, the limited-decoction system allows raw grain to be used directly in the mash-tun.

**Subsequent Stages:** The clear wort moves from the mash-tun to the copper for boiling with hops, then to the hop back to separate insoluble constituents. It then passes through a cooler and refrigerator to a collecting vessel for revenue assessment before finally entering fermenting vessels to become "green" beer.

## Technical Variables and Alternatives
The quality of beer depends on the kind of malt and the mashing temperature. Higher temperatures (ranging from 140° to 160° F. for English beers) cripple the enzyme diastase, which converts starch into soluble products. Higher temperatures produce more non-fermentable dextrin, resulting in "dry" beers like pale or stock ales (typically mashed at 150° to 154° F.). Lower temperatures produce more fermentable maltose, resulting in sweet, mild running ales (typically 149° to 154° F.), while stouts and black beers are usually mashed at 148° to 150° F.

To address the inefficiencies of the ordinary mash-tun—such as the loss of extract during sparging or incomplete starch conversion—the filter press process was introduced to Great Britain by P. Meura. This method allows for a finer grind and uses a filter press to separate wort from solid matter, leading to increased yields.
