# Delhi and the Delhi Empire

The city of Delhi served as the center of a vast imperial power that exerted influence across the Indian subcontinent for several centuries. Its history is marked by the rise and fall of various dynasties, the expansion of its frontier, and its eventual transition into a titular authority under British influence.

## Early Imperial Expansion and Administration
The Delhi empire established significant strategic outposts to maintain its northern frontiers. One such location was Budaun, which became a very important post on the northern frontier after its capture by Kutb-ud-din in 1196. During the 13th century, the town's governors, Rukn-ud-din Firoz and Shams-ud-din Altamsh, eventually attained the imperial throne. Altamsh is credited with building a handsome, imposing mosque in Budaun in 1223, constructed largely from the materials of an ancient Hindu temple.

The empire also extended its reach into the Deccan. In 1294, Ala-ud-din Khilji captured the fort of Deogiri. The rajas of this region were considered by the Mussulmans at Delhi to be the rulers of all the Deccan, but following the capture, they were reduced to paying tribute. When tribute fell into arrear, the Mahommedans occupied Deogiri again in 1307, 1310, and 1318.

## The Capital and Territorial Shifts
The seat of government was not always fixed in Delhi. In 1339, Mahommed ben Tughlak Shah decided to move the capital to Deogiri, renaming it Daulatabad ("Abode of Prosperity"). He made arrangements to transfer the entire population of Delhi to this new capital, though the project was interrupted by troubles in the north and revolts by Mussulman governors in the Deccan.

Later, the Delhi emperors maintained possession of Daulatabad until after the death of Aurangzeb, at which point it fell to the first nizam of Hyderabad. The importance of Daulatabad had decayed by this time because the emperors had moved the seat of government to Aurangabad.

## The Later Mogul Empire and British Influence
By the late 18th century, the real power of the Mogul emperors had diminished. Following the defeat of Shah Alam at Buxar in 1764, actual power resided with the East India Company. Although the emperor remained a titular figure, official proclamations continued to be worded under "The King's Realm and the Company's rule."

The symbolic nature of this sovereignty was evident in the Company's financial activities. After the battle of Plassey on June 23, 1757, the Company was permitted to establish a mint as a visible sign of territorial sovereignty in India. The first coin issued by this mint on August 19, 1757, still bore the name of the Delhi emperor.

## Bahadur Shah II and the Mutiny of 1857
Bahadur Shah II served as the last of the Mogul emperors of Hindustan from 1837 to 1857. Described as feeble in character and old during the Mutiny of 1857, he became a figurehead used by the mutinous soldiery in Delhi. These soldiers were controlled by a council known as the Barah Topi, or Twelve Heads. Papers seized after the fall of Delhi revealed that Bahadur Shah II felt disrespect and discourtesy from this council.

During the assault on Delhi, the emperor fled to the Tomb of Humayun, located six miles from the city, where he was captured by Major Hodson. In January 1858, he was brought to trial for complicity in the murder of Europeans and for rebellion. His defense argued that he had been a mere instrument of the mutineers. The trial lasted over two months, ending on March 29, when he was found guilty and sentenced to life imprisonment. He was transported to Rangoon, where he died on November 7, 1862.
