# The Wholesale Bookseller

In the structure of the book trade, particularly as it developed during the 19th century, the wholesale bookseller serves as the critical distributing agency positioned between the publisher and the retail bookseller. This intermediary role is essential because it is simply impossible for a retail bookseller to stock even half of the books being published. Consequently, the retailer relies on the wholesale bookseller for miscellaneous supplies.

## Role and Function in the Trade
The wholesale bookseller acts as a central hub for the distribution of literature. In London, Paternoster Row served as the center of this industry for over a hundred years; there, collectors from retail shops were actively engaged in obtaining books ordered by the reading public. Country booksellers similarly utilize these agencies to obtain their miscellaneous supplies.

The scale of these operations can be immense, with some leading establishments constantly keeping over 1,000,000 books in stock. These houses are also primary sources of information regarding available books. Furthermore, the wholesale bookseller plays a decisive role in the lifecycle of a new publication. When a publisher first "subscribes" or shows a new book to these agencies, the number of copies subscribed can determine the ultimate fate of the book.

## Distinctions Between Trade Roles
By the 19th century, a fundamental distinction emerged between the publisher and the bookseller. The publisher's business involves the production of books from an author's manuscripts, acting as the intermediary between the author and the bookseller. Booksellers, in a restricted sense, act as intermediaries between the author/publisher and the general public.

While the general trade of booksellers consists of selling books—either by retail or wholesale—the roles have occasionally overlapped. A publisher may also function as a wholesale bookseller. In some instances, these functions have amalgamated; for example, in 1902, the London *Times* directly sold supplementary volumes to the 9th edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica*, which they had also published. Additionally, the *Times* Book Club, started in 1905, combined the business of bookselling with a subscription library, utilizing the publicity means of a newspaper to push book sales.

## The Retail Bookseller and Trade Regulation
The retail bookseller is considered the third partner in the publishing process. To protect the interests of retailers, a London booksellers' society was established in 1890. This society aimed to control trade details, arrange prices generally, and restrict discounts to 25%. This organization later expanded its operations to cover the entire United Kingdom, becoming "The Associated Booksellers of Great Britain and Ireland."

Retail bookselling also encompasses specialized trades:
*   **Old or Second-hand Books:** This is regarded as a higher class of business that requires knowledge of bibliography, as transactions involve individual books rather than large numbers of copies. These dealers sometimes replenish stocks by purchasing "remainders"—books that have ceased to sell with the publisher—and offering them as bargains.
*   **Periodical Trade:** This sector grew during the 19th century, aided by the introduction of attractive illustrations, the ability to purchase books by instalments, and the abolition of the duty upon paper and the newspaper stamp.

## International Contexts
The nature of bookselling has varied by region and era. In the 16th and 17th centuries, the Low Countries (including Antwerp, Leiden, Utrecht, and Amsterdam) were the chief centers of the bookselling world, known for the work of Plantin, Blauw, and Jansen, as well as the pocket classics produced by the Elzevirs.

In the United States, bookselling grew more slowly than printing. While printing was introduced to Boston (1676), Philadelphia (1685), and New York (1693), few people were engaged in the trade at the start of the 19th century, and scholars primarily imported books from Europe. Following the second war, the demand for books rose alongside education and newspapers. American publishers initially reprinted the works of British authors—such as Wordsworth, Byron, and Scott—without payment to the authors or proprietors. Between 1820 and 1852, half of the authors in the "American" catalogue were British. Eventually, due to the Civil War, restrictive duties to protect native industry, and high labor costs, American publishers began making liberal offers for early sheets of new publications. Over time, the American publishing business became assimilated to the conditions found in Europe, with Boston, New York, and Philadelphia remaining the primary bookselling centers.
