# The Kalat State and the Reign of Nasir Khan II

The Kalat state, located in Baluchistan, has a history marked by shifting alliances with regional powers and the British government. The political landscape of the region was significantly shaped by the leadership of various khans and their interactions with external empires, including those of Persia, Afghanistan, and Great Britain.

## Early History and the First Nasir Khan
In the 18th century, the sovereignty of the fruitful plain including the chief town of Gandava was held by the Kambaranis. During this period, Nadir Shah of Persia invaded Hindustan and established authority in Baluchistan while stationed at Kandahar. Nadir Shah initially maintained Abdulla Khan in the government of the country. Following Abdulla Khan's death in a battle against the nawabs of Sind, he was succeeded by his eldest son, Haji Mahommed Khan. However, Haji Mahommed Khan's rule was characterized by oppressive taxation and a tyrannical lifestyle, which alienated the population.

Nasir Khan, the second son of Abdulla Khan, had acquired the confidence of Nadir Shah while accompanying him to Delhi. Returning to Kalat, Nasir Khan was welcomed as a deliverer by the population. After stabbing his brother to death, he ascended the *musnud*. In 1739, Nadir Shah appointed him *beglar begi* (prince of princes) of all Baluchistan.

Nasir Khan was a warlike and politic prince who fortified the city of Kalat and reorganized the internal government of his provinces. Following the death of Nadir Shah in 1747, he acknowledged Ahmad Shah (Durani), the king of Kabul. In 1758, Nasir Khan declared his independence, leading to a military conflict. Although Nasir Khan initially routed the Afghan army, he was later worsted in a pitched battle by the king. After a vigorous defense of Kalat, a peace treaty was reached. The terms stipulated that the king would marry Nasir Khan's cousin and that the khan would pay no tribute, though he was required to furnish troops for the king's armies in exchange for a cash allowance equal to half their pay. Nasir Khan later received several districts in perpetual sovereignty as a reward for his services in the wars of Kabul. He died in June 1795, succeeded by his fourteen-year-old son, Mahmud Khan.

## Conflict and the Rise of Nasir Khan II
Under Mahmud Khan, the Kalat state suffered from sanguinary broils and the loss of allegiance from various provincial governors, resulting in a significant reduction of the dominions. By 1839, during the British advance through the Bolan Pass toward Afghanistan, the ruler of Baluchistan, Mehrab Khan, was viewed by the British as treacherous. General Willshire led 1,050 men to assault Kalat, storming the citadel and killing Mehrab Khan and over 400 Baluches. 

Subsequent inquiries revealed that the treachery—specifically the plundering of British convoys in the Bolan Pass and Kach Gandava—was actually the work of Mehrab Khan's vizier, Mahommed Hussein, and allied chiefs. The vizier had manipulated British political officers to ruin Mehrab Khan in hopes of seizing power for himself, as Mehrab's successor was a child. Following a period of instability where a British-established governor was overpowered and the British reoccupied the area under General Nott, the British recognized the youthful son of Mehrab Khan, Nasir Khan II, in 1841.

## The Treaty of 1854 and British Relations
Between the British conquest of Sind in 1843 and 1854, there was no notable diplomatic intercourse between the British and the Baluch states. In 1854, under Governor-General the Marquess of Dalhousie, General John Jacob was tasked with concluding a treaty between the British government and the Kalat state under the chieftainship of Nasir Khan II.

Executed on May 14, 1854, this treaty annulled a previous offensive and defensive agreement from 1841. Under the new terms, Nasir Khan II, his heirs, and his successors bound themselves to:
* Oppose all enemies of the British government to the utmost.
* Act in subordinate co-operation with the British government in all cases.
* Refrain from entering negotiations with other states without British consent.

Additionally, the treaty allowed British troops to occupy any positions within the territory of Kalat deemed advisable by British authorities. Nasir Khan II was also required to prevent his subjects from plundering within or near British territory.

## Succession and Decline
Nasir Khan II performed the provisions of the 1854 treaty loyally until his death in 1856. He was succeeded by his brother, Mir Khodadud Khan, who was twelve years old at the time. Khodadud Khan's ascension was preceded by his forceful suppression of a rebellion by turbulent chiefs who had attempted to depose him in favor of his cousin, Sher dil Khan.

During the Indian rebellion of 1857, Major Henry Green was appointed as a British political agent at Kalat to advise the Khan on controlling the tribes. This arrangement continued until 1863. During the absence of the then-agent, Major Malcolm Green, Sher dil Khan attacked and severely wounded Khodadud Khan. Sher dil Khan was proclaimed Khan, but his rule was brief; he was murdered in the Gandava Pass in early 1864.

Khodadud Khan was re-elected as chief by the same men who had previously overthrown him. However, he struggled to govern his unruly subjects. Because the British government was unwilling to provide the material assistance necessary to maintain order, the country fell into anarchy. Due to the breach of several provisions of the 1854 treaty, diplomatic relations between the British and the Kalat state were discontinued after the end of 1874.
