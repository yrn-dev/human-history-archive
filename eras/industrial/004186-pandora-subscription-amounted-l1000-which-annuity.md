# Pandora

The name Pandora appears in historical and mythological records in several distinct contexts, ranging from ancient Greek legends to the works of later artists.

## Greek Mythology and Legends
In the Hesiodic traditions, Pandora is a central figure in legends relating to heroes and men. According to these accounts, Pandora is associated with the introduction of mischiefs arising from women. While common versions of the story suggest that Pandora brought a cask with her, other interpretations clarify that the cask was already in the custody of man or Epimêtheus, and Pandora's role was the "fatal treachery" of removing its lid.

This act is compared to the instance where the companions of Odysseus forced open a closed bag of unfavorable winds given by Æolus, leading to the ruin of Odysseus's hopes. The concept of two casks on the threshold of Zeus—one containing benefits and the other evils—is described as Homeric. In the case of Pandora's cask, the diseases and evils remained inoperative while shut; however, the same influence that released them ensured that Hope remained a powerless prisoner inside the cask.

Additionally, the names Protogeneia and Pandora are listed by Photius and Suidas as the names of two Parthenoi. There is also a reference in the Iambi of Hippônax to the sacrifice of a Pandora, which appears to refer to a daughter of Erechtheus.

## Artistic Representation
In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, Pandora served as the subject of a painting by Barry. Barry had been a member of the Royal Academy and served as a professor of painting starting in 1782, receiving a salary of L30 per year. Following his expulsion from the academy in 1799—which occurred shortly after he published the eccentric *Letter to the Dilettanti Society*—Barry faced financial difficulties.

To assist him, the earl of Buchan organized a subscription to provide Barry with a larger house so that he could finish his picture of Pandora. This subscription raised L1000, which was used to purchase an annuity. Barry subsequently fell ill on February 6, 1806, and died on February 22 of that year. He was interred in St Paul's on March 14.
