# Types of Bridges

Bridges are categorized primarily by the mechanical forces acting upon their principal members. These classifications include arched bridges, where the principal members are in compression; suspension bridges, where the principal members are in tension; and girder bridges, in which half of the principal members' components are in tension and half are in compression. In some instances, bridges are constructed as mixed types.

## Structural Components
A bridge is divided into two primary sections: the superstructure and the substructure.

The superstructure contains the main supporting members, which may consist of arched ribs, an arch ring, suspension ropes or chains, or a pair of beams, trusses, or girders. The bridge flooring rests upon these supporting members and varies based on the bridge's purpose. Large bridges may also include wind-bracing to provide stiffness against horizontal forces.

The substructure consists of the foundations below ground level and the piers and abutments. Foundations are often costly and difficult to construct because bridge sites are not always naturally adapted for heavy structures. Piers sustain vertical loads, while end piers or abutments must resist the pull of suspension chains, the thrust of an embankment, or the oblique thrust of an arch.

## Design Considerations and Materials
The selection of a bridge type depends on several complex factors:
*   **Cost and Materials:** For moderate spans, concrete, masonry, or brick are used. Steel is more economical for longer spans and is imperative for very long spans.
*   **Permanence and Maintenance:** Masonry and concrete are more durable than metal, which in turn is more durable than timber.
*   **Aesthetics:** Masonry bridges are generally preferred for their appearance, particularly in towns, while metal arch bridges are considered less objectionable than most girder forms.

## Specific Bridge Types

### Arched Bridges
Metal arch bridges have a long history, beginning with the first iron bridge constructed by Abraham Darby and John Wilkinson between 1773 and 1779 at Coalbrookdale over the Severn. This bridge featured five cast iron arched ribs and a center span of 100 ft. Other early cast iron examples include the Wearmouth bridge (1793-1796) with a 235 ft. span, the Southwark bridge (1814-1819) with a 240 ft. center span, and the Austerlitz and Carrousel bridges in Paris. In 1858, M.C. Meigs erected an aqueduct bridge in Washington with a 200 ft. span, using two cast iron pipes as arched ribs.

Modern examples include the Niagara Falls and Clifton steel arch, which features a two-hinged parabolic braced rib arch. The Victoria Falls bridge over the Zambezi, completed in 1905 and designed by Sir Douglas Fox, is a combination of arch and girder. It has a total length of 650 ft. and a parabolic center arch with a 500 ft. span.

### Suspension Bridges
Suspension bridges became viable for longer spans at the end of the 18th century with the introduction of iron link chains, and later, wire ropes. These bridges eliminate the need for the compression members found in girders and the extensive stiffening required for metal arches. However, they require massive anchorages and lofty towers. Their primary defect is flexibility, though they can be stiffened with bracing and girders to become mixed-type bridges. This stiffened variety is expected to be the standard for very great spans; for example, a 3200 ft. span bridge has been projected for New York.

### Girder and Cantilever Bridges
In the United States, railway bridges often follow standardized designs. Riveted plate girders are used for spans up to 50 ft., riveted braced girders for 50 to 75 ft., and pin-connected girders for longer spans. 

Cantilever bridges, such as the Jubilee bridge over the Hugli designed by Sir Bradford Leslie, utilize specific girder types. The Jubilee bridge features Whipple Murphy girders with curved top booms, a central double cantilever 360 ft. long, and side span girders of 420 ft.

### Movable Bridges
Movable bridges are designed to be closed for road, railway, or aqueduct use, but opened to allow navigation. One such type is the lifting bridge, where the platform is suspended from girders by chains and counterweights at the four corners, allowing it to be lifted vertically.

## Construction and Engineering
Some bridges are built out from the abutments, using the completed sections as erecting stages for lifting appliances. In the construction of the St Louis bridge, half arches were built from each pier to balance the load, supported by chains from skeleton towers. The Douro bridge used side span girders that overhung the great span by 105 ft. to provide a platform for suspending the arch.

The pressure exerted on the substructure varies by bridge type. Girder superstructures create vertical pressure on piers. Arched superstructures require abutments that transmit resultant thrust to the foundation in a safe direction. Suspension bridge abutments must be stable enough to withstand the maximum pull exerted by the chains.
