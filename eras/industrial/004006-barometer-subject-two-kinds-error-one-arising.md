# Barometers and Measurement Errors

A barometer is an instrument used to measure the pressure or weight of the atmosphere. These instruments vary in design, from those using mercury to those employing metallic chambers. While some devices, known as baroscopes, merely indicate variations in atmospheric pressure without providing quantitative data, others, called barographs, automatically record these variations.

## Types of Barometers

The ordinary or mercurial barometer typically consists of a tube approximately 36 inches long, containing mercury and hermetically closed at the top. There are several specific configurations of this instrument:

*   **Cistern Barometer:** The tube is placed with its open end in a basin, or cistern, of mercury. Pressure is measured by the difference in height between the mercury in the tube and the mercury in the cistern.
*   **Siphon Barometer:** This version dispenses with the cistern; instead, the tube is bent into a siphon shape. The height is determined by taking the difference between the readings of the lower and upper limbs.
*   **Aneroid Barometer:** Derived from Greek terms meaning "not wet," this instrument uses no liquid. It relies on the volume changes of an exhausted metallic chamber under varying pressures.
*   **Sympiezometer:** Invented in 1818 by Adie of Edinburgh, this is a revived form of Hooke's marine barometer. It uses a glass tube with a small chamber at the top and an open cistern below, filled with glycerin. While sensitive and suitable for travel or use at sea, it is not intended for exact observation and has been largely superseded by the aneroid barometer.

## Errors in the Cistern Barometer

The cistern barometer is subject to two primary types of error: capillarity and the error of capacity.

### Capillarity
Capillarity occurs when a small-bore glass tube is plunged into mercury; the mercury level in the tube remains somewhat below the level of the mercury in the vessel, creating a convex surface. This capillary depression is inversely proportional to the tube's diameter. In standard barometers with a diameter of about one inch, this error is less than .001 of an inch. Because capillarity depresses the column height, an addition must be made to the observed height to determine the true pressure.

### Error of Capacity
The error of capacity relates to the changing level of the mercury surface in the cistern. The height of the barometer is the perpendicular distance between the cistern's mercury surface and the upper surface of the mercurial column. When the barometer falls (for example, from 30 to 29 inches), an inch of mercury flows from the tube into the cistern, raising the cistern level. Conversely, when the barometer rises, mercury flows from the cistern into the tube, lowering the cistern level. Because the scales are usually engraved on fixed brass cases with a fixed zero-point, these constant changes in the cistern level can cause readings to be too high or too low.

## Correcting and Eliminating Errors

There are several methods for addressing these inaccuracies:

1.  **Manual Correction:** One may ascertain the "neutral point" (where the scale's zero is exactly at the cistern surface height) and the rate of error as the barometer rises or falls above that point, applying a proportional correction.
2.  **Fortin's Barometer:** This instrument entirely removes the error of capacity. It features a glass cylinder for the upper part of the cistern so the mercury level is visible. The bottom is a flexible leather bag operated by a screw. A small piece of ivory at the top of the cistern interior coincides with the zero of the scale. The screw allows the user to raise or lower the mercury level until it exactly meets the ivory point. In some versions, the cistern is fixed and the scale is moved instead.
3.  **Siphon Design:** The siphon barometer requires no correction for either capacity or capillarity. However, its functionality can be hindered by impurities contracted by the mercury in the lower limb, which is in open contact with the air.
4.  **Temperature Adjustment:** In the sympiezometer, a sliding-scale and thermometer are added to correct for errors caused by varying temperatures affecting the pressure of the contained air.

## Measurement Scales and Standards

Barometric height is expressed in English inches in America and England. In France and most of Europe, the metric system is used, measuring height in millimetres (where one millimetre is the thousandth part of a metre, equaling 39.37079 English inches). Russia used half-lines (one-twentieth of an English inch) until 1869, after which it adopted the metric scale. Some European countries use the Paris line (0.088814 in.).

Because different scales have different standard temperatures—the English measure at 62° Fahr., the old French at 61.2°, and the metric at 32°—observations must be reduced to the same temperature to neutralize expansion caused by heat.

## Specialized Phenomena and Recording

**Barometric Light** is a luminous glow emitted by mercury in a barometer tube when shaken. First observed by Jean Picard and later studied by Francis Hawksbee, this glow is caused by electricity generated by the friction between the air and the mercury. Hawksbee demonstrated that a Torricellian vacuum is not required for this to occur, as the glow appears even when air is only partially rarefied.

To create a self-registering mercurial barometer, a lens concentrates light onto the top of the mercurial column. A sheet of sensitized paper moves on a drum behind a screen with a vertical slit. The opaque mercury casts a shadow, while the area above the mercury allows light to pass, creating a continuous photograph of the column's height.
