# BANK RATE

## Definition and General Concept
The bank rate is a term utilized within financial circles to describe the rate of discount charged by a leading bank or the state in chief monetary centres. This rate is distinguished from the open-market rate.

## The Bank of England and Market Fluctuations
In the English money market, the bank rate of discount is characterized by constant fluctuations. These fluctuations have increased since the passing of the Bank Act of 1844. The act divided the bank's specie into two parts, and the smaller of these parts—the "Reserve" in the banking department—must absorb the full strain of any demands for specie or notes. 

Between the implementation of the act in 1844 and the end of 1906, there were more than 400 changes in the rate. These severe fluctuations occur even in ordinary years, and the hopes expressed by Sir Robert Peel in 1844 that the act would end commercial crises were not realized.

## The Reichsbank and Private Banks
In Germany, the Reichsbank utilizes its bank rate as a tool to protect its gold reserves. An arrangement exists between the Reichsbank and four private note-issuing banks to ensure they work in harmony. Under the amending act of June 7, 1899 (specifically Artikel 7, S. 2, binding after January 1, 1901), private note-issuing banks are subject to the following rules regarding the official published rate of discount:

*   If the official rate reaches or exceeds 4%, the private banks are bound not to discount below that rate.
*   At other times, private banks may not discount at more than 1/4% below the official Reichsbank rate.
*   If the Reichsbank itself discounts at a rate lower than the official rate (a fact it must announce in the *Gazette*), private banks may not discount at more than 1/8% below that lower rate.

## Interest Rates and Panic Prevention
The relationship between interest rates and market stability is a point of significant discussion. In the case of the Reichsbank and the treasury of the empire of Germany, the objective is to prevent panic and provide necessary banking accommodation. Consequently, a moderate rate of 5% has generally been charged, although it rose to 7% for a short period in 1899. This moderate approach resulted in higher profit and allowed business to proceed normally; between 1881 and the end of 1906, the duty on the extra issue amounted to £839,052, providing relief for taxation.

Conversely, proposals to grant the Bank of England automatic power to augment note issues on securities faced hindrances from the government and treasury. A proposal by Mr. Lowe (later Lord Sherbrooke) in 1873 suggested a charge of 12%. Such a high rate was viewed as presupposing panic, as high rates are generally associated with business risks. Because of this, the negotiations failed, and the act of 1844 remained unaltered.

## International Comparisons
The stability of the bank rate varies by nation based on specie reserves. In France, the Bank of France maintains a very large specie reserve—holding an average of £115,000,000 in gold and £42,000,000 in silver in 1906. This reserve is generally larger than that held by the Bank of England, which was forced to borrow £3,000,000 in gold from the Bank of France during the Baring crisis in the autumn of 1890. 

The large reserve in France has provided stability to French trade and allowed the bank to manage business without the numerous fluctuations in the rate of discount that are constant in England. This results in a low rate of interest for borrowed capital, benefiting French industry and trade, despite the heavy burden the reserve places on the bank's shareholders.
