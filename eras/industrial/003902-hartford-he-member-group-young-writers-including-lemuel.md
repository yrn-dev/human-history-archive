# The Hartford Wits

The "Hartford Wits" were a group of young writers based in Hartford, Connecticut, who hold a recognized place in American literary history. Among the members of this circle were Lemuel Hopkins, David Humphreys, John Trumbull, and Joel Barlow.

## Joel Barlow and the Hartford Circle
Joel Barlow (1754–1812) was a prominent member of the Hartford Wits. Born in Redding, Fairfield county, Connecticut, on March 24, 1754, Barlow was educated at Yale, where he graduated in 1778 and remained as a post-graduate student for an additional two years. During the revolutionary war, from September 1780 until the conflict's conclusion, he served as a chaplain in a Massachusetts brigade.

Barlow relocated to Hartford in 1783. In July 1784, he established a weekly newspaper titled the *American Mercury*, remaining connected to the publication for one year. He was subsequently admitted to the bar in 1786. During his time in Hartford, Barlow contributed to the *Anarchiad*, a series of papers characterized as satirico-political. In 1787, he published *The Vision of Columbus*, an ambitious and long poem that earned him a significant literary reputation and became widely read.

## Later Career of Joel Barlow
In 1788, Barlow traveled to France to serve as an agent for the Scioto Land Company with the goals of enlisting immigrants and selling lands. Although he induced a company of Frenchmen to emigrate to America—who eventually founded Gallipolis, Ohio—the Scioto Land Company failed disastrously in 1790. Barlow appeared to be unaware of the company's fraudulent nature.

While residing in Paris, Barlow adopted advanced republican political views and became a liberal in religion. He spent several years abroad, including significant time in London, where he was a member of the "London Society for Constitutional Information." In 1792, he became a citizen of France and published *Advice to the Privileged Orders*, a volume of radical essays that the British government proscribed.

Barlow later served as the American consul at Algiers from 1795 to 1797, a period during which he negotiated a treaty with Tripoli (1796) and secured the release of American prisoners held for ransom. He returned to the United States in 1805 and lived near Washington, D.C. In 1811, he was appointed American plenipotentiary to France to secure indemnity or restitution for confiscated American property and to negotiate a commercial treaty with Napoleon.
