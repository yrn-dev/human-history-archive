# Draught

In the context of boilers and industrial furnaces, draught refers to the mechanism by which air is supplied for combustion and the resulting products of combustion are removed. The method of producing this draught varies significantly depending on the application, the type of fuel used, and the required rate of combustion.

## Types of Boiler Draught
In most boilers, the draught necessary for combustion is "natural," meaning it is produced by a chimney. However, in marine environments, while natural draught remains common, many installations utilize "forced" draught arrangements to increase efficiency and power.

### Forced Draught Systems
There are two primary systems for forced draught in marine boilers:
*   **Closed Stokehold:** In this system, the stokehold compartment of the vessel is sealed so that air can only exit through the fires. Fans drive air into the compartment to maintain an air pressure higher than the outside atmosphere. This method is used almost universally in war vessels and some fast passenger ships. In large vessels, the air pressure typically corresponds to a height of 1 to 1-1/2 inches of water, though torpedo boats and similar craft may use up to 4 inches. This pressure operates in addition to the chimney-draught provided by the funnel's height.
*   **Closed Ashpit (Howden System):** In this arrangement, the stokehold remains open. Fans drive air through tubes located in the uptake, where the air is heated by the products of combustion. This heated air is then delivered partly into the ashpit below the fire and partly into a casing around the furnace front, entering the furnace above the fire.

In locomotive boilers, the draught is produced by the exhaust steam or the blast.

## Combustion Rates and Boiler Design
The type of draught employed directly affects the rate of fuel combustion and the physical design of the boiler. With natural draught, combustion rates are approximately 15 to 20 lb. of coal per square foot of grate area per hour. Forced draught allows for much higher rates:
*   **Larger vessels (moderate pressure):** 20 to 35 lb. per sq. ft.
*   **Express boilers (torpedo boats/similar craft):** 70 to 80 lb. per sq. ft.

These "express boilers," often used in cruisers and torpedo-boat destroyers to maximize power relative to weight, utilize a high degree of forced draught and feature tubes of smaller diameter that are more closely arranged.

The choice of draught also influences tube dimensions. Boilers using natural or chimney draught generally have tubes with an outside diameter of 3 or 3-1/4 inches and lengths rarely exceeding 7 feet. In contrast, those employing forced draught typically use tubes with a 2-1/2 inch diameter and lengths of 8 to 8-1/2 feet. Regardless of size, a clear space of 1-1/4 inches is almost always maintained between tubes.

## Industrial and Specialized Applications
Draught mechanisms are also critical in non-boiler industrial settings:

### Refuse Destructors
The Beaman and Deas destructor utilizes a closed ash-pit with forced draught. Air is supplied by fans at a pressure of 1-1/2 to 2 inches of water, discharged through 12-inch diameter blast pipes into a hermetically closed ash-pit. This forced draught serves to keep the fire-bars cool and reduce wear and tear.

### Brick-Kilns
Brick-kilns are categorized by their draught direction:
*   **Up-draught kilns:** Older forms where combustion products pass from the fire-mouth through flues into the bottom of the firing-chamber and exit directly out the top.
*   **Down-draught kilns:** Modern plans where products of combustion are introduced near the crown and drawn downwards through the bricks to flues connected to an independent chimney. These are more economical in fuel consumption and provide a more regular fire.

## Fuel Considerations and Waste Heat
The nature of the fuel can impact the draught. Wood, as a flaming fuel, is well-suited for marine and locomotive boilers. However, when a rapid draught is used with wood, ignited particles are often projected from the chimney due to a lack of cohesion in the cinders, necessitating the use of spark-catchers made of wire gauze.

To improve efficiency, "fuel economizers" (tubes containing water) may be placed between the boiler and the chimney to capture heat from gases that are still well above the temperature required to maintain an active draught. In iron manufacture, waste heat from exhaust gases is often saved by passing flames through air-heating apparatus or steam boiler flues. The Siemens regenerator further optimizes this by passing spent gases through cellular masses of thin firebricks to reduce their temperature to 300 or 400 degrees before they reach the chimney.
