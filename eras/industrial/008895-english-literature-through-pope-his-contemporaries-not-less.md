# The Influence of Boileau and the Development of 18th-Century English Literature

## The Influence of Boileau
The French writer Boileau held a significant position in French literature as the first to establish rules for versification and emphasize the value of workmanship. His influence extended to English literature, specifically through Alexander Pope and his contemporaries, though this impact was less durable than it was in France. 

Boileau's maxims influenced English letters through a translation revised by Dryden and through Pope's *Essay on Criticism*, which is described as a magnificent imitation of Boileau's work. Furthermore, Boileau's mock heroic poem, *Le Lutrin* (1674), provided the model for Pope's *The Rape of the Lock*, although the English work is considered superior in its richness of imagination. Boileau's critical work, which included the analysis of various verse compositions and the inculcation of *bon sens* in his *L'Art poetique*, has been rehabilitated by recent writers despite some individual instances of undue harshness.

## The Age of Pope and the Verse-Epistle
Alexander Pope is regarded as the great writer of epistles in English, and the glory of this kind of verse belongs to him. While the verse-epistle had been employed earlier by writers such as Samuel Daniel, Ben Jonson, John Donne, and later Dryden, Pope achieved brilliant and complete success in the form. His "Eloisa to Abelard" (1717) was modeled on Ovid's *Heroides*, while his *Moral Essays* adopted the Horatian formula. His "Epistle to Dr Arbuthnot" is noted as a work that has not been surpassed, or at least has only been equalled, by similar poetry in French or Latin.

Beyond the epistle, Pope was a central figure in the "cosmopolitan age" of 18th-century English letters. He was the first Englishman to treat letter-writing as an art on a considerable scale and is cited, alongside Churchill, as a representative of the satire, one of the fashionable literary types of the century.

## Characteristics of 18th-Century English Literature
The 18th century is described as a "veritable index-museum of English prose." During the reign of Anne (1702-1714), literature became increasingly metropolitan or suburban as provincial feeling diminished and books began to penetrate all classes of society. This period saw a development of nationality in letters and the emergence of commercial considerations.

The literature of this era is characterized as being essentially rational, social, and translatable, though it is noted as being deficient in eccentricity, exquisiteness, and intensity. It was pre-eminently an age of prose, featuring various styles:
* **Aristocratic:** Bolingbroke, Addison, and Berkeley.
* **Gentlemanly:** Fielding.
* **Keen and logical controversy:** Butler, Middleton, Smith, and Bentham.
* **Rhythmic and balanced:** Johnson and his admirers.
* **Limpid and flowing:** Hume and Mackintosh.

## Evolution of Literary Types and Consumption
The 18th century witnessed a shift in how literature was produced and consumed. The market ceased to be regulated by what learned men wished to write and instead shifted toward what an increasing multitude wanted to read. This was marked by the transition from the folio to the octavo; the arrival of the octavo is associated with the enrolment of man as a reader, while the novel is associated with the attachment of woman.

This shift led to the decay of printed drama, theology, epic poetry in ponderous tomes, and lyrical verse. In their place, new forms flourished:
* **The Essay and Satire:** Represented by Addison, Steele, Swift, Goldsmith, Pope, and Churchill.
* **The Novel:** Prepared for by the growth of plain biography (Defoe) and the fancy essay (Addison).
* **Other developments:** A significant growth in criticism, journalism, and topography.

## Later Developments and the Aesthetic School
Following the 18th century, English literature saw the emergence of the virtuoso or aesthetic school, of which John Keats (1795-1821) was the first. Keats viewed artistic beauty as a kind of religion. Unlike his contemporaries—such as Byron, who focused on himself, or Coleridge, who was a metaphysician—Keats centered his work on the enchantment of the subject, as seen in "A Nightingale," "A Greek Urn," and the season of "Autumn." 

Keats is identified as a forerunner to Tennyson, the "greatest of the word-painters," particularly through his medievalising poems: "The Eve of St Agnes," "The Pot of Basil," and "La Belle Dame Sans Merci." His poetry is credited with giving a new beauty to the English language.
