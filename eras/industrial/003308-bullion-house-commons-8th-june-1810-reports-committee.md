# Bullion, House of Commons, 8th of June 1810; Reports, Committee of

The *Report, together with Minutes of Evidence and Accounts, from the Select Committee on the High Price of Gold Bullion* is a parliamentary paper issued by the House of Commons on the 8th of June 1810. This document, commonly referred to as the *Bullion Report*, was the result of a parliamentary committee investigation into the depreciation of bank-notes.

## Leadership and Objectives
The investigation was moved in 1810 by Francis Horner, who served as the chairman of the parliamentary committee. Horner was a specialist in political economy and was noted for his intellectual and moral influence over the House of Commons. Despite a speaking style described as earnest and nervous rather than eloquent, he was capable of maintaining the attention of the House for hours on technical subjects including currency, coinage, and finance.

The primary objective of the committee was to investigate the depreciation of bank-notes. Through the efforts of Horner and the resulting *Bullion Report*, the paper-issue of English banks was checked. This process served to reinstate silver and gold in their true positions as circulating media.

## Context of Bullion
In a general economic sense, bullion refers to gold and silver from mines that have been brought to a standard of purity. The term is historically linked to the French word *bouillon*, with the concept of "boiling" being transferred in English to the melting of metal; consequently, early usage of the term in English acts (such as one from 1336) referred to a mint or "melting-house." By an act of 1451, the word was recorded as referring to the precious metal itself in the mass.

In the context of commerce, bullion—whether appearing as bars, ingots, or coins—is generally subject to assay as well as weight in the London market to determine its value. Because gold and silver are used as media of exchange, nations strive for a common degree of fineness. The proportion of carats of alloy to carats of fine gold, and the alloy in silver, are prime elements of value for bullion and manufactured articles such as jewelry and plate.

## Related Parliamentary Inquiries
The *Bullion Report* of 1810 is part of a broader history of House of Commons inquiries into banking and financial distress. Subsequent reports and committee activities include:
*   The *Reports, Committee of Secrecy on Bank of England Charter* (1832).
*   The *Select Committee on Banks of Issue* (1840, 1841, and 1875).
*   The *Secret Committee on Commercial Distress* (First and Second Reports, 1848).
*   The *Select Committee on Bank Acts* (1857 and 1858).

The 1857 and 1858 reports on the Bank Acts followed a crisis that served as the last occasion for an official inquiry. Evidence from the governor, Mr. Sheffield Neave, and deputy-governor, Mr. Bonamy Dobree, detailed a period of wild alarm where the bank sold £3,000,000 in government securities at a loss and exchequer bills were scarcely saleable.
