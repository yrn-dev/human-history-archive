# Banking Failures and Development in Ireland

## Early Banking Failures and Instability
The number of banks that failed in Ireland during earlier times was described as extraordinary. According to a report from the committee of Irish exchanges that sat in 1804, there were fifty registered banks at that period, all of which failed. Sir Robert Peel, in a speech delivered on June 9, 1845, regarding the Bank Act of that year, noted that these failures led to "the most fearful distress."

The instability of the era was further reflected in the views of the common people. In one instance, a Dublin banker named Beresford became very unpopular, leading a large group of "ignorant country people" to collect a quantity of his notes and burn them publicly. As the promises to pay on demand were consumed, the crowd cried out, "What will he do now; his bank will surely break."

## Legislative Constraints and the Bank of Ireland
The early history of Irish banking was characterized by legislation that was less favorable to the creation of a dependable and steady system than the legislation found in England. In 1695, several principal merchants in Dublin attempted to form a public bank for Ireland based on the model of the Bank of England, but this proposal received no favor for many years.

The Bank of Ireland was eventually established and began business in 1783, with David La Touche, junior, serving as the first governor and two other members of the La Touche family serving on the first board of directors. While the bank was very successful, there was extreme jealousy toward rival establishments. The act that formed the Bank of Ireland stipulated that no company or society exceeding six in number—with the exception of the Bank of Ireland—could borrow or take up money on their notes or bills payable on demand.

This restriction hindered the establishment of other banks for a long time. It was not until 1821 that the act was modified to allow banking companies exceeding six in number to be established, provided they were at a distance of 50 miles from Dublin. An additional act was passed in 1824 to explain the ambiguity of the 1821 act. The 50-mile limit was finally withdrawn in 1845.

## Development and the Bank Act of 1845
The development of banking in Ireland closely followed the process seen in England, where circulation preceded and fed deposits. Customers were brought to bank counters by the ready acceptance of notes, and the system was built gradually through the efforts of excellent managers.

Under the legislation of 1845, the business of banking in Ireland achieved a level of success and steadiness equal to any other part of the United Kingdom. Sir Robert Peel implemented a different policy for Ireland and Scotland than he did for England. Under the acts of 1844–1845, Irish and Scottish banks were permitted to exceed their authorized issues if they held specie (gold) equal to the amount of the excess. This was designed to ensure that the circulating medium—consisting of both notes and specie—would fluctuate as if it were a metallic circulation only, thereby ensuring the uniform equivalency of bank notes to coin.

## Banking Infrastructure and Status (1844–1906)
By 1844, the *Banking Almanac* recorded 180 banking offices in Ireland. At that time, there were 45,417 inhabitants for every office. By 1906, the number of offices had increased to 777, reducing the ratio to 5,738 inhabitants per office.

Ireland maintained the same limit of authorized circulation established by Peel's Act of 1845, and none of the six banks that held the privilege of issue at that time had lost it by 1906. Of the nine banks operating in Ireland, six possessed the power of issuing notes. The joint-stock banks operating in Ireland by 1906 included:
* Bank of Ireland (est. 1783)
* Hibernian Bank (est. 1824)
* Provincial Bank (est. 1825)
* Northern Banking Co. (est. 1825)
* Belfast Banking Co. (est. 1827)
* National Bank (est. 1835)
* Ulster Banking Co. (est. 1836)
* Royal Bank (est. 1836)
* Munster Bank, Ltd. (est. 1864)

The Hibernian Bank, Royal Bank, and Munster Bank, Ltd. were not banks of issue. In May 1907, the authorized issue for 12 private banks was £482,744 and for 17 joint-stock banks was £1,084,836, though actual circulation remained significantly lower.
