# Drift

In the context of ballistics, drift refers to the phenomenon where an elongated shot fired from a rifled gun does not travel within a vertical plane. Instead, the shot moves as if the mean plane of its trajectory is inclined to the true vertical at a small angle, typically 2 or 3 degrees. This angle is known as the permanent angle of deflection, denoted as [delta]. To ensure a shot hits its intended mark, the back sight must be tilted to the vertical at this same angle.

## Physical Causes and Characteristics
The exact reason for drift is not yet fully understood, though it is evidently a gyroscopic effect. This is demonstrated by two primary factors: the direction of the drift is reversed if the rifling twist is changed from right-handed to left-handed, and the effect increases as the rotation of the shot increases.

In a vacuum, the axis of an elongated shot would move parallel to itself. However, in the presence of air, a couple caused by sidelong motion tends to position the axis at right angles to the trajectory's tangent. For a rotating body, this causes the axis to precess about the tangent. Simultaneously, frictional drag damps the nutation, causing the axis of the shot to follow the tangent of the trajectory very closely. In the case of a right-handed twist, the point of the shot is observed to be slightly to the right and above the tangent.

The resulting movement is as if a mean sidelong thrust, expressed as w tan [delta], is acting on the shot from left to right, thereby deflecting the plane of the trajectory at angle [delta] to the vertical.

## Theoretical Limitations and Comparisons
Despite these observations, no formula derived from theoretical principles and physical data has yet been invented to calculate a definite magnitude for [delta]. 

A similar effect is observable in sports such as cricket, base-ball, golf, and tennis. However, the effect in these sports is explained by the inequality of pressure caused by a vortex of air carried along by the rotating ball. This deviation occurs in the opposite direction of the drift observed in artillery practice. Because of this discrepancy, artillerists continue to seek a definitive theory and crucial experiment to explain the phenomenon.
