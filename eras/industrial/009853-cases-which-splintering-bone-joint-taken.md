# Bone Fractures and Joint Complications

## Overview of Bone Fractures
A bone fracture may occur through direct violence, where the bone is broken at the point of impact (generally resulting in a transverse fracture), or through indirect violence caused by a strain (generally resulting in an oblique fracture). In some instances, fractures are caused by muscular action, such as a violent cough breaking a rib. While fully developed bones break across, the soft bones found in young people may simply bend, a condition referred to as willow fracture or green stick fracture.

Fractures are categorized as either simple or compound. A simple fracture is comparable to a subcutaneous laceration of the soft parts. A compound fracture is analogous to an open wound, which may be caused by the force that broke the bone or by a sharp point of the fractured bone piercing the skin. Compound fractures carry a higher risk because they allow germs of suppuration to reach the injured bone, which can lead to blood-poisoning, osteomyelitis, necrosis (death of large pieces of bone), and delayed union.

## Diagnosis and General Treatment
In most fracture cases, *crepitus*—the feeling produced when two rough osseous surfaces rub together—can be detected. However, crepitus is absent if the bone is merely bent or if the broken extremities are driven into one another, known as an impacted fracture.

To achieve firm bony union, the fragments must be accurately apposed. The process of putting the broken ends together is called "setting the fracture," and splints are used to provide the necessary rest. It is generally advisable to use a splint to fix the joint located above or below the site of the fracture.

## Splintering into a Joint
In cases where a splintering of the bone into a joint has occurred, there is a risk of effusion into the tendon sheaths and the joint, particularly if tendons have also been injured. This effusion may organize into fibrous tissue, which can lead to permanent stiffness; this outcome is especially common in elderly patients.

To prevent this stiffness and keep the tendons and joint free, care must be taken through passive movement and gentle exercises during the healing process. For example, in a fracture located close to the wrist joint, the splint must be arranged to allow the patient to move the thumb and fingers. Additionally, the splint must be removed daily so the fingers and wrist can be straightened, bent, and exercised gently.

## Specialized Fracture Cases
Treatment for a recent fracture of the patella (knee-cap) varies among surgeons. Some utilize a back-splint or plaster of Paris splints, awaiting a fibrous bond of union. While many recover a useful limb this way, some are left with an enfeebled limb if the fragments remain wide apart. 

Alternative surgical methods include:
* **Lister's Method:** Opening the knee-joint and securing fragments in close apposition using a stout wire suture to ensure solid bony union.
* **Barker's Method:** Using a large needle to pass a strong silver wire vertically around the fragments without opening the joint, intended to reduce the risk of septic micro-organisms invading the joint.
* **Sutures:** Using silk or metal sutures passed circumferentially around the fragments to maintain contact.

## Related Bone Diseases
Septic osteomyelitis is an infective disease where micro-organisms germinate in damaged bone and may enter the blood-stream, potentially causing blood-poisoning. The disease often begins in the growing tissue between the cartilaginous end and the diaphysis (the main part of the bone shaft), frequently causing pain above or below the wrist, elbow, ankle, or knee. In infants, these germs may move into a neighboring joint, resulting in acute arthritis of infants.

Treatment for such diseases involves making an incision to the bone, scraping out the affected area, and disinfecting it with a solution of corrosive sublimate. In serious cases, the shaft of the bone may need to be opened to clear pus and germs. If the diaphysis is found loose in a large abscess cavity, or if there is grave constitutional disturbance and long-continued suppuration, immediate or later amputation of the limb may be necessary to save the patient's life.
