# Education and Language Laws of 1867

The law of 1867 and the accompanying constitution established critical frameworks for education and linguistic rights within the empire, particularly concerning the relationship between the German-speaking population and the various Slav races.

## The Law of 1867 and Slav Education
Under the law of 1867, it was required that education in elementary schools located in Slav districts be conducted in either Slovenian or Czech, depending on the specific district. While this established a baseline for instruction, the Slav populations sought further concessions. They requested that even in towns where they constituted only a small minority, they should not be forced to attend German schools but should instead have their own schools provided. This demand was granted by Prazak, who served as the minister of education under Count Taaffe.

The introduction of Slavonic languages into higher and middle schools became a point of contention with the Germans, who maintained that German was the *Kultur-sprache*. For example, Count A. Auersperg (Anastasius Gruen) once brought the entirety of Slovenian literature into the diet of Carniola to argue that the Slovenian language was an unsuitable substitute for German as a medium for higher education.

## Constitutional Rights and "Customary" Languages
The constitution of 1867, specifically Article 19, codified the right of all races in the empire to preserve and use their own nationality and language. It recognized the equality of all "customary" (*landesueblich*) languages in public life, offices, and schools. The state mandated that in territories where multiple races resided, educational and public institutions must be arranged so that each race could receive necessary education in its own language without being compelled to learn a second *Landessprache*.

The practical application of this constitutional right depended on the government's determination of which languages were considered "customary." This led to ongoing disputes:
* **Germans** demanded that German be recognized as a customary language in every part of the empire, including Galicia and Dalmatia.
* **Czechs** in Bohemia claimed their language should be recognized as customary even in districts that were almost entirely German, such as Reichenberg. Conversely, Germans argued that the Czech language should only be recognized in areas where there was a considerable population of that race.

## Regional Applications and Developments
The impact of the 1867 regulations varied across different territories:

**Dalmatia**
Between 1872 and 1876, a series of edicts and laws were issued to determine the recognition of Slavonic idioms. Previously, Italian—the language of a small minority in seaport towns—had been used for all business. The new laws raised Croatian to equality with Italian, leading to the introduction of Croatian in all schools. Consequently, nearly all education in Dalmatia became Croatian-led, although Italian remained essential for the maritime population and maintained a presence in a few towns, such as the ancient capital, Zara.

**Galicia**
In Galicia, Beust promised several concessions to secure the support of Polish deputies for the Ausgleich. These promises included a separate board for Galician education and the establishment of Polish as the language of instruction in all secondary schools. However, the use of Ruthenian was restricted to elementary schools under strict limitations.

## Demographic Shifts and Linguistic Trends
Economic factors drove a westward movement of Slav populations, who were increasing more rapidly than Germans, into manufacturing districts and towns. While Germans had hoped that education would lead these populations to cease using their native languages, the opposite occurred. The movement of the population spread Slav languages, leading to increased demands for special schools and the appointment of men of their own nationality to government offices. This trend was observed in Styria, Bohemia, and Vienna, where a Czech school was founded following a great increase in the Czech population.
