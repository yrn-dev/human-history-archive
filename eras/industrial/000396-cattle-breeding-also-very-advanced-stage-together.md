# Cattle-Breeding and Livestock Management

Cattle-breeding is a global agricultural industry practiced across diverse climates and terrains, ranging from the high plateaus of the Himalayas and the Andes to the lowland plains of Europe and Africa. The industry encompasses the development of specialized breeds for dairy production, beef, and labor, often utilizing crossing techniques to improve stock.

## Dairy Breeding and Standards
In the dairy sector, specific breeds are recognized for their distinct production qualities. According to prize schedules from the annual London dairy show, primary dairy breeds include the Shorthorn, Jersey, Guernsey, Red Polled, Ayrshire, Kerry, and Dexter. The Dexter is identified as a cross between the Kerry and an unknown breed; while larger than the Kerry, it is noted to lack the Kerry's fine qualities.

Butter-test trials have highlighted the peculiarities of these breeds. Shorthorns, Red Polls, and Kerries produce milk with small fat globules, making them better suited for milk production. Conversely, Jerseys and Guernseys produce milk with larger-sized fat globules, which is richer and more profitable for conversion into butter. For example, data from 1895-1900 shows the Jersey breed had a butter yield of 1 lb. 10(1/4) oz. and a milk-to-butter ratio of 19.15, while the Red Polled had a yield of 1 lb. 4(3/4) oz. and a ratio of 30.29.

To ensure the quality of dairy characters, strict stipulations are placed on the breeding of bulls. Bulls must be of proved descent from dams that have won prizes in butter tests or milking trials from high-class agricultural societies, such as the British Dairy Farmers' Association.

## Regional Practices and Specialized Breeds
Cattle-rearing varies significantly by geography and local environment:

*   **Europe:** In the higher clay grounds of certain provinces, cattle-rearing is practiced alongside horse-breeding and the production of cheese and butter. In some regions, native cattle breeds have been significantly improved through crossing.
*   **The Americas:** In the Mojos and Chiquitos llanos, cattle were introduced by Jesuit founders. Despite transportation costs and inaccessibility, the soil and climate have supported an unfailing supply of beef for inland markets. In the south, the llanos of Tarija and Chuquisaca sustain large herds on well-watered plains and elevated districts. Beef is often prepared in *saladeros* through salting and sun-drying for home markets, while hides are exported.
*   **Africa:** In the Bahr-el-Ghazal province, cattle and camels are both numerous. Certain Arab tribes, specifically the Baggara, specialize exclusively in breeding cattle.
*   **Asia:** In Tibet and the Himalayan provinces, the Yak is a primary domesticated breed. While some inferior Yaks are used for ploughing, the riding Yak is described as a handsome animal with a stately hump and twisted horns. In less elevated tracts, breeds crossed with common Indian cattle are more prevalent; some of these crossbred cows are noted as excellent milkers, surpassing both parent stocks.

## Livestock Integration and Land Use
Cattle-breeding is frequently integrated with other livestock and agricultural activities. In moist climates where cereals struggle to grow, dairy-farming is often the primary industry. In such regions, cattle typically occupy the lower slopes of mountains, while goats and sheep utilize the higher ridges.

In other areas, cattle-breeding is a staple interest alongside the cultivation of crops like barley and oats. In the Don region, cattle-breeding is considered important, and cattle are among the primary exports along with horses, sheep, and hides.

## Historical Improvements in Breeding
The systematic improvement of livestock was advanced by figures such as Robert Bakewell (1725-1795). Working at Dishley, Leicestershire, Bakewell applied theoretical knowledge to improve the breeds of oxen and sheep. His "new Leicestershire long-horn" (also known as Dishley cattle) and his Leicestershire sheep gained international reputations. Bakewell also established the Dishley Society to ensure purity of breed and pioneered the large-scale trade in ram-letting.
