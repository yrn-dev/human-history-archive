# BONDED WAREHOUSE

A bonded warehouse is a facility established either by the state or by private enterprise used for the lodgment of goods liable to duty. These goods remain in the warehouse until the required duty upon them has been paid.

## Historical Development in England
Prior to the implementation of the bonded warehouse system in England, importers were required to pay duties on imported goods at the time of importation. Alternatively, they could provide the revenue authorities with a bond and security to ensure future payment. This previous system created several inconveniences:
* Importers often struggled to find sureties.
* To raise the necessary funds for duty, importers frequently had to sell goods immediately, often during periods of depressed markets and low prices.
* Because duties were paid in a lump sum, the price of goods increased by the amount of interest on the capital needed for the payment.
* Competition was hindered because the importation of heavily taxed articles required large amounts of capital.
* It was difficult to grant exporters an exact equivalent drawback on goods that had already paid duty.

To address these issues and prevent revenue frauds, Sir Robert Walpole proposed a warehousing system for wine and tobacco in his "excise scheme" of 1733. Although the proposal was initially unpopular, the system was officially adopted in 1803. An act that year mandated that imported goods be placed in warehouses approved by customs authorities, with importers providing "bonds" for the payment of duties upon the removal of the goods. This practice gave rise to the terms "bonding" and "bonded."

## Legal Framework and Administration
The Customs Consolidation Act of 1853 removed the requirement for giving bonds and established various provisions to secure the payment of customs duties for warehoused goods. These regulations were further detailed in the Customs Consolidation Act 1876, the Customs and Inland Revenue Act 1880, and the Revenue Act 1883. 

Under the act of 1876, these facilities are referred to as "king's warehouses." Section 284 of that act defines them as any place provided by the crown or approved by the commissioners of customs for the deposit of goods to ensure the security of the goods and the duties due upon them.

## Operations and Market Preparation
While goods are held "in bond" within the warehouse, the owner is permitted to perform various processes to prepare the goods for the market. These activities include:
* Roasting coffee.
* Repacking and mixing tea.
* Bottling, mixing, vatting, and racking of spirits and wines.
* Manufacturing certain types of tobacco.

The system allows for specific allowances regarding leakage, evaporation, or waste that occurs during these processes.

## Customs and Revenue Control
To prevent frauds on the revenue, the customs system utilizes a "Bill of Victualling" (or "Victualling Bill"). In its modern application, this is an order from a customs export officer for the drawback of stores or for the shipment of stores from a bonded warehouse. These stores are allowed based on the number of passengers and crew on a ship departing for an oversea voyage. 

The bill is created by the master of the ship and countersigned by the collector of customs. Any articles taken on board are not considered stores unless they appear on the victualling bill. If such stores are relanded anywhere in the United Kingdom without sanction from customs officers, they are forfeited. In such cases, the owner and the master are each liable for a penalty of £100 or treble the value of the stores.
