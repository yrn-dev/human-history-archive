# Recording Deflectometers and Bridge Impact Analysis

The study of bridge deformations and the stresses caused by moving trains has been advanced through the use of specialized measurement tools and the collation of trial data. Significant contributions to this field were made by S.W. Robinson and F.E. Turneaure, whose work was published in the *Transactions of the American Society of Civil Engineers* (Trans. Am. Soc. C.E.).

## Measurement Instrumentation and Methodology
F.E. Turneaure, writing in volume xli of the *Trans. Am. Soc. C.E.*, utilized a recording deflectometer along with two recording extensometers to measure the deformations of tension members and girders. While these observations are noted as being difficult and subject to potential error due to the inertia of the instruments, the process was conducted with significant care.

## Impact and Vibration Dynamics
The analysis of moving loads on bridges reveals that stresses from live loads are consistently greater than those produced by the same load resting quietly on the structure. This increase in stress is more pronounced on main girders that are short rather than long, and is more significant on flooring girders than on main girders.

Several factors contribute to these increased stresses:
* **Engine Components:** Rapidly changing forces are created by the vertically unbalanced moving parts of the engine. Specifically, locomotive balance weights have a large effect in causing vibration.
* **Track Conditions:** Shocks are produced by inequalities of level at the ends of rails. Additionally, centrifugal and lurching actions occur because rails are not perfectly smooth or straight, which alters how loading is distributed.
* **Cumulative Effects:** In certain instances, vibrations are cumulative, resulting in values that exceed the impact action of any single event.

## Effects of Speed and Span Length
The relationship between train speed, span length, and deflection is characterized by the following general observations:
* **Low Speeds:** At speeds below 25 miles per hour, there is not much vibration.
* **High Speeds:** The speed of the train does not affect the mean deflection; it only influences the magnitude of the vibrations.
* **Span-Specific Deflection:** For girder spans of less than 50 feet, the increase in deflection due to impact at speeds of 40 or 50 miles per hour can reach 40% to 50%. This percentage drops rapidly as spans increase, falling to approximately 25% for spans of 75 feet.
* **Stress Distribution:** The percentage increase in boom stresses caused by impact is roughly equivalent to the increase in deflection, though the increase in web bracing bars is somewhat greater.

## Analysis of Dead and Live Loads
E.H. Stone, also published in *Trans. Am. Soc. C.E.* (vol. xli), analyzed measurements of deflection from official trials of bridges in India. Stone found that the increment of deflection caused by impact depends on the ratio of dead load to live load. He inferred that the augmentation of stress for any member—resulting from causes included in the impact allowance—will maintain the same percentage for the same ratios of live to dead load stresses.
