# The Banims

The Banims were Irish literary figures whose contribution to literature is primarily estimated through the merits of the *O'Hara Tales*. While their later works demonstrate considerable ability, they are characterized as being sometimes prolix and marked by an imitation of the Waverley Novels that is too evident. In contrast, the *Tales* are regarded as masterpieces of faithful delineation, providing an able and true depiction of the lights and shadows of Irish peasant character and strong passions. Although the authors have been accused of straining for melodramatic effect through striking and sometimes horrible incidents, they are noted for giving little attention to the more joyous and lighter side of Irish character found in the works of Samuel Lover.

## John Banim

John Banim (1798–1842) was an Irish novelist who was sometimes referred to as the "Scott of Ireland." Born in Kilkenny on April 3, 1798, he entered Kilkenny College at the age of thirteen, where he focused specifically on painting and drawing. He continued his artistic studies for two years at schools associated with the Royal Society in Dublin before returning to Kilkenny to teach drawing. During this time, he fell in love with a pupil; however, the young woman's parents intervened and removed her from the city. She died two months later, an event that left a permanent and severe impact on Banim's health.

In 1820, Banim settled in Dublin to pursue literature. His early works included a poem titled *The Celts' Paradise* and a play, *Damon and Pythias*, which was performed at Covent Garden in 1821. Following a brief visit to Kilkenny where he married, he collaborated in 1822 with his elder brother, Michael, to plan a series of tales illustrating Irish life. Their goal was for these works to serve Ireland in the same capacity that the Waverley Novels served Scotland. To support himself, Banim moved to London and wrote for the stage and various magazines. In 1824, he published a volume of miscellaneous essays anonymously titled *Revelations of the Dead Alive*.

## Collaborative Works and Later Career

In April 1825, the first series of *Tales of the O'Hara Family* was published, achieving decided and immediate success. This collaborative effort included *Crohoore of the Bill Hook*, which was written by Michael Banim. A second series followed in 1826, featuring the Irish novel *The Nowlans*. Due to the decline of John Banim's health, subsequent efforts by the "O'Hara family" were almost entirely the work of Michael Banim. These included *The Croppy, a Tale of 1798* (1828), which contained some wonderfully vigorous passages despite being considered hardly equal to the earlier tales. Other works received with considerable favor included *The Denounced*, *The Mayor of Windgap*, *The Smuggler*, and *The Ghost Hunter* (written by Michael).

By 1829, John Banim's circumstances had become much straitened, leading him to travel to France. While he was abroad, a movement was initiated to relieve his financial needs.

## Michael Banim

Michael Banim (1796–1874) was the elder brother of John Banim. He was a key collaborator in the creation of the *Tales of the O'Hara Family*, contributing specifically the powerful story *Crohoore of the Bill Hook*. As John's health failed, Michael became the primary producer of the "O'Hara family" works, authoring *The Ghost Hunter* and taking the lead on later publications in the series.
