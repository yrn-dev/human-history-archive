# The Clearing House and Cheque Systems

## The London Clearing House
The purely city banks established a "Clearing House" by 1776, though records from the Grasshopper in 1773 regarding a quarterly charge for a "Clearing-room" suggest an earlier, less organized system of settlement. In 1810, a dedicated house was acquired for this purpose, at which time forty-six banking houses settled their accounts with one another. By 1844, twenty-six of the forty-nine London banks were connected to the Clearing House; at that time, membership was restricted to private banks. Joint-stock banks were admitted in 1854. By 1906, membership consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank.

The Bank of England has never been a member of the Clearing House. While it "clears on one side"—meaning its claims on clearing bankers are processed through the house—the claims of clearing bankers against the Bank of England are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times daily. Additionally, banks located at Charing Cross or in Fleet Street did not belong to the Clearing House.

## Evolution of Cheque Clearing
In 1858, arrangements made by Sir John Lubbock (Lord Avebury) added the clearing of country cheques to the system. This "country clearing" significantly aided business by allowing a cheque drawn on a distant English village to be handled as conveniently as one drawn on London. 

The volume of clearings has grown substantially over time. In 1839, London clearings totaled £954,401,600 with 29 banks; by 1906, this figure rose to £12,711,334,000 with 18 banks. The annual circulation shown by the London Clearing House exceeds £12,000,000,000. When including exchanges from large towns in Scotland, Ireland, and England, as well as internal bank settlements, the total yearly turnover in cheques is estimated to be at least twice the amount registered by the London Clearing House.

## The Nature and Law of Cheques
A cheque is defined as a bill of exchange drawn on a banker and payable on demand. While most laws applicable to bills payable on demand apply to cheques, specific rules exist due to the relationship between the banker (drawee) and the customer (drawer). A person with a bank account is legally entitled to draw on it via cheques, though the right to overdraw requires an agreement. 

A banker's authority to pay a customer's cheque is terminated by a notice of the customer's death or a countermand of payment. Regarding the presentation of a cheque, the drawer is not absolutely discharged if the holder fails to present it within a reasonable time; the drawer is only discharged to the extent of actual damage suffered due to the delay.

## Crossing Systems and Fraud Prevention
To facilitate safe transmission by post and guard against fraud, a system of "crossing" was developed, ensuring crossed cheques are payable only through specific channels. Legislative recognition began with the 19 and 20 Vict. c. 95, which was amended in 1858. A consolidating act followed in 1876, and these provisions were later re-enacted with slight modifications in the Bills of Exchange Act 1883 (Sections 76 to 82).

There are two primary types of crossing:
*   **General Crossing:** Two parallel lines are drawn across the cheque with the words "& Co." written between them. Such cheques cannot be paid over the counter and must be presented by a banker.
*   **Special Crossing:** The name of a specific banker is added, meaning the cheque can only be presented through that particular banker.

Furthermore, a cheque may be marked "not negotiable." Under the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, if a banker pays a crossed cheque contrary to its crossing, they are liable to the true owner for any resulting loss and may be unable to charge the customer. However, protection is granted under Section 80 and the proviso to Section 79 if the banker pays according to the ostensible tenor.

## The Collecting Banker
The collection of crossed cheques is considered virtually incumbent upon a banker. Under Section 82, a collecting banker is granted qualified immunity if they meet specific conditions: they must receive the cheque and money from a customer with whom they have existing business relations; the cheque must already be crossed generally or specially to them (a banker's own crossing under Section 77 is inefficacious); and they must act in good faith and without negligence.

Negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care for the true owner. To avoid this, the banker must ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct, verify authority for "per procuration" endorsements, and refuse cheques marked "account payee" for any other account. The House of Lords ruled in *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* (1903) that the banker must act strictly as an agent or "conduit pipe." If a bank credits cheques as cash before the money is actually received, it is viewed as taking the cheque as an owner, which debars the bank from the protection of Section 82.

## International Comparison
The United States has developed a clearing system of higher development than any other country except perhaps Great Britain. As of September 30, 1907, aggregate clearings in about 112 leading U.S. cities reached $154,662,515,258. The New York Clearing-House handles a dominant share; in 1906, its clearings accounted for 67.2% of the total for 55 of the larger cities. These volumes fluctuate based on business prosperity and stock-exchange transactions.
