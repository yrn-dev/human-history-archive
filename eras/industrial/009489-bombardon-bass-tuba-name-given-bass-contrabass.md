# BOMBARDON

The bombardon, also referred to as the bass tuba, is the name given to the bass and contrabass brass wind instruments used in military bands. In an orchestral setting, the instrument is called the bass tuba. The term "bombardon" is derived from the Italian *bombardone*, which referred to the contrabass pommer (bombard) that served as the bass of medieval orchestras prior to the invention of the fagotto. Beyond wind instruments, the name is also applied to a bass reed stop on the organ with a 16 ft. tone.

## History and Development
The bombardon was the first bass wind instrument to be fitted with valves. In its early stages, it was known by several names, including *clavicor*, *bass horn*, or *corno basso*. The original *corno basso* featured a narrow bore and horizontal pistons. It is distinct from the keyed bass horn, which evolved into the ophicleide.

In Prussia, the Jager military bands introduced the keyed bass horn as their bass instrument in 1829, followed by the bombardon (or valve-ophicleide) in 1831. By 1835, these were superseded in the Guards by the bass tuba, which was invented by J.G. Moritz and W. Wieprecht.

## Construction and Classification
The bombardon and the euphonium—of which the bombardon is the bass—resulted from applying valves to the bugle family, a process that also produced saxhorns. The primary distinction between tubas (including the bombardon) and saxhorns is the width of the conical bore. Tubas possess a sufficiently wide conical bore to produce fundamental sounds with immense power and a rich, full quality.

This structural difference led to a classification system in Austria and Germany, where brass winds are categorized as "half" (*Halbe*) or "whole" (*Ganze*) instruments. Older narrow-bore instruments like saxhorns are "half instruments" because their tubes must theoretically be twice as long as an organ pipe of the same note to produce a sound; for example, a French horn sounding the 8 ft. C of an organ pipe requires a 16 ft. tube, making C the second harmonic. Conversely, instruments based on the bugle's wide conical bore can produce the fundamental sound clearly. Consequently, the tubes of tubas are half the length of older instruments, matching the length of an organ pipe of the same pitch.

## Forms and Technical Specifications
The modern bombardon exists in two primary forms:
*   **Upright model:** Used for stationary band music.
*   **Circular model (Helicon):** Designed to be worn around the body with the large bell resting on the left shoulder, similar to the Roman *cornu*. This design is more convenient for carrying the heavy instrument while marching.

The instrument possesses a chromatic compass of 3-1/2 to 4 octaves, with a harmonic series consisting of the 1st through 8th harmonics. Because of the immense length of the air column, the lowest valve notes are difficult to produce as the lips often lack the power to synchronize with such a deep pitch; these notes are frequently inaudible unless doubled an octave higher by another bombardon.

## Operation and Regional Variations
Bombardons typically utilize three or four pistons to alter the pitch. In most regions, these lower the pitch by 1, 1/2, 1-1/2, and 2-1/2 tones, though in Belgium, the intervals are 1, 1/2, 2, and 3 tones. These valves work by either admitting wind into additional tubing to lower the pitch or cutting off a length of tubing to raise it. In Germany and Austria, a "cylinder action" is often used, replacing the pump or piston with a four-way brass cock operated by cranks and a key.

Regarding notation, bombardons are generally treated as non-transposing instruments (written as sounded), except in Belgium and France, where transposition is the standard practice.

## Global Usage
The bombardon is utilized in the military bands of Austria. In Germany, however, it has been superseded by a bass tuba that differs slightly in construction and form from the bass tubas and bombardons used in France, Belgium, England, and Austria.
