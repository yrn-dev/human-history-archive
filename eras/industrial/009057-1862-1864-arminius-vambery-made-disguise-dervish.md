# Arminius Vambéry and Central Asia

Arminius Vambéry was a writer and traveler whose works provide significant historical and geographical documentation of Central Asia. His contributions to the archive's records include *Travels in Central Asia* (1864), *Sketches of Central Asia* (1868), and *History of Bokhara* (1873). His writings are cited as primary authorities on the subject of dervishes in Central Asia.

## Observations on Dervishes
In his accounts, Vambéry discusses the nature of dervishes, a term derived from a Persian word meaning "seeking doors" or "beggar," which is equivalent to the Arabic term *faqir*. Within Islam, a dervish generally refers to a member of a religious fraternity. While the term can apply to both mendicant and non-mendicant members, in Persia and Turkey, it more specifically denotes a wandering, begging religious figure.

These fraternities encompass a wide variety of lifestyles, including those that are solitary, secular, married, celibate, monastic, stationary, free, wandering, or ascetic. The theology guiding these groups is a form of Sufi'ism. Within these fraternities, those credited with miraculous powers granted by God due to the greatness of their faith are known as *walis*, while begging friars are referred to as *fakirs*.

Vambéry specifically notes that the Kashmiri Dervishes are pre-eminent among their Mahomedan brethren. He characterizes them as being particularly skilled in secret arts, cunning, and the practice of exorcisms.

## The Region of Bokhara
Vambéry's historical work focuses heavily on Bokhara, a region known to ancient peoples as Sogdiana. This area was located too far east to ever fall under Roman dominion, though it experienced numerous bloody revolutions throughout Asian history. The foundation of its capital is attributed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab.

Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, Sogdiana was part of the Seleucidae empire and shared a similar fate to Bactria. Later, the nomad Yue-chi moved into the valley of the Oxus from the east, eventually becoming a settled power. Under King Kadphises I, who is believed to have ascended the throne around A.D. 45, the dominions extended from Bokhara to the Indus.

## Religious and Political Transitions in Sogdiana
The region was later reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty. By the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand. Islam was introduced shortly after the Arab conquest of Persia, which took place between 640 and 642, and it quickly became the dominant faith.

During the early centuries of Mahommedan rule, the district—then called Sughd—was one of Central Asia's most flourishing and celebrated regions. It contained two major cities: Samarkand, which generally served as the seat of government, and Bokhara, which was highly regarded as a center of learning and religion. The region was also known during the early middle ages as *Ma wara 'l Nahr* or *Ma-vera-un-nahr*, translated into classical titles as Transoxiana.

## Later Conquests
The region saw various shifts in power over the following centuries:
* **The Seljuks:** At the end of the 11th century, Malik Shah, the third of the Persian Seljuk dynasty, crossed the Oxus and subdued the lands watered by the Jaxartes and the Oxus.
* **The Khwarizm:** In 1216, Mahommed Shah Khwarizm subdued Bokhara.
* **The Mongols:** In 1220, Jenghiz Khan wrested control from Mahommed Shah Khwarizm. Although the country was devastated by Jenghiz Khan, it recovered some prosperity under his son, Ogdai Khan, who is described as benevolent and humane.
* **Timur:** The descendants of Ogdai Khan held possession until 1369, when Timur (Tamerlane) conquered the region and established his capital at Samarkand.
