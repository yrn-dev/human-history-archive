# Gravimetric Density (G.D.)

Gravimetric density (G.D.) is a term peculiar to artillerists. It is utilized to distinguish between the specific gravity (S.G.) of a powder filling a given volume while in a state of gas and the specific gravity of the separate cord of powder or solid grain.

## Conceptual Definition and Calculation
In the context of interior ballistics, gravimetric density represents the specific gravity of a weight of powder ($P$ lb) when it fills a known volume ($C$ cub. in.) in a state of gas. The reciprocal of gravimetric density is referred to as the gravimetric volume (G.V.), which is the ratio of the volume of the gas to the volume of an equal weight of water.

The calculation of G.D. varies based on the units of measurement used:
*   **British Units:** If a charge of $P$ lb of powder is placed in a chamber of volume $C$ cub. in., the formula is $\text{G.D.} = 27.73P/C$. In this system, the gravimetric volume is $\text{G.V.} = C/27.73P$. The factor 27.73 is based on the weight of water at 62°F, where 1 lb of water bulks 27.73 cub. in. (or 1728 cub. in. of water weighs 62.35 lb). In some instances, a factor of 27.68 is used, which corresponds to a temperature of 54°F (12°C) and a water density of approximately 62.4 lb per cub. ft.
*   **Metric Units:** When measuring $P$ in kg and $C$ in litres, the formulas are simplified to $\text{G.D.} = P/C$ and $\text{G.V.} = C/P$, requiring no additional factor.

## Physical Examples and Specific Gravity
The difference between the specific gravity of a solid material and its gravimetric density as a charge is illustrated by the packing of materials. For a lump of solid lead, a charge composed of closely packed equal spherules of lead shot will have a G.D. equal to $0.7403$ of the S.G. of the solid lead lump. Similarly, for a bundle of cylindrical sticks of cordite, the G.D. of the charge is $0.9067$ of the S.G. of a single stick of cordite.

## Application in Ballistics
Gravimetric density is a critical factor in the design and safety of artillery. The chief problem in interior ballistics is adjusting the G.D. of the charge relative to the weight of the shot. This adjustment ensures that as the shot advances during the combustion of the charge, the maximum pressure does not exceed a safe limit.

For example, in a 6-in. range table, a charge may be described as having a weight of 13 lb 4 oz. ($P = 13.25$), a G.D. of 0.504, and consisting of size 30 cordite. In this instance, the figure 55.01 represents the specific volume of the charge (cubic inches per lb) filling the chamber in a state of gas; the product of 55.01 and 0.504 equals 27.73. The resulting chamber capacity ($C$) is 730 cub. in., which is the equivalent length of the chamber (E.L.C.), measuring 25.8 in. or 2.15 ft. of bore. If the shot were unable to move, the closed chamber pressure at a G.D. of 0.5 would be nearly 49 tons per sq. in., a level considered too great to be safe.

## Pressure and Work
The relationship between pressure ($p$ in tons per sq. in.) and gravimetric density is often plotted on a curve. The work ($E$) in foot-tons realized by the expansion of 1 lb of powder from one gravimetric volume to another can be inferred from these values. If the average pressure is $p$ and the gravimetric volume changes by $\Delta v$ (a volume change of $27.73\Delta v$ cub. in.), the work done is $27.73p\Delta v$ inch-tons, or $\Delta E = 2.31 p\Delta v$ foot-tons.

Experimental data on cordite-gas shows that at standard atmospheric pressure and 0°C, one gramme occupies 700 ccs. When compressed into 5 ccs at the temperature of explosion, the gas reaches a pressure of 16 tons per sq. in. (approximately 2440 atmospheres). Using the gas equation, this results in an absolute centigrade temperature of 4758, or 4485°C (8105°F).
