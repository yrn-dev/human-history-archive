# BOPP, FRANZ (1791-1867)

Franz Bopp was a German philologist whose early publications are credited with the beginning of the science of comparative grammar. His research provided extensive insights into the domestic usages, tenets, practices, and affinities of ancient Indo-European nations, as well as their original seats.

## Early Life and Education
Born in Mainz on September 14, 1791, Bopp's family moved to Aschaffenburg, Bavaria, due to the political instability of the era. He received a liberal education at the Lyceum, where he was introduced to the literature and languages of the East through the lectures of Karl J. Windischmann. Windischmann, along with J.J. Gorres, G.F. Creuzer, and the brothers Schlegel, held a great enthusiasm for Indian philosophy and wisdom. Bopp's interest in the sacred language of the Hindus was further stimulated by the 1808 book by Fr. Schlegel, *Uber die Sprache und Weisheit der Indier*, a work that had a significant influence on German historians and philosophers of the time.

## Studies in Paris
In 1812, the Bavarian government funded Bopp's travel to Paris to pursue the study of Sanskrit. During his time there, he associated with several eminent figures, including L.M. Langles, S. de Sacy, and A.L. Chezy. He was particularly influenced by Alexander Hamilton (1762-1824), who had acquired knowledge of Sanskrit while in India and had collaborated with Langles to produce a descriptive catalogue of the Sanskrit manuscripts held in the Imperial library.

At the Imperial library, Bopp utilized a rich collection of Sanskrit manuscripts, many of which had been brought from India by Father Pons in the early 18th century. He also studied Sanskrit books produced by the Serampore and Calcutta presses.

## Philological Contributions
After four years of study in Paris, Bopp published his first work in 1816 at Frankfort-On-Main, titled *Uber das Conjugationssystem der Sanskritsprache in Vergleichung mit jenem der griechischen, lateinischen, persischen und germanischen Sprache*. This publication featured a preface written by Karl J. Windischmann.

In this work, Bopp did not seek to prove that Sanskrit shared a common parentage with German, Latin, Greek, and Persian, as that fact had already been established. Instead, his objective was to trace the common origin of their grammatical structures. This research, characterized by a "microscopical" attention to linguistic phenomena, set the trajectory for his entire professional life. His influence extended to other scholars; for example, the English philologist John William Donaldson based his 1839 work, *The New Cratylus*, largely on Bopp's comparative grammar.

## Legacy and Death
Bopp's academic achievements were widely accepted and celebrated. To honor his contributions, a fund known as *Die Bopp-Stiftung* was established in Berlin on May 16, 1866—the fiftieth anniversary of Windischmann's preface to Bopp's first book. This fund was created to promote the study of comparative grammar and Sanskrit, receiving contributions from admirers and pupils across the globe.

Despite his professional fame, Bopp died a poor man on October 23, 1867. He was remembered by those who knew him for his rare modesty, unselfishness, genuine kindliness, and devotion to his friends and family.
