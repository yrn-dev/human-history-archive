# The Practice of Boiler, Bridge, and Girder Shops

The practice of boiler, bridge, and girder shops is often treated together due to the employment of similar materials and methods. The primary materials used across these disciplines are steel and iron. The predominant methods involve the working of rolled sections and plates, which are used more extensively than the bars and rods typically found in a smithy.

## Industrial Specialization and Mechanization
Modern manufacturing has seen a general tendency toward specialization, leading firms to restrict their product range to increase volume. Consequently, the era where a single shop could simultaneously produce several classes of boilers and undertake bridge and girder work has passed. This shift is driven by the growth of machine tools and a decrease in hand work.

Highly specialized machines now perform almost every distinct operation on boiler or bridge sections. Repetitive operations are managed through a system of templeting. For example, when producing twenty or fifty similar boilers annually, templets ensure that every flange, hole, plate, or stay is identical. In many instances, self-acting drilling machines pitch and drill plates immediately without the need for marking. Similarly, hundreds of bracing bars for girders and bridges are punched or drilled from a templet bar, allowing them to be installed without further fitting or adjustment.

## Comparative Construction Methods
Despite shared materials, there are significant divergences in the construction of boilers versus bridges and girders:

*   **Flanging and Bending:** Flanging—the bending of plate edges to facilitate union with other plates—is central to boilermaking for furnace flues, tube-plates, and end-plates. It is scarcely used in bridge and girder work. Additionally, plates are bent into cylindrical shapes for furnaces and shells in boiler work, a practice not used in girder work.
*   **Welding:** Welding is far more common in boiler work, particularly for stand pipes and furnace flues. In these shops, welding primarily involves the edges of long, thin plates joined in successive "heats." While much of this remains hand work, large shops utilize steam-driven power hammers to close welds.
*   **Drilling and Punching:** In boiler work, holes are generally drilled through the seams of adjacent plates. In bridge work, bars or plates are usually punched or drilled individually.

## Boiler Engineering and Safety
Boilers must withstand high pressures (typically 175 to 200 lb. per sq. in. for locomotive boilers) and high temperatures. To prevent grooving or fractures, they are constructed with materials combining strength with high ductility and are designed for elasticity and freedom of movement. This is achieved through judicious staying, the limitation of tube lengths, and the flanging of specific parts.

A critical evolution in safety involved the drilling of rivet-holes. Previously, holes were punched, often resulting in misalignment. Workers used a "drift"—a tapered rod driven by hammer blows—to force holes into alignment. This process stressed the plates and enlarged cracks, contributing to boiler explosions. While high-class boiler work now exclusively uses drilling, average bridge and girder work still employs punching and reamering because their service conditions are less severe.

## Locomotive Boiler Structure
A locomotive boiler consists of a cylindrical barrel and a nearly rectangular fire-box. The fire-box sides are secured to the shell by stays approximately 1 inch in diameter, pitched 4 inches apart. Depending on the boiler size, the fire-box top is supported by either longitudinal girder stays or vertical stays connecting the fire-box crown to the shell crown. 

Copper tube plates are provided with freedom of motion upwards to allow for expansion. Typical tubes are 1-3/4 inches in diameter. Some designs include water-tubes; for example, a London & South-Western Railway Co. boiler features 112 seamless steel water-tubes (2-3/4 inches in diameter) expanded into the fire-box side plates.

## Bridge and Girder Construction
In girder bridges, a fundamental distinction exists based on the mode of support:
*   **Independent or Discontinuous:** Main girders are supported only at the ends. This is used in bridges like the Hawkesbury, Benares, and Chittravatti to avoid risks associated with pier settlement.
*   **Continuous:** Girders are supported at two or more points, which allows for an economy of weight (approximately 49% for dead load and 16% for live load in three-span bridges). However, settlement of supports can alter stress distribution and render the bridge unsafe.
*   **Cantilever:** This system introduces hinges at points of contrary flexure, removing ambiguity regarding stresses. Cantilevers can be built member-by-member from piers without temporary scaffolding, which prevents the interruption of navigation and simplifies building in deep water.

An example of American girder construction is the Ohio bridge on the Cincinnati & Covington railway (1888). This bridge features independent polygonal girders with a center span of 550 ft. and side spans of 490 ft. It utilizes mild steel for the main structure and wrought iron for wind-bracing, stringers, and cross girders, with a total weight of approximately 5000 tons of metal.
