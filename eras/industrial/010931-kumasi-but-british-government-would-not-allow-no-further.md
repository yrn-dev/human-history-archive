# Kumasi

Kumasi is a city in the Ashanti region that served as the capital of the Ashanti kingdom. Historically, it functioned as a center of culture and governance, and later became the primary distributing center for the Ashanti region and its hinterland.

## Early History and British Relations
In the early 19th century, Kumasi was a city of high culture with broad, regular streets; its main avenue measured 70 yards in width. The city featured a large market-place to the south-east and a "Spirit House" located in a grove of trees, which served as a place of execution. The royal palace was constructed of red sandstone and was believed to be modeled after Dutch buildings at Elmina. In the first quarter of the 19th century, the population is estimated not to have exceeded 20,000.

Early diplomatic relations with the British were volatile. In 1817, a mission consisting of T.E. Bowdich, W. Hutchinson, and Frederick James visited Kumasi. Negotiations became strained when the king demanded quit rents for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort, leading the king to call the emissaries "cheats and liars" before a treaty was eventually concluded. In 1820, Mr. Joseph Dupuis arrived in Kumasi as a consul and drew up a treaty acknowledging Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory. However, this treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, and the government in London.

Following the defeat of Sir Charles M'Carthy in 1824, the governor's skull was used as a royal drinking-cup at Kumasi. After a period of chronic warfare and a defeat at Dodowah in 1826, the Ashanti king was forced in 1831 to purchase peace from George Maclean for 600 oz. of gold and provide his son as a hostage.

## Conflict and Decline
Under King Kwaka Dua I (who succeeded Osai Okoto in 1838), the city experienced a period of peace and trade. However, tensions returned in 1863 over the British refusal to surrender a runaway slave-boy and a fugitive chief. Although the Ashanti were victorious in two battles, the British government denied Governor Richard Pine's request to advance on Kumasi.

Following the death of Kwaka Dua I in 1867 and a subsequent civil war, Kofi Karikari took the throne, declaring that his business would be war. This led to the war of 1873-74. On February 6, 1874, the forces of Sir Garnet Wolseley blew up the red sandstone royal palace. The city was only partially rebuilt after the British withdrawal, and its decline was marked; a new royal palace was constructed of clay rather than brick, and wide stretches of the former town became grass-grown.

## Political Transition and the 1900 Rebellion
After the war of 1873-74, the Ashanti placed Mensa on the "golden stool"—the symbol of sovereignty. Mensa's rule was characterized by tyranny and human sacrifices until he was displaced in 1883. Following further civil war, Prince Prempeh (Kwaka Dua III) was chosen as king on March 26, 1888, with the sanction of the Gold Coast government.

Tensions rose again in 1893 when Prempeh closed roads to traders. The British demanded that Prempeh accept a protectorate and a resident at Kumasi. When Prempeh failed to reply to these demands by October 31, 1895, he was eventually deported. Following this, Captain Donald W. Stewart was installed as a British resident at Kumasi. A fort was built and garrisoned by the Gold Coast constabulary, and the city's affairs were administered by chiefs under British guidance.

In 1900, a serious rebellion broke out involving the Kumasi, Kokofu, and Adansi tribes. The conflict was sparked partly by the governor of the Gold Coast, Sir F. Hodgson, who demanded that Ashanti chiefs pay 4,000 oz. of gold yearly and reproached them for hiding the golden stool since 1896. Kumasi warriors attacked a party of Hausa sent to find the stool and subsequently threatened the British fort. Sir F. and Lady Hodgson, along with Basel missionaries including Mr. Ramseyer, took refuge in the fort. The garrison was eventually reinforced by Lagos constabulary and forces under Major A. Morris. The rebellion was finally crushed after the Kumasi were beaten at Obassa on September 30, 1900.

## Infrastructure and Economy
The physical landscape around Kumasi consists of dense forest. Communication was historically limited to tortuous forest paths too narrow for wheeled vehicles, though a 141-mile wide road was eventually cut from Cape Coast to Kumasi. Travel in the bush often utilized palanquins borne by four carriers. The city is connected to the coast and Northern Territories via telegraph lines and a well-organized postal service.

A railway from Sekondi, passing through the Obuassi and Tarkwa gold-fields, reached Kumasi in September 1903. This infrastructure led to a rapid expansion of the town and a revival in trade, allowing Kumasi to supplant coast towns as the distributing center for the region.

The economy is supported by several products:
*   **Agriculture:** Rubber trees, kola nuts, and tobacco. Since 1905, many cocoa plantations have been established, particularly in the eastern districts.
*   **Mining:** Gold is found in the western districts. The Obuassi mines provided most of the gold exported from the Gold Coast in 1902. In 1905, the gold output from Ashanti was 68,259 oz., valued at £254,790.
*   **Revenue:** The government obtains revenue from liquor licenses, caravan taxes, contributions from gold-mining companies, and rents from government land.
