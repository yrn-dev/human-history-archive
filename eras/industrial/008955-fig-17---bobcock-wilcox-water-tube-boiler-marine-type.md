# Babcock & Wilcox Water-tube Boiler (Marine Type)

The Babcock & Wilcox marine boiler is a specialized water-tube boiler utilized extensively in the British and American navies, as well as in merchant steamers and various yachts. It belongs to the broader class of water-tube boilers, characterized by the fact that water is primarily contained within numerous small tubes rather than a large tank, with the fire typically positioned beneath these tubes to form the majority of the heating surface.

## Design and Construction
The marine version of the Babcock & Wilcox boiler consists of a horizontal cylindrical steam-chest positioned transversely above a group of elements. The entire assembly is enclosed within an iron casing that is lined with firebrick. 

The boiler's internal structure is organized around "elements." Each element is composed of a front header and a back header, which are connected by numerous water-tubes. These tubes are designed with a considerable inclination to facilitate the circulation of water. The headers are constructed from wrought steel; while the outer pairs are flat on their outer portions, the other headers are sinuous on both sides, with these sinuosities fitting into one another.

## Tubing and Gas Circulation
The boiler employs tubes of two different sizes to optimize the heating process:
* **Large tubes:** The return tubes between the back headers and the steam-chest, as well as the two lower rows of tubes, have an outside diameter of 3-15/16 inches.
* **Small tubes:** The remaining tubes have an outside diameter of 1-13/16 inches.

The smaller tubes are arranged in groups of two or four corresponding to nearly all the sinuosities of the headers. This specific arrangement is intended to ensure that furnace gases are well mixed and maintain maximum contact with the heating surfaces.

## Connections and Water Flow
The connectivity of the marine boiler is designed to manage steam and water movement efficiently:
* **Front Headers:** The upper ends of the front headers are located directly beneath the steam-chest and are connected to it via short nipples. The bottom ends are connected to a square-section pipe that extends across the boiler's width. Nearly vertical tubes provide additional connections between this cross-pipe and the bottom of the steam-chest.
* **Back Headers:** The upper ends of the back headers are connected to the steam-chest by two long horizontal tubes, while the bottom ends of these headers remain closed.

## Maintenance and Fittings
To allow for the securing of tubes within the headers, access holes are provided on the side of the header opposite the tubes. For tubes grouped in fours, a hole is provided for each tube. For groups of two tubes, a single larger hole is used. 

These access holes are of two types:
1. **Oval holes:** These are closed by fittings similar to those found in land-based Babcock & Wilcox boilers.
2. **Conical holes:** These are larger on the inside and are closed by special conical fittings. In these fittings, the bolt and the conical portion are a single forging, and the nut is close-ended.

## Materials and Thermal Storage
In accordance with general standards for naval water-tube boilers, seamless steel tubes are invariably used. For marine boilers of all types, plates and stays are almost always made of mild steel.

In certain applications where work is intermittent, a "thermal storage" system may be employed. This involves placing a large cylindrical storage vessel above the boiler. Feed-water is delivered into the upper part of this vessel and runs over extended surfaces exposed to steam, raising its temperature to the evaporation point. During periods of low steam demand, excess feed-water accumulates in this chamber. When an extra supply of steam is required, the feed-pump is stopped, and the boiler is fed using the hot water stored in the chamber. This system also serves to deposit many feed impurities in the chamber, where they are harmless and easily removed.
