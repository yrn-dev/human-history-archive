# Common Ministers and the Ausgleich of Austria-Hungary

The administration of common affairs between Austria and Hungary was governed by the principle of dualism, which established a formal parity between Hungary and the other territories of the king. This system necessitated a specific structure for common governance and a periodic process of financial and economic settlement known as the "Ausgleich" (compact or compromise).

## The Common Ministers
Under the system of dualism, three ministers were appointed for common purposes:
1. Minister for Foreign Affairs
2. Minister for War
3. Minister for Finance

These common ministers were responsible to the Delegations. The Minister of War exercised control over the common army, although the laws regarding army recruitment required separate votes in each of the two parliaments. The Minister of Finance was responsible for presenting the common budget to the Delegations; however, the Delegations lacked the power to raise money or vote on taxes. Once the budget was passed, the necessary funds had to be provided by the separate parliaments.

## The Delegations
The constitutional right of political control and the voting of money for common affairs were exercised by the Delegations. Each Delegation consisted of sixty members chosen for a one-year term. One-third of these members were elected by the Upper Houses—the Austrian Herrenhaus and the Hungarian Table of Magnates—while two-thirds were elected by the Austrian and Hungarian Houses of Representatives.

The monarch summoned the Delegations annually, alternating the meetings between Vienna and Budapest. To maintain the parity of dualism and prevent the Delegations from overshadowing the parliaments, the two bodies met separately. They communicated their decisions in writing, with documents translated into German or Magyar. If three such written interchanges failed to produce an agreement, a common plenary session was held. In these joint sessions, an equal number of members from both delegations voted in silence without discussion; a simple majority was sufficient for a decision, which then required the sanction of the monarch for validity. The Delegations held the right to independently formulate resolutions and to arraign or call the common ministers to account.

## The Ausgleich and the Settlement of 1877
The general settlement between Austria and Hungary, the Ausgleich, occurred every ten years. This process involved a complex bargain where concessions in one area, such as commercial policy, were offset by gains in another, such as financial agreements. These negotiations were often fraught with difficulty due to national jealousy and race hatred within the two parliaments.

The settlement of 1877 was a significant example of this balancing act. In this agreement, Austria granted Hungary a share in the management of the bank. Conversely, Austria benefited from an arrangement regarding the payment of bonuses on exported sugar. Other points of contention included the increased duty on petroleum and coffee, which fell more heavily on Austria, leading the Austrians to seek a larger quota of common expenses from the Hungarians. There was also a dispute regarding whether Hungary was responsible for a debt of 80 million gulden to the bank.

The negotiations for this settlement began in 1876 and lasted two full years, concluding in June 1878. Because the previous arrangements expired at the end of 1877, they had to be prolonged until the middle of 1878. The process required the two ministries to first agree on bill drafts, which were then reviewed by committees from both parliaments through verbal discussions and notes before final parliamentary debates.

## Banking and Currency Reforms
The 1877 settlement had a direct impact on the monarchy's financial institutions. Prior to this, the Austro-Hungarian Bank (established in 1867) was entirely Austrian in its direction, though it maintained branches in Hungary. Because the Hungarians lacked sufficient credit to establish their own national bank, the 1877 settlement converted the institution into a truly Austro-Hungarian bank. This included the establishment of a head office at Pest in addition to the one in Vienna, with management divided between the two countries. This specific arrangement was later renewed in 1887.

These institutional changes contributed to an improvement in financial conditions that eventually allowed the government to address currency reform. This later operation, begun in 1892, focused on three goals: the redemption of *Staatsnoten* (government notes issued during the war of 1866), the resumption of cash payments by the bank, and the substitution of a gold standard for the silver standard (gulden).
