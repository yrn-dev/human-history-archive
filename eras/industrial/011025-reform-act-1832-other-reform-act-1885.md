# The Reform Acts of 1832 and 1885

The Reform Acts of 1832 and 1885, along with associated redistribution legislation, represented a significant shift in the parliamentary representation of various English counties and boroughs. These measures aimed to address systemic issues in the electoral system, including the existence of "rotten boroughs" and the imbalance of representation relative to population.

## The Reform Act of 1832

The Reform Act of 1832 was a central piece of legislation for the Whig government that took office in 1832. The policy of parliamentary reform was a priority for Prime Minister Lord Grey and Lord Durham, the latter of whom is credited as the chief author of the proposals. Lord Durham chaired a "committee of four"—which included Lord Duncannon, Sir James Graham, and Lord John Russell—to draw up the scheme submitted to parliament. Lord John Russell was selected by Durham to introduce the bill in the House of Commons. The process was marked by political tension, including a deadlock between the House of Commons and the House of Lords, which led Lord Durham to urge the creation of new peers to overcome resistance.

The 1832 Act sought to purge the system of "medieval dross" and "noxious rubbish." In Cornwall, which was notorious for its rotten boroughs, the impact was severe. For example, in an area near Liskeard that had a population of only 14,224 on the eve of the Act, there had been nine parliamentary boroughs returning eighteen members; these were drastically reduced. Fourteen Cornish boroughs were disfranchised, including Bossiney, Camelford, Lostwithiel, East Looe, West Looe, and Fowey. Lostwithiel, which had been represented by two members from 1305 to 1832, was merged into the county.

Other regional changes included:
* **Durham:** The county was reorganized to return two members for two divisions, while the boroughs of Sunderland, South Shields, and Gateshead gained representation.
* **Dorsetshire:** The borough of Corfe Castle was disfranchised, and the county returned three members.
* **Buckinghamshire:** Total representation for the county and its boroughs was reduced from fourteen to eleven members.
* **Ashburton:** This borough was deprived of one of its two members.

Parallel to these changes, Thomas Francis Kennedy played a prominent role in the construction of the Scottish Reform Act. Following the Whig accession to office in 1832, Kennedy contributed to further Scottish reforms, including burgh reform and the reform of sheriff courts.

## The Redistribution Act of 1885

The subsequent redistribution of seats in 1885 further altered the parliamentary landscape, often continuing the trend of disfranchising smaller boroughs in favor of broader county representation.

In Cornwall, the 1885 Act ended the separate representation of several boroughs that had survived the 1832 reforms, specifically Bodmin, Helston, Launceston, Liskeard, and St Ives. Additionally, the two members previously returned by Truro and the combined representation of Penryn and Falmouth were removed.

Other notable changes under the 1885 legislation included:
* **Buckinghamshire:** The boroughs were entirely deprived of representation, and the county returned three members for three divisions.
* **Dorsetshire:** Following the earlier disfranchisement of Lyme Regis in 1868, the remaining boroughs were disfranchised in 1885.
* **Durham:** The boroughs of Hartlepool, Stockton, and Darlington, which had returned one member each since 1868, lost their representation.
* **Ashburton:** The borough was deprived of its final remaining member.

## Legal and Political Context

The drive toward reform was influenced by a general interest in "law reform" and the utilitarian ideas of Jeremy Bentham. Bentham advocated for the overthrow of "judge-made law" and the removal of obsolete medieval legal remnants such as benefit of clergy and trial by battle. His influence extended to figures like Brougham and Romilly. This intellectual climate of "political regeneration" was also championed by individuals such as Thomas Francis Kennedy and Lord Cockburn, who sought to improve pauper administration and parish schools while removing corrupt legal procedures.
