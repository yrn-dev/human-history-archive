# Francis Asbury and Methodist History

## Life of Francis Asbury
Francis Asbury (1745–1816) was an American clergyman born on August 20, 1745, at Hamstead Bridge in the parish of Handsworth, near Birmingham, in Staffordshire, England. Born to poor parents, Asbury attended the village school of Barre for a brief period before being apprenticed at age fourteen to a maker of tongues, also known as "buckle chapes." 

Asbury's conversion to Methodism occurred in his thirteenth year, and by the age of sixteen, he had become a local preacher. Described as a fluent and simple speaker, his success led John Wesley to enroll him as a regular itinerant minister in 1767. In 1771, Asbury volunteered for missionary work in the American colonies. Upon his arrival in Philadelphia in October of that year, the Methodist movement in the colonies was small, consisting of scarcely 300 converts. Asbury's efforts infused new life into the movement, resulting in the membership of several congregations more than doubling within a single year.

## Leadership in America
In 1772, John Wesley appointed Asbury as "general assistant" to oversee the work in America. Although Thomas Rankin, an older preacher, superseded him in 1773, Asbury remained practically in control of the operations. 

The outbreak of the War of Independence brought challenges to the Methodist community, which then numbered several thousand. Because they refused to take the prescribed oath, Methodists fell under unjust suspicion of Loyalism. This climate led many ministers, including Thomas Rankin, to return to England. Asbury chose to remain at his post due to his duties and sympathies toward the colonies. Despite being threatened and arrested once, he continued his itinerant preaching. Eventually, hostility from authorities in Maryland forced him into a two-year exile in Delaware, where he remained active. In 1782, he was reappointed to supervise the affairs of Methodist congregations in America. In 1784, John Wesley took the radical step of appointing Francis Asbury and the Rev. Thomas Coke as "bishops" or superintendents of the church in the United States, an action taken in disregard of the Established Church's authority.

## Legacy and Commemoration
The life and work of Francis Asbury have been documented in several historical accounts and biographies. These include *The Pioneer Bishop; or, The Life and Times of Francis Asbury* by W.P. Strickland (1858), *Bishop Asbury* by F.W. Briggs (1874), and *Francis Asbury* by H.M. Du Bose (1909). Other significant works include J.B. Wakeley's *Heroes of Methodism* (1856) and W.C. Larrabee's two-volume work, *Asbury and His Co-Laborers* (1853). Additionally, Asbury's own *Journals* (published in three volumes in 1852) serve as both a history of his life's work and a valuable commentary on the industrial and social history of the United States during its first forty years.

Asbury's name is also preserved in Asbury Park, a city in Monmouth County, New Jersey. Founded in 1869 and named in honor of the Rev. Francis Asbury, the city was incorporated as a borough in 1874 and chartered as a city in 1897.
