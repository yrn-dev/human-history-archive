# Tank Boilers and the Galloway Design

## Classification of Boilers
Boilers are generally categorized into two distinct classes: "tank" boilers and "water-tube" boilers. Tank boilers contain relatively large quantities of water and typically feature an outer shell that is wholly or partially cylindrical, a design that provides necessary strength without requiring a large number of stays. These are generally internally fired, with the furnace plates surrounded by water to create the most efficient heating surfaces. After leaving the furnace, combustion products are directed into a chamber and then through flues or numerous small tubes to transmit heat to the water.

In contrast, water-tube boilers contain water mainly within numerous small tubes, which form the major portion of the heating surface. The fire is usually placed beneath these tubes, and both are enclosed in a casing of brickwork or other fire-resisting material. While tank boilers generally maintain close contact between heating surfaces and water, water-tube boilers require specific design provisions to maintain water circulation through the tubes.

## The Lancashire and Galloway Boilers
Among large stationary tank boilers, the "Lancashire" boiler and its modification, the "Galloway" boiler, are the most commonly used forms. These boilers typically range from 26 to 30 feet in length with diameters between 6-1/2 and 8 feet. 

The Lancashire boiler features two cylindrical furnace flues that extend for the entire length of the boiler. In the Galloway modification, the two furnaces extend only about 9 or 10 feet into the boiler. They lead into a large flue or chamber that extends from the furnace end to the end of the boiler. This chamber is fitted with a number of "Galloway" tubes.

These Galloway tubes are conical and feature a flange at each end for connection to the furnace plate. They are proportioned so that the diameter of the large end is slightly greater than the flange of the small end, allowing for easy removal and replacement. These tubes serve several purposes: they increase the heating surface, stiffen the flue, promote water circulation, and improve evaporative efficiency by mixing the flue gases.

## Construction and Pressure
Furnace flues are typically constructed in sections 3 to 3-1/2 feet long. Each section consists of a plate bent into a cylindrical form with a welded longitudinal joint and flanged ends. These sections are joined using an "Adamson" joint, which prevents rivets or double thicknesses of plate from being exposed to the fire and acts as a stiffening ring to prevent the flue from collapsing.

Working pressures vary based on the engine being supplied:
* Older boilers: approximately 60 lb. per sq. in.
* Compound engines: 100 lb. to 120 lb. per sq. in.
* Triple expansion engines: 150 lb. to 170 lb. per sq. in.
* Some constructions: up to 200 lb. per sq. in.

For requirements where a diameter of less than 6-1/2 feet is needed, a "Cornish" boiler is used; this is a type similar to the Lancashire boiler but containing only one furnace.

## Flue Arrangements and Heat Recovery
In certain boiler types, brickwork is constructed to create one central flue along the bottom and two side flues extending up the sides near the water level. Typically, flue gases are divided upon leaving the internal flue, with half returning along each side flue to the front, then passing downward into the central flue to travel under the boiler to the back end before entering the chimney. This arrangement can be reversed to promote more rapid water circulation, though this requires two dampers and is unsuitable for boilers where heavy deposits form on the bottoms.

To improve fuel economy, "fuel economizers"—consisting of numerous water-containing tubes—may be placed between the boiler and the chimney. Additionally, waste heat from other sources, such as reverberatory and air furnaces used in iron manufacture, can be saved by passing flames through the flues of steam boilers.

## Integration with Destructors
Boilers are sometimes installed to utilize high temperatures from destructor stations. In these systems, refuse is fed into furnaces and passes through a combustion chamber into a main flue, which then leads to the boilers where the heat is absorbed. 

At a site in Edinburgh, a dust-catching apparatus designed by the Horsfall Furnace Syndicate is situated between the destructor cells and the boiler. This apparatus consists of a circular brickwork block 18 feet in diameter and 13 feet 7 inches high. It features a spiral flue winding upwards and an interior domed chamber 6 feet in diameter and 12 feet high. This system maintains a steady temperature between 1500º and 1800° F, serving as a heat reservoir that helps maintain equable steam pressure in the boiler over long periods.
