# Kentucky (1887–1891)

The period between 1887 and 1891 in Kentucky was marked by significant administrative transitions, the development of its constitutional framework, and the growth of its industrial and educational sectors.

## Executive Leadership and Governance
From 1887 to 1891, the governor of Kentucky was Simon Bolivar Buckner. Born in Hart County in 1823, Buckner had a distinguished military career prior to his governorship. A graduate of West Point (1844), he served as an assistant professor of geography, history, and ethics at the institution between 1845 and 1846. He fought in the Mexican War, receiving brevet promotions to first lieutenant for gallantry at Churubusco and to captain following the storming of Chapultepec. 

Buckner's political and military involvement during the American Civil War was complex; he initially served as commander of the state guard during Governor Beriah Magoffin's attempt to maintain Kentucky's neutrality. However, in September 1861, he openly supported the Confederate cause, eventually attaining the rank of lieutenant-general. He is noted for surrendering Fort Donelson in February 1862 after the escape of Generals Pillow and Floyd. Following his term as governor, Buckner remained active in politics, serving as the candidate for vice-president of the United States in 1896 for the National or "Gold" Democrats.

## Constitutional Development
The year 1890 was a pivotal moment for the state's legal structure. Governor Buckner served as a member of the Kentucky constitutional convention of 1890. The proceedings and debates of this convention were documented in a four-volume official report published in 1890. This process culminated in the Constitution of 1891, which is contained within the *Official Manual for the Use of the Courts, State and County Officials and General Assembly of the State of Kentucky*.

## Industry and Natural Resources
During the late 19th century, Kentucky's economy was heavily influenced by its mineral wealth, particularly coal. While the state's mineral resources were considered important and valuable, they remained relatively underdeveloped during this era. Bituminous coal served as the principal mineral. Data from the archive indicates a significant increase in coal production toward the end of the century; for instance, the output in 1889 was 2,399,755 short tons, a figure that grew to 6,766,984 short tons by 1902.

The state's coal production was divided between the western coalfield (covering 5,800 sq. m.) and the eastern coalfield (covering approximately 11,180 sq. m.). By 1907, the western district's output exceeded that of the eastern district. Additionally, Kentucky established itself as the largest producer of cannel coal in the Union.

Other regional economic activities included the tobacco industry, which was the chief interest of cities such as Hopkinsville, alongside trade in coal and other agricultural products. Manufacturing in these areas included the production of flour, bricks, lime, dressed lumber, and carriages and wagons.

## Education and Institutions
The late 19th century saw the establishment and growth of various educational and professional institutions. In 1887, the Louisville College of Dentistry was established as a department of Central University. For the African American population, the Kentucky Normal and Industrial School was maintained at Frankfort, having been established in 1886.

In the city of Danville, the community remained an educational hub. It was the site of the Kentucky state institution for deaf mutes (established 1823) and Caldwell College for women (established 1860). The city also hosted a Presbyterian theological seminary founded in 1853.
