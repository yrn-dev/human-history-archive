# Bankruptcy

Bankruptcy is the legal status of a debtor who has been declared by a judicial process to be unable to pay his debts. While the terms "bankruptcy" and "insolvency" are sometimes used interchangeably, they have distinct legal and commercial meanings. Insolvency occurs when a person's financial liabilities exceed their means of meeting them. An insolvent person may continue business by using credit to pay old debts with new ones, or they may regain solvency without creditors becoming aware of their condition. Furthermore, a debtor may be insolvent without becoming bankrupt if they can reach a voluntary arrangement with creditors. However, a person cannot be a bankrupt without being insolvent, as bankruptcy is the legal declaration of that insolvency and initiates a statutory system for administering the debtor's property, removing it from their personal control.

## National Systems of Bankruptcy

### France
In France, bankruptcy is governed by the Commercial Code of 1807 and the law of June 9, 1838. Under Article 437 of the code, bankruptcy is defined as the state of a trader unable to meet commercial engagements. The French system distinguishes between simple insolvency (*faillite*) and insolvency involving fraud or misconduct (*banqueroute simple* or *banqueroute frauduleuse*). 

Only a trader can be declared bankrupt, and the debt used to obtain adjudication must be a commercial debt, as the laws are designed exclusively for the protection of commerce. A trader does not need to be insolvent to be made a bankrupt; suspending payment is sufficient. This applies to individual traders and commercial companies of all kinds. Adjudication can occur via the trader's own petition, a creditor's petition, or *proprio motu* by the court. A petitioning debtor must file a declaration of suspension within fifteen days at the Tribunal of Commerce, providing a true account of assets, debts, profits, losses, and personal expenses.

Upon adjudication, the Tribunal of Commerce appoints a *syndic provisoire* to manage the estate and a *juge commissaire* to supervise the syndic. Bankruptcy ends through a sale of assets (*union*), an ordinary composition (*concordat*), or a composition by relinquishment of assets. Notably, a discharge in bankruptcy leaves unpaid balances as a debt of honour. This reflects a resolution from the French Revolution's National Convention stating that any man who contracts a debt should never be free from liability to pay it; consequently, a trader is not rehabilitated until every penny is paid.

### Italy
Regulated by the Commercial Code of 1883, Italian bankruptcy is restricted to merchants, defined as those who engage in commercial business as a habitual profession, including merchant companies. Proceedings may be initiated by the debtor, a creditor for a commercial debt, or the court. While the amount of debt is immaterial, bankruptcy can only be declared if there is insolvency.

The judgment deprives the bankrupt of the right to administer their affairs and appoints a trustee to realize property under the supervision of a judge and a commission of creditors. All movable and immovable property is sold by auction and distributed in dividends. Bankruptcy may also be closed by an arrangement, provided it has the assent of creditors representing three-fourths of the indebtedness. Italian law does not recognize composition before bankruptcy. Additionally, bankrupts may face criminal proceedings for offenses such as failing to keep books as prescribed by law.

### United States
The United States bankruptcy system is embodied in the National Bankruptcy Act of 1898 and the act of 1903. Unlike the French or Italian systems, any person—trader or non-trader—may avail themselves of the act. However, corporations may be petitioned against but cannot petition themselves.

Acts of bankruptcy include removing property to hinder creditors, transferring property to prefer a creditor while insolvent, allowing a creditor to obtain a preference, making a general assignment for creditors, or admitting in writing an inability to pay and a willingness to be adjudicated bankrupt. Notably, the non-payment of debts is not itself an act of bankruptcy; a debtor can only be adjudicated bankrupt on the ground of indebtedness with their own written consent.

Involuntary proceedings require the debtor to owe $1,000 or more, and a petitioning creditor's debt must be at least $500. Insolvency is defined practically: the aggregate of property at fair valuation must be insufficient to pay debts. Administration is handled by district courts through referees, and creditors appoint a trustee.

## Legal Procedures and Discharges

### Discharge and Qualifications
A court may grant a bankrupt a discharge absolutely or under specific conditions. In certain jurisdictions, a discharge may be refused or suspended for at least two years if the bankrupt committed a criminal offense connected to the bankruptcy, failed to keep proper books, traded after knowing they were insolvent, or contributed to the bankruptcy through gambling, rash speculation, or culpable neglect. Other grounds for refusal include contracting debts without a reasonable probability of payment or giving an undue preference within three months preceding the receiving order.

An order of discharge generally releases the debtor from obligations, except for debts incurred by fraud, debts due to the crown, and other public obligations requiring Treasury consent.

### Disqualifications and Annulment
A bankrupt is disqualified from serving as a member of the legislature, a justice of the peace, mayor, alderman, or in various other public roles (such as school or burial boards) during their bankruptcy or until five years after discharge. These restrictions may be waived if the bankruptcy is annulled or if the court certifies the bankruptcy was caused by misfortune without misconduct. An adjudication may be annulled if the court finds it should not have been made, if debts are paid in full, or if a composition is approved.

## Historical and Regional Variations

### England and Scotland
In England, non-traders were originally excluded from bankruptcy courts and instead used the "court for relief of insolvent debtors," where they could avoid imprisonment by surrendering property but were not discharged from debts. Later, non-traders were permitted to obtain the benefits of bankruptcy laws, including discharge.

In Scotland, bankruptcy law arose as a remedy against the frauds of insolvent debtors. An act of the Scottish parliament in 1621 declared that debtors should not fraudulently diminish funds belonging to creditors. The act of 1696 defined a "notour bankrupt" as a debtor who, while under diligence, was imprisoned, fled, or absconded, and was subsequently found insolvent by the lords of session. Unlike the old English system, Scottish law does not recognize the distinction between traders and non-traders.
