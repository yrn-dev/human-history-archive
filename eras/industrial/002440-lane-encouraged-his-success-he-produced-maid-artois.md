# Drury Lane

Drury Lane is a location in London associated with a long history of theatrical production, residential life, and artistic performance.

## The Theatre Royal, Drury Lane
The Theatre Royal in Drury Lane was established by Thomas Killigrew, an English dramatist and wit. In 1660, Killigrew and Sir William Davenant received a patent to erect a new playhouse that would operate independently of the censorship of the master of the revels. While Killigrew's company, known as the King's Servants, initially performed at the Red Bull, Killigrew built the original Theatre Royal in Drury Lane for them in 1663. By 1664, Killigrew had visited Rome to secure scene decorators and singers with the intention of hosting four opera seasons of six weeks each per year.

In the 18th and 19th centuries, the theatre remained a central hub for English drama and opera. Hugh Kelly, an Irish dramatist, had his first comedy, *False Delicacy*, produced there by Garrick on January 23, 1768. Kelly also produced *A Word to the Wise* at Drury Lane on March 3, 1770, though the production was abandoned following riots during the first two performances. Other notable actors associated with the theatre include Robert Baddeley, who was a regular member of the company in London by 1763 and found great success in servants' parts and low comedy. Baddeley later bequeathed funds to provide wine and cake in the Drury Lane green-room every Twelfth Night, a tradition that became a regular institution.

The theatre also served as a venue for the Kemble family. John Philip Kemble made his first appearance there on September 30, 1783, as Hamlet. He and his sister appeared together at Drury Lane on November 22, 1783, in Moore's *The Gamester* and Shakespeare's *King John*. In 1835, the composer Michael William Balfe had his work *Siege of Rochelle* produced and rapturously received at the theatre. Following this success, Balfe produced *The Maid of Artois* at Drury Lane on May 27, 1836, a production further enhanced by the singing of Malibran.

## Management and Administration
The management of Drury Lane saw various leadership changes and challenges. Alfred Bunn was appointed stage-manager in 1823. By 1833, Bunn undertook the joint management of Drury Lane and Covent Garden. His tenure was marked by vigorous opposition and a legal battle over the abolition of patent theatres; while a bill was passed in the House of Commons, it was thrown out by the House of Lords following a petition by Bunn. Bunn faced further difficulties with his company and the lord chamberlain, as well as a personal quarrel with the tragedian Macready, who assaulted him. Despite being declared a bankrupt in 1840, Bunn continued to manage Drury Lane until 1848. Artistically, his tenure was considered highly successful, as he produced the principal works of Balfe in an attempt to establish English opera, often translating the libretti himself.

## Other Associations with Drury Lane
Beyond the Theatre Royal, the area of Drury Lane was home to prominent residents and other theatrical ventures. In 1610, the poet Donne became acquainted with Sir Robert Drury of Hawsted, a wealthy gentleman who provided Donne and his wife an apartment in his large house located in Drury Lane.

Additionally, the area hosted other theatrical companies. Edward Kynaston, an English actor known for his beauty and his skill in playing female parts, set up a company in the Cockpit in Drury Lane after working as a clerk in a book-shop.
