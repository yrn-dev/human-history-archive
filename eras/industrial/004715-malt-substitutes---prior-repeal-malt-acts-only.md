# Malt Substitutes

In the United Kingdom brewing industry, malt substitutes are materials used in the manufacture of beer as alternatives or supplements to malted grain. These materials include sugar, unmalted corn, and preparations of rice and maize.

## Historical Regulation and Usage
Prior to the repeal of the Malt Acts, sugar was the only permitted substitute for malt in the United Kingdom. The use of sugar increased significantly over a twenty-five year period, rising from 295,865 cwt. in 1870 to 1,136,434 cwt. in 1880, and reaching 2,746,615 cwt. by 1905. This represents a practical trebling of sugar usage during that time, while the quantity of malt employed did not increase materially.

Following the repeal of the malt tax, other substitutes gained favor. By 1905, brewers were using 125,671 bushels of unmalted corn and 1,348,558 cwt. of rice, maize, and similar preparations. Statistical data shows a steady increase in the percentage of substitutes used in brewing:
*   1878: 6.05%
*   1883: 8.06%
*   1890: 12.48%
*   1895: 16.66%
*   1905: 23.22%

## Causes for the Increase in Substitutes
The shift toward substitutes is attributed to several complex factors. Legally, the repeal of the malt tax allowed brewers to utilize the surplus diastatic energy in malt to transform starch from non-malted grains into sugar. This diastatic enzyme is present in malted barley in great excess.

Operationally, the use of a proportion of substitutes simplifies and accelerates brewing operations, allowing brewers to increase their turn-over by producing more beer in a given time using the same plant. Economically, certain substitutes are cheaper than malt, providing a means to reduce costs amidst keen modern competition.

Furthermore, it is argued that prohibiting substitutes would not increase the use of domestic barley, as the supply of home barley suitable for malting is limited. A return to a "malt and hops only" policy would likely increase the use of foreign barley. Sugar and prepared cereals are considered better diluents than sun-dried foreign barleys because they contain less nitrogen.

## Quality and Application
While substitutes offer economic and operational advantages, excessive use leads to poor quality beer. Better class brewers typically limit substitutes to 15-20% to avoid a loss of flavor and quality.

Different types of beer utilize different combinations of materials:
*   **Pale Ales:** Made from pale malt (often a mixture of English and fine foreign malts like California or Smyrna) alone, or pale malt combined with glucose, invert sugar, rice, or a small amount of flaked maize.
*   **Running Beers (Mild Ale):** Produced from a mixture of sugar, flaked goods, and amber and pale malts.
*   **Stout:** Made from a mixture of roasted (black), amber, and pale malts, sometimes with the addition of flaked maize or a little sugar.

## Taxation and Legal Framework
The use of substitutes has been closely tied to UK taxation. Sugar was first permitted in brewing in 1847. A sugar tax was imposed in 1850 (1s. 4d. per cwt.), which fluctuated and reached 11s. 6d. in 1874 before being repealed in 1880. A general sugar tax was reintroduced in 1901 at 4s. 2d. and under; currently, sugar used in brewing is subject to both this general tax and the beer duty.

The Free Mash-Tun Act of 1880 shifted the duty from malt to the beer (or wort), effectively leaving brewers unrestricted in their choice of corn, sugar, or other saccharine substitutes, provided they were not drugs or deleterious articles. However, "saccharin" (a coal-tar derivative) was prohibited in 1888 because it created an apparent palate-fulness that exceeded the real gravity of the beer by roughly 4°, causing a loss in revenue.

Under the 1880 Act, equivalents were established: 28 lb weight of sugar or 42 lb weight of corn were deemed equivalent to one bushel of malt. Until 1896, preparations such as flaked maize and rice were classed as corn or malt regarding their wort-producing powers, but after 1896, they were classified as sugar.
