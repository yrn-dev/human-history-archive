# The Transition from Wrought Iron to Mild Steel in Bridge Construction

The evolution of bridge materials has progressed from the timber, masonry, brick, and concrete used in medieval and classical times to the adoption of metals. Beginning in the late 18th century, wrought iron was introduced, initially used in combination with cast iron or timber. By the middle of the 19th century, wrought iron was used on a large scale for suspension road bridges and entirely for great girder bridges, such as those at Saltash near Plymouth and over the Menai Strait.

## The Introduction of Mild Steel
From 1880 onwards, mild steel (also referred to as ingot metal) was introduced as a replacement for wrought iron. Mild steel is characterized as a tougher, stronger, and better material. Because of its superior tenacity and toughness, it has wholly superseded wrought iron in bridge construction, with the exception of girders with a span of less than 100 ft. 

The use of metal has enabled the construction of great spans ranging from 500 to 1800 ft. However, the early introduction of mild steel was sometimes treacherous, leading to accidents with obscure causes. It was later understood that quality varies significantly based on thermal treatment during manufacture or small differences in composition. Consequently, precise specifications of quality and rigorous testing of supplied materials became necessary. There remains ongoing controversy regarding the safe percentage of carbon or degree of hardness permitted for steel used in structures.

## Material Properties and Comparisons
Structural wrought iron exhibits different properties depending on the direction of rolling. In the direction of rolling, it has an ultimate elongation of 8% or 10% in 8 inches and a tenacity of 20 to 22½ tons per sq. in. Across the direction of rolling, the tenacity drops to approximately 18 tons per sq. in. and the elongation to 3% in 8 inches. In contrast, steel shows only a small difference in quality across different directions.

## Application in Girder Bridges
The expansion of railways since 1830 necessitated a vast number of bridges, most of which were girder bridges. These structures are designed so that approximately half of the superstructure is in compression and half is in tension. Girder bridges are considered convenient and economical; while they require more material than a chain or arch, they are easier to erect and impose only a vertical load on abutments and piers, avoiding the horizontal thrust associated with suspension chains or arches.

A notable example of these materials is the Ohio bridge on the Cincinnati & Covington railway, constructed in 1888 by the Phoenix Bridge Company. This bridge features a center span of 550 ft. and side spans of 490 ft. While the rest of the bridge is made of mild steel, the wind-bracing, stringers, and cross girders are wrought iron. The total weight of the steel and iron across the three spans is approximately 5000 tons.

## Other Metallic and Composite Applications
Before the dominance of mild steel, early braced bridges in America and England often used a combination of metals: cast iron (typically in circular or octagonal tubes) for compression members and wrought iron for tension members. An example is the Newark Dyke bridge (1851-1853), which utilized cast iron hollow castings for the top flange and struts, and flat wrought iron links for the ties and lower flange. Similarly, the early American Fink truss used cast iron for compression and wrought iron for tension.

In suspension bridges, wrought iron has also played a significant role. The Clifton bridge over the Avon uses wrought iron stiffening girders and saddles, though it employs steel rollers on the piers. The Niagara bridge, built between 1852 and 1855, originally featured a timber stiffening girder that was replaced by an iron one in 1880, before the entire structure was replaced by a steel arch in 1896-1897 to accommodate heavier railway traffic.

Additionally, the St Louis bridge (completed 1874) utilizes arches of cast steel. Its double ribs of steel tubes are clasped together using wrought iron couplings.

## Recent Developments
The most recent change in bridge materials is the introduction of concrete strengthened with steel bars, also known as armoured concrete or ferro-concrete, specifically for arched bridges.
