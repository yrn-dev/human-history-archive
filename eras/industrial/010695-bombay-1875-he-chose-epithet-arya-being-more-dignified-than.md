# Bombay in 1875

## Urban Development and Infrastructure
By 1875, Bombay had entered a period of unrestricted development following the annexation of the Deccan and the British victory over the Mahrattas. The city's growth was driven by several key factors, including the foundation of cotton factories, the opening of the Suez Canal, and the influence of railways, with the first railway line having been completed in 1853. 

The city's maritime infrastructure was particularly significant due to the rise of the tide, which reached an average of 14 feet and spring tides of 17 feet, making Bombay the only important location near the sea in India capable of supporting large-scale docks. A major milestone in this infrastructure occurred in 1875, when the Prince of Wales laid the foundation-stone for Prince's dock. This dock, which later opened in 1879, measured 1000 feet broad by 1460 feet long, providing a water area of 30 acres.

## Population and Economic Trends
The population of Bombay experienced a rapid increase during the 19th century. In 1838, the population stood at 236,000, and by 1891, it had more than doubled to 821,000. While the city generally prospered, this growth was interrupted in 1866-1867 by a financial crisis caused by a fall in cotton prices following the end of the American war. Despite this, the city recovered and reached a peak of prosperity by 1891, before suffering a subsequent decade of decline in both trade and population due to the plague.

## Religious and Social Movements
The year 1875 also marked a significant social development in Bombay with the establishment of the first *Samajes* by Dayanand. Having found that his views received scanty acceptance among the Brahmans, Dayanand turned his efforts toward the masses. In establishing these organizations, he adopted the epithet "Arya," as he considered it to be more dignified than the term "Hindu," which he viewed as slightly contemptuous. Following the establishment of the *Samajes* in Bombay, Dayanand conducted a series of successful tours involving public debates with Christian missionaries and orthodox pundits before his death in 1883 at Ajmere.

## Philological Context of "Aryan"
The term "Arya" used by Dayanand shares a linguistic history explored by philologists such as Max Muller. In a linguistic sense, Muller used "Aryan" as a short term for the body of Indo-Germanic or Indo-European languages. He defined "Aryas" simply as those who speak these languages, regardless of their blood, color, or anatomical characteristics. 

Historically, the word was used as a national name in Persia, Bactria, and India. Within Sanskrit, the term appears in different forms: *arya* (the origin of Aryan) and *arya*, the latter of which appears in the *Rig Veda* as an epithet for deities. Some interpretations suggest *aryas* may be the genitive of *ari*, meaning "pious, devoted, [or] active." Scholars use the compound "Indo-Aryan" to describe the branch of the Eastern Indo-Europeans who settled in India, distinguishing them from the Iranians.
