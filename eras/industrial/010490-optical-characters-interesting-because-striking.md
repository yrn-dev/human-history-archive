# Optical Characters and Investigations

In the study of mineralogy and physics, optical characters refer to the way substances interact with light, particularly regarding refraction, polarization, and dispersion. These characteristics are essential for identifying minerals and understanding the internal structure of crystals.

## Optical Properties of Borax and Boracite

Borax, a mineral with the composition $\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7 + 10\text{H}_2\text{O}$, belongs to the monoclinic system. It is noted for having particularly interesting optical characters due to the striking crossed dispersion of its optic axes. When a section of a borax crystal is cut parallel to its plane of symmetry and viewed through convergent polarized light, the resulting optic figure is symmetrical only in relation to the central point. There is a measurable difference in the optic axes based on the color of light: the plane for red light is inclined at 2 degrees to that for blue light, and the angle between the optic axes is 3 degrees greater for red light than for blue light.

Boracite presents a different set of optical anomalies. While its general characters align with cubic symmetry, examination in polarized light reveals it to be doubly refracting, a discovery first made by Sir David Brewster in 1821. Thin sections of boracite show a division into optically biaxial areas and twin-lamellae. Through specific section cutting, it can be demonstrated that a rhombic dodecahedral crystal of boracite is actually composed of twelve orthorhombic pyramids with apices meeting at the center. 

The optical characters of boracite change when heated; at 265 degrees, the crystals suddenly become optically isotropic, though the complex internal structure returns upon cooling. This suggests the existence of two crystalline modifications: a cubic modification stable above 265 degrees and an orthorhombic or monoclinic modification stable at lower temperatures.

## Optical Characteristics of Silicate Minerals

Various silicate minerals exhibit distinct optical properties used for classification:

*   **Epidote:** This monoclinic mineral's optical constants vary depending on the amount of iron present (ranging from 5-17% $\text{Fe}_2\text{O}_3$). It exhibits strong pleochroism, typically showing green, yellow, and brown colors.
*   **Allanite:** The crystallographic and optical characters of allanite are similar to those of epidote. It also displays strong pleochroism with greenish-brown, yellowish-, and reddish-brown colors. However, allanite is easily altered by hydration, a process that renders the mineral amorphous and optically isotropic.
*   **Bytownite:** A plagioclase felspar found in basic igneous rocks like basalt and gabbro, bytownite possesses optical and physical characters that place it closer to the anorthite end of the series than to albite.

## Historical Optical Investigations

Several individuals have contributed significantly to the understanding of optical phenomena and defects:

**Robert Hooke**
Hooke's optical investigations led him to observe the phenomenon of diffraction, independently of F. M. Grimaldi. He also anticipated the doctrine of interference and adopted an imperfect form of the undulatory theory of light. Additionally, Hooke suggested a system of optical telegraphy.

**Jean Baptiste Biot**
A French physicist, Biot's most important work was of an optical character. He focused specifically on the polarization of light. His observations in this field earned him the Rumford medal of the Royal Society in 1840 and provided the necessary foundations for the polarimetric analysis of sugar. He also conducted research into the refractive properties of various gases alongside F.J.D. Arago.

**John Dalton and Optical Defects**
The investigator John Dalton studied optical defects, specifically color-blindness. One such case was Yule, whose color-blindness was of particular interest to Dalton. In 1859, Dalton sent Yule skeins of colored silks to name. This optical peculiarity was present in Yule's elder brother and across five generations of their mother's family, though it notably passed from uncle to nephew rather than from parent to child.
