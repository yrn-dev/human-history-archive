# Water-Tube Boilers and Specialized Heating Systems

Water-tube boilers are engineered systems used in various capacities, including naval vessels, land-based installations, and locomotives. These systems utilize tubes to facilitate the heating of water and the production of steam, with designs varying based on the intended application and required pressure.

## The Belleville Boiler
The Belleville boiler was one of the earliest water-tube boiler forms to achieve general use within the British navy. Its architecture is divided into two primary components: the boiler proper and the economizer. Both sections consist of several sets of elements positioned side by side. The elements of the boiler proper are located directly over the fire, while the economizer elements are situated in the uptake above the boiler. The space between these two sections serves as a combustion chamber.

Each element is constructed from seamless steel tubes that are straight and connected at their ends via screwed joints to junction-boxes made of malleable cast iron. These boxes are arranged vertically; except for those at the very front (upper and lower), each box connects the upper end of one tube to the lower end of the next. Boxes at the rear are close-ended, whereas those at the front feature a small oval hole closed by an internal door with a cross-bar and bolt, allowing for the cleaning and examination of the tube interiors.

The tubes in the boiler proper generally have an external diameter of 4-1/2 inches. Their thickness varies by row: the two lowest rows are 3/8 inch thick, the following two are 5/16 inch, and the remaining tubes are approximately 1/5 inch thick. The lower front box of each boiler element connects to a square-section horizontal cross-tube known as a "feed-collector," which spans the width of the boiler. 

The economizer follows a similar construction to the boiler proper, but utilizes shorter and smaller tubes, which are generally 2-3/4 inches in diameter. The lower boxes of these economizer elements are connected to a horizontal feed pipe supplied by a feed-pumping engine.

## The Babcock & Wilcox Boiler
Designed for land purposes, the Babcock & Wilcox boiler features a horizontal cylinder that acts as a steam chest with dished ends and two riveted cross-boxes at the bottom. A sloping nest of tubes is positioned beneath the cylinder, with the fire located under the upper end of this nest. To ensure the products of combustion circulate properly and pass between the tubes three times, brickwork baffles are placed between the tubes and between the nests and the cylinder.

The nest of tubes is composed of separate elements, each consisting of a front and back header made of sinuous wrought steel connected by tubes. The front headers' upper ends connect to the front cross-box via short tubes, while the lower ends remain closed. The back headers' upper ends connect to the back cross-box through longer pipes, and their lower ends connect via short pipes to a horizontal mud drum equipped with a blow-off pipe and cock. These headers include oval holes for fixing and cleaning tubes, secured by outer cover plates to maintain watertightness. While usual pressures are around 180 lb per sq. in., some special cases can reach 500 lb per sq. in.

## Locomotive and Other Boiler Types
Locomotive boilers typically consist of a cylindrical barrel and a nearly rectangular fire-box. The fire-box sides are secured to the shell by stays approximately 1 inch in diameter, spaced 4 inches apart. In smaller boilers, the top is stayed by longitudinal girder stays; larger boilers use vertical stays to connect the flat fire-box crown to the shell crown. The tube plate is made of copper.

While ordinary tubes are usually 1-3/4 inches in diameter, some specialized designs incorporate water-tubes. For example, a boiler used by the London & South-Western Railway Co. utilizes 112 seamless steel water-tubes, each 2-3/4 inches in diameter. These are arranged in two clusters of 56, positioned above the fire-bridge and near the fire-door end. These tubes are expanded into the fire-box side plates and supported by stay bars. Typical pressures for locomotive boilers range from 175 to 200 lb per sq. in.

Other cylindrical boilers include vertical types used for small installations or ship auxiliaries. The simple vertical boiler features a tapering fire-box and an internal smoke stack. Some include cross-tubes approximately 9 inches in diameter made of 3/8-inch plate. Other variations include the "Tyne" boiler, which uses a hemispherical furnace and inclined water-tubes of about 9 inches in diameter, and the "Victoria" and "Cochran" boilers, which utilize internal combustion chambers.

## The Niclausse Boiler
Used in the British and other navies, as well as electric-light installations on land, the Niclausse boiler consists of a horizontal steam-chest with elements arranged side by side over the fire. Each element features a rectangular header—usually malleable cast iron or steel—fitted with two rows of inclined seamless steel tubes sloping 6 degrees downward toward the rear. 

The tubes have a specialized construction: the back ends are reduced in diameter and closed with screwed cap-nuts, while the front ends are thickened by upsetting. The sides of the tubes are retained while the upper and lower parts are cut away, creating a "lanterne." A thin sheet steel water-circulating tube is fitted inside each generating tube, open at the bottom and secured to a smaller lanterne at the top.
