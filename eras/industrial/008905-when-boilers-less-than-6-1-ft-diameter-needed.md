# Boiler Types and Specifications

The design and construction of boilers vary significantly based on their intended application, the required heating surface, and the available installation space. These range from large stationary tank boilers to specialized express boilers used in naval vessels.

## Stationary and Tank Boilers
Large stationary boilers commonly include the "Lancashire" boiler and its modification, the "Galloway" boiler. These typically measure 26 to 30 feet in length with diameters between 6-1/2 and 8 feet. The Lancashire boiler features two cylindrical furnace flues that extend the full length of the boiler. In contrast, the Galloway boiler's furnaces extend only about 9 or 10 feet, leading into a large flue or chamber containing several Galloway tubes that run to the end of the boiler. These conical tubes are designed with a large end slightly greater than the flange of the small end for easy replacement; they serve to stiffen the flue, promote water circulation, and improve evaporative efficiency.

When a boiler with a diameter of less than approximately 6-1/2 feet is required, a "Cornish" boiler is used. This type is similar to the Lancashire boiler but contains only one furnace.

Working pressures for these boilers vary by the engine type they supply:
* Older boilers: approximately 60 lb. per sq. in.
* Compound engines: 100 to 120 lb. per sq. in.
* Triple expansion engines: 150 to 170 lb. per sq. in.
* Some constructions: up to 200 lb. per sq. in.

## Vertical and Small Installation Boilers
For small installations or areas where floor space is limited, vertical cylindrical boilers are employed. The simple vertical boiler consists of a plain, slightly tapering vertical fire-box with an internal smoke stack traversing the steam space. Except in the smallest sizes, these are fitted with cross tubes (approximately 9 inches in diameter and made of 3/8-in. plate) that are slightly inclined to facilitate water circulation. This type is frequently used for auxiliary purposes on ships.

Other vertical types provide more heating surface:
* **Tyne Boiler:** Features a hemispherical furnace where combustion products enter an upper chamber traversed by several vertical water-tubes and four or more inclined water-tubes of about 9 inches in diameter.
* **Victoria Boiler:** Produced by Messrs Clarke, Chapman & Co., this boiler uses a hemispherical furnace and leads gases to an internal combustion chamber and then through horizontal smoke-tubes to a side smoke-box.
* **Cochran Boiler:** Similar to the Victoria, but the combustion chamber has a "dry" back with firebrick-lined doors for easy access to tube ends instead of a water space.

## Marine and Express Boilers
The cylindrical multitubular return tube boiler is widely used in merchant steamers, with sizes up to 17 feet in diameter and working pressures usually between 160 and 200 lb. per sq. in. (occasionally reaching 265 lb. per sq. in.). These are available as single-ended (one to four furnaces) or double-ended (furnaces at both ends).

"Express boilers" are water-tube boilers used in cruisers and torpedo-boat destroyers to maximize power relative to weight. They utilize a high rate of combustion via "forced draught" and smaller diameter tubes.
* **Yarrow Boiler:** Consists of a large cylindrical steam chest and two lower water-chambers connected by straight tubes. In large vessels, tubes are 1-3/4 inches in external diameter. In large express versions, the two rows nearest the fire are 1-1/4 inches, with the remainder being 1 inch.
* **Thornycroft Boiler:** Uses three chambers connected by tubes that are mostly straight but bent at the ends. It employs "water-walls" where outer rows of tubes form walls at their lower parts and inner rows form walls at their upper parts.
* **Reed Boiler:** Features longer, more curved tubes than the Normand boiler and lacks water-walls. Its tubes are fitted with screw and nut connections for quick replacement.

## Locomotive Boilers
Locomotive boilers consist of a cylindrical barrel and a nearly rectangular fire-box. The fire-box sides are stayed to the shell by 1-inch diameter stays pitched 4 inches apart. The crown of the boiler is supported by vertical stays in larger boilers, while smaller boilers use longitudinal girder stays. 

Ordinary tubes are usually 1-3/4 inches in diameter. Some models include seamless steel water-tubes across the fire-box. For example, a boiler for the London & South-Western Railway Co. features 112 water-tubes (2-3/4 inches in diameter) arranged in two clusters of 56. The typical pressure for locomotive boilers is between 175 and 200 lb. per sq. in.

## Structural Components and Stays
To maintain integrity under pressure, various staying methods are used:
* **Combustion Chambers:** Flat sides and backs are stayed to the shell or each other using screw stays with nuts inside the chambers. Tops are usually stayed by strong girders or vertical stays attached to T bars.
* **End Plates:** Longitudinal stays passing through the boiler length secure end plates in the steam space.
* **Tube Plates:** These are strengthened by stay tubes. Tube diameters vary by draught type: 3 or 3-1/4 inches for natural/chimney draught (rarely over 7 feet long) and 2-1/2 inches for forced draught (8 to 8-1/2 feet long). A clear space of 1-1/4 inches is typically maintained between tubes.
