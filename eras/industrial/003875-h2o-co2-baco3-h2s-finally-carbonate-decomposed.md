# Carbonate Formation and Decomposition in Natural and Chemical Processes

The formation of carbonates and the subsequent release of gases such as sulphuretted hydrogen are processes observed both in biological decomposition and specific chemical reactions. These processes involve the interaction of carbon dioxide, water, and various minerals or chemical compounds.

## Biological Formation of Calcium Carbonate
In natural environments such as cloacae, rivers, and the mud of marshes, anaerobic bacteria play a significant role in the circulation of carbon. These bacteria decompose cellulose—which is originally produced by chlorophyll action on atmospheric carbon dioxide using energy from the sun—by first hydrolysing it and then splitting the products into marsh gas and carbon dioxide.

When calcium sulphate is present during this process, the nascent methane induces a specific chemical reaction. This results in the formation of calcium carbonate, water, and sulphuretted hydrogen. This biological mechanism provides an explanation for why sulphuretted hydrogen and marsh gas occur in bogs. It is also highly probable that similar putrefactive changes occurring in the undigested cellulose remains within the intestines of herbivorous animals produce these same gases.

## Chemical Reactions involving Carbonates and Sulphides
Beyond biological decomposition, carbonates are utilized in the preparation of various chemical compounds. For example, potassium chromate ($\text{K}_2\text{CrO}_4$) can be prepared by neutralizing a solution of potassium bichromate using either caustic potash or potassium carbonate.

The interaction of certain compounds with sulphuretted hydrogen also leads to specific transformations. When potassium chromate is heated in a current of carbon bisulphide or sulphuretted hydrogen, it yields a mixture of sulphide and chromium sesquioxide.

## Industrial Production of Carbon-Based Gases
While not resulting in carbonates, the industrial manipulation of carbon, water, and oxygen produces various combustible gases. One such process is the creation of "water gas" (also known as "blue gas"), which burns with a non-luminous blue flame. This is produced via the endothermic reaction $\text{C} + \text{H}_2\text{O} = \text{CO} + \text{H}_2$. 

A secondary, often unwelcome reaction, $\text{C} + 2\text{H}_2\text{O} = \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2$, converts carbon into the incombustible form of carbon dioxide. In industrial settings, this is managed through alternating operations:
*   **Blowing-up:** A current of air raises the fuel temperature to approximately 1200 deg. C.
*   **Steaming:** Superheated steam is injected until the temperature drops to about 900 deg. C., at which point too much carbon dioxide begins to appear in the product.

## Thermal Values of Carbon Combustion
The energy associated with these carbon reactions varies based on the end product. One gram-molecule of carbon produces 97 great calories when burning to $\text{CO}_2$, which is the maximum effect obtainable. In the production of water gas ($\text{CO} + \text{H}_2$), the combustion of the product yields 126 great calories. However, because the oxygen is furnished from water—which requires energy to decompose (68.5 calories for liquid water or 57.6 calories for steam)—the net energy must be balanced. The difference represents the heat that must be expended from an external source to facilitate the reaction of one gram-molecule of carbon with one gram-molecule of steam.
