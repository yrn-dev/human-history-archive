# George Boole and the Treatise on Differential Equations

George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician born in Lincoln on November 2, 1815. The son of a studious tradesman of limited means who provided his first lessons in mathematical science, Boole did not initially show extraordinary mathematical powers, favoring classics instead. He began studying higher mathematics at age seventeen, though his progress was hindered by a lack of efficient help. Professionally, Boole remained in the scholastic profession throughout his life; he served as an assistant-master at a private school in Doncaster at age sixteen, established a school in Lincoln (which he later moved to Waddington), and was appointed professor of mathematics at Queen's College in Cork in 1849. In 1855, he married Mary Everest, who later wrote educational works based on his principles.

## The Treatise on Differential Equations

During his lifetime, Boole completed only two systematic treatises on mathematical subjects. The first, the well-known *Treatise on Differential Equations*, was published in 1859. This work is regarded as a valuable contribution to its field, combining profound investigation into the philosophy of the subject with elementary exposition. 

The *Treatise on Differential Equations* embodies many of Boole's most important discoveries. Specifically, the 16th and 17th chapters provide a lucid account of a general symbolic method. The skillful and bold application of this method led to Boole's primary discoveries and included a general method in analysis that he had previously described in a famous memoir printed in the *Philosophical Transactions* for 1844.

In the final years of his life, Boole worked constantly to extend his research to produce a second edition of the *Treatise on Differential Equations* that would be more complete than the first. This effort included spending part of his last vacation researching in the libraries of the British Museum and the Royal Society. However, this second edition was never finished. The manuscripts he left upon his death were so incomplete that Isaac Todhunter, who received them, found it impossible to use them for a second edition of the original treatise. Consequently, Todhunter printed these manuscripts in 1865 as a supplementary volume.

## Other Mathematical and Logical Works

Following the *Treatise on Differential Equations*, Boole published a *Treatise on the Calculus of Finite Differences* in 1860, which was intended as a sequel. Beyond these two treatises, Boole was known to the public as the author of three or four distinct publications that became standard works and numerous abstruse papers. His published output consisted of approximately fifty scattered articles and a few separate publications.

His earliest published paper, "Theory of Analytical Transformations," appeared in the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* in 1839. This publication initiated a friendship with the journal's editor, D.F. Gregory, which lasted until Gregory's premature death in 1844. Boole's contributions to journals were extensive:
* **Cambridge Mathematical Journal** (and its successor, the *Cambridge and Dublin Mathematical Journal*): 22 articles.
* **Philosophical Magazine** (third and fourth series): 16 papers.
* **Philosophical Transactions** (Royal Society): 6 important memoirs.
* **Other publications**: Papers in *Crelle's Journal*, the *Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh*, the *Transactions of the Royal Irish Academy*, and the *Bulletin de l'Academie de St-Petersbourg* for 1862 (where he was listed as G. Boldt). He also published a paper on the mathematical basis of logic in the *Mechanic's Magazine* in 1848.

## Contributions to Logic

Boole is noted as probably the first English mathematician since John Wallis to write on logic, with the exception of Augustus de Morgan. His approach to logic was driven by a profound confidence in symbolic reasoning. In the spring of 1847, he published a pamphlet titled *Mathematical Analysis of Logic*, though he later viewed this as an imperfect and hasty exposition. He considered his 1854 work, *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, on which are founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities*, to be the mature statement of his views.

Boole did not view logic as a branch of mathematics, but he identified a deep analogy between algebraic symbols and those representing logical forms and syllogisms. He proposed that logic could be seen as mathematics restricted to the quantities 0 and 1. In this system, unity (1) represented the universe of thinkable objects, while literal symbols (such as x, y, z) acted as elective symbols similar to adjectives or substantives. For example, if x represents "horned" and y represents "sheep," the combination of x and y performed on unity represents "horned sheep." Boole demonstrated that these elective symbols follow the same primary laws of combination as algebraical symbols, allowing them to be multiplied, added, subtracted, and divided.

## Mathematical Philosophy

Boole's principal characteristic was his unrivalled power and skill in tracing results, coupled with a perfect confidence in any result obtained by treating symbols according to their primary conditions and laws. He was among the most eminent mathematicians to perceive that symbols of operation could be treated as distinct objects of calculation, separate from symbols of quantity.
