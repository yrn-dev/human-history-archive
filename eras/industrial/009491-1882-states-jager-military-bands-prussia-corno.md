# The Bombardon and Bass Tuba

## Definition and Etymology
The bombardon, also referred to as the bass tuba, is the name assigned to the bass and contrabass instruments of the brass wind section within military bands. In an orchestral setting, these instruments are called the bass tuba. The term "bombardon" is derived from *bombardone*, the Italian word for the contrabass pommer (bombard). Before the fagotto was invented, the bombardone served as the bass for orchestras during the medieval period. Additionally, the term is used to describe a bass reed stop on the organ with a 16 ft. tone.

## Development and Early Forms
The bombardon was the first bass wind instrument to be equipped with valves. In its early stages, it was known by several names, including *clavicor*, *bass horn*, or *corno basso*. These names were often applied to the instrument's role as a bass within the wind section rather than to the specific design of the instrument itself. 

The original *corno basso* was a brass instrument characterized by a narrow bore and pistons that were set horizontally. This is distinct from the bass horn with keys, which eventually evolved into the ophicleide. Another variation, the German-made valve-ophicleide in F, featured three vertical pistons and a wider bore; however, it was considered a "half instrument" due to its measurement of approximately 12 ft.

## Use in Prussian Military Bands
The adoption of these instruments in Prussia followed a specific timeline. According to A. Kalkbrenner, in a biography of W. Wieprecht published in 1882, the *corno basso* (specifically the keyed bass horn) was introduced as the bass instrument in the Jager military bands of Prussia in 1829. This was followed by the introduction of the bombardon, or valve-ophicleide, in 1831. By 1835, these instruments were superseded in the Guards by the bass tuba, which was invented by J.G. Moritz and W. Wieprecht.

## Comparison with Other Wind Instruments
While the bombardon and bass tuba serve the bass role in brass sections, other instruments provide different tonal ranges in military and orchestral settings. For example, the basset horn (known in German as *Basshorn* or *Bassethorn*) is a member of the clarinet family and serves as the tenor. Unlike the brass instruments, the basset horn is a wood-wind instrument made of box-wood or cocus. 

In some military bands, the alto clarinet in Eb is used as a substitute for the basset horn. However, the basset horn differs from the alto clarinet by having a metal bell instead of a pear-shaped contracted bell, a wider bore, and a longer tube. The basset horn also features four additional long keys operated by the right thumb, allowing for an extended compass of two tones downward to F.
