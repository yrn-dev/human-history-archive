# Payment of Premiums in Building and Loan Associations

In the United States, building and loan associations function as private corporations designed for the accumulation of savings and the provision of loans for the construction of homes. These institutions—which include co-operative banks, homestead aid associations, mutual loan associations, and savings fund and loan associations—utilize a variety of financial mechanisms to manage their common treasury.

## Premium Structures and Loan Awards
The capital of a building and loan association is derived from several sources, including entrance fees, fees for transferring stock, forfeitures, fines for failing to pay fixed instalments, and periodical payments from members. A significant portion of this revenue comes from interest and premiums paid by borrowing members.

When a member wishes to borrow funds for the erection of a house, the money is typically not lent at regular interest rates. Instead, the funds are put up for auction, usually during an open meeting held at the time dues are paid. The loan is awarded to the member who bids the highest premium. To secure such a loan, the borrower must provide suitable security, which is usually the lot upon which the house will be built, and must pledge their shares of stock in the association.

## Variations in Premium Plans
There is a vast array of methods used to determine and pay premiums. In different parts of the United States, nearly seventy different plans for the payment of premiums relating to real estate loans are in vogue. While these plans vary, the general practice is for the borrower to continue making regular payments of interest and dues until the pledged shares reach their maturing value.

The nature of the premium itself can vary based on the association's rules:
* **Fixed Rates:** Some associations establish a fixed premium rate. In these cases, loans are awarded by lot or in the order that applications were received.
* **Excess Interest:** The premium may consist of the amount the borrower pays over and above the legal interest rate.
* **Advance Payments:** The premium may take the form of a specific number of interest or dues payments made in advance.

Detailed calculations regarding these premium plans, as well as the approximately twenty-five different plans for the distribution of profits and methods for withdrawals, are documented in the ninth annual report of the U.S. commissioner of labour.

## Comparison with Other Financial Methods
The building and loan method offers members the benefits of a savings bank and the accrual of compound interest. However, the economic efficiency of this method compared to ordinary savings banks is a subject of debate. In some instances, the premium required to secure a loan increases the total interest to a level that makes the building and loan method more expensive than ordinary borrowing. Despite this, these associations exert a moral influence on their members by encouraging the regular payment of instalments.

## Related Concepts of Premiums in Other Sectors
The term "premium" appears in various other financial and legal contexts within the archive:
* **Insurance:** In marine insurance, an insurance broker is employed by the assured to pay premiums to the underwriter. In fire insurance, companies fix premium rates based on the building's size and the specific trade for which it was erected. During wartime, fire insurance contracts may lapse if the first annual premium due three months after peace is not paid, while life insurance contracts may be restored through the payment of accumulated premiums with interest.
* **Banking and Bonds:** In the history of U.S. banking, the currency of the Second Bank of the United States sometimes bore a premium when exchanged for silver in the interior, southern, and western sections of the Union. Additionally, certain Austrian government bonds were issued under schemes where some were drawn by lot for payment at a premium.
