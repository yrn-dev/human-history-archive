# British Colonies

The British colonies and dominions have historically been characterized by a wide variety of legal systems, administrative structures, and economic policies, often reflecting the specific local conditions of each territory.

## Administration and Governance
Administrative structures varied significantly across the empire. In British Honduras, colonists were completely independent from 1638 to 1786, electing their own magistrates. Their customs and precedents were codified as "Burnaby's Laws" following a visit by Admiral Sir W. Burnaby in 1756. The home government appointed a superintendent in 1786, and an executive council was established in 1839, followed by a legislative assembly in 1853. By 1862, British Honduras was declared a colony with a lieutenant governor subject to the governor of Jamaica. In 1870, the legislative assembly was replaced by a legislative council, and by 1884, the lieutenant governor became an independent governor and commander-in-chief. The colony was divided into six districts: Belize, Corosal, Orange Walk, the Cayo, Stann Creek, and Toledo.

In the Nyasaland Protectorate, the districts surrounding the Shiré province and Lake Nyasa were administered directly by the imperial government via a governor, assisted by an executive and a nominated legislative council of at least three members. Conversely, the provinces of North Western and North Eastern Rhodesia were governed by two administrators of the British South Africa Chartered Company, who consulted with the colonial office and the governor of Nyasaland.

In South Africa, Sir Benjamin D'Urban served as governor of the Cape Colony starting in January 1834. His tenure saw the establishment of municipal councils and a legislative council. D'Urban also extended the colony's boundary to the Kei river to protect the eastern province from Xosa Kaffir invasions, though he was later instructed by Lord Glenelg, the secretary for the Colonies, to surrender this annexed territory.

## Legal Systems and Judicial Practices
The British empire maintained a diverse array of laws, including those of Scotland, England, the Cape Colony, and the province of Quebec. Because of this variety, British courts continued to use domicile as the only available criterion for determining the legal character of a British subject.

In British Honduras, English common law was valid, modified by local enactments and the *Consolidated Laws of British Honduras*, which were published between 1884 and 1888. Appeals from this colony could be taken to the supreme court of Jamaica or the privy council.

Regarding bankruptcy and insolvency, laws varied by region. In the Dominion of Canada, the British North America Act 1867 gave the Dominion parliament exclusive legislative power over bankruptcy, though no Dominion act existed after the 1875 act was repealed in 1880. Consequently, provincial legislatures handled bankruptcy fragmentarily, with Ontario following English law and Quebec following a French code based on *cessio bonorum*. In Cape Colony and Natal, English law was substantially followed, while the Transvaal utilized the Insolvency Law, No. 13 of 1895 (Roman-Dutch law). English-patterned laws were also used in Jamaica, Hong Kong, Mauritius, Gambia, Trinidad, Tobago, Grenada, and the Straits Settlements.

Judicial corporal punishment was generally regulated along the lines of modern English legislation. While the flogging of women was prohibited in the British colonies and British India, some colonies, such as Queensland, maintained a larger list of offences punishable by whipping than England.

## Economic and Land Legislation
Trade within the empire was historically restricted to benefit British merchants. The Navigation Act of 1660 required that goods from colonies be transported on British ships, with the master and three-fourths of the mariners being English. These restrictions lasted until the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846 and the subsequent repeal of the Navigation Acts in 1849. In 1846, colonies without responsible government were empowered to abolish differential duties on foreign trade.

Several colonial states and provinces adopted homestead laws. In Quebec, settlers on public lands could receive a patent with an exemption of up to 200 acres for themselves and their descendants. In Ontario, applicants could obtain up to 200 acres of unappropriated public land for farming if they built a house, occupied it for five years, and cultivated at least fifteen acres; however, the exemption from seizure for debts was limited to twenty years and did not cover taxes. Liberal homestead and exemption laws were also present in New Zealand, South Australia, West Australia, New South Wales, Queensland, British Columbia, and Manitoba.

## Financial Institutions
Legislation regarding building societies in several colonies mirrored that of the mother country. In the Dominion of Canada, these were sometimes called "loan companies." While not restricted to real estate loans, approximately 90% of their advances were secured by such property. By the end of 1904, these societies had liabilities to the public of £21,000,000 and to stockholders exceeding £13,000,000, with uncalled capital of £5,000,000 and a current loan balance of £28,000,000. In Australia, the movement progressed in several colonies, although Victoria experienced a crisis in which many building societies suffered severe losses.
