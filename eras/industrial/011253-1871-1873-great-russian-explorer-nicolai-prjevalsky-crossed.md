# Nicolai Prjevalsky

Nicolai Prjevalsky was a prominent Russian explorer of the 19th century whose expeditions significantly advanced the scientific understanding of the geography and hydrography of Central Asia. His work focused primarily on the vast central regions of the Asiatic continent, specifically the highlands of Tibet and the deserts of Mongolia.

## Early Expeditions and Geographical Discoveries
Between 1871 and 1873, Prjevalsky crossed the Gobi desert, traveling from the north to Kansu in western China. During this period, he mapped the hydrography of the Tsaidam region and defined its geography; this area is noted as the source of the great rivers of Burma, Siam, and China. His southward penetration during this journey brought him to within a month's march of Lhasa.

In 1876, Prjevalsky visited the Lop Nor and discovered the Altyn Tagh range. He continued his explorations in 1879, following the Urangi river to the Altai Mountains. Through this journey, he demonstrated the extraordinary physical changes that had occurred in the heart of the Asiatic continent since the time Jenghiz Khan had massed his armies in those provinces. During this expedition, he traversed the Gobi from Hami to Sachu and crossed and named the Dzungarian extension of the Gobi desert. He also visited the sources of the Salween and the Hwang-ho (Yellow river) before returning to Russia.

## Fourth Exploration and Mapping
Prjevalsky's fourth journey took place between 1883 and 1885. He traveled to Sining, a major trade center in the Chinese borderland, and proceeded through northern Tibet. This route involved crossing the Altyn Tagh to reach Lop Nor and utilizing the Cherchen-Keriya trade route to reach Khotan, from where he followed the Tarim to Aksu.

Prjevalsky's explorations provided critical data for modern map construction. Specifically, the longitude of Sachu (or Saitu) in Mongolia was revised based on the map accompanying the account of his fourth exploration, with the adopted value being 94 deg. 54' E. of Greenwich. This differed from the value of 94 deg. 26' he had provided during his second and third explorations.

## Legacy and Comparative Research
Prjevalsky is regarded as one of the greatest modern Asiatic explorers. His tracks were later followed in 1885 by Andrew Dalgleish and Arthur Douglas Carey, who added new information to the map of Asia. Other Russian explorers, such as Roborovski and Pevtsov, built upon his work in 1889-1890 and 1894, adding further knowledge regarding the northern borders of Tibet and the topography of western Chinese Turkestan.

His discovery of the Lop-nor has been a subject of geographical discussion. While the discovery of "Prjevalsky’s Lob-nor" is not in doubt, later travelers noted discrepancies in the water levels. For instance, Prince Henri d’Orléans observed that the water had receded thirteen years after Prjevalsky's visit, and suggested that the lake Prjevalsky saw was not the same as the mass of water indicated on Chinese maps. Some researchers suggest that because the soil and water spreads in this part of Asia lack fixity, several lakes may have co-existed or shifted over time.
