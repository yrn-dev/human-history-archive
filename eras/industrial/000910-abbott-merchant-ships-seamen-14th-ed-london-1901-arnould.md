# Merchant Ships and Seamen

The study of maritime activities, including the operation of merchant ships and the legal and social status of seamen, is documented through various historical records and legal authorities. These sources cover a range of topics from the technical aspects of navigation and insurance to the legal obligations of mariners and the industrialization of port towns.

## Legal Authorities and Maritime Literature
Several key texts serve as authorities on the legalities of the sea. ## The Status and Liability of Seamen
Historically, a distinction existed between the liability of sailors and other individuals, dating back to the 16th century. In 1556, an act of Philip and Mary indicated that watermen of the Thames sought exemption from the press as a privileged body, though they were ultimately declared liable, including for service as soldiers on shore.

In 1563, during the fifth year of Queen Elizabeth, "an Act touching politick considerations for the maintenance of the Navy" was passed to define the liability of sailors. This act protected mariners and fishermen from being forced to serve as soldiers on land or sea, unless they were serving under a captain of a ship or vessel for a "special exploit" typical of mariners. While originally limited to ten years, this act was renewed multiple times and eventually prolonged indefinitely in 1631, during the sixteenth year of the reign of Charles I.

Further regulations were introduced via the Vagrancy Act of 1597, which declared disreputable characters and disorderly serving-men liable to be impressed into the fleet. The "Takers"—later known as the Press Gang—were required to present commissions to two justices of the peace to select able men. However, enforcement was inconsistent, as justices in coast districts were often involved in the shipping trade themselves. This system led to various issues: pressed sailors frequently deserted with their "imprest money," naval officers complained about the poor quality of the men provided, and Press Gangs were accused of illegal arrests and extortion. Later, in 1703, during the reign of Queen Anne, an act was passed to protect the coal trade and encourage navigation and the increase of seamen.

## Navigation and Cartography
The history of seafaring is closely tied to the development of charts. Mediterranean seamen utilized *Portolano* charts, which were based on sailing directories. These charts, first mentioned in connection with the Crusade of Louis XI in 1270, originated even earlier. In the eastern Mediterranean, they used materials available before the time of Ptolemy, while the western delineations are believed to be later, possibly due to Catalan seamen.

These charts relied on estimated distances and bearings between capes and principal ports, with coastlines filled in via detailed surveys. Before the introduction of the compass, bearings were determined by observing the heavens. While Guiot de Provins suggests the compass was used as early as 1205, it only became fully serviceable after Flavio Gioja of Amalfi attached the needle to the compass card in the early 14th century. Because these charts originated without the aid of the compass, they are referred to as *Portolano* charts rather than *Compass* or *Loxodromic* charts.

## Maritime Industry and Port Towns
Maritime activity is central to the economy of various English towns. Brixham, a seaport in Devonshire, serves as the headquarters for Devonshire sea-fisheries and maintains a large coasting trade. Its industries include shipbuilding and the manufacture of sails, paint, and ropes. The town also supports the British Seamen's Orphans' home, where boys are trained as apprentices for the merchant service.

In Durham, the town of Darlington contributes to maritime infrastructure through the manufacture of ships' engines.

## Historical Maritime Conflict
The vulnerability of merchant shipping is illustrated by historical naval raids. In one instance, Teleutias led a squadron into the harbor of Peiræus, catching the city unprepared. While he ordered his captains to disable triremes, he forbade damaging the beaks of his own ships by attempting to disable merchant ships. Teleutias' seamen boarded larger merchant vessels to seize portable goods and men. Additionally, several smaller vessels and their cargoes, as well as merchantmen carrying corn and other goods, were captured and towed away.
