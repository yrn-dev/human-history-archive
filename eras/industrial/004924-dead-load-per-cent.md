# Dead Load in Per Cent

In structural engineering, specifically regarding bridge design, the dead load refers to the weight of the permanent components of the structure. This includes the main girders, wind-bracing, and flooring. While generally reckoned to be uniformly distributed, the distribution of weight in main girders must be calculated and accounted for in large spans.

## Components and Calculation of Dead Load
The specific weight of the dead load varies based on the type of bridge and its components:
*   **Railway Bridges:** The weight of rails and sleepers is estimated at 0.2 to 0.25 tons per foot run for each line of way. Cross girders and rail girders contribute an additional 0.15 to 0.2 tons.
*   **Footways:** If a footway is added to the structure, an allowance of approximately 0.4 ton per foot run may be applied.
*   **Road Bridges:** Due to the wide variation in flooring characters, no general rule for their weight is provided.

The weight of main girders increases as the span increases. Consequently, every bridge type has a limiting span; beyond this point, the stresses caused by the dead load exceed the assigned limit of working stress.

## Dead Load and Live Load Ratios
Engineers analyze the relationship between dead load (permanent weight) and live load (moving weight) to determine the excess of deflection and straining action. The following ratios illustrate the impact of dead load as a percentage of the total load on the excess of deflection and stress due to a moving load:

| Dead Load (% of total) | Live Load (% of total) | Ratio of Live to Dead Load | Excess of Deflection and Stress (%) |

| 10% | 90% | 9 | 23% |
| 20% | 80% | 4 | 13% |

## Working Stress and Safety Factors
The working stress for combined dead and live loads is calculated using a factor of safety that is twice as great for live loads as it is for dead loads. For iron (where $f_1 = 7\frac{1}{2}$ tons per sq. in.) and mild steel (where $f_1 = 9$ tons per sq. in.), the working stress ($f$) varies based on the ratio of the loads:

*   **All Dead Load:** Iron is 7.5 tons per sq. in.; Mild Steel is 9.0 tons per sq. in.
*   **Live Load = Dead Load:** Iron is 4.9 tons per sq. in.; Mild Steel is 5.9 tons per sq. in.
*   **All Live Load:** Iron is 3.7 tons per sq. in.; Mild Steel is 4.5 tons per sq. in.

Practical experience indicates that while stresses of 5 tons per sq. in. for iron or $6\frac{1}{2}$ tons per sq. in. for steel are safe for long bridges with a large ratio of dead to live load, they are not safe for short bridges where the weight of the bridge is small and stresses are mainly due to live load.

## Impact on Shear and Deflection
A girder typically supports both a dead load and a travelling live load. The total shear is distributed by adding the dead load shear to the maximum travelling load shear of the same sign. 

To ensure a girder becomes straight under its working load (the total dead and live load), it is constructed with a camber, or upward convexity, equal to the calculated deflection. According to the approximate rule provided by W.J.M. Rankine, the working deflection is $\delta = l^2/10,000h$, where $l$ represents the span and $h$ represents the depth of the beam.
