# Birmingham and the Political Transition of the 1860s

## John Bright and the Birmingham Constituency
During the mid-19th century, the city of Birmingham served as a significant political base for John Bright. Bright's relationship with the constituency was marked by periods of absence and return. In 1857, following a "penal dissolution" by Lord Palmerston, Bright had been rejected by Manchester. However, in August of that same year, while he was absent and ill, Birmingham elected him to parliament without a contest. 

Bright returned to parliament in 1858, where he became a prominent voice for reform. He seconded the motion that led to the fall of Lord Palmerston's government in February 1858 and subsequently supported the second administration of Lord Derby. During this time, Bright successfully advocated for the transfer of the government of India from the East India Company to the crown and the admission of Jews to parliament.

## Political Agitation and the 1865 Election
By the 1860s, Bright had established himself as a protagonist of parliamentary reform, a struggle in which he remained central for nine years. He was known for his influential oratory in the House of Commons, utilizing popular phrases such as "a free breakfast table" and the metaphor that "you cannot get twenty wagons at once through Temple Bar" to describe the withdrawal of the Reform Bill in 1860.

In June 1865, parliament was dissolved. Following this dissolution, Bright was returned for Birmingham without opposition. This period of his career was characterized by intense personal grief, as his close friend and colleague, Richard Cobden, had died in March 1865. Bright described their twenty-year relationship as an "intimate and almost brotherly friendship."

## The Death of Palmerston and Shift in Power
The political landscape shifted in the early autumn of 1865 following the death of Lord Palmerston. This event brought Lord John Russell into power. For the first time, Bright provided his support to the government. However, the administration faced immediate challenges; Lord John Russell's fourth Reform Bill was introduced but ultimately defeated by the Adullamites.

The defeat of the Reform Bill led to the installation of the Derby-Disraeli ministry. Bright viewed the accession of Lord Derby as a "declaration of war against the working classes." In response, Bright roused the great towns to demand reform, reaching the culminating point of his political leadership during the winter of 1866-1867.

## Later Career and Ministerial Appointment
Following the eventual passage of the Reform Bill, which included a clause for minority representation, Bright was again returned for Birmingham in the autumn of 1868, alongside two Liberal colleagues. 

When Mr. Gladstone assumed power, he implemented a program of land and church reform in Ireland—measures that Bright had long urged. Consequently, Gladstone appointed Bright as the president of the Board of Trade. This appointment granted Bright the title of Right Honourable and membership in the privy council, cementing his status as a recognized leader of the Liberal party both within parliament and across the country. In this capacity, he spoke strongly in favor of the Irish Church Bill and expressed his views on the House of Lords, warning that they might meet "accidents not pleasant for them to think of" if they threw themselves "athwart" the course of the nation.
