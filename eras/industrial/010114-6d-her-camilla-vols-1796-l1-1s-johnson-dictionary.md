# 18th Century Publishing and Selected Works

## Printing and Book Production
During the 18th century, England and France experienced notable improvements in presswork, type, and paper. By the end of the century, these improvements extended to Italy and Germany. Books of this era became generally elegant and neat. Book illustration saw a revival through English works illustrated by other French artists and Gravelot, as well as the French *livres-a-vignettes*. In the final quarter of the century, woodcut illustrations—which became known as wood engravings due to the use of the graver—underwent a great revival heralded by the work of Bewick.

Regarding bookbinding, the average quality of work improved, although the best binders of the 18th century remained inferior to those of the 17th century until Roger Payne appeared. Trade bindings shifted away from the common use of calf and sheepskin, with books mostly being cased in paper boards.

## Commercial Trends and Pricing
The publishing of poetry by subscription at high prices was a lucrative practice followed by Pope and Prior, though Pope also sold single poems for 1s. or 1s. 6d. Novels were typically published in several volumes. While prices at the start of the century were mostly 1s. 6d. per volume and remained steady for many years, they rose again toward the close of the century.

For example, the works of Miss Burney showed this price progression: *Evelina* (3 vols., 1778) sold for 7s. 5d., *Cecilia* (5 vols., 1782) sold for 12s. 6d., and *Camilla* (5 vols., 1796) sold for L1:1s.

## The Works of Miss Burney
Miss Burney's *Evelina* was a continued success, read by figures such as Edmund Burke and Sir Joshua Reynolds. Burney was introduced to Richard Brinsley Sheridan and the "bluestocking ladies," including Elizabeth Montagu. While she attempted to write a comedy titled *The Witlings* with encouragement from Sheridan and Mrs. Thrale, the piece was suppressed following a "hissing, groaning, catcalling epistle" from her "daddies."

Her subsequent novel, *Cecilia; or, Memoirs of an Heiress* (5 vols., 1782), was published by Messrs Payne & Cadell, who paid the author L250. While some friends felt the writing was too modeled on Johnson's and Horace Walpole believed the characters spoke too uniformly, the work was more carefully constructed than *Evelina* and showcased what Johnson called her gift for "character-mongering."

Burney's later novel, *Camilla: or A Picture of Youth* (5 vols., 1796), was issued by subscription because money had become a serious object for the author. Jane Austen was among the subscribers. Dr. Burney reported to Horace Walpole that his daughter had made L2000 from the book, a sum that was almost certainly increased later. Despite this financial success, Horace Walpole expressed a low opinion of the work in a letter to Miss Hannah More, suggesting the author had "reversed experience" and lacked insight. However, the book was admired by "judicious persons," including Jane Austen in *Northanger Abbey*.

## Samuel Johnson's Dictionary
Dr. Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755), published in two folio volumes, cost L4:4s. in sheets and L4:15s. in boards. The foundation for this great work was the *Dictionarium Britannicum* (dated 1730, though likely published in 1721), compiled by the English philologist and Seventh-day Baptist Nathan (or Nathaniel) Bailey. Bailey's work was considered a great improvement over previous attempts at a universal etymological English dictionary.
