# British India: Geography and Administration

## Territorial Boundaries and Foreign Contact
British India has maintained a geographical position that avoids actual contact with one great European power in Asia to the north and west, while establishing contact with another to the east. In the east, the Mekong river serves as a limit for both British interests in Burma and French interests in Tongking. For approximately 200 miles, the channel of the Mekong defines the limits of occupation between French and British subjects, acting as the boundary between Tongking and the Shan States.

The boundaries of Burma on the Chinese frontier are not yet fully demarcated. At a latitude level with Mogaung, near the northern end of the Burmese railway system, the boundary is defined by the eastern watershed of the Nmaikha (one of the two great northern affluents of the Irrawaddy). The line then moves southwards to a position south-east of Bhamo at latitude 24 degrees, defining the northern edge of the Shan States before striking the Mekong river at approximately latitude 21 degrees 45'. Around the parallel of 20 degrees N. lat., the boundary leaves the Mekong to run westwards toward the Salween; it then follows the eastern watershed of the Salween basin to divide Siam from the Lower Burma provinces.

## Administrative Divisions and Provinces
The British presence in India is characterized as a patchwork of native states held together by provinces under direct British rule. One such province is the Bombay Presidency, which consists of British districts and native states managed by a governor. This territory extends from 13 deg. 53' to 28 deg. 45' N. and from 66 deg. 40' to 76 deg. 30' E. It is bounded by the Arabian Sea to the west, Madras and Mysore to the south, Indore, Hyderabad, and the Central Provinces to the east, and Rajputana, the Punjab, and Baluchistan to the north. 

The Bombay Presidency includes the settlement of Aden and contains the native state of Baroda and Portuguese settlements in Goa, Damaun, and Diu. Excluding Aden but including Sind, the total area is 188,745 sq. m., with 122,984 sq. m. under British rule and 65,761 sq. m. under native rule. As of 1901, the population was 25,468,209. The province is divided into twenty-six districts and four commissionerships: Sind, the southern or Carnatic, the central or Deccan, and the northern or Gujarat. Native states within this presidency total 353 separate units, including groups such as the southern Mahratta states, North and South Gujarat, and the Deccan Satara Jagirs.

Other notable locations include Behar (or Bihar), a town in the Patna district of Bengal situated on the right bank of the river Panchana. While Behar once had silk and muslin manufactures, trade shifted toward Patna and other areas better served by the railway and the river Ganges. Additionally, the indigo industry in Behar was ruined by synthetic products created by German chemists.

## Political Evolution and the Crown
The British ascent to supremacy in India was initially led by the East India Company, which acted as a quasi-independent state, levying revenues, conducting wars, and sending ambassadors to Asiatic powers. The Company's power eventually spread to Oudh, Sind, and Assam. 

This arrangement changed following a serious mutiny of the Bengal Army in 1857. The mutiny, attributed largely to the unimaginative rule of company officials, was not a popular insurrection and was marked by instances of Indian kindness toward British fugitives. British leaders, including Nicholson and Lawrence, fought against overwhelming odds to regain control. By April 1859, the conflict ended, and the empire of the East India Company was annexed to the British Crown. Under the "Act for the Better Government of India," the Governor-General became a Viceroy representing the Sovereign, and a Secretary of State for India, responsible to the British Parliament, replaced the Company. In 1877, Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India.

## Global Context and Empire
India, including its native states, forms a significant part of the British Empire's northern hemisphere holdings. Together with Canada and the United Kingdom, they cover 5,271,375 sq. m. In the eastern hemisphere, India, the United Kingdom, South Africa, and Australasia occupy a combined 6,925,975 sq. m. 

The British Empire occupies nearly one-quarter of the earth's land surface (approximately 12,000,000 sq. m. of the estimated 52,500,000 sq. m.). While much of the empire is in temperate zones suitable for white settlement, the southern half of India and Burma are notable exceptions. The variety of climates and the alternation of seasons across these vast territories allow the summer produce of one region to supply the winter requirements of another.
