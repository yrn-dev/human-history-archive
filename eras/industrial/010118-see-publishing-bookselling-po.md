# Publishing and Bookselling

The book trade is characterized by a gradual distinction between the roles of the publisher and the bookseller. While publishers were originally booksellers themselves, they evolved into a separate class of literary promoters who manage the production of books from an author's manuscripts. In this professional chain, the publisher acts as the intermediary between the author and the bookseller, while the bookseller serves as the intermediary between the publisher and the reading public.

## Professional Roles and Distribution
By the 19th century, the distinction between the publisher (the promoter) and the bookseller (the shopkeeper) became fundamental. Booksellers are categorized into several types of trade: retail, wholesale, the periodical trade, and the trade in old or second-hand books. 

The wholesale bookseller serves as a critical distributing agency between the publisher and the retail bookseller. Because retail shops cannot stock every published title, they rely on wholesalers for miscellaneous supplies. In London, Paternoster Row served as the center of this industry for over a hundred years, where collectors from retail shops obtained books ordered by the public. Leading wholesale establishments may keep over 1,000,000 books in stock. These agencies are also where publishers first "subscribe" or show new books; the number of books subscribed by wholesalers can often determine the ultimate fate of a publication.

Retail booksellers, as the third partner in the publishing process, have sought to protect their interests through organization. In 1890, a London booksellers' society was established to control trade details, arrange prices, and restrict discounts to 25%. This organization later expanded to cover the entire United Kingdom as "The Associated Booksellers of Great Britain and Ireland."

## Specializations in the Book Trade
The trade in second-hand or old books is considered a higher class of business, as it requires bibliographic knowledge and focuses on individual books rather than mass copies. Dealers in this sector sometimes acquire "remainders"—books that have ceased to sell with the publisher—to offer as bargains.

The periodical trade expanded during the 19th century, aided by the introduction of attractive illustrations, the ability to purchase books by instalments, and the abolition of the duty upon paper and the newspaper stamp. Early examples of this growth include *Chambers's Journal* and the *Penny Magazine*.

Recent developments have seen a trend toward amalgamating publishing and bookselling functions. For example, in 1902, *The Times* of London directly sold supplementary volumes to the 9th edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* which it had also published. In 1905, *The Times* Book Club combined the business of bookselling with a subscription library, utilizing the publicity means of a newspaper to drive book sales.

## Regional History and Development
During the 16th and 17th centuries, the Low Countries were the primary center of the bookselling world. Notable names from this era include Plantin, Blauw, and Jansen, with imprints from Antwerp, Leiden, Utrecht, and Amsterdam. The Elzevirs produced pocket classics, while the towns of St Omer and Douai produced English polemical works.

In America, the history of the trade began with Spanish settlements, where presses in cities like Mexico produced linguistic, historical, and primarily religious publications. In the United States, printing was introduced to Boston (1676), Philadelphia (1685), and New York (1693). While Benjamin Franklin helped make the trade illustrious, few people were engaged in it at the start of the 19th century, and scholars primarily imported books from Europe.

Following the second war, printing presses multiplied. American publishers took advantage of the lack of international copyright by reprinting the works of noted British authors—such as Wordsworth, Byron, Moore, Southey, and Scott—without paying the authors or proprietors. Between 1820 and 1852, half of the authors in the "American" catalogue were British. Later, due to the Civil War, restrictive duties to protect native industry, and high labor costs, American publishers began making liberal offers for early sheets of new publications. Boston, Philadelphia, and New York remained the dominant bookselling centers as the American publishing business eventually assimilated to European conditions.

## Notable Figures and Legislation
Several individuals have left a mark on the trade. John Dunton (1659–1733) was an English bookseller and author who published the *Athenian Mercury* and wrote *Life and Errors of John Dunton*. In America, John Bartlett (1820–1905) was a publisher and compiler associated with Little, Brown & Co. in Boston; he is known for his *Familiar Quotations* (1855) and a comprehensive concordance to the dramatic works of Shakespeare (1894). William Thomas Lowndes (1798–1843), the son of a London bookseller, compiled the first systematic work of its kind, *The Bibliographer's Manual of English Literature* (1834).

Legally, the first English Copyright Act of 1709 included provisions for booksellers. It allowed individuals to complain to the archbishop of Canterbury if a book's price was unreasonably high. If the complaint was found valid, the price would be reduced, and booksellers charging more than the fixed price could be fined £5 per copy. However, this specific enactment apparently remained a dead letter.
