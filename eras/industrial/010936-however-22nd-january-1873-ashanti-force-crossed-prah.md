# The Ashanti War of 1873-74

The Ashanti War of 1873-74 was a conflict between the Ashanti Empire and British forces in West Africa. The war was the culmination of escalating tensions regarding territorial sovereignty, the treatment of European captives, and the control of coastal forts.

## Causes of the Conflict
Two primary events served as the immediate causes of the war. The principal cause was the transfer of Elmina fort from the Dutch to the British on April 2, 1872. The Ashanti viewed the Elmina as their subjects and the territory as Ashanti land. Furthermore, the king of Ashanti held the "custom-note," through which he received an annual payment from the Dutch—originally a ground rent for the fort, though the Dutch characterized it as a present for trade purposes. The British occupation of this territory was greatly resented by the Ashanti.

A secondary, minor cause involved the captivity of four Europeans. In June 1869, an Ashanti force invaded Krepi, a territory beyond the Volta, and captured members of the Basel mission: Mr. J. Kuhne, Mr. Fritz A. Ramseyer, and his wife and infant son (the child died shortly thereafter due to privation). Additionally, a French trader named Monsieur M.J. Bonnat was captured elsewhere. These prisoners were taken to Kumasi. Although negotiations for their release had begun, they remained prisoners at the time of the Elmina fort sale.

## Outbreak and Military Campaign
The Ashanti delayed the start of hostilities until their preparations were complete, while officials on the Gold Coast believed the risk of war was remote. However, on January 22, 1873, an Ashanti force crossed the Prah and invaded the British protectorate. The Ashanti forces defeated the Fanti, stirred up disputes at Elmina, and encamped at Mampon near Cape Coast, causing significant alarm among the local inhabitants.

In response, the British Parliament voted £800,000 for an expedition to punish the assailants and defend the territory. Sir Garnet Wolseley was dispatched as the British administrator. Upon landing at Cape Coast on October 2, Wolseley found that the Ashanti had been decimated by fever and smallpox and were preparing to return home. Despite this, Wolseley determined to march to Kumasi. He was supported by Captain John Glover, the administrator of Lagos, who led a force of native levies to cooperate from the east and take the Ashanti in the rear.

## Aftermath and Political Consequences
One result of the war was that several states dependent on the Ashanti declared their independence and sought British protection. The colonial office refused this protection, an inaction that contributed to the eventual reconsolidation of Ashanti power.

Following the war, the Ashanti deposed King Kofi Karikari and placed his brother, Mensa, on the golden stool, which served as the symbol of sovereignty. Mensa's reign was characterized as tyrannous and marked by repeated human sacrifices. He also violated nearly every article of the Fomana treaty; of the 50,000 oz. of gold stipulated as an indemnity, only 4,000 oz. were paid.

## Subsequent Leadership and British Intervention
In 1883, a revolution displaced Mensa, and he was succeeded by Kwaka Dua II, who died in June 1884. The former king, Kofi Karikari, also died in June 1884. After a period of desolating civil war, Prince Prempeh (taking the name Kwaka Dua III) was chosen as king on March 26, 1888. His election occurred with the sanction of an officer of the Gold Coast government.

While peace and prosperity initially returned, fresh trouble arose in 1893 between the Ashanti and tribes of the protectorate, leading Prempeh to close roads to traders. Because the region was included in the British sphere of influence by international agreement, the British government intervened. A mission demanded that Prempeh fulfill the 1874 treaty, accept a British protectorate, and receive a resident at Kumasi. Prempeh declined to treat with the governor of the Gold Coast and sent informal agents to England, but the secretary of state refused to receive them. Prempeh failed to reply to the British demands within the three-week grace period, which expired on October 31, 1895.
