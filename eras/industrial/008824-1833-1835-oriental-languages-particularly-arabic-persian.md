# Oriental Languages and Philology (1833-1835)

During the early to mid-19th century, the study of Oriental languages—specifically Arabic, Persian, and Sanskrit—was a central focus for various European scholars, philologists, and travelers. This period saw significant academic activity in centers such as St Petersburg, Berlin, and Paris, where researchers worked to translate ancient texts, compile dictionaries, and analyze the linguistic relationships between Asiatic languages.

## Academic Study in St Petersburg and Germany
The university of St Petersburg served as a critical hub for the study of Oriental languages. Between 1833 and 1835, Otto von Bohtlingk studied Arabic, Persian, and Sanskrit at this institution. Bohtlingk, a German Sanskrit scholar born in St Petersburg in 1815, later expanded his studies in Berlin and Bonn. His academic career led to his attachment to the Royal Academy of Sciences in St Petersburg in 1842, where he eventually became an ordinary member in 1855.

Bohtlingk's contributions to Indian and comparative philology were extensive. His early work included an edition of Panini's *Acht Bucher grammatischer Regeln* (1839-1840), which served as a criticism of the philological methods used by Franz Bopp. He later republished this work with a full translation in 1887 as *Paninis Grammatik mit Ubersetzung*. His other significant works include *Vopadevas Grammatik* (1847), *Uber die Sprache der Jakuten* (1851), and translations of the *Chhandogya-upanishad* and *Brihadaranyaka-upanishad* (both 1889). His most substantial achievement, the *Sanskrit-Worterbuch*, was a seven-volume dictionary completed over twenty-three years (1853-1875) with the assistance of Albrecht Weber and Rudolf Roth.

## Contributions of European Orientalists
Other scholars of the era focused on the intersection of Arabic and other Oriental languages. Count Carlo Ottavio Castiglione, an Italian philologist from Milan, dedicated his principal work to Arabic and other Oriental languages. His scholarly output included *Monete cufiche del Museo di Milano* (1819) and a 1826 geographical and numismatic memoir regarding the eastern part of Barbarie, referred to by Arabs as Afrikia. Castiglione also worked on Gothic versions of various epistles, publishing versions of the second epistle to the Corinthians in 1829, and others between 1834 and 1839.

In Germany and France, Heinrich Julius Klaproth (1783-1835) was a prominent Orientalist and traveler. Klaproth studied Asiatic languages early in life and published the *Asiatisches Magazin* (1802-1803). His career included an appointment at the academy in St Petersburg, a mission to China in 1805, and an ethnographical and linguistic exploration of the Caucasus from 1807 to 1808. He eventually became a professor of Asiatic languages and literature, residing in Paris until his death on August 28, 1835.

## Linguistic Influence and Translation
The influence of Arabic is noted as being widespread across various Moslem nations, where the Arabic alphabet is commonly used and a large portion of the vocabulary in languages such as Persian, Turkish, Malay, and Hindustani is borrowed from Arabic.

The translation of ancient texts often involved multiple languages. For example, the *Pancha-tantra*, an old Indian collection, was translated into Persian by the physician Barsuyah (under the title *Fables of Bidpai*) and into Arabic by Abdolla Ibn Mokaffa (as *Kalila and Dimnah*). From the Arabic version, these stories passed into European languages. Other examples of cross-linguistic transmission include fables translated from Arabic into Greek by Simeon Seth (A.D. 1100) and from Turkish into French in 1540.

## Philological Observations and Etymology
Philologists have also analyzed "Orientalisms" and the etymology of specific terms. In some historical texts, words such as *bonus* (ebony) and *calamanz* (pencases) are identified as representations of the Persian *abnús* and *ḳalamdàn*. Similarly, the term *Mulhete* is linked to the Arabic *Muláhidah* (meaning "Heretics"), and the word *Safators* is suggested by Sir H. Rawlinson to be a derivation of *dafátir* (meaning "registers or public books").

Etymological debates also surrounded the term "Saracens." While some suggested derivations from the name Sarah or the village of Saraka, others proposed Arabic origins signifying an "Oriental situation" or a "thievish character." Alternative theories suggest the word comes from *Zara* or *Zaara* (the Desert), or *Sarrik* (a robber), or *Scharkioun* (Eastern).
