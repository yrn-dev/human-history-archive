# Bankruptcy Law and Procedure

The law of bankruptcy has developed primarily as a product of commercial development. Its growth has been slow, gradual, and tentative, often oscillating between the conflicting interests of various parties depending on which interest held temporary predominance. Because of this nature, the history of bankruptcy legislation in England is marked by fluctuating tendencies.

## Evolution of the English Court System
The jurisdiction for bankruptcy was originally exercised by commissioners appointed by the lord chancellor. In 1831, a special court of bankruptcy was established, featuring four judges as a court of review, six commissioners, and official assignees tasked with distributing the assets of the bankrupt. During this period, non-traders were excluded from this court; instead, they utilized the "court for relief of insolvent debtors." In that court, non-traders could avoid imprisonment by surrendering their property, though they were not discharged from their debts, meaning any subsequently acquired property remained liable.

Both the bankruptcy court and the court for relief of insolvent debtors were eventually abolished. Non-traders were later permitted to access bankruptcy laws, including the possibility of a discharge. In 1869, the system of official assignees was removed and a new court of bankruptcy was created, led by one of the vice-chancellors as chief judge and supported by subordinate registrars or inferior judges. This court was also eventually abolished. Currently, bankruptcy business is administered by a judge of the high court appointed by the lord chancellor, with high court registrars handling judicial routine in the London district and county-court judges performing similar duties throughout the rest of the country.

## Legislative Reforms and the Act of 1883
The Bankruptcy Act of 1869 is noted for its failure, specifically regarding "liquidation by arrangement" and "composition" clauses, which functioned as private arrangements by resolution. A report from the comptroller in bankruptcy indicated that after ten years of operation, only about 8% of the 13,000 annual failures in England and Wales were subject to the act's important provisions for preventing abuses by professional agents and insolvent debtors. The remaining 92% escaped provisions regarding the conduct of agents and the examination and discharge of bankrupts. While many proceedings were handled by agents of high standing, the lack of effective control and the facilities for collusive and fraudulent arrangements lowered professional morale.

This led to widespread demand for reform from the business community, resulting in thirteen bills being introduced to the House of Commons in the decade following 1869. In 1879, a large group of merchants and bankers in the city of London sent an influential memorial to the prime minister. Following exhaustive inquiries by Mr. Chamberlain, the president of the Board of Trade, the Bankruptcy Act 1883 was enacted.

## Deeds of Arrangement and Private Agreements
The Deeds of Arrangement Act 1887 addresses insolvency matters that do not fall strictly within bankruptcy law. Under the Bankruptcy Acts of 1849 and 1861, non-official arrangements by deed between a debtor and creditors were officially recognized and could be binding on all creditors, including those who did not assent. The 1869 act stopped recognizing such deeds, but the aforementioned "composition" clauses allowed for similar private arrangements. Under the Act of 1883, no arrangements by resolution or deed have force against dissenting creditors unless the bankruptcy courts approve them after a full investigation.

Private arrangements remain binding as voluntary contracts between a debtor and the creditors who assent to them. However, they are treated as assignments of property: they are deemed fraudulent if the benefit is limited to some creditors, or they become acts of bankruptcy if extended to all. Such assignments can be invalidated if made within three months prior to a receiving order petition.

## Irish and Italian Systems
Irish bankruptcy law is regulated by the Irish Debtors Act 1872 and the statutes of 1857 and 1872. While it corresponds in main features to older English enactments and incorporates modifications from the English act of 1869, Ireland never imported the system of liquidation by arrangement and composition without court control. Further legislation includes a 1888 act establishing local bankruptcy courts in certain districts and a 1889 act applying English provisions regarding preferential payments. The Deeds of Arrangement Act 1887 and the Irish Deeds of Arrangement Amendment Act 1890 align the publicity of such deeds in both countries.

In Italy, bankruptcy is governed by the Commercial Code of 1883. Only those defined as merchants—those who engage in commercial business as a habitual profession, including merchant companies—can enter bankruptcy court. Proceedings can be initiated by the debtor, a creditor for a commercial debt, or ordered by the court. Bankruptcy is declared only upon proof of insolvency. The court nominates a trustee to realize property under the supervision of a judge and a commission of creditors; all movable and immovable property is sold by auction. Bankruptcy can also be closed via an arrangement if creditors representing three-fourths of the indebtedness assent. Italian law does not recognize composition before bankruptcy, and bankrupts may face criminal proceedings for offenses such as failing to keep legally prescribed books.

## Administration and Statistics
The English bankruptcy system is self-supporting and does not charge taxpayers. Funding is provided by fees charged to bankrupt estates, interest on balances in bankruptcy estates accounts, and interest on unclaimed funds from former acts. These funds cover salaries for official receivers, remuneration for county court registrars, pensions, prosecution costs, and incidental expenses.

Board of Trade reports indicate that in the ten years ending December 31, 1905, there were 43,141 insolvencies with estimated liabilities of £61,685,678 and assets of £26,001,417. Compared to the ten years ending 1883, average annual liabilities of bankrupt estates decreased from £22,380,000 to £6,168,567. During the latter period, there was also an annual average of 3,426 private arrangements outside the Bankruptcy Acts, involving an estimated annual liability of £4,166,354. These figures suggest a significant diminution in trading insolvency and a decrease in reckless speculation and fraudulent trade.
