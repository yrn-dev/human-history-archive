# Draw and Bascule Bridges

Draw and bascule bridges are a category of movable bridges designed to allow the passage of ships or other obstructions. The original type of this construction is the fortress draw-bridge, which utilizes a single leaf, or bascule, that rotates around a horizontal hinge located at one abutment. In this configuration, the bridge is supported by abutments at both ends when closed and is raised using counterweights and chains.

## Bascule Bridge Design and Mechanics
A more common variation of the bascule bridge consists of two leaves, with one hinged at each abutment. When these bridges are closed, the two bascules are locked at the center. To achieve balance at the hinge, each bascule is prolonged backwards beyond the hinge point; this prolongation sinks into the piers as the bridge opens.

Modern bascule bridges often employ hydraulic machinery for operation. For example, some designs utilize four main girders per bascule. Over the river, these are lattice girders with transverse girders spaced 12 feet apart. The floor is divided into rectangles of 3 feet by 3½ feet by longitudinal and subsidiary transverse girders, covered with buckled plates and a roadway of dowelled pine blocks.

The mechanical specifications for such bridges can be substantial. In certain instances, the bascules rotate through an angle of 82° on steel shafts 48 feet long and 21 inches in diameter. These shafts may feature eight bearings consisting of rings of live rollers 22 inches long and 4-7/16 inches in diameter. The weight of each leaf, including ballast, can reach 1070 tons, with 365 tons of that weight consisting of counterweight located in the bascule chambers of the piers. Such bridges can typically be lifted or lowered in one to one and a half minutes.

## Notable Examples

### Tower Bridge, London
The Tower Bridge in London is a suspension bridge that incorporates a secondary bascule bridge in its center span to permit ship passage. The structure features two main towers in the river and two on the shore abutments to support suspension chains. The opening section consists of two bascules pivoted near the pier faces and rotating in a vertical plane. 

When the bascules are raised, a 200-foot width between the main river piers remains unobstructed up to the high-level foot-bridge, which sits 141 feet above Trinity H.W. The total length of the bridge is 940 feet, with approaches measuring 1260 feet to the north and 780 feet to the south. The width between parapets is generally 60 feet, narrowing to 49 feet across the center span. 

The bascules of Tower Bridge each contain four main girders projecting 100 feet beyond and 62 feet 6 inches within the face of the piers. The axis of rotation is located 5 feet 7 inches below the roadway and 13 feet 3 inches inside the face of the piers. These bascules are rotated by pinions driven by hydraulic engines working in steel sectors with a 42-foot radius.

### Konigsberg Road Bridge
At Konigsberg, a road bridge features two fixed spans of 39 feet and a central span of 60 feet between bearings (41 feet clear), utilizing balanced bascules over the center. Each bascule is composed of two main girders with stringers and cross girders. These main girders are 6 feet deep at the hinge and diminish to 1 foot 7 inches at the center of the span, hung on horizontal shafts 8-5/8 inches in diameter. 

The counterweight system consists of a 12-foot long depressed cantilever arm, which is overlapped by a fixed platform that sinks into a masonry recess upon opening. This bridge is operated by hydraulic power via an accumulator with a 34-ton load, supplying pressure water at 630 lb per sq. in. This system allows the bridge to open in 15 seconds and close in 25 seconds.

## Historical References
Historical records indicate the use of draw-bridges in various contexts. In the accounts of Marco Polo, a wooden draw-bridge is mentioned as stretching 470 *ch’i* long to the west over a lake toward the palace *Hing-sheng kung*.
