# The Keyed Bugle and Related Instruments

The keyed bugle is a wind instrument that evolved from the natural bugle, serving as a nucleus for the development of various brass band instruments during much of the 19th century. It is characterized by its conical bore and a system of keys that allow for a wider range of notes than the natural bugle.

## Structural Characteristics and Acoustics
The bugle is distinguished from the trumpet primarily by the shape of its tube; while the trumpet is cylindrical, widening only at the bell, the bugle is conical. Both instruments utilize cup-shaped mouthpieces, but they differ in the shape of the basins where they open into the tube. The trumpet's basin is angular, producing a brilliant blaring tone, whereas the bugle's basin is bevelled, resulting in a more veiled but penetrating quality.

The tonality of the keyed bugle is determined by the second harmonic produced by the open tube when the first key remains open. In the original patented instrument, Halliday added a sixth key that functioned as the first and remained open in the normal position. Closing this key produces B flat, maintaining the same series of harmonics as the open tube. As successive keys are opened, the harmonic series becomes shorter.

The instrument's bore is proportioned to its length to allow for the playing of fundamental tones in the first two series, though these notes are never used. Similarly, harmonics above the sixth are avoided due to doubtful intonation. In contrast, the ophicleide—which serves as the bass of the keyed bugle—possesses a bore wide enough to produce fundamentals of satisfactory quality.

## Usage and Tonality
The keyed bugle was most frequently used in B flat. To achieve this, a crook for B flat was often added to a bugle in C. Additionally, military bands frequently employed the soprano bugle in E flat. In standard notation, the key of C is used.

The natural bugle's actual compass consists of eight notes, though the first or fundamental note is never used due to poor quality; consequently, only five notes are required for various bugle-calls.

## Military History and Evolution
In England, the bugle's history as a military instrument is closely linked to the creation of the light infantry. During the 17th century, the bugle gradually superseded the drum as the instrument for signals and duty, a transition facilitated by improvements in firearms and the abandonment of infantry armor. By the end of the 18th century, the bugle was the recognized signal instrument for the light infantry, while the trumpet remained the instrument of the cavalry. A general order officially introduced the bugle as a minor badge for the light infantry on December 28, 1814.

The popularity of the Royal Kent bugle (or keyed bugle) peaked in the army around 1856. In the Royal Artillery, a bugle-band was established to replace the drum and fife band. Under the leadership of Trumpet-major James Lawson, the band's capabilities were expanded. Finding the service bugle's scale too restricted, Lawson added a valve attachment, transforming the bugle into a chromatic instrument similar to a saxhorn. This led to the addition of other copper instruments, including tenor horns in B flat, euphoniums, horns in E flat, and bass tubas. In 1869, this ensemble was renamed the R.A. Brass Band, and it eventually merged into the Mounted Band in 1877.

## The Principle of Crooks and Valves
Crooks are interchangeable spiral tubes that vary in length—ranging from 16 inches for the B flat alto to 125 inches for the B flat basso—and taper to a quarter-inch diameter at the mouthpiece. A crook is named after the fundamental tone it produces when added to the instrument's body. By lengthening the tube, the crook lowers the pitch and changes the key. While crooks allow a performer to play in multiple keys on one instrument, they are merely transposing agents and cannot make an instrument chromatic because they require time to adjust.

This principle of adding tubing to deepen pitch is shared by valves and slides. Valves act as an instantaneous application of the crook principle, allowing the performer to lower individual notes of the harmonic series by a semitone, a tone, or a tone and a half. This provides a permanent chromatic compass without the need for manual adjustment.
