# John Bright

John Bright (1811–1889) was a prominent British statesman and a recognized leader of the Liberal party in both parliament and the country. Born on November 16, 1811, in Rochdale, he was the eldest son of Jacob Bright, a respected Quaker and cotton mill owner, and Martha Wood, a woman of refined taste and strong character.

## Early Life and Education
Bright's ancestry included John Gratton, a friend of George Fox and a persecuted preacher of the Society of Friends. As a child, Bright was delicate and attended several schools, including a boarding school kept by Mr. William Littlewood, the Ackworth school, a school at York, and a school at Newton near Clitheroe. While he stated he learned little Greek and Latin, he developed a deep love for outdoor pursuits and English literature, the latter fostered by his mother. At age sixteen, he entered his father's mill and eventually became a partner in the business.

## Early Political Activity and the Anti-Corn Law League
Bright's political interests were likely first sparked by the Preston election. In Rochdale, he participated in two major agitations: one opposing a local church-rate and another for parliamentary reform, which successfully secured a member for Rochdale under the Reform Bill.

Bright became a leader of the movement against the Corn Laws alongside Richard Cobden. While Cobden's argumentative style was initially better received in the House of Commons, Bright became the "undisputed master of the feelings of his audiences" through his "more stately genius" and rhetorical appeals. Despite an early instance where he was heard with such impatience in the House that he was forced to sit down, he established his position in the House of Commons during the 1845 session through a successful inquiry into the Game Laws.

## Parliamentary Career and Policy Positions
Bright was elected for Manchester in July 1847, without a contest. Throughout his parliamentary life, he held several consistent positions:
* **Religious and Social Issues:** As a Nonconformist, he opposed clerical control of national education and spoke against church-rates and the Irish Established Church. He also opposed capital punishment and flogging in the army.
* **Legislation:** He opposed legislation that restricted hours of labour and introduced a bill to repeal the Game Laws. He described Lord John Russell's Ecclesiastical Titles Bill as a "little, paltry, miserable measure."
* **International Affairs:** He pleaded for peace and delivered impassioned, though fruitless, opposition to the Crimean War. His most memorable speech occurred on February 23, 1855, featuring a moving appeal to the prime minister. He also supported Cobden in negotiations for a commerce treaty with France and took the side of the North during the American Civil War.

## Reform Agitations and Later Years
Bright was a protagonist of parliamentary reform for nine years. He spoke at massive gatherings in Manchester, Bradford, Glasgow, and Edinburgh. In the House of Commons, he was the most influential voice in debates on Reform Bills from 1859 to 1867. He was known for producing popular cries, such as "a free breakfast table" and the observation that "force is not a remedy" regarding Irish discontent.

After being rejected by Manchester in 1857, he was elected for Birmingham in August of that year. In 1858, he helped pass measures for the transfer of the government of India to the crown and the admission of Israelites (Jews) to parliament. 

In 1868, Bright was returned for Birmingham again and accepted the post of president of the Board of Trade under Mr. Gladstone, which made him a member of the privy council. He continued to urge Irish reform in land and church.

## Recognition and Legacy
In his later years, Bright received significant social and literary recognition. He was elected lord rector of the university of Glasgow in 1882, though he admitted to Dr. Dale that he was weary of public speaking and that his mind was "almost a blank." He received an honorary degree from the university of Oxford in 1886, and a statue was erected in his honor at Birmingham in 1888.

The 3rd marquess of Salisbury characterized Bright as the greatest master of English oratory of several generations, noting his ability to maintain a "robust, powerful and vigorous style" to express noble thoughts at a time when eloquence had been depressed.
