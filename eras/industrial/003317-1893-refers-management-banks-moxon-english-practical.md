# Management of Banks and Banking Literature

The study and management of banking are documented through a variety of historical treatises, legal frameworks, and professional handbooks. These sources detail the evolution of banking practices, the management of financial institutions, and the impact of economic panics on the banking system.

## Banking Literature and Practical Management
A significant body of literature exists regarding the principles and practice of banking. J. B. Attfield's 1893 work, *English and Foreign Banks*, specifically refers to the management of banks. Other key texts on the practical application of banking include T. B. Moxon's *English Practical Banking* (10th edition, 1899) and J. George Kiddy's *The Country Banker's Handbook* (4th edition, 1903). George Rae also provided practical hints on the subject in *The Country Banker* (1886).

Theoretical and historical perspectives are provided by authors such as J. W. Gilbart in *The History, Principles and Practice of Banking* (revised by A. S. Michie, 1882) and Thomson Hankey in *Principles of Banking* (1867). Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873) is noted as a brilliant depiction of the city at that time, which later prompted a reply from A. S. Cobb in *Threadneedle Street* (1891).

## The Bank of England and London Markets
The Bank of England has maintained a practical, though perhaps not entirely legal, monopoly on the issuance of notes in London since its establishment. It was gradually surrounded by various private banks, some of which held considerable power. 

The London money market and the Bank of England's operations have been the subject of extensive analysis. These include A. Crump's *The Key to the London Money Market* (1872) and W. Y. Duncan's three-volume *Notes on the Rate of Discount in London* (published between 1822 and 1873). R. H. Inglis Palgrave also examined the money markets of England, France, Germany, Belgium, and Holland in his work *Bank Rate and the Money Market* (1903).

## Banking Law and Stock Brokerage
Banking practice is governed by specific legal statutes and customs. For example, Leeman's Act (1867) mandates that contracts for the sale of joint-stock bank shares are void unless the registered numbers of the shares are stated. While the Stock Exchange may practice disregarding this act, such a custom is not binding on a client to make a contract of sale valid. However, if a client instructs a broker to follow this practice, the broker is entitled to be indemnified for money paid on the client's behalf.

Stockbrokers, who contract for the sale of shares and stocks, differ from brokers proper because stocks and shares are not classified as "goods." Consequently, the requirement for a written memorandum enacted by the Sale of Goods Act 1893 does not apply to them. This allows principals to bring actions for the sale of stocks and shares even in the absence of a written memorandum.

## Economic Instability and the Panic of 1893
Banking systems are susceptible to volatility caused by legislation and economic depressions. In the United States, silver legislation passed by Congress in 1878 and 1890 created unease, leading banks to discriminate against silver certificates and silver dollars in their cash holdings.

In 1893, the treasury began to lose gold heavily. In response, leading bankers from Chicago, Baltimore, Philadelphia, Boston, and New York formed a combination to replenish government reserves by turning over a large portion of their holdings. Despite this, the panic of 1893 led to the suspension of approximately 150 national banks, although 84 of these eventually resumed business. The subsequent period of liquidation saw a decline in the number of banks from 3,856 on June 1, 1893, to 3,585 by June 1, 1899. During this same window, aggregate capital fell from $698,454,665 to $610,028,895.

## Professional Profiles in Banking
The intersection of banking and public life is exemplified by Walter Bagehot (1826-1877). The son of a banker in Langport, Somerset, Bagehot joined his father's business, Stuckey & Co., in the west of England. While serving as the editor of the *Economist* newspaper from 1860 until his death, Bagehot managed the bank's London agency, where he attended to London affairs and lent surplus money in "Lombard Street."
