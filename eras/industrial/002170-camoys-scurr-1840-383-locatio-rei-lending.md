# Camoys v. Scurr (1840) and Locatio Rei

The legal case of *Camoys v. Scurr* (1840, 9 C. & P. 383) serves as a precedent within the broader legal framework of bailment, specifically concerning the responsibilities of a bailee when goods are provided for a specific purpose.

## Understanding Bailment
Bailment is defined in law as the delivery of goods from one individual, known as the bailor, to another, known as the bailee. This delivery occurs under a contract—which may be express or implied—for a specific purpose. The terms of the bailment dictate that once the purpose is fulfilled, the goods must be redelivered to the bailor, dealt with according to the bailor's directions, or kept until the bailor reclaims them.

The classification of bailments generally follows the system established by Chief Justice Holt in the 1704 case of *Coggs v. Bernard*. This system divides bailments into distinct categories based on the nature of the agreement and the reward involved.

## Categories of Bailment
The archive identifies three primary types of bailment:

**Depositum**
This is a bailment without reward where the bailee keeps goods for the bailor. In this arrangement, the bailee is not permitted to use the entrusted items. The bailee is held liable for gross negligence, though they are not liable for ordinary negligence. An example of this occurred in *Giblin v. McMullen* (1868), where a banker who received no payment for holding a customer's securities was not held liable for their theft by a cashier because gross negligence was not proven.

**Commodatum**
Also known as a loan, this occurs when useful chattels or goods are lent to a bailee *gratis* (free of charge) for the bailee's use. Because the loan is gratuitous and provides a sole advantage to the bailee, the bailee is expected to be a person of competent skill capable of caring for the item. Consequently, the bailee is required to exercise great diligence in protecting the goods and can be held responsible for even slight negligence. For instance, in *Bringloe v. Morrice* (1676), it was determined that a person lent a horse to ride was not justified in allowing their servant to ride it.

**Locatio Rei**
This category refers to lending for hire. When goods are hired, the bailee is obligated to exercise the level of diligence that a prudent man would apply toward his own property.

## The Precedent of Camoys v. Scurr
The case of *Camoys v. Scurr* (1840) provides a specific nuance to the rules of bailment regarding the purpose of the delivery. In this instance, a vendor had a horse for sale and allowed the defendant to have the horse specifically for the purpose of trying it. The court held that, under these circumstances, the defendant had the right to allow a competent person to try the horse. This differs from the stricter requirements of *Commodatum* (as seen in *Bringloe v. Morrice*), as the specific purpose of the bailment—testing a horse for purchase—informed the scope of the bailee's permissible actions.
