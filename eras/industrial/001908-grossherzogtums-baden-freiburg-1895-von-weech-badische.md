# Grossherzogtum Baden

The Grossherzogtum Baden (Grand Duchy of Baden) is a German state with its capital at Karlsruhe. Its history is characterized by territorial expansion, constitutional struggles between liberal and clerical forces, and a significant role in the broader political movements of Germany.

## Geography and Population
At the start of the 19th century, Baden was a margraviate with a population of 210,000 and an area slightly exceeding 1,300 square miles. Through subsequent acquisitions of territory, its area increased to 5,823 square miles. As of 1905, the population reached 2,009,320, with a density of approximately 330 people per square mile. About half of the population is classified as rural, living in communities of fewer than 2,000 inhabitants.

The population is of diverse origins: those south of the Murg are descended from the Franks, those to the north from the Alemanni, and the Swabian plateau is populated by another race. The population is most densely concentrated in the north and near the Swiss town of Basel. In 1905, the religious composition was approximately 60% Roman Catholic, 37% Protestant, 1.5% Jews, and others of various confessions.

## Religion and Education
Internal politics in Baden have centered largely on the conflict between Liberalism and Clericalism. On June 28, 1859, a concordat was signed with the Holy See, which facilitated the establishment of religious orders and placed education under clerical oversight. This sparked a constitutional struggle that concluded in 1863 with a victory for Liberal principles; while priests retained a share in management, the communes became responsible for education.

Further tensions arose under the premiership of Julius von Jolly (1823–1891), who introduced Liberal constitutional changes in 1867, including freedom of the press, compulsory education, and the responsibility of ministers. That same year, a law required candidates for the priesthood to pass government examinations. This was resisted by the archbishop of Freiburg; following the archbishop's death in April 1868, the see remained vacant. The introduction of civil marriage in 1869 and the 1870 proclamation of papal infallibility further escalated the strife.

This period, known as the "Kulturkampf," saw the government encourage the formation of Old Catholic communities. Reconciliation with Rome was not achieved until 1880, after the fall of Jolly's ministry, and the archbishopric of Freiburg was refilled in 1882.

## Government and Society
The grand-duke is a Protestant. The Evangelical Church is governed by a synod—consisting of a "prelate," seven nominated, and 48 elected lay and clerical members—and a nominated council. The Roman Catholic archbishop of Freiburg serves as the metropolitan of the Upper Rhine.

Public education is entirely managed by the government. The state hosts a celebrated technical college at Karlsruhe and two universities: a Protestant university at Heidelberg and a Roman Catholic university at Freiburg-im-Breisgau.

The political landscape of Baden mirrored general German trends. In 1891, the National Liberals held a majority of only one in the diet. By 1893, they required Conservative support to maintain their position. In 1897, the opposition won a majority through a coalition of Radicals (*Freisinnige*), Social-democrats, Socialists, and Ultramontanes.
