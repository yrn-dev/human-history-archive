# Imperial Federation and Sir John Colomb

## Concepts of Imperial Federation
Imperial federation emerged as a subject of discussion and literature within the British Empire as a response to the increasing pressure of foreign enterprise. The concept sought to establish a federation of the empire to serve common interests. Early instances of this discourse appeared in New Zealand as early as 1852, and the general association of the Australian colonies officially put forward a similar suggestion in London in 1857.

The movement toward imperial consolidation grew as a reaction against the "Manchester school" period (approximately 1845 to 1875), during which colonial self-government was often viewed as a preliminary step toward inevitable separation. By 1872, Lord Beaconsfield repudiated this view, describing the constitutions of the colonies as part of a "great policy of imperial consolidation." In 1875, W. E. Forster advocated for imperial federation as a mechanism to replace dependence with association.

## Organizational Efforts and Literature
The establishment of the Imperial Federation League in 1884, with W. E. Forster as its first president (followed by Lord Rosebery), represented a significant step in the movement. Further efforts to seek and accept colonial opinion on imperial organization and defence were conducted through the Colonial Conferences starting in 1887, which were renamed the Imperial Conference in 1907.

The subject inspired a growing body of literature. Key works on the topic include G.R. Parkin's *Imperial Federation* (1892) and Sir John Colomb's *Imperial Federation, Naval and Military* (1886). Other related works on British imperialism and policy include those by Sir C.P. Lucas, H.E. Egerton, H.J. Mackinder, Sir J.R. Seeley, Sir Charles Dilke, and Sir G.S. Clarke.

## Sir John Colomb
Sir John Charles Ready Colomb, K.C.M.G., contributed to the discourse on imperial organization through his 1886 work, *Imperial Federation, Naval and Military*. ## Related Imperial and Naval Contexts
The broader context of imperial defence and naval organization involved various high-level committees and figures. Winston Spencer Churchill, who served as Under Secretary of State for the Colonies and President of the Board of Trade, attended the Committee of Imperial Defence and proposed a Naval War Staff. 

The British Empire also recognized distinction in its naval and military services through the Order of Merit, founded by King Edward VII.
