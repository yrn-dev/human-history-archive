# The Bassoon

The bassoon (known in French as *basson*, in German as *Fagott*, and in Italian as *fagotto*) is a woodwind instrument featuring a double reed mouthpiece. It serves as the bass member of the oboe family. The German and Italian names derive from a perceived resemblance to a bundle of sticks, a characteristic resulting from the bassoon being the first instrument of its type to be doubled back upon itself. Its direct ancestor was the bass pommer, which measured 6 feet in length and was straight. J. J. Quantz credits France with the transformation of the bombard (pommer) into the bassoon.

## History and Development
During the 15th and 16th centuries, the unwieldy nature of bass pommers led to efforts to create a more practical orchestral bass by doubling the long tube, thus transforming the pommer into a fagotto. While some sources, such as the work of Theseus Ambrosius Albonesius (1539), ascribe the invention of the bassoon to Afranio, a canon of Ferrara, evidence suggests that Afranio's "phagotus" was a different instrument. Afranio's design, executed by Ravilius of Ferrara, consisted of twin columns played by bellows, which Mersenne categorized with the Neapolitan *sourdeline* (a complex musette) rather than the true bassoon.

Technical improvements to the instrument were later pursued by makers such as Sax in Belgium, Triebert and Jancourt in France, and Cornelius Ward and Morton in England. In Germany, Carl Almenraeder introduced improvements based on acoustic principles. One such correction addressed the A hole, which was previously too small and positioned too high, causing a damped tone and lowered pitch. Almenraeder replaced the single hole with two medium-diameter holes covered by an open key, resulting in a full and accurate tone. His 16-key model was widely adopted in Germany and followed by makers such as Heckel of Biebrich. Additionally, at the instigation of Wagner, W. Heckel produced bassoons with an extra key that extended the compass downwards to A.

## Technical Specifications and Acoustics
The bassoon possesses an eight-foot tone. Its normal compass extends from Bb bass to Ab treble. Modern instruments may use additional mechanisms to extend this range to C or F; these extremely high notes are referred to as *vox humana* due to their sweetness. 

The pitch of the bassoon is a twelfth below that of the oboe. While both instruments have a lowest note of B, the bassoon's fundamental scale is F major. Notes from F down to Bb are extra notes produced by interlocking keys on the long joint, operated by the left thumb. The fingering system is complex:
* **First Register:** Seven semitones (Bb to E) are produced via keys in the bell and long joint.
* **Middle Register:** The next eight notes produce both a fundamental tone and a harmonic octave via increased breath pressure.
* **Upper Register:** Remaining notes are achieved through overblowing the fundamental scale a twelfth (up to Ab) and using cross fingering.
* **Vox Humana:** Notes from A to Eb are produced using small harmonic holes opened by keys at the top of the wind joint.

## Use by Composers
The bassoon has been a favorite of most great masters, with the exception of Handel. Notable uses of the instrument include:
* **Beethoven:** Utilized the bassoon extensively in his symphonies, writing original and beautiful independent parts.
* **Bach:** Included parts for two bassoons in his mass in B min.
* **Mozart:** Composed a concerto in Bb for bassoon with orchestra (Kochel, No. 191). He also utilized two bassoons in his *Requiem*.
* **Weber:** Wrote a concerto for bassoon in F (op. 75) scored for full orchestra.

The bassoon also appears in chamber music alongside other instruments. For example, Mozart wrote a short adagio for bassoon and two basset horns.
