# The Smyth Casing-in Machine

The Smyth casing-in machine is a specialized piece of equipment used in commercial bookbinding to paste the sides of a book and attach the cover. This process is part of a broader system of machine-binding, which is largely the result of German or American ingenuity.

## Technical Specifications and Capacity
The Smyth casing-in machine is designed to handle books of various dimensions. It can process books ranging from 1/4 to 3 inches in thickness and from 4 to 22 inches in length. In terms of production speed, the machine is capable of covering between 10 and 15 books per minute.

## Context within Machine-Binding
The Smyth machine is part of a mechanized binding process that differs from hand-binding. In hand-binding, books are sewn on bands with loose ends drawn through the boards and strongly fastened. In contrast, machine-covering for sewn books generally only pastes the loose ends of the sewing tapes to the inner surfaces of the boards. Consequently, a "cased" book is more likely to slip out of its cover once the paste fixing it perishes, although it may last a long time if it is not used frequently.

Most modern bookbinding machines are driven by power. Because they must be accurately set to a specific book size, they are not suitable for binding books of different sizes; their full advantage is realized only when producing a large edition of a single book.

## Related Binding Processes
The casing-in process is preceded by other mechanized steps:
*   **Case-making:** A large, complicated machine uses over fifty springs to create covers. It feeds cardboard for the boards and back, along with a roll of cloth. The cloth is glued, the boards and back are placed, and the corners are cut by rectangular gouges. Hollow raisers and rollers then turn up and press the cloth. This machine can produce 1,200 cases per hour.
*   **Rounding and Backing:** Sewn books with glued backs are forced between tight rollers to create a rounded shape. They are then pressed against a steel rounding plate with a polished groove. This process creates a ledge along the outer edge of the back, allowing boards to hinge on a line outside the natural boundary of the book, preventing the destructive tendency of the book to pull away the vellum or paper when opened. These machines can process approximately 750 volumes of the same size per hour.
*   **Sewing:** Books are sewn using machines that either utilize bands (flat or round) or a chain stitch. Band-working machines pull the return thread through the upper and lower edges of the back, while chain stitch machines bring the thread back inside each section, which can result in a flattened back that complicates the covering process.
