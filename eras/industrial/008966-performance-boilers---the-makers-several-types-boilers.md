# Performance and Classification of Boilers

## General Classification
Boilers are generally divided into two distinct classes: "tank" boilers and "water-tube" boilers, though some types combine properties of both. Tank boilers feature an outer shell that is partially or wholly cylindrical, a design that provides necessary strength without requiring a large number of stays. These are typically internally fired, with furnace plates surrounded by water to create efficient heating surfaces. Combustion products move from the furnace into a chamber and then through flues or small tubes to transfer heat to the water.

In contrast, water-tube boilers contain water primarily within numerous small tubes, which form the majority of the heating surface. The fire is usually positioned beneath these tubes, and both are enclosed in a fire-resisting casing of brickwork or other materials. In some designs, water-tubes entirely surround the fire, ensuring the casing is not exposed to direct fire. While tank boilers easily maintain heating surfaces in contact with water, water-tube boilers require specific design provisions to maintain water circulation.

## Tank Boiler Varieties
Cylindrical boilers vary based on installation needs and space. Where floor space is limited or installations are small, vertical boilers are used. The simple vertical boiler consists of a plain vertical fire-box and an internal smoke stack. In larger versions, cross tubes (approximately 9 inches in diameter and made of 3/8-inch plate) are fitted with a slight inclination to aid water circulation. Other vertical types include the "Tyne" boiler, which uses a hemispherical furnace and an upper combustion chamber with inclined and vertical water-tubes, and the "Victoria" boiler, which utilizes a hemispherical furnace and horizontal smoke-tubes leading to a side smoke-box. The "Cochran" boiler is similar but features a "dry" back combustion chamber with firebrick-lined doors for easier access to tube ends.

Other cylindrical forms include the "Cornish" boiler, which contains one furnace and is used when diameters are less than about 6-1/2 feet. The "Galloway" boiler features two furnaces extending 9 or 10 feet into the boiler, leading into a large flue containing "Galloway" tubes. 

The cylindrical multitubular return tube boiler is used almost universally in merchant steamers. These can reach 17 feet in diameter with working pressures typically between 160 and 200 lb per sq. in., though some reach 265 lb per sq. in. They are categorized as single-ended (furnaces at one end) or double-ended (furnaces at both ends). Single-ended boilers usually have three furnaces, though they can range from one to four.

## Water-Tube and Naval Boilers
The Thornycroft-Schulz boiler is a type used extensively in the German and British navies. It consists of four chambers and a fire-grate arranged in two separate portions. Its outermost rows of tubes form water-walls at the bottom and allow gases to pass between them at the top. Circulation is managed via large-diameter outer circulating pipes connecting the water- and steam-chambers, while several nearly vertical down-comers are provided in the center for the middle water-chamber.

## Materials and Construction
Mild steel is almost invariably used for plates and stays in marine and ordinary land boilers. Shell plates and long stays typically have a tensile strength of 28 to 32 tons per sq. in., while furnaces, flues, and short-screwed stays use a softer steel (26 to 30 tons per sq. in.). Tubes in these boilers are usually lap-welded wrought iron, though naval water-tube boilers invariably use seamless steel tubes.

Locomotive boilers utilize mild steel shells, but the fire-box plates are made of copper (though steel is used in America). Fire-box side stays are copper or special bronze, and other stays are steel. Tubes in locomotives are typically brass (either 70% copper and 30% zinc, or two parts copper to one part zinc), though steel or copper are occasionally used.

## Performance, Maintenance, and Accessories
Boiler efficiency results published by makers are often obtained under the most favorable circumstances and may not reflect ordinary working conditions. More accurate data comes from British admiralty marine boiler trials, which used skilled observers to weigh coal and measure feed-water while using high-quality South Welsh coal.

To maintain safety and efficiency, boilers require specific accessories. Water levels must remain above the highest heating surfaces, monitored via a glass water-gauge and three try-cocks. Feeding is managed by hand or automatic float mechanisms, utilizing pumps or injectors and a "check-valve" to regulate admission.

Feed-water impurities, such as lime or magnesia salts, can precipitate as mud or scale. Scale obstructs heat transmission and can cause plates to overheat and weaken. While mud is removed via blow-off cocks, scale requires periodical cleaning. Consequently, when feed-water is very impure, easy-to-clean boilers like the Cornish, Galloway, and Lancashire types are preferred over water-tube or multitubular boilers. Alternatively, "softeners" are used to precipitate impurities through chemical reagents and heat before the water is filtered and pumped into the boiler.
