# London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon and Sec. 17 of the Revenue Act

The legal case of *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* (1903), A.C. 240, provides critical interpretations regarding the liabilities and protections afforded to bankers under English law, specifically concerning the collection of crossed cheques and the nature of bank drafts.

## The Role of the Collecting Banker
In *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon*, the House of Lords provided a final exposition on section 82 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, which grants qualified immunity to bankers who collect crossed cheques for their customers. To qualify for this protection, a banker must satisfy several specific conditions:

*   **Customer Relationship:** The banker must receive the cheque and the resulting money from a customer, defined as a person with whom the bank has existing and definite business relations.
*   **Crossing Requirements:** The cheque must already be crossed, either specially or generally, to the banker. Any crossing the banker performs themselves under section 77 is considered absolutely inefficacious for this purpose.
*   **Good Faith and Due Care:** The banker must act in good faith and without negligence. Negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care in the interest of the true owner of the cheque, rather than necessarily the customer.

To avoid a finding of negligence, the banker is required to ensure that endorsements are ostensibly correct and to verify authority in cases of endorsement per procuration. Furthermore, the banker must not accept a cheque for a private account if the face of the document indicates the holder is an agent, an official, or holding it for partnership purposes. Cheques marked "account payee" must not be taken for any account other than the one indicated.

## The "Conduit Pipe" Principle
The *Gordon* case further established that a banker only maintains protection under section 82 as long as they act strictly as an agent for the customer or as a "conduit pipe." If a banker assumes the position of the owner or transferee of the cheque, they no longer fulfill the condition of receiving money solely for the customer.

In the specific instance of *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon*, the bank followed a practice of crediting cheques as cash in its books before the money had actually been received. The court held that this practice was equivalent to taking the cheques as an owner or transferee, thereby barring the bank from the protections offered by section 82.

## Section 17 of the Revenue Act 1883
Section 17 of the Revenue Act 1883 addresses specific documents that are not classified as negotiable instruments, such as those issued by corporations that direct bankers to make payments upon the presentation of a signed, specific attached receipt. 

While these documents are denied the status of negotiable instruments, section 17 applies the "crossed cheques" sections of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 to them. Consequently, a banker who pays one of these documents while it is crossed—provided they do so in accordance with the crossing and without any indication that the document has been transferred—could likely claim immunity under section 80. However, because section 17 expressly provides that these orders for payment are not negotiable, it is probable that a banker would only be protected if they took the document from the specified payee.

## Related Banking Protections and Liabilities
The legal framework surrounding these instruments includes other statutory protections:
*   **Stamp Act 1853:** Under section 19, a banker paying a draft or order payable to order on demand is relieved of liability if the endorsement was unauthorized or forged. *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* confirmed that this protection extends to drafts drawn by a bank branch on its own head office.
*   **Bills of Exchange Act 1882:** Section 60 extends similar protection to cheques. However, the definition of cheques as "bills drawn on a banker payable on demand" excludes the types of corporate documents governed by section 17 of the Revenue Act. 
*   **Payment Liabilities:** While the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 does not directly prohibit a banker from paying a crossed cheque contrary to its crossing, section 79 stipulates that the banker is liable to the true owner for any resulting loss and may be unable to charge the customer for that amount. Protection is instead granted under section 80 and the proviso to section 79 when a banker pays according to the instrument's ostensible tenor.
