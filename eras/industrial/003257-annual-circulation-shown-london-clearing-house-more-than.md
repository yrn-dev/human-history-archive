# The London Clearing House

The London Clearing House is a central mechanism for the settlement of large transactions within the United Kingdom. In the modern era, nearly every large transaction in the UK is settled via cheque through a series of ledger transfers, with specie and notes serving only as "small change" for fractional payments. This system is facilitated by the fact that every bank in the United Kingdom maintains an agent in London.

## Circulation and Turnover
The annual circulation registered by the London Clearing House exceeds £12,000,000,000. This paper circulation is described as the most easily worked circulating medium in existence, possessing a capacity to expand to meet demand and contract once strain has passed.

When considering the total yearly turnover of cheques, the figures from the London Clearing House represent only a portion of the activity. The total turnover is estimated to be at least twice as great as that registered by the London Clearing House when the following are included:
* Returns from clearing houses in large towns across Scotland, Ireland, and England.
* Daily exchanges and settlements between different offices of banks with numerous branches.
* Cheques drawn by one bank customer and paid to another.

## Historical Growth and Statistics
The earliest authentic statement regarding clearing is found in the 1841 *Appendix to the Second Report, Committee of House of Commons, Banks of Issue*. Historical data shows a significant increase in the volume of clearings and changes in the number of participating banks:
* **1839:** £954,401,600 (29 banks)
* **1840:** £978,496,800 (29 banks)
* **1899:** £9,150,269,000 (19 banks)
* **1900:** £8,960,170,000 (19 banks)
* **1906:** £12,711,334,000 (18 banks)

## Organizational Evolution
City banks had associated themselves into a "Clearing House" by 1776, though records from "the Grasshopper" suggest a less organized system of settlement existed as early as 1773, noting a quarterly charge for the use of a clearing-room. A dedicated house for this purpose was established in 1810, at which time 46 banking houses settled accounts there.

The membership of the Clearing House has evolved over time:
* **1844:** Of the 49 banks in London, 26 were connected to the Clearing House; at this time, membership was restricted to private banks.
* **1854:** Joint-stock banks were admitted to the body.
* **1906:** The membership consisted of 19 banks in total: 18 joint-stock banks and one private bank.

## Operational Scope and Limitations
The Bank of England has never been a member of the Clearing House. However, it "clears on one side," meaning its claims on clearing bankers are processed through the Clearing House, while the claims of clearing bankers on the Bank of England are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times daily. Additionally, banks located at Charing Cross or Fleet Street did not belong to the Clearing House.

In 1858, arrangements made by Sir John Lubbock (Lord Avebury) added the clearing of country cheques to the system. This "country clearing" provides significant assistance to business by allowing a cheque drawn on a distant English village to be handled as conveniently as one drawn on London.

The system operates on a foundation of well-founded trust; users do not typically inquire about the bank's reserves or the stock of gold held by the bank on which a cheque is drawn.
