# The Establishment of British Suzerainty

## The British South Africa Company and Barotseland
British suzerainty in the regions north of the Middle Zambezi was established through a charter granted to the British South Africa Company in October 1889. This charter permitted the company to establish rule in these areas, provided they were not included within Portuguese dominions. The status of the Barotse kingdom was further clarified by a treaty between Portugal and Great Britain on June 11, 1891, which declared the kingdom to be within the British sphere of influence.

The precise western limits of Barotseland were the subject of a dispute between the two contracting powers. This matter was referred to the king of Italy for arbitration. In his award dated May 30, 1905, the king fixed the frontier at the Kwando river as far north as 22deg E., then following that meridian up to 13deg S. The boundary then followed that parallel east to 24deg E., and finally followed that meridian to the frontier of the Belgian Congo.

## Administration and Local Governance
The British South Africa Company maintained friendly relations with Lewanika, the paramount chief of the Barotse. To manage the region, the company appointed an administrator to reside in the country. Administrative infrastructure included the appointment of district commissioners and magistrates, as well as the raising of a native police force commanded by a British officer. Despite these measures, the company did not interfere in the internal affairs of the Barotse, and relations between the Barotse and the British remained uniformly friendly.

## The Role of Missionaries
While the Chartered Company provided the administrative framework, missionaries were the pioneers of Western civilization in the region. F. S. Arnot, an Englishman, spent two years in the country between 1882 and 1884. In 1884, the Societe des Missions Evangeliques de Paris established a mission that yielded good results. The first agent of this mission was Francois Coillard (1834-1904), who had previously worked in Basutoland and dedicated the remainder of his life to the Barotse.

Coillard was an admirer of British institutions and desired that the country eventually fall under British jurisdiction. However, to protect the interests of his mission, he initially sought to delay the arrival of white men in the country. Consequently, it was against Coillard's advice that Lewanika petitioned the "Great White Queen" to assume a protectorate over his dominions.

## Other British Imperial Acquisitions
The establishment of British influence in other regions often followed similar patterns of charters and treaties. For example, the Indian section of the empire was acquired between the 17th and 19th centuries via a royal charter granted to the East India Company by Queen Elizabeth in 1600. This was later transferred to the imperial government in 1858. Specific acquisitions within India included:
* **Madras:** The first territorial possession of the East India Company, acquired via treaty with its Indian ruler and subsequent conquest. It was raised to a presidency in 1683, ceded to France in 1746, and recovered in 1748.
* **Bombay:** Trade was established in 1608. It was ceded to the British crown by Portugal in 1661 and transferred to the East India Company in 1668.

In Africa, Great Britain occupied Egypt militarily in 1882, though Egypt did not form part of the British empire. Great Britain claimed a share of the administration of the former Sudan provinces of Egypt by "right of conquest," leading to an agreement on January 19, 1899, which established joint sovereignty between Egypt and Great Britain over the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan.
