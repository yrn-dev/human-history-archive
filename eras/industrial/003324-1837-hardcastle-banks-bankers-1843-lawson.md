# Banking and Financial History

The history of banking is documented through a vast array of theoretical treatises, institutional histories, and statistical records. These sources track the evolution of banking from early monetary tracts to the complex systems of the late 19th and early 20th centuries, covering the development of central banks, regional banking houses, and the regulation of currency.

## Banking Literature and Theory
The study of banking is supported by a wide range of publications. Early works include *De Monetarum Augmento, variatione et diminutione, Tractatus varii* (1509) and a 1697 proposal by A. D. of Grey's Inn regarding the supply of money to the King. Later theoretical and practical works include Lord King's *Thoughts on Bank Restrictions* (1804), William Cobbett's *Paper against Gold and Glory against Prosperity* (1815), and Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873). 

Practical guides for the profession include J. George Kiddy's *The Country Banker's Handbook* (1903) and George Rae's *The Country Banker* (1886). General information on the subject is further preserved in the *Dictionary of Political Economy* edited by R. H. Inglis Palgrave (1894-1906) and various international dictionaries of finance and commerce from France and Germany. Professional journals such as the *Bankers' Magazine* (commenced 1844) and the *Journal of the Institute of Bankers* (commenced 1879) also provide historical records of the industry.

## The Bank of England and British Banking
The Bank of England has been the subject of numerous historical accounts, including works by T. Fortune (1802), John Francis (1847), and Sir F. Schuster (1906). Beyond the central bank, British banking history is documented through the study of specific banking houses and regional centers. This includes L. H. Grindon's work on Manchester banks (1877), C. H. Cave's history of banking in Bristol (1899), and J. Hughes' study of Liverpool banks and bankers from 1760 to 1837.

In Scotland, the banking system is detailed in works by A. W. Kerr and Robert Somers, while the development of banking in Ireland is covered by Malcolm Dillon.

## United States Banking and State Systems
In the United States, banking practices varied by state and period. In New York, the "safety-fund system" was introduced in 1829 to protect bank-notes by having banks contribute to a fund to cover deficits of failing banks. However, this fund was exhausted following significant failures during the panics of 1837 and 1857. Additionally, the state did not control the issue of notes before 1843, leading to illegal over-issues in some instances.

Other states experienced financial instability due to state-chartered banks. For example, one state chartered a bank in 1819 that failed, requiring the state to borrow money in 1831 to redeem depreciated notes. A second state bank chartered in 1835 suspended payment two years later and was liquidated in 1843. This state also faced a credit decline after appropriating $10,000,000 in 1837 for railroads and other improvements, a policy that was abandoned in 1840.

## Note-Issue and Deposit Trends
Before the Civil War, the power of note-issue was a more critical banking resource than the deposit system. In 1835, the combined capital and circulation of state banks was approximately $335,000,000, while deposits were only $83,000,000. By 1907, this ratio shifted significantly, with national bank deposits reaching $4,322,000,000 compared to $1,430,000,000 in capital and circulation.

The circulation of state banks saw significant fluctuations:

## International Banking Systems
In France, beyond the major joint-stock banks, a large number of provincial banks operate with prudence. As of 1898, 182 of these firms belonged to the French Country Bankers' Association, operating in 66 of France's 86 departments. Some of these banks utilize a system of small branches similar to the English method.

In Germany, the "Reichsbank" (Imperial Bank of Germany) is the primary institution, alongside approximately 140 other banks. Five specific banks hold the right of uncovered note-issue:

## Banking Obligations and Liability
Bankers face specific risks regarding the payment of bills accepted payable at the bank, which is not considered an essential obligation like the payment of cheques. Furthermore, the responsibility of a banker for valuables held in safe custody has been a point of legal contention. Following a case involving Mrs. Langtry and the Union Bank of London in 1896, the Central Association of Bankers clarified that banks are liable for the wrongful conversion of goods delivered to the wrong person. However, in cases of loss or theft, a banker is viewed as a gratuitous bailee and is only liable if they failed to exercise the care of an ordinary prudent man, taking into account the use of strong-rooms and the nature of the goods.
