# British Military Campaigns and Administration

The British Empire expanded and maintained its territories through a combination of diplomatic treaties, strategic annexations, and military campaigns across Asia and Africa. These operations ranged from large-scale colonial conquests to frontier expeditions and the management of internal rebellions.

## Campaigns in Southeast Asia and the Himalayas

In Burma, the British Empire expanded through conquest following a breach of the 1826 treaty of Yandabo, which had required the court at Ava to protect British ships in Burmese waters. After the governor of Rangoon behaved outrageously toward the masters of the "Champion" and "Monarch" and the king provided no redress, Lord Dalhousie prepared for a campaign in the autumn of 1852. Dalhousie focused on medical supplies, boat transport, and rations, while resolving differences between naval forces and military contingents from Madras and Bengal. Under the command of General Godwin, Martaban was captured on April 5, 1852, followed by Bassein and Rangoon. A second campaign began in October, leading to the capture of Pegu and Prome. On December 20, 1853, the province of Pegu was annexed. Dalhousie subsequently consolidated these possessions by uniting Tenasserim and Arakan, placing Colonel Arthur Phayre in sole charge of the province.

In the Himalayan region, British involvement with Nepal began with a campaign in 1814-15. While Nepal remained independent in its internal administration, it became closely related to Great Britain, agreeing to receive a British resident and allowing the government of India to control its political relations with other states. In Sikkim, a military expedition in 1850 was prompted by the imprisonment of Sir Joseph Hooker and Dr. A. Campbell, the superintendent of Darjeeling. This resulted in the annexation of a portion of the hills and the Sikkim *tarai* at the foot of the hills, as well as the cessation of the allowance previously granted to the raja. Additionally, the Bhutan campaign of 1864 resulted in the acquisition of hill territory east of the Tista, which was added to the jurisdiction of Darjeeling in August 1866.

## West African Campaigns and the Ashanti

British military efforts in West Africa involved several expeditions against the Ashanti. Historical records of these campaigns include the *Narrative of the Ashantee War* (1874) by Sir Henry Brackenbury, the account of the 1873-74 expedition in *Coomassie* by Sir H.M. Stanley, and the account of the 1895-96 expedition, *The Downfall of Prempeh*, by General R.S.S. Baden-Powell. Other documented efforts include the 1900 Ashanti Campaign and the relief of Kumasi.

In one such operation to crush a rebellion after the relief of Kumasi, Colonel Willcocks commanded a force that included 134 British officers, 35 British non-commissioned officers, and 3,368 natives, consisting of Sikhs and Yaos from Central Africa, as well as Ashanti levies. On September 30, the Kumasi were defeated at Obassa. The remaining two rebel chiefs in the field were captured by December 28, and peace was restored by the start of 1901. The campaign resulted in 1,007 total casualties, including those who died of disease; among these, nine British officers were killed in action, six died of disease, and forty-three were wounded.

## Frontier Operations and Internal Security in India

In the North-West Frontier Province, the town of Kohat served as a military base for the southern Afridi frontier. In 1901, a military road was opened through the Kohat Pass to Peshawar. Kohat was the starting point for Sir William Lockhart's expedition against the Afridis and Orakzais during the Tirah campaign of 1897-98.

The British military presence in India also faced internal challenges. In the years following a great war, the British military caste was described as intellectually stagnant and unimaginative. This led to instances of "spasmodic violence," including the massacre of an unarmed political gathering at Amritzar, where nearly two thousand people were wounded or killed. These events, along with humiliating outrages and floggings, were brought to the attention of the British public by the Hunter Commission of 1919.

## Naval Warfare and Global Conflict

During the conflict involving Germany, the British navy faced an unrestricted submarine campaign. In January 1917, a complete blockade of France and Great Britain was proclaimed, and neutral shipping was warned to leave British seas. This indiscriminate sinking of ships led the United States to enter the war on April 6, 1917. The British navy responded by developing resourceful anti-submarine devices in the air, on the surface, and underwater. While the British public had to adopt food rations to avoid social disorder and famine, the regulations were ably administered.
