# Analysis of Weights in Engineering and Commerce

The measurement of weight in tons is applied across various disciplines, including locomotive engineering, bridge construction, maritime trade, and agricultural production. These measurements are used to determine structural stresses, vessel capacity, and commodity yields.

## Locomotive Weights and Load Distribution
Locomotives are categorized by their function, with distinct weight profiles for different types of engines. 

### Engine Classifications
*   **Goods Engines:** These engines exhibit total weights ranging from 75.65 to 78.80 tons. Their maximum axle loads vary between 13.65 and 16.00 tons, with tons per foot of wheel base ranging from 2.00 to 2.03.
*   **Tank Engines:** These are generally lighter, with total weights between 47.00 and 60.80 tons. Their maximum axle loads range from 15.29 to 17.54 tons, and they show a higher variance in tons per foot of wheel base, from 2.23 to 3.03.
*   **Other Heavy Locomotives:** Some heavy locomotives reach total weights between 84.35 and 98.90 tons, with maximum axle loads between 16.00 and 19.00 tons.

### Bending Moment and Equivalent Loads
To analyze the impact of heavy locomotives on tracks, equivalent uniform loads are calculated based on the span. For a span of 5.0 feet, the equivalent load is 7.6 tons per foot run; this decreases as the span increases, reaching 1.97 tons per foot run for a span of 100.0 feet.

## Bridge Engineering and Load Calculations
In bridge construction, engineers distinguish between different types of loads to ensure structural integrity.

### Load Types
*   **Live or Temporary Load:** For railway bridges, this is the weight of the heaviest train likely to cross. For road bridges, it is the weight of a heavy wagon, traction engine, or a uniformly distributed dense crowd.
*   **Dead Load:** This comprises the weight of the superstructure, including wind-bracing, flooring, and main girders. In railway bridges, rail girders and cross girders weigh 0.15 to 0.2 tons per foot, while rails and sleepers weigh 0.2 to 0.25 tons per foot run per line of way. Adding a footway adds approximately 0.4 ton per foot run.
*   **Other Forces:** Engineers account for longitudinal drag (approximately one-seventh of the train's weight), horizontal wind pressure, and centrifugal loads on curved bridges. Temperature variations (commonly assumed at 70° C or 126° F in Europe) can create stresses of one ton per square inch for every 12° change if expansion is prevented.

### Structural Examples
*   **The Ohio Bridge:** This American girder bridge features a center span of 550 feet and side spans of 490 feet. The total weight of its steel and iron is approximately 5000 tons.
*   **Douro Viaduct:** The center span of this bridge weighs 727 tons, with a rolling load taken at 1.2 ton per foot.
*   **Riga Pontoon Bridge:** This structure utilizes iron pontoons, each with a weight of 22 tons and a displacement of 180 tons.

## Maritime and Commercial Tonnage
Tonnage is used as a primary measure for shipping capacity and trade volume, particularly in the Danube region.

### Shipping Trends
Trade in the lower Danube saw a significant increase in vessel size. Sailing ships of 200 tons register were replaced by steamers reaching 4000 tons register, which could carry a deadweight of nearly 8000 tons. Between 1847 and 1860, British ships entering the Danube averaged 193 tons; by 1893, 905 vessels entered with a total of 1,287,762 tons.

## Agricultural and Chemical Weight Standards
Weight measurements are also critical in the assaying of precious metals and the tracking of dairy production.

### Assay Tonnage
In the assaying of gold and silver ores, results are expressed in ounces to the ton (using the troy ounce and a 2000-pound avoirdupois ton). To facilitate metric calculations, the "assay ton" (A.T. = 29.166 grammes) is used. In this system, one milligram of gold or silver found in one assay ton of ore represents one ounce per ton.

### Dairy Production
Agricultural data tracks milk production in tons. For example, between 1890 and 1899, annual milk production fluctuated, with a 10-year average of 7,906,874 tons. This milk is used to produce butter and cheese; calculations are based on yielding 49 cwt (or 531 lb) of butter or 220 lb of cheese per ton of milk.
