# The Bird-Organ

The bird-organ, known in French as the *serinette*, *turlutaine*, or *merline*, was a very small variety of barrel-organ utilized during the 18th and 19th centuries. These instruments were diminutive in scale; for example, one specimen held in the collection of the Brussels Conservatoire measures approximately 8 x 5-1/2 x 2-3/4 inches. This specific instrument is designed in the form of a book, with the title "*Le chant des oiseaux, Tome vi.*" appearing on its back.

## Technical Specifications and Construction
The bird-organ described by V. C. Mahillon contains ten pewter stopped pipes. These pipes provide the scale of G, supplemented by Fb and A two octaves higher (notation: G4 A5). This particular instrument is capable of playing eight tunes.

The broader category of the barrel-organ—which includes the bird-organ—is a portable organ played mechanically by turning a handle. The instrument derives its name from the cylinder, or barrel, which is studded with pins and staples of various lengths. These are set at definite intervals according to the requirements of the music. As the cylinder rotates, these pins raise balanced keys that open valves, allowing a stream of air from the wind-chest to enter the pipes.

The mechanical process is driven by a handle attached to a shaft, which uses a worm working in a fine-toothed gear on the barrel-head to rotate the cylinder. This same motion operates the bellows via connecting rods and cranks, forcing air into a reservoir and then into the wind-chest. Typically, one complete revolution of the barrel plays one complete tune. To change the tune, the performer uses a notch-pin in the barrel-head.

## Historical Context and Variations
The barrel-organ has a long history originating in the Netherlands as early as the middle of the 15th century. Early examples were not portable; for instance, the organ-builder Jehan van Steenken (also known as Aren) was appointed "Master of organs which play of themselves" by the duke of Burgundy. These imposing instruments were transported from Bruges to Brussels. In 1615, Solomon de Caus published detailed diagrams of a large stationary barrel-organ powered by hydraulic power, which was built into a wall one foot thick. De Caus' version featured a drum with movable pins, allowing the performer to rearrange them to create new tunes.

While the diminutive bird-organ and the "hand-organ" of itinerant musicians were common, larger versions were used in churches and chapels during the 18th and early 19th centuries to play voluntaries, chants, and hymns. These elaborate instruments could feature three or four cylinders set in a circular revolving frame. Examples include an instrument built by Wright for Fulham church and a large four-barrel instrument constructed by Bishop for Northallerton church in 1820.

## Cultural Impact and Usage
In England, these instruments were often referred to as "Dutch organs," a name that persisted even for the small hand-organs used by street musicians. References to these instruments appear in descriptions of the Bartholomew Fair at the start of the 18th century, where a band consisting of a Dutch organ, double drum, violin, pipes, tambourine, and Turkish jingle was available for hire.

The instruments were sometimes viewed with social or legal scrutiny; one author, Mr. Stephens, proposed to parliament that anyone keeping an organ in a public house should be fined £20 and barred from being an ale-draper. Conversely, they could be highly prized; in 1737, Horace Walpole sought a piece of music that could play eight tunes, noting that Handel and other great musicians claimed it was beyond their capabilities. Such an organ was eventually sold in a lottery for £1,000.

In the realm of composition, Mozart wrote an *Andante* specifically for a small barrel-organ.
