# Denmark (1798–1801)

The period between 1798 and 1801 in Denmark was characterized by a vibrant intellectual landscape and a shift in geopolitical stability that culminated in a violent collision with Great Britain.

## Intellectual and Literary Life
During this era, Denmark was home to a diverse array of prose writers and poets. Among the eminent prose writers of the period were Werner Abrahamson (1744–1812), recognized as the first aesthetic critic produced by Denmark, and Johan Clemens Tode (1736–1806), who was noted for his work in various sciences and specifically as a medical writer. Historical documentation was advanced by Ove Mailing (1846–1829), a collector of historical data, and Peter Frederik Suhm (1728–1798), whose eleven-volume *History of Denmark* (published between 1782 and 1812) provided a vast amount of original material. Other notable figures included the historian Ove Guldberg (1731–1808) and theologians such as Nicolai Edinger Balle (1744–1816), the bishop of Zealand, and Christian Bastholm (1740–1819).

The literary scene was further enriched by a group of poets born in Norway who were associates and fellow-students in Copenhagen. This group included Nordahl Brun, Claus Frimann (1746–1829), Claus Fasting (1746–1791), Christian H. Pram (1756–1821), and Edvard Storm (1749–1794). These writers introduced a style distinct from that of the native Danes, utilizing imagery of the mountains and rivers of Norway to enrich the Danish language with new phrases and words. While they are viewed by Norwegians as the founders of Norwegian literature, their primary appeal and place were among the Danes.

## Foreign Residents and Visitors
The intellectual atmosphere of the region attracted foreign figures. Charles Victor de Bonstetten (1745–1832), a Swiss writer and liberal patrician from Bern, resided in Denmark from 1798 to 1801. During these years, he lived with his friend Fredirika Brun. Bonstetten had previously served as a bailiff in Saanen and Nyon and governed the Italian-speaking districts of Lugano, Locarno, Mendrisio, and Val Maggia for the Swiss Confederation. His time in Denmark followed his return to private life after the French revolution of 1798 in Switzerland.

## Geopolitical Conflict and the British Collision
The political stability of Denmark was disrupted by its relationship with Russia. In 1800, the Danish government was persuaded by the tsar to join the second Armed Neutrality League, an agreement Russia had already concluded with Sweden and Prussia. 

Great Britain responded to this alliance by implementing an embargo on the vessels of the three neutral powers. To enforce this, Britain dispatched a significant fleet to the Baltic under the command of Nelson and Parker. On April 2, 1801, the British forces attacked. Although the Danes offered a gallant resistance despite being unprepared and surprised, the outcome was decisive. The Danish fleet was destroyed and the capital was bombarded. Having been abandoned by Russia, Denmark was forced to submit to a peace that was disadvantageous.
