# Isaac Newton and Related Intellectual Works

## Isaac Newton: Life and Scientific Contributions
Isaac Newton (1642–1727) was born in the same year that Galileo died. He is credited with completing the modern clear vision of the starry universe through his discovery of the law of gravitation. His work represents a period of fruitful and peaceful scientific labor characterized by a shift toward a "close, naked, natural way of speaking" in prose, a style also seen in the works of the botanist Ray.

Newton's major publications include the *Principia*, which appeared in its entirety in 1687, and his work on *Optics*. The latter was translated into a new version in 1787 by a former court doctor who had previously presented memoirs on heat, light, and electricity to the Académie des Sciences.

In the field of physics, the fundamental hypothesis of science assumes a system of bodies in motion—such as the planets and the sun—where each body is attracted to all others according to Newton's law. The motion of these bodies is expressed through Newton's three laws of motion. This system utilizes an analytic method where the position of each body is defined as a material particle by a system of co-ordinate axes rather than verbal descriptions, using differential equations to express changes in co-ordinates.

## Influence and Contemporary Reception
Newton's standing among his peers was pre-eminent. His contemporary, Barrow, was considered by English mathematicians to be second only to Newton. While Barrow's *Method of Tangents* approximated the reasoning that led Newton to the doctrine of ultimate ratios, Barrow's own substantial contributions to science are regarded as being of less importance.

Newton's influence extended into later centuries, though his theories were sometimes challenged. One scientist, who studied electricity and light, faced hostility from the academicians of the Académie des Sciences for his temerity in differing from Newton. Despite this, the rejection was viewed by Goethe as an example of scientific despotism.

## The Works of Boole
The archive contains records of reflections on scientific, philosophical, and religious questions delivered as addresses by Boole. Among these four addresses are *The Genius of Sir Isaac Newton*, *The Right Use of Leisure*, *The Claims of Science*, and *The Social Aspect of Intellectual Culture*.

Beyond these addresses, Boole is noted for his *Laws of Thought*, which established a general symbolic method of logical inference. This system allowed for the drawing of conclusions logically contained within premises through purely symbolic treatment. The *Laws of Thought* also attempted to discover a general method in probabilities to determine the probability of an event logically connected to a given system of events.

## Concepts of Leisure and Education
The archive provides varying perspectives on the concept of leisure. In the context of social behavior, decorum is described by Mr. Veblen in *Theory of the Leisure Class* as a "product and an exponent of the leisure-class life," where etiquette, table manners, and tea ceremonies became highly developed sciences.

In the realm of education, a historical conception existed where a "small hard nucleus of compulsory work" was combined with a "wide margin of leisure." This structure was considered beneficial for "the best men" who utilized their free time in the "right way," though it left "weak men" uneducated. This led to a reaction favoring longer hours and a wider curriculum, which eliminated leisure for both mischief and study.

## The Development of Experimental Philosophy
Newton's work followed a trajectory of experimental philosophy initiated by earlier figures. Roger Bacon preached experiment, and Dr. Gilbert (1540–1603) of Colchester was among the first to practice it, specifically regarding magnetism. These efforts influenced Francis Bacon, Lord Verulam (1561–1626), who is often called the "Father of Experimental Philosophy," though some, such as Sir R. A. Gregory, describe him as the "apostle" rather than the founder of the scientific method.

Francis Bacon's *The New Atlantis* envisioned a "palace of invention" and a "temple of science" where the pursuit of knowledge would be organized with high efficiency. While some critics, such as Liebig, argued that little science actually proceeds according to Bacon's rules, the spirit of induction, observation, and method eventually spread across all thought, typified by John Locke's *Essay concerning Human Understanding* (1690).
