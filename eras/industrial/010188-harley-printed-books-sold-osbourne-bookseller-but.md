# The History of Bookselling and Book Collecting

The trade in books is of a very ancient date, evolving from the early practices of poets and orators who recited works in public to encourage audiences to purchase written copies. These early transcripts were often taken down *viva voce* and sold to wealthy buyers. In the book of Jeremiah, the prophet is depicted dictating to Baruch the scribe, illustrating the role of scribes as the earliest booksellers who supplied copies upon demand.

## Ancient and Medieval Trade
In antiquity, the demand for books grew significantly. Plato is recorded to have paid one hundred minae for three small treatises by Philolaus the Pythagorean, and Aristotle is noted to have possessed an extensive library. The founding of the Alexandrian library around 300 B.C. further stimulated Athenian booksellers, known as *biblion kapaeloi*. In Rome, libraries became common household furniture toward the end of the republic. Booksellers, referred to as *librarii* or *bibliopolae*, operated shops (*taberna librarii*) primarily in the Vicus Sandalarius and the Argiletum, where lists of available books were posted on doors or side posts. During the time of Augustus, the Sosii were prominent booksellers. A law passed by Justinian securing scribes' property in their materials is viewed as a possible early germ of modern copyright law.

By the 12th century, bookselling became established in Bologna and Paris, driven by universities and lawyers. These dealers, called *stationarii* (possibly from the open stalls where they worked), sold books on commission and provided writing materials—which retained the name "stationery"—as well as binding and decorating services.

## Bookselling in England
In England, the spread of Christianity increased the demand for sacred texts, such as the Gospels. In 671, Benedict Biscop brought a cargo of books from France to the abbey at Wearmouth. By 1403, text writers or stationers (*stacyoneres*) who sold manuscript copies of prayers, the Creed, and the Paternoster near St Paul's in London formed a gild. 

At Oxford, "sworn" university stationers held a monopoly on supplying pupils with books, though they sometimes acted as pawnbrokers. A 1373 statute attempted to restrict non-sworn booksellers to prevent the cheating of owners and the loss of valuable books from the city.

The introduction of printing shifted the trade. Early printers often served as editors and booksellers. For example, Antony Koburger, who brought printing to Nuremberg in 1470, operated sixteen shops and used agents across Christendom. In Westminster, Wynkyn de Worde, successor to Caxton's press, maintained a shop in Fleet Street.

## Censorship and Regulation
The Reformation and religious dissensions under Henry VIII and Edward VI increased the demand for books, but the Tudor and Stuart monarchs restricted the press. In 1529, Henry VIII granted the first king's printer patent to Thomas Berthelet, requiring books to be licensed; possessing unlicensed books was a punishable offense. The Company of Stationers was incorporated in 1556 to repress "obnoxious" books, and the Star Chamber later exercised effective censorship.

While Queen Elizabeth interfered primarily with books from Roman Catholics or those touching royal prerogatives, bookselling flourished toward the end of her reign and during the reign of James. Although Archbishop Laud introduced arbitrary restrictions, these were largely removed during the Commonwealth. By 1658, the growth of the trade was evident in the publication of *A Catalogue of the most Vendible Books in England* by W. London. The office of Licenser of the Press was restored during the Restoration and lasted until 1694.

In 1709, the first English Copyright Act allowed individuals to complain to the archbishop of Canterbury if a book's price was unreasonably high. If the complaint was founded, the price was reduced, and booksellers charging more were fined £5 per copy, though this enactment apparently remained a dead letter.

## Book Collecting and Public Libraries
Between the 16th and 18th centuries, book collecting often served a public purpose. Many large buyers bequeathed their collections to institutions:
* Sir Thomas Smith (d. 1577) left his books to Queens' College, Cambridge.
* Archbishop Parker left his to Corpus Christi College, Cambridge.
* Sir Thomas Bodley refounded the Oxford university library, which also received contributions from Laud and Selden.
* The libraries of Archbishop Williams and Archbishop Usher went to St John's College, Cambridge and Trinity College, Dublin, respectively.
* Thomas Howard, earl of Norfolk (d. 1646), had his scientific and mathematical books given to the Royal Society.
* Pepys left his library to Magdalene College, Cambridge.
* George I bought Bishop Moore's books and presented them to Cambridge university library.
* Archbishop Marsh founded a library at Dublin after purchasing Stillingfleet's printed books.

The trend toward public ownership is further seen in the Harleian MSS, which were offered to the nation below their value, and the collections of Sir Hans Sloane (including 50,000 printed books). These, along with the Cotton manuscripts, led to the foundation of the British Museum in 1753. Conversely, some collections were sold privately; for instance, Harley's printed books were sold to Osbourne the bookseller, and the accumulations of Thomas Rawlinson (d. 1725) were sold at auction.

## American Bookselling and Modern Developments
Printing reached Boston in 1676, Philadelphia in 1685, and New York in 1693. Early American bookselling was limited, with scholars importing books from Europe. After the second war, printing presses multiplied. Because there was no international copyright, American publishers reprinted works by British authors—such as Scott, Byron, and Wordsworth—without payment to the authors. Between 1820 and 1852, half the authors in the "American" catalogue were British. Following the Civil War, American publishers began making liberal offers for early sheets of new publications.

By the 19th century, a fundamental distinction emerged between the publisher (literary promoter) and the bookseller (shopkeeper). The wholesale bookseller became a critical intermediary, particularly in London's Paternoster Row. 

Physical book production also evolved. Glazed calico was used around 1820, followed by cloth cases around 1830. Wood-engraving was the primary illustration method until the late 19th century, when it was replaced by photographic reproduction. In the 1890s, the Kelmscott Press under William Morris set new standards for beauty in type and ornament. Additionally, the market shifted around 1894 from expensive three-volume editions (31s. 6d.) to more affordable single-volume novels at 6s. each.
