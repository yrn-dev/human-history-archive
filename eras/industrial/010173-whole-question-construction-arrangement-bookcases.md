# The Construction and Arrangement of Bookcases

A bookcase is an article of furniture designed as a shelved receptacle for the storage of books, which may be oriented either horizontally or perpendicularly.

## Historical Evolution
In the era when books were written by hand and remained excessively scarce, they were stored in small coffers that the wealthy carried during their journeys. As regal palaces and religious houses accumulated manuscript volumes, these were stored in cupboards or upon shelves; the modern bookcase is a direct descendant of these cupboards. 

The evolution of the bookcase progressed when doors were discarded for the sake of convenience. However, early arrangement methods differed from modern practices. Volumes were either piled on their sides or, if placed upright, were ranged with their edges facing outwards and their backs to the wall. Because titles were often inscribed on the band of parchment, vellum, or leather used to close the book, the title appeared on the fore-edge rather than the back. This practice changed following the invention of printing, which made books cheaper; subsequently, it became standard to write titles on the back and place the edges inwards.

## Materials and Design
Oak was commonly used for early bookcases and continues to be regarded as the most appropriate wood for a stately library. The Bodleian library at Oxford contains the oldest bookcases in England, positioned in the final two years of the 16th century; these provide the earliest extant examples of flat wall-cases featuring shelved galleries.

During the second half of the 18th century, great English cabinet-makers attempted to soften the severe appearance of long book-shelves using pilasters and carved cornices. Both Sheraton and Chippendale designed and produced numerous bookcases, many of which featured fret-work frames and glazing with little lozenges. Sheraton's satinwood bookcases are noted for an alluring grace that has rarely been equalled. French cabinet-makers of the same period also produced successful small ornamental cases. Materials used included rosewood, mahogany, satinwood, and other choice exotic timbers, which were frequently mounted with gilded and chased bronze or inlaid with marqueterie. Additionally, dwarf bookcases were often topped with a slab of choice marble.

In 20th-century public libraries, materials shifted toward industrial options:
* **Iron:** Used in the British Museum, where cowhide covers the shelves.
* **Steel:** Used in the library of Congress in Washington.
* **Slate:** Used in the Fitzwilliam library at Cambridge.

## Systems of Arrangement
There are three primary systems for arranging bookcases:
1. **Flat against the wall:** The simplest placement of cases.
2. **Stacks:** Ranges arranged parallel to one another with only enough space between them to allow a librarian to pass. This system is used in public libraries where economy of space is essential.
3. **Bays or Alcoves:** Cases that jut out into the room at right angles to the wall-cases. This system is considered handsome and utilizes space to great advantage; the library of the city of London at the Guildhall is cited as a peculiarly effective example of this arrangement.
