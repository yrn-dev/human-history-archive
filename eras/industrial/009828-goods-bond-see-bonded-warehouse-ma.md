# BOND

The term "bond" refers to a variety of legal, financial, and structural instruments, as well as a historical social class. In legal and financial contexts, it generally denotes an obligation or a security for payment or performance.

## Financial and Legal Bonds
Bonds serve as instruments of indebtedness or security. A **debenture bond** is secured solely by a company's covenant, lacking a fixed or floating charge on assets. In contrast, **Lloyd's bonds** are instruments under a railway company's seal that admit indebtedness to an obligee for goods supplied or work completed, including a covenant for payment with interest on a future date. These were used by railway companies to increase indebtedness without technically violating their charters.

Other specialized bonds include:
*   **Bottomry Bonds:** A contract of hypothecation where a ship's owner or master borrows money for emergencies (such as necessary repairs) by pledging the ship's keel or bottom as security. If the voyage is completed safely, the obligee is repaid with interest; if the ship is totally lost, the obligee loses the investment.
*   **Post Obit Bonds:** Bonds given by a legatee or expectant heir, payable upon or after the death of the person from whom they expect an inheritance. These may be set aside if the obligee exacted unconscionable terms.
*   **Heritable Bonds:** A term in Scots law referring to a bond for money joined with a conveyance of land, held by a creditor as security.
*   **Recognizance:** Unlike a standard bond, a recognizance is entered into before a court of record, making it an obligation of record.

Under English law, bonds intended to secure actions contrary to legal policy are void. Examples include marriage brocage bonds (to procure a marriage) or bonds given to a woman for future cohabitation.

## Bonded Warehouses
A bonded warehouse is a facility established by private enterprise or the state where goods liable to duty are stored until the duty is paid. Before their establishment in England, importers had to pay duties immediately or provide a bond with security. This older system caused several inconveniences: importers often lacked sureties, were forced to sell goods during market depressions to raise funds, and faced higher prices due to the interest on the capital required for lump-sum payments. Additionally, it stifled competition for heavily taxed articles and complicated the process of granting drawbacks to exporters.

To address these issues and prevent revenue fraud, Sir Robert Walpole proposed a warehousing system for wine and tobacco in 1733, though it was not adopted until 1803. Under the 1803 act, goods were placed in customs-approved warehouses, and importers provided "bonds" for payment upon the removal of the goods. The Customs Consolidation Act 1853 removed the requirement for giving bonds, with subsequent provisions detailed in the Acts of 1876, 1880, and 1883. These are also known as "king's warehouses," defined as places provided by the crown or approved by customs commissioners for the security of goods and their due duties.

While goods are "in bond," owners may perform processes to fit them for market, including:
*   Roasting coffee.
*   Repacking and mixing tea.
*   Bottling, mixing, vatting, and racking of spirits and wines.
*   Manufacturing certain types of tobacco.

Allowances are made for leakage, evaporation, and waste resulting from these processes.

## Structural Bonding in Masonry
In brickwork, "bonding" refers to the method of arranging bricks. **Flemish bond** involves placing headers and stretchers alternately in the same course, resulting in the same appearance on each face. This method requires the use of closers. While Flemish bond provides a neater appearance and is often selected for the external walls of domestic buildings, it is considered less strong and less perfect than English bond due to a weaker transverse tie and a higher number of joints caused by the use of half bricks. Consequently, English bond is preferred for engineering works and manufacturing buildings where compactness and strength are of highest importance.

## Historical Social Class
In historical Norwegian contexts, "bondes" refers to a class of people, often associated with land and local governance. They participated in "Things" (assemblies) and held sacrifice-festivals, such as midsummer and mid-winter sacrifices at Maeren. The bondes' army was capable of stripping the country of men able to carry arms. Historical accounts describe them as being particularly fond of the newest counsel. In conflicts with the sovereign, such as those involving King Olaf, the bondes demonstrated the ability to organize large armed forces and resist royal demands, including the invitation to adopt Christianity.

## International Debt Obligations
Bonds are also used as sovereign guarantees. Following world war events, the Commission required Germany to issue bonds to cover claims. These included:
*   20,000,000,000 marks gold (payable by May 1, 1921, without interest).
*   40,000,000,000 marks gold (bearing 2-1/2 per cent interest from 1921–1926, then 5 per cent, with a 1 per cent sinking fund starting in 1926).
*   An undertaking for 40,000,000,000 marks gold bonds at 5 per cent interest.

Certificates of beneficial interest may be issued to represent these bonds or delivered goods.
