# Excess of Deflection

In the context of structural engineering and bridge construction, the excess of deflection refers to the additional straining action and deformation caused by a moving load when compared to the same load remaining at rest on the structure.

## Factors Influencing Excess Deflection
The increment of deflection and stress caused by a moving load is influenced by several mechanical and environmental factors. If a vertical load is applied suddenly without velocity, the resulting stress and deformation are momentarily double those of a load at rest. However, in practical bridge applications, loads are applied such that deflection increases with speed, creating stresses greater than those of a gradually applied load and inducing vibrations around a mean position.

Several specific causes contribute to these increased stresses:
* **Rail Conditions:** Because rails are not perfectly smooth or straight, lurching and centrifugal actions occur, altering how the load is distributed.
* **Mechanical Imbalance:** The moving parts of an engine that are unbalanced vertically create rapidly changing forces.
* **Rail Geometry:** Shocks are produced by inequalities of level at the ends of the rails.

These factors ensure that live load stresses exceed those of a resting load. This increment is more pronounced on short main girders than on long ones, and is larger on flooring girders than on main girders.

## Impact and Load Ratios
The increment of deflection due to impact is linked to the ratio of dead load to live load. Data collated by E.H. Stone from official trials of Indian bridges regarding the center deflections of main girders provides the following ratios:

| Dead Load (% of total) | Live Load (% of total) | Ratio of Live to Dead Load | Excess Deflection and Stress (%) |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| 10 | 90 | 9 | 23 |
| 20 | 80 | 4 | 13 |
| 30 | 70 | 2.3 | 8 |
| 40 | 60 | 1.5 | 5.5 |
| 50 | 50 | 1.0 | 4.0 |
| 70 | 30 | 0.43 | 1.6 |
| 90 | 10 | 0.10 | 0.3 |

Stone inferred that the augmentation of stress for any member would follow the same percentage for these same ratios of live to dead load stresses.

## Effects of Speed and Span
Research conducted by S.W. Robinson and F.E. Turneaure—the latter using recording extensometers and a recording deflectometer—highlighted the role of locomotive balance weights in causing vibration. In some instances, these vibrations are cumulative, exceeding the value of any single impact action.

The relationship between speed, span, and deflection is characterized by the following:
* **Low Speeds:** At speeds below 25 meters per hour, there is not much vibration.
* **Moderate Speeds:** At 40 or 50 meters per hour, the increase in deflection due to impact can reach 40% to 50% for girder spans shorter than 50 feet.
* **Longer Spans:** The percentage of increase decreases rapidly as spans lengthen, dropping to approximately 25% for 75-foot spans.
* **Member Stress:** The percentage increase in boom stresses due to impact is roughly equal to that of deflection, while the increase in web bracing bars is somewhat greater.
* **Mean Deflection:** The speed of the train affects the magnitude of vibrations but has no effect on the mean deflection.

## Calculation and Mitigation
To account for calculated deflection, girders may be constructed with a "camber," which is an upward convexity. This allows the girder to become straight under its working load. For riveted girders, a smaller modulus of elasticity (approximately 17,500,000 lb per sq. in.) is used for the first loading compared to a solid bar due to the yielding of joints.

Deflection ($\delta$) can be estimated using the approximate rule $\delta = l^2/10,000h$, where $l$ represents the span and $h$ represents the depth of the beam, based on usual bridgework stresses from total dead and live loads.
