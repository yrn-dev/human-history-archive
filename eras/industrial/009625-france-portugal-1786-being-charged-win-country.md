# French Diplomatic Mission to Portugal (1786)

## The Mission of the Marquis de Bombelles
In 1786, the French government appointed Marc Marie, Marquis de Bombelles, as its ambassador to Portugal. Bombelles was a French ecclesiastic and diplomatist born in Bitsch, Lorraine, who had previously served in the army during the Seven Years' War before entering the diplomatic service in 1765.

The primary objective of Bombelles' appointment in 1786 was to win over Portugal to the Family Compact. However, his efforts to achieve this diplomatic goal were unsuccessful. The archive attributes this failure to two specific factors: the madness of the queen and the subsequent death of the king.

## Subsequent Career of Marc Marie de Bombelles
Following his tenure in Portugal, Bombelles was transferred to Vienna in early 1789. His diplomatic career was interrupted by the French Revolution, leading to his removal from his post in September 1790. Despite this, he remained loyal to Louis XVI and conducted secret missions to various sovereigns to secure aid for the king.

In 1792, Bombelles emigrated and lived in retirement in Switzerland after the Battle of Valmy. Following the death of his wife in 1804, he moved to the monastery of Brunn in Austria. He later entered the clergy in Prussia as the bishop of Oberglogau. He eventually returned to France in 1815 and served as the bishop of Amiens starting in 1819. He died in Paris in 1822.

## Contemporary Portuguese Context (1786)
The year 1786 was also marked by internal Portuguese political friction. Jose Francisco Correa da Serra, a Portuguese man of science and politician born in 1750, found himself in conflict with the Holy Office due to his use of the privilege to publish the transactions of the Portuguese Academy of Sciences without censorship. As a result of this conflict, Correa da Serra fled to France in 1786, where he remained until the death of Pedro III.

## Broader European and Global Relations
During the late 18th century, France, Portugal, Spain, Britain, and Holland were engaged in a complex competition for ascendancy within Europe, which was further complicated by their struggles for dominion overseas. 

In the Americas, the Portuguese Empire included Brazil. Later, in 1807, the monarchy of Portugal fled to Brazil after French armies under Napoleon occupied the mother country. This shifted the dynamic of the relationship, making Portugal a dependency of Brazil until Brazil declared itself a separate Empire under Pedro I in 1822.

Additionally, Portugal maintained a presence in Africa at Whydah, where it held a "fort." Although this fort, along with those held by France, England, and Brazil, was ungarrisoned and in a ruinous condition, Portugal and France used the existence of these forts to lodge energetic protests in 1852 against an English blockade of the Dahomeyan coast intended to prevent the slave trade.
