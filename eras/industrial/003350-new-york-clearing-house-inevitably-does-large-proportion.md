# The New York Clearing-House

## Overview and Scale of Operations
The system of clearing cheques in the United States has reached a level of development surpassed perhaps only by Great Britain. Clearing-houses operate in approximately 112 leading cities across the country. For the year ending September 30, 1907, the aggregate clearings reached a total of $154,662,515,258.

The New York Clearing-House handles a large proportion of this national business. In 1906, its clearings accounted for 67.2% of the total clearings across 55 of the larger cities. The volume of these clearings is subject to significant fluctuation, driven by the business prosperity of the United States and the volume of transactions on the stock exchange.

## Influence and Membership
The New York Clearing-House possesses significant influence over the banking situation, primarily through the Clearing-House Committee. This committee has the ability to refuse emergency aid to banks that are unwisely conducted. This authority was utilized during the panic of 1907 to remove several speculative financial interests from the control of national banks.

During this period, membership in the Clearing-House was limited to the sub-Treasury, state banks, and national banks. The money-market and exchanges relied on the weekly reports of condition issued by these members every Saturday as an index of financial health. However, this index was sometimes misleading or incomplete because trust companies did not provide regular weekly reports. To rectify this, the state superintendent of banking announced in early 1908 that he would use legal powers to require trust companies to submit weekly reports, creating a more complete mirror of New York's banking conditions.

## International Comparison
The American system shares similarities with the United Kingdom, where nearly every large transaction is settled via cheque through ledger transfers, with specie and notes used only for fractional amounts. A large portion of these UK transactions are managed by the London Clearing House, which has an annual circulation exceeding £12,000,000,000. This system is facilitated by the fact that every bank in the United Kingdom maintains an agent in London.
