# Alkylated Compounds

In organic chemistry, alkylation refers to the introduction of alkyl groups into a molecular structure, a process that can significantly alter the physical and chemical properties of the resulting compounds. This process is observed across various chemical series, including amino-azo compounds, indazoles, and glyoxalines.

## Alkylated Amino-Azo-Benzenes
Amino-azo compounds are typically red or yellowish brown. The specific shade of the compound is influenced by its composition; while an increase in the number of amino groups leads to browner shades, the introduction of alkylated amino groups results in redder shades. 

Alkylated amino-azo-benzenes are formed through the coupling of diazonium salts with alkylated amines. This reaction is possible provided the amines contain a free para position relative to the amino group. Research conducted by H. Goldschmidt and A. Merz indicates that the velocity of the formation of these amino-azo compounds depends on the nature of the reagents rather than their concentration. Furthermore, when coupling the hydrochloride of a tertiary amine with diazobenzene sulphonic acid, the reaction occurs between the acid and the base released by the hydrolytic dissociation of its salt. This process occurs most rapidly when the weakest acid is used.

## Alkyl Indazoles and Isindazoles
Indazoles are weak bases that crystallize readily. When indazoles are heated with alkyl iodides, they are converted into alkyl indazoles. A known series of compounds that are isomeric with these alkyl derivatives are called isindazoles. These isomers are considered to be derived from a specific ring system and can be prepared by using zinc dust and either water or acetic acid to reduce nitroso-ortho-alkylamino-acetophenones.

## Alkylation in Glyoxalines
Glyoxalines (also known as imidazoles) are basic in character. In these compounds, the imide hydrogen is replaceable by various groups, including metals and alkyl groups. Imidazole itself, first prepared by H. Debus, crystallizes in thick prisms and is soluble in water and alcohol.

## Mixed Azo Compounds and Nitro-Alkyl Derivatives
Mixed azo compounds are characterized by an azo group (.N:N.) that is united with an aromatic radical on one side and a radical of the aliphatic series on the other. These can be formed by the union of a diazonium salt with the sodium or potassium salt of a nitroparaffin. 

E. Bamberger has demonstrated that nitro-alkyl derivatives behave as though they possess the constitution of hydrazones. When these derivatives are heated with dilute alkalies, they split into an acid hydrazide and an alkaline nitrite. Examples of mixed azo compounds include benzene-azo-methane, a yellow oil boiling at 150°C, and benzene-azo-ethane, a yellow oil boiling at approximately 180°C. Benzene-azo-ethane can be converted into the isomeric acetaldehyde-phenylhydrazone by standing with 60% sulphuric acid.
