# Geodetic Surveys in Russia

## European Russia and Triangulation
The progress of geodetic surveys in Russia has extended across the European half of the empire. A direct system of triangulation, executed with the highest scientific precision, connects St Petersburg with Tiflis, located on the southern slopes of the Caucasus. Furthermore, St Petersburg is connected to Greenwich via European systems of triangulation. Consequently, Russia has adopted the Greenwich meridian as the zero for all its longitude values.

Within European Russia, surveys conducted in the manner of other European countries since 1816 have been limited to the more densely peopled governments. For the majority of other regions, only "military surveys" exist. Various specific governments, including the Crimea, Bessarabia, Volhynia, Kief, and Moscow, have been published on a scale of 1:24,000. Finland was re-surveyed between 1870 and 1895, with a map on a scale of 1:42,000 nearing completion.

## Challenges in Asiatic Russia
Beyond the eastern shores of the Caspian Sea, the implementation of first-class triangulation for direct geodetic measurements has not been possible. The surveys of Asiatic Russia are separated from those of Europe by the width of the Caspian. The rigorous geodetic methods used in Europe were found to be hindered by the arid nature of the trans-Caspian deserts, which proved to be an insuperable obstacle.

Because of these conditions, Russian geography in central Asia relies on means other than direct measurement to determine coordinate values for latitude and longitude. The initial starting point for the trans-Caspian triangulation of Russia is the astronomical observatory at Tashkent. This specific triangulation is ranked as second-class and currently extends beyond Osh to the Pamir frontier. Exact surveys based upon triangulation in Asia extend as far east as Chinese Turkestan, at a longitude of approximately 75 degrees East of Greenwich.

## Determination of Longitude
The longitude of the Tashkent observatory was determined differentially with Pulkova using the telegraph. Three independent values were established:
* In 1875, via Omsk and Ekaterinburg: 2 35 52.151"
* In 1891, via Orenburg and Saratov: 2 35 52.228"
* In 1895, via Baku and Kiev: 2 35 51.997"

These values fall within a range of 0^S.25, making it improbable that the mean value contains an error larger than 0^S.10.

## Cartographic Publications
Several maps provide coverage of the Russian empire. The most readily available map of the entire country is the 10-verst map (1:420,000), published between 1865 and 1880 and known as General J. A. Strelbitzki's. Additionally, a topographic map on a scale of 1:126,000, which covers the country of the Don Cossacks, Poland, and the whole of western Russia, is intended to be extended across the entire empire.
