## Engineering and Bridge Design
In the study of bridge construction, specifically concerning beam girder or truss bridges, various "W" variables are used to determine stresses and weights. A main girder typically consists of a vertical web and an upper and lower flange (also referred to as a boom or chord). 

### Determination of Stresses
When analyzing a beam with a system of loads denoted as $W_1, W_2, ... W_n$, the reaction at the right abutment ($R_2$) is calculated as $W_1x_1/l + W_2x_2/l...$, while the reaction at the left abutment ($R_1$) is the sum of all loads minus $R_2$. The total shear ($S$) at any given section $ab$ is determined by $R$ minus the summation of all loads to the left of that section.

### Weight Calculations
The weight of main girders ($W_g$) is designed to carry their own weight as well as $W_l + W_f$. The formula $W_g = (W_l + W_f)k/(1-k)$ is used, where $k(W_l + W_f)$ can be derived from a provisional design that neglects $W_g$. Empirical relations provided by Waddell allow for the comparison of girder weights per foot run ($w_1, w_2$) based on live loads ($p$) and spans ($l_1, l_2$). 

Additionally, a partially rational approximate formula for the weight of main girders ($w_3$) is expressed as $w_3 = (w_1 + w_2)l^2 / (Cds - l_2)$ or $w_3 = (w_1 + w_2)lr / (Cs - lr)$. In these equations:
* $w$ is the total live load per foot run of the girder.
* $w_2$ is the weight of the platform per foot run.
* $l$ is the span in feet.
* $s$ is the average stress on the gross section of metal.
* $d$ is the depth of the girder at the center.
* $r$ is the ratio of span to depth ($l/d$).
* $C$ is a constant specific to the girder type.

### Shear Analysis
To find the greatest shear when concentrated loads travel over a bridge, the loads are advanced from the left abutment. If $W_1$ is at section $C$ and $R$ is the resultant of the loads, the shear at $C$ is $Rn/l$. If the loads advance so that $W_2$ is at $C$, the shear becomes $R(n+a)/l - W_1$ (plus any reaction $d$ at $B$ due to additional loads).

## Physics and Density Measurements
In the context of determining absolute density ($\Delta$), $W$ represents the weight of a body in air at a specific temperature ($t^\circ$) and pressure ($p$ mm), while $W_1$ represents the weight in water. 

A highly accurate formula for absolute density $\Delta(0^\circ/4^\circ)$ is given as:
$\Delta(0^\circ/4^\circ) = (\rho\alpha W - \delta W_1) / (W - W_1)$
In this formula, $\rho$ is the density of the water, $\delta$ is the density of the air, and $\alpha$ is $(1 + \alpha t^\circ)$, where $\alpha$ is the coefficient of cubical expansion of the body. Less accurate versions of the formula include $\Delta = \rho W / (W - W_1)$ and $\Delta = W / (W - W_1)$.

In chemistry, regarding the depression of the freezing point (F.P.), $W$ is used to denote the weight of water and $w$ the weight of salt in a given volume of solution.
