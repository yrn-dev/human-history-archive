# Centre

The term "centre" is utilized across various disciplines, including engineering, cartography, physics, urban planning, and military strategy, to denote a middle point, a focal axis, or a central administrative or geographic location.

## Engineering and Infrastructure
In the construction of large-span bridges, the centre span often serves as the primary structural measurement. For example, the Ohio bridge on the Cincinnati & Covington railway, constructed in 1888 by the Phoenix Bridge Company, features a centre span of 550 ft. (measured centre to centre of piers), with a centre girder measuring 545 ft. in length and 84 ft. in depth between pin centres. Similarly, the Red Rock cantilever bridge over the Colorado river possesses a centre span of 660 ft., and the St Louis bridge over the Mississippi has a centre span of 520 ft. in the clear, with a centre arch rise of 47½ ft.

In the Lansdowne bridge at Sukkur, the span to the centres of the end uprights is 820 ft. The structure's back guys are subject to significant stress due to factors including the wind pressure, the rolling load on half the bridge, the weight of the cantilever, and the half weight of the centre girder. Additionally, the St Louis bridge employs steel double ribs where the vertical bracing between upper and lower members—spaced 12 ft. apart centre to centre—consolidates them into a single arch.

## Cartography and Projections
In map-making, the centre is the reference point from which distances and angles are calculated. In the Zenithal Equal-area Projection applied to Africa, a central point is selected on the equator. The spherical distance of any point from this centre is denoted as [theta], and the latitude and longitude are calculated with reference to that centre. In such systems, exaggeration of angle, area, or linear scale is a function of the distance from the centre only, regardless of the azimuth.

Under the General Theory of Conical Projections, parallels of latitude are circular arcs that share a centre at the point of divergence of the meridian lines. While some methods of development assume the pole is in the centre for simplicity, any point on the surface of a sphere may be designated as the centre.

## Physics and Instrumentation
In the context of magnetic needle readings, the centre of the vertical circle and the centre of the axle are critical for accuracy. Reading both ends of a needle helps avoid errors that occur if the prolongation of the axle does not pass through the centre of the vertical circle. Furthermore, reversals of the needle are performed to eliminate errors caused by the needle's centre of gravity being displaced from the centre of the axle, whether that displacement is parallel to the length of the needle or at a right angle to it.

## Urban and Political Centres
The term also describes the focal points of human settlement and governance. In Bristol, the business centre is located in a district where Corn Street, Wine Street, High Street, and Broad Street radiate from a centre situated close to the Floating Harbour.

On a geopolitical scale, the "centres of empire" shifted over time. The Roman system's centre lay far to the west of ancient centres such as the river valleys of Egypt and Mesopotamia. While this allowed Rome to incorporate Nordic Aryan-speaking peoples and Greek populations, the empire struggled to maintain control in Persia or Central Asia because those regions were too far from its administrative centres.

## Military Strategy
In military operations, the "centre" refers to the middle portion of a battle line. During the conflict in the West involving German and French armies, German forces attempted to drive back the French left and left centre. Simultaneously, the French centre struck forward on either side of Metz, targeting the German left and left centre in an attempt to rupture the German line.

## General Applications
The term is frequently associated with circular objects, often referred to as a "bull's eye." This includes the boss or protuberance left in the centre of a sheet of blown glass, the central division of a target, or a ring-shaped block with a hole through the centre for the passage of a rope.
