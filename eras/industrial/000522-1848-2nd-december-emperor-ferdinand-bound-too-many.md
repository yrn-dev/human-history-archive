# 1848

The year 1848 was marked by significant political upheaval and revolutionary activity across Europe, particularly within the Austrian Empire and France, leading to shifts in governance, leadership, and financial policy.

## Revolutions in the Austrian Empire
In the Austrian Empire, the revolution of 1848 in Vienna was the culmination of growing popular discontent. Prior to the unrest, the empire had been led by Emperor Ferdinand I, who had succeeded Francis I on March 2, 1835. Ferdinand I was personally amiable but suffered from epilepsy, which rendered him incapable of ruling and necessitated a veiled regency. This leadership vacuum, combined with divided counsels at the center, accentuated administrative vices and allowed political agitation to spread.

Resistance grew across various regions of the monarchy. In Hungary, the diet showed signs of vigorous life, while Slav populations became restive under German-Magyar domination. In Bohemia, the Czech literary movement evolved into an organized resistance to the established order, adopting the watchword "Repeal." Simultaneously, the "Illyrian" movement among southern Slavs sought a revival of the "Triune Kingdom" (comprising Dalmatia, Croatia, and Slavonia) independent of the Hungarian crown. In the German provinces, national movements and advanced revolutionary views gained traction despite the efforts of Metternich's police and censors.

## The Fall of Vienna and Political Transition
The fall of revolutionary Vienna had a cascading effect, practically involving the fall of the revolutions in Pest and Frankfort. The victory of the imperial forces was reinforced by commendations from Emperor Nicholas of Russia and the victorious army of Radetzky in Italy. The military execution of Robert Blum—a deputy of the German parliament whose person should have been sacrosanct—served as a global signal of the victory's moral implications.

Following these events, a new ministry was formed that aligned with imperial sentiment and the traditions of the Habsburg monarchy, while remaining inimical to the German ideals of Frankfort. This ministry was led by Prince Felix Schwarzenberg, described as an unscrupulous and strong "army-diplomat." On November 27, a proclamation was issued stating that maintaining Austria as a united state was necessary for both Europe and Germany.

## Accession of Francis Joseph
On December 2, 1848, Emperor Ferdinand I abdicated the throne. He was considered an ineffective instrument for the new policy due to too many personal obligations to revolutionary parties. He was succeeded by his nephew, Francis Joseph.

The proclamation of the new emperor acted as a challenge to German unionists and Magyars. The text stated that the crown was determined to preserve its lustre but was prepared to share rights with the representatives of its peoples, expressing trust that, with the aid of God, the monarchy could unite all its races and countries into one great body politic.

## Events in France
In France, the revolution of 1848 also led to significant political changes. On February 24, 1848, Lazare Hippolyte Carnot pronounced in favor of the republic and was subsequently chosen by Lamartine as the minister of education in the provisional government. Carnot attempted to organize primary school systems, proposing laws for free and obligatory primary instruction and secondary education for girls. However, his opposition to purely secular schools—arguing that the schoolmaster and the minister were the two columns of the republic—led to his resignation on July 5, 1848.

Additionally, Louis Lucien Bonaparte, the son of Lucien Bonaparte, traveled to France for the first time in 1848. Following the revolution, he was elected as a deputy for Corsica on November 28, 1848.

## Financial Measures of the Bank of France
The instability of 1848 was reflected in the financial operations of the Bank of France, specifically regarding its note circulation. The limits of this circulation were extended twice during the year: first on March 15, 1848, to 350 million francs (approximately £14,000,000), and again on April 27 and May 2, 1848, to 452 million francs (approximately £18,000,000).
