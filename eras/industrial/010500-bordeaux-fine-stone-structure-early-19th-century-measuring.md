# Bordeaux

Bordeaux is one of the most extensive and finest cities in France, located in south-western France. It serves as the capital of the department of Gironde. The city is situated on the west (or left) bank of the Garonne river, approximately 60 miles from the sea, within a plain that includes the Medoc wine-growing district.

## Geography and Infrastructure
The Garonne river forms a semicircle at this location, which separates the city proper on the left bank from La Bastide, an important suburb on the right bank. The city is connected to La Bastide by two bridges: a railway bridge and the Pont de Bordeaux. The Pont de Bordeaux is a fine stone structure dating from the early 19th century and measures 1534 feet in length.

The city is accessible by rail via the Orleans railway (359 miles south-southwest of Paris) and the Southern railway (159 miles northwest of Toulouse). As of 1906, the population of Bordeaux was 237,707.

## City Layout and Architecture
The urban landscape of Bordeaux is characterized by a crescent of busy, wide quays. Looking west from the Pont de Bordeaux, one can see lofty mansions, factories, and warehouses, with towers and steeples rising behind them. 

At the center of these quays is the Place des Quinconces. This area is planted with plane trees and features two huge statues of Montesquieu and Montaigne. The square terminates at the quays with two rostral columns that function as lighthouses. Additionally, the west side of the square contains a monument dedicated to the Girondin deputies who were proscribed in 1793 under the convention.

From the south-west corner of the Place des Quinconces, the city opens into the Place de la Comedie, which houses the Grand Theatre, a structure from the 18th century. The city center is composed of narrow, winding streets where urban life is concentrated. Outside this central quarter, the streets are quiet and narrow, lined with low white houses, which differ from the high tenements typically found in other large French towns. The entire city is encircled by a semicircle of boulevards, beyond which are the suburbs of Begles, Talence, Merignac, Cauderan, and Le Bouscat. The city's primary promenades include the Place des Quinconces, the allees de Tourny, and the beautiful public garden.

## History
Bordeaux has a history marked by religious and political volatility. The reformed religion attracted many adherents to the city; following the massacre of St Bartholomew, nearly 300 inhabitants were killed. The 17th century was further characterized by disturbance, as the city served as the chief support for the Fronde. In 1675 and 1653, royal measures led to insurrections that required the deployment of troops for repression.

The middle of the 18th century was a period of architectural and commercial activity. During this time, the marquis de Tourny, the intendant of Guienne, improved the city by laying out public squares and widening the streets.

During the Revolution, Bordeaux served as the headquarters for the Girondists. Consequently, the city suffered almost as severely as Marseilles and Lyons during the Reign of Terror. Under the rule of Napoleon I, the city's commerce was greatly reduced. In 1814, Bordeaux declared for the house of Bourbon. Subsequently, Louis XVIII granted the title of duc de Bordeaux to his grand-nephew, who is better known as the comte de Chambord. In 1870, as Germans approached Tours, the French government was transferred to Bordeaux.
