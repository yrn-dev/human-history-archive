# The Anatolian Railway and the Bagdad Railway Scheme

The Anatolian Railway and the broader Euphrates Valley (or Bagdad) railway scheme were significant infrastructure projects in Asia Minor during the late 19th and early 20th centuries, involving complex international financial arrangements and geopolitical competition.

## The Bagdad Railway Scheme
The proposal for the Euphrates Valley railway was brought forward prominently in 1899. While the Russian government initially proposed to undertake the project, these proposals were rejected. Subsequent British proposals were opposed by the Germans, who claimed preference because they already controlled the line to Konia in Asia Minor. 

Following this, the Porte granted a provisional convention to a German company, and an irade was obtained in 1902. By 1903, discussions emerged regarding placing the line under international control. This prospect was of particular interest to England, as the line would have provided a short route to India when connected with fast steamship services in the Persian Gulf and the Mediterranean. However, the British government ultimately decided that the proposals for international control did not offer sufficient security.

## Financial Arrangements
The final financial agreement for the capital of the line was divided among several international parties:
* **German financiers:** 40%
* **French (via the Imperial Ottoman Bank):** 30%
* **Austrian, Swiss, Italian, and Turkish:** 20%
* **Anatolian Railway Company:** 10%

## Construction and Expansion
The line was completed from Konia through Eregli to Bulgurli in 1904. Between 1904 and 1905, the first two sections of the Bagdad railway, totaling 117 miles, were constructed to Eregli and Karaman. In 1908, an irade sanctioned further extension across the Taurus to Adana, and continuing to Helif near Mardin, covering a distance of 522 miles.

## Impact on Konia
The advent of the Anatolian railway around 1895 began to restore prosperity to Konia. Prior to this, the city had decayed following the breakup of the Seljuk state and the rise of Ottoman sovereignty. By 1882, Konia had become impoverished; its population had dwindled, and its palace and walls had been largely pulled down for building materials, leaving the city resembling a "city of the dead." The railway's arrival, alongside the provision of a good supply of drinking water by Ferid Pasha (who governed the vilayet before becoming Grand Vizier in 1903), helped revitalize the city. While some ruinous sacred buildings remained, others were patched up, and the remaining fragments of the city walls were wholly removed.
