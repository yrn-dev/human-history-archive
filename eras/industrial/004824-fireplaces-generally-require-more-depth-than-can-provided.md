# Fireplaces and Chimney Construction

The construction of fireplaces and their associated ventilation systems is a specialized aspect of the bricklayer's art, requiring precision and skill to ensure the efficient removal of smoke and the prevention of down-draughts.

## Structural Requirements and the Chimney Breast
Because fireplaces generally require more depth than can be provided by the thickness of a standard wall, they necessitate a projection known as the "chimney breast" to contain both the fireplace and the flues. In certain instances, particularly when dealing with an external wall, this projection may be constructed on the back.

## Flue Design and Engineering
Every fireplace must be equipped with its own separate flue. To maximize economy and efficiency, multiple flues are often collected into a single "stack." This arrangement is beneficial because the heat from one flue assists the up-draught in the flues adjoining it. From an aesthetic perspective, stacks are preferred over multiple single flue chimneys, which are considered unsightly when protruding from various parts of a roof. Architects of the Elizabethan period and later periods were noted for their ability to treat these stacks as architectural features.

The internal construction of the flue requires precision to ensure its capacity remains consistent throughout. To minimize resistance to the up-draught, bends must be designed as gradual curves. Additionally, each flue should incorporate at least one bend of no less than 60° to intercept down-draughts. To prevent smoky chimneys, individual flues must be separated by smoke-proof divisions, or "withes," which are typically half a brick in thickness.

Standard dimensions for flues vary by use:
*   **Ordinary grates:** 14×9 inches.
*   **Kitchen stoves:** 14×14 inches.

The outer wall of a chimney stack may be made 9 inches thick. While pargetting is used, fireclay tubes (available in circular or rectangular transverse sections) are frequently employed due to their superior durability and cleanliness, despite being more expensive.

## Installation and Environmental Factors
To prevent smoke, the chimney shaft should be carried well above the roofline and, if possible, higher than adjacent buildings, as nearby structures can cause down-draughts. In cases where this height is unattainable, revolving cowls or patent chimney-pots are used.

In specific architectural settings, such as modern British barracks, fireplaces are typically positioned at the opposite end of the room from the door. In these environments, the chimney flue is used to warm an extract shaft, which helps maintain air circulation within the room.

## Integration with Flooring
The installation of fireplaces and flues often impacts the efficiency of single flooring. Single flooring consists of wood joists (ranging from 2 to 4 inches thick and 5 to 11 inches deep) resting on walls or partitions. Because it is often impossible to get a bearing on the wall when fireplaces and flues are present, a process called "trimming" is used. This involves supporting the end of a joist by tenoning it into a "trimming joist" (the timber morticed for the tenon), while the intermediate timbers are referred to as "trimmed joists."

## Fuel Characteristics
When wood is used as fuel in open fireplaces, the first heating produces volatile products that release an acrid, penetrating odour into the apartment, though this is generally regarded as agreeable.
