# William Booth and the Salvation Army

## Early Life and Religious Development
William Booth, the founder and "general" of the Salvation Army, was born in Nottingham on April 10, 1829. At fifteen years of age, he experienced a strong religious turn under the influence of the Wesleyan Methodists, eventually becoming a local preacher within that body. In 1849, Booth moved to London. According to his own account, his passion for preaching in the open air led to his severance from the Wesleyans. 

He subsequently joined the Methodist New Connexion and was ordained as a minister. However, he left this organization in 1861 because he was not employed in the active "travelling evangelization" he desired. During this period, in 1855, he married Catherine Mumford; the couple had four children and both engaged in preaching, first in Cornwall and later in Walsall and Cardiff.

## Foundation of the Salvation Army
While in Walsall, Booth first organized a "Hallelujah band," consisting of others and converted criminals who testified publicly about their conversion. In 1864, Booth returned to London, where he continued his services in the open air and in tents. He founded a body that underwent several name changes, known successively as the East London Revival Society, the East London Christian Mission, and the Christian Mission, before becoming the Salvation Army in 1878.

The Salvation Army is a quasi-military organization, and Booth based its "Orders and Regulations" on those of the British army. The organization operates through several specific methods:
* Holding processions and outdoor meetings.
* Visiting private houses, prisons, and public-houses.
* Conducting meetings in factories, theatres, and other unusual buildings.
* Utilizing the language of everyday life and popular song-tunes.
* Encouraging every convert to act as a daily witness for Christ in both private and public settings.

## Public Reception and Expansion
The early "campaigns" of the Salvation Army met with violent opposition. A "Skeleton Army" was formed specifically to break up meetings, and for many years, the followers of Booth were subjected to imprisonment and fines for breaking the peace. These disorders became rare after 1889.

The work of the Army expanded internationally, reaching the United States in 1880 and Australia in 1881, eventually spreading to India, Ceylon, the European continent, and other regions. William Booth served as an indefatigable speaker, organizer, and traveller to support this growth.

## Social Reform and Later Recognition
In 1890, "General" Booth published a work titled *In Darkest England, and the Way Out*. In this text, he proposed ten expedients to remedy vice and pauperism:
1. The city colony
2. The farm colony
3. The over-sea colony
4. The household salvage brigade
5. Rescue homes for fallen women
6. Deliverance for the drunkard
7. The prison-gate brigade
8. The poor man's bank
9. The poor man's lawyer
10. Whitechapel-by-the-Sea

A large portion of this scheme was implemented thanks to liberal subscriptions of money. Although Booth's work was received with ridicule and opposition for many years, this shifted toward the end of the 19th century to widespread sympathy as the results of his genius were realized.

The complete change in public perception was marked by the active encouragement of King Edward VII. At the King's instance, Booth was officially invited to attend the coronation ceremony in 1902. By 1905, when the "general" went on a progress through England, he was received in state by the corporations and mayors of many towns. His work also received cordial encouragement from authorities in the United States and elsewhere.
