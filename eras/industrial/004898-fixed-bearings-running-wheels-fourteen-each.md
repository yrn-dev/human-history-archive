# Runcorn Bridge Trolley and Related Mechanical Systems

The Runcorn bridge is a cable suspension structure featuring stiffening girders that spans the Mersey and the Manchester Ship Canal. The bridge consists of a single 1000 ft. span, with one span on one side and four approach spans of 55½ ft. on the other. Its low-level approach roadways are 35 ft. wide, flanked by 6 ft. wide footpaths on each side.

## The Bridge Car and Propulsion
A car is suspended from the bridge and is propelled electrically from one side to the other. This car measures 55 ft. in length and 24½ ft. in width, and it is carried by a trolley that runs on the underside of the stiffening girders, which are positioned 82 ft. above the river. The driver controls the electric motors from a cabin located on the car.

The propulsion system utilizes two steel-clad series-wound motors, each providing 36 B.H.P. For a test load of 120 tons, the system generates a tractive force of 70 lb per ton, which is sufficient for maintaining speed against wind pressure and for acceleration. Power is supplied by two gas engines, each of 75 B.H.P., one of which serves as a spare.

## Trolley Construction and Wheels
The trolley consists of an articulated frame that is 77 ft. long, composed of five sections coupled together with pins. Fixed to this frame are the bearings of the running wheels, with fourteen wheels located on each side. The system is equipped with magnetic brakes and auxiliary handbrakes.

On the opening day of the bridge, the system transported vehicles and passengers at a rate exceeding 2000 people per hour. The total time required to cross the bridge is 3 or 4 minutes. The total cost for the structure was £133,000.

## General Principles of Bearings and Bushes
In broader mechanical contexts, bearings are used to facilitate rotation. Ball bearings are universally employed, consisting of rows of balls running between two hardened steel ball-races: the "cup" (stationary member) and the "cone" (rotating member). One of the four ball-races is axially adjustable to ensure the bearing runs without shake.

To prevent surfaces of the same metal from seizing and abrading each other, "bushes" are frequently inserted into machinery bearings. A bush is a lining made of a dissimilar metal. For example, a shaft made of wrought iron or mild steel may run in a bush made of bronze, cast iron, hard cast steel, or Babbitt metal. Babbitt metal is particularly noted for its low melting point, allowing it to be cast directly about the shaft.

## Application in Bicycles
The modern bicycle utilizes these mechanical principles in its frame and drive system. The steering wheel is mounted in a fork, and the steering tube is mounted by ball bearings in the socket tube. 

In the rear wheel hub, the spindle has two cones screwed onto it—one against a shoulder and one that is adjustable. The hub-shell is made of mild steel, and the cups are forced into the ends of the shell and soldered. A thin internal tube guides oil to the balls, while a thin washer retains the oil.

The drive system may include a free-wheel clutch, which allows the pedals to remain at rest while the bicycle continues to run. There are three main types of free-wheel clutches: friction cone, ratchet, and roller (the latter having fallen into disfavour). The friction cone type is often combined with a brake inside the hub, known as a coaster hub. Some bicycles also utilize two-speed gears, such as the Sunbeam gear at the crank-axle or the "Hub" gear, which uses a planet carrier and an annular wheel to alter gear ratios.
