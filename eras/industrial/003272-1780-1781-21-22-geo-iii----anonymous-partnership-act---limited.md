# Anonymous Partnership Act of 1780-1781

The Anonymous Partnership Act was a piece of legislation enacted during the reign of George III, specifically identified as 21 and 22 Geo. III. This act established specific legal frameworks for partnerships, introducing the concept of limited liability.

## Provisions and Limitations
Under the terms of the Anonymous Partnership Act of 1780-1781, limited liability was permitted, provided that it did not exceed the sum of £50,000. However, the act contained a specific exclusion regarding the nature of the businesses that could utilize these provisions: the "business of banking or discounters of money" was expressly excluded from the act.

## Context of Partnership Legislation
The Anonymous Partnership Act followed earlier legislative efforts to regulate partnerships in England. In 1741 (15 Geo. II), partnerships were authorized for the purposes of manufacture and trade. Those earlier partnerships were subject to stricter limitations than the 1780-1781 act, as they were restricted to a maximum of nine members and a capital stock that could not exceed £10,000 at any time.

## Related Banking Restrictions
The exclusion of banking from the Anonymous Partnership Act aligned with a broader history of restrictive banking laws in England. The Bank of England, incorporated in 1694, operated under a framework where subsequent acts in 1697 and 1708 prohibited the establishment of other joint-stock banks within England. The 1708 act specifically forbade any partnership exceeding six persons from borrowing or taking up money on bills or notes payable on demand, or at any time less than six months from the borrowing. 

These restrictions remained largely unchanged until the act of 1826 and a second Banking Act of the same year, the latter of which finally allowed co-partnerships of more than six persons (joint-stock companies) to be established, provided they were located beyond 65 miles from London.

## Other Financial Regulations of the Era
During the mid-to-late 18th century, other laws targeted the financial conduct of bankers. An act from 1759 (33 Geo. II) restricted bankers from making marriage settlements on grandchildren, children, or sons and daughters that would be valid against creditors, regardless of whether the settlement was for valuable consideration or if the creditors existed at the time of the grant. Furthermore, after May 10, 1760, any dispositions of leasehold or real interest by bankers to children were rendered void against creditors. Additionally, bankers were prohibited from issuing receipts or notes that bore interest after the same date in 1760.
