# Trade and Economic Shifts in the Mid-19th Century

The mid-19th century was marked by significant fluctuations in international trade, influenced by geopolitical conflicts, administrative restructuring, and infrastructure development. These changes are evident in the economic records of various regions, ranging from the Caribbean to Asia Minor and Siberia.

## The Bahamas and Blockade-Running
The economic history of the Bahamas during this period was heavily impacted by external political events. In 1784 and 1786, parliament voted sums to indemnify the descendants of the old lords proprietors, and the islands were formally reconveyed to the crown. While the Bahamas initially showed some progress, the finances were unfavourably affected in 1848 by the separation of the Turks and Caicos Islands, which had previously been the most productive salt-producing islands. Additionally, the abolition of the slave-trade in 1834 likely influenced the fortunes of landed proprietors.

A major shift occurred in 1861 when the Federals closed the southern ports of America, leading to the rise of the blockade-running trade. This trade reached its peak at the start of 1865; in January and February of that year alone, 20 steamers arrived at Nassau, importing 14,182 bales of cotton valued at L554,675.

The impact of this activity is highlighted by comparing trade figures from 1860 (before the closing of the southern ports) and 1864. In 1860, imports were L234,029 and exports were L157,350. By 1864, these figures had risen dramatically, with imports reaching L5,346,112 and exports totaling L4,672,398. Despite the extravagance and excitement in Nassau, the Bahamas as a whole likely benefited little. While the government paid off a debt of L43,786, the period saw an increase in crime and the prevalence of sickness. Once the trade ceased, the steamers and crews departed within a few months, and New Providence returned to a state of quietude.

## Administrative and Infrastructure Developments
In other parts of the world, the mid-19th century saw structural changes to governance and transport. In 1864, the vilayet of Konia was formed in Asia Minor. This was achieved by adding the western half of Adana and part of south-eastern Anadoli to the old eyalet of Karamania. The resulting vilayet, which includes parts of Phrygia, Lycaonia, Cappadocia, Cilicia, and Pamphylia, is divided into five sanjaks: Nigdeh, Hamid-abad, Buldur, Adalia, and Konia. The region's economy is primarily pastoral and agricultural, with industries focused on the manufacture of silk and cotton stuffs and carpet-weaving. Its principal exports include minerals, salt, wool, cereals, cotton, livestock, and opium, while imports consist of petroleum, rice, coffee, and cloth-goods.

In Siberia, infrastructure improvements were made to facilitate movement around the southern shore of a lake. Between 1863 and 1864, a highway was constructed, a process that required blasting cliffs. This route was later followed by the trans-Siberian railway after 1905.

## Statistical Considerations in Trade
The recording of imports and exports is subject to significant statistical difficulties. The "balance of trade" can be a pitfall for the unwary because published returns often provide estimated values rather than actual international purchase and sale figures. For example, a recorded sale may fail if the debtor does not pay. 

Furthermore, different nations employ different valuation methods. The United Kingdom values imports at the port of arrival and exports at the port of departure. In contrast, the United States values imports at the port of shipment rather than the port of arrival (which would include freight and other charges). These differing methodologies mean that conclusions drawn from import and export records regarding excesses at different times are difficult to determine.
