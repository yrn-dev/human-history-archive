# Water-Tubes in Boiler Construction

In the engineering of boilers, the materials and methods of installation vary depending on the type of boiler and its intended application. Water-tubes, specifically those used in naval and locomotive contexts, are characterized by their material composition and the manner in which they are secured to the boiler structure.

## Materials of Boiler Tubes
The materials used for boiler tubes differ based on the boiler's design. In ordinary marine and land boilers, tubes are typically constructed from lap-welded wrought iron. However, in water-tube boilers designed for naval purposes, seamless steel tubes are invariably employed. 

Locomotive boilers utilize a variety of materials for their components. While the shells are generally mild steel, the tubes are usually made of brass, consisting of either 70% copper and 30% zinc or a weight ratio of two parts copper to one part zinc. In some instances, copper or steel tubes are used. Specifically, when water-tubes are incorporated into locomotive boilers, they are made of seamless steel.

## Application in Locomotive Boilers
In certain locomotive boilers, water-tubes are fitted to extend across the fire-box. An example is found in a boiler used by the London & South-Western Railway Co., which features a total heating surface of 2727 sq. ft. and a grate area of 31.5 sq. ft. This specific boiler contains 112 seamless steel water-tubes, each with a diameter of 2-3/4 in. 

These tubes are organized into two clusters of 56 tubes each; one cluster is positioned above the fire-bridge, while the other is located closer to the fire-door end of the boiler. To secure them, the seamless steel water-tubes are expanded into the fire-box side plates. Additionally, the outer shell side plates are supported by stay bars that pass directly through these water-tubes.

## Naval Water-Tube Boiler Designs
Various naval boiler designs utilize seamless steel for their tubing, though the method of attachment varies by model.

### The Belleville Boiler
The Belleville boiler, an early form used by the British navy, consists of a boiler proper and an economizer. Both sections use elements composed of straight seamless steel tubes. In the boiler proper, these tubes generally have an external diameter of 4-1/2 in., with thicknesses varying by row: the two lower rows are 3/8 in. thick, the next two are 5/16 in., and the remaining tubes are approximately 1/5 in. thick. The economizer uses similar construction but with smaller, shorter tubes, generally 2-3/4 in. in diameter. These tubes are connected to junction-boxes of malleable cast iron via screwed joints.

### The Niclausse and Durr Boilers
The Niclausse boiler, used in the British and other navies as well as in electric-light installations, employs seamless steel tubes of a peculiar construction. These tubes are fitted into headers; the front ends are thickened by being upset and turned to gauge, while the back ends are reduced in diameter, screwed, and closed with cap-nuts. 

The Durr boiler, used in the German and British navies, also utilizes tubes secured to a water-chamber. Unlike the Niclausse, these tubes are secured to the inner plate only by conical joints and are forced into position using a small portable hydraulic jack.

## Comparison with Other Tubing Methods
While many water-tubes are expanded into plates, other designs prioritize ease of replacement. In the Reed boiler, tubes are not expanded into the tube plate; instead, they feature specially designed screw and nut connections at each end to allow for quick removal and replacement. Similarly, stay tubes in other contexts are expanded into plates and then beaded over, though they are also screwed at both ends.
