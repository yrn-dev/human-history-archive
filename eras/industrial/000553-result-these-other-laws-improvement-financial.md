# Financial Improvements and Legislative Reform

Throughout history, various governments have enacted laws and policies intended to stabilize financial conditions, reform currencies, and protect the economic well-being of their populations. These efforts have ranged from the restructuring of national banks and currency standards to the implementation of social protections and the regulation of debt.

## Currency and Banking Reform in Austria
In Austria, the enactment of specific laws led to an improvement in financial conditions, which allowed the government to address the long-delayed reform of its currency. Prior to these reforms, the currency was split between paper money issued by the Austro-Hungarian Bank and silver (gulden), known as the "Austrian currency" introduced in 1857. 

The Austro-Hungarian Bank had originally been entirely Austrian in 1867, though it maintained branches in Hungary. Due to a lack of credit to establish its own national bank, Hungary agreed to a settlement in 1877 to convert the institution into an Austro-Hungarian bank. This new arrangement divided management between the two countries and established head offices in both Vienna and Pest; this structure was later renewed in 1887.

Financial instability had previously forced the government to authorize the suspension of cash payments in 1848, and subsequent wars in 1859 and 1866 prevented the renewal of these payments, resulting in an inconvertible paper currency. Furthermore, during the war of 1866, the government violated the bank's charter by issuing its own notes (_Staatsnoten_) valued at 312 million gulden to raise funds. Consequently, an operation begun in 1892 sought to achieve three goals: the redemption of the _Staatsnoten_, the resumption of cash payments by the bank, and the substitution of a gold standard for the silver standard.

## Financial Institutions in France
France experienced a series of attempts to establish a bank of issue. The _Banque Generale_ was founded in 1716 by John Law, the author of the _Systeme_ and the _Mississippi Scheme_. This institution became the _Banque Royale_ in 1718 with notes guaranteed by King Louis XV, but it ended in 1721. A subsequent attempt at reconstruction in 1767 was suppressed in 1793.

Following a period of financial difficulties that prevented the immediate establishment of a bank in 1796, Napoleon I and his minister Mollien founded the Bank of France in 1800. The bank was created to supply loanable capital at a moderate charge and to support French industry and trade. While departmental banks with the privilege of issue existed under a 1803 law—with nine such banks remaining by the end of 1847—they were absorbed into the Bank of France in 1848. The Bank of France then held an exclusive privilege of issue, later taking over the Bank of Savoy in 1863.

## State Debt and Industrial Policy in the United States
In one instance of unfortunate state financial and industrial policy, a state legislature chartered a bank in 1819 to do business on state credit because money was scarce. This bank failed within a few years, leading the state to borrow money in 1831 to redeem depreciated notes. A second state bank chartered in 1835 suspended payment two years later and was liquidated in 1843.

Additionally, the state attempted a system of internal improvements, including a 1836 loan for the Illinois and Michigan canal and a 1837 appropriation of $10,000,000 for railroads and other improvements. This experiment failed, resulting in a heavy debt and a decline in state credit, leading to the abandonment of the policy in 1840. Eventually, through the efforts of Governor Thomas Ford (1800-1850), a movement to repudiate the debt was defeated. A plan was adopted to reduce the debt without excessive taxation, and by 1880, the debt was practically extinguished.

## Social and Legal Financial Protections
Legislative efforts have also targeted the financial condition of the poor and victims of crime. In ancient Greece, Solon enacted an amendment to the law of debtor and creditor and prohibited contracts based on the security of the body. This prohibition produced a vast improvement in the condition and character of the poorer population.

In Austria, the influence of those adopting principles of Christian Socialism, such as Baron von Vogelfang and Rudolf Mayer, led to the introduction of a law limiting interest rates. These figures, including Prince Aloys v. Liechtenstein, cooperated with the government to improve the material conditions of the people, which had been neglected during the period of Liberal government.

In the United States, the President's Task Force on Victims of Crime reported in December 1982 that victims' financial, emotional, and personal wounds had gone unattended. This led to a series of laws to improve the treatment of victims, including the Victim and Witness Protection Act of 1982, the Victims of Crime Act of 1984, the Victims' Rights and Restitution Act of 1990 (VRRA), the Violent Crime Control and Law Enforcement Act of 1994, the Antiterrorism and Effective Death Penalty Act of 1996, the Victim Rights Clarification Act of 1997, and the Justice for All Act of 2004. The latter included the CVRA (enacted October 30, 2004), which aimed to make victims full participants in the criminal justice system.
