# Oil Separators

In the operation of marine boilers, steam from engines is frequently condensed and reused as feed-water. This process often introduces difficulties due to oil that passes over with the steam. To manage this, oil separators and filtration methods are employed to maintain the efficiency of the boiler.

## The Problem of Oil Emulsion
While feed-filters are used to stop coarser particles of oil from entering the system, some oil becomes "emulsified." In this state, the oil is suspended in the water in extremely minute particles that are small enough to pass through even the finest filtering materials. 

When this emulsified water reaches the boiler and the water evaporates, the oil is left behind as a thin film on the heating surfaces. This film is problematic because it prevents the water from making actual contact with the plates, which can cause serious operational trouble.

## Methods of Separation
To overcome the difficulty of oil emulsion, a small quantity of lime solution is uniformly mixed with the water. As the temperature of the water rises, the lime precipitates. These precipitated minute particles appear to attract the small globules of oil, causing them to aggregate into sizes large enough to deposit themselves in the quiet parts of the boiler. Once aggregated, these deposits can be removed periodically through cleaning or by blowing them out. Despite this method, the archive indicates that further work is required before the difficulty of oil in these systems is thoroughly removed.

## Related Marine Boiler Systems
Oil separation is part of a larger cycle of water management in marine boilers. To compensate for water lost through engine use, condenser condensation, and leakages in the evaporation cycle, "evaporators" are used to distill fresh water from sea-water. 

These evaporators operate on a consistent principle: they are fed with sea-water, and the heat necessary for evaporation is provided by live steam from the boilers, which is admitted into copper pipe coils. The steam evaporated from the sea-water is directed into the condenser or the low-pressure receiver of the steam-engine, while the water condensed in the copper coils is returned directly to the feed-water. Brine produced during this process is removed via blow-out means.

## Lubrication and Oil Types
The presence of oil in steam systems is often a result of the lubrication required for cylinders and valves. In these components, lubricants must possess high viscosity at working temperatures and must not evaporate appreciably. It is critical that the lubricant does not decompose and liberate fatty acids, as this would choke steam passages with metallic soaps and corrode the metal. 

For these reasons, pure mineral oils of the highest viscosity are recommended for steam cylinders and valves, mixed with only the minimum amount of fixed oil necessary for efficient lubrication. In specific cases, such as marine engines operating in hot locations, "blown" oil (also known as "soluble castor oil") may be used; this is a mixture of mineral oil and rape or other vegetable oils that has been artificially thickened by blowing air through the heated oil.
