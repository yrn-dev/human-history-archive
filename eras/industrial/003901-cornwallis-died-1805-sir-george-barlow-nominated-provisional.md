# Sir George Barlow and the Transition of Power in India

## Early Career and Administrative Work
Sir George Hilaro Barlow (1762–1847) was an Anglo-Indian statesman who began his professional service in 1778 upon his appointment to the Bengal Civil Service. During his tenure, he played a significant role in the administration of the region, most notably in 1788 when he carried into execution the permanent settlement of Bengal. In 1803, he was created a baronet.

## Provisional Governorship and Economy
The political leadership of British India shifted in 1805 following the death of the Marquess of Cornwallis. Upon Cornwallis's passing, Sir George Barlow was nominated as the provisional governor-general. Barlow's tenure in this capacity was characterized by a strong passion for retrenchment and economy. This approach to administration led to a unique historical distinction: he is known as the only governor-general to have diminished the area of British territory.

Despite his nomination, the home government rejected his appointment, and Lord Minto was appointed to the position instead.

## Governorship of Madras and Later Life
Following his time as provisional governor-general, Barlow was appointed as the governor of Madras. His administration there was marked by a lack of tact, which resulted in a mutiny of officers in 1809. This event was noted for its similarity to a previous mutiny that had occurred under Clive.

Due to these developments, Barlow was recalled in 1812. He spent the remainder of his life in retirement until his death in February 1847.

## The Marquess of Cornwallis
The transition of power in 1805 was precipitated by the death of Charles Cornwallis, the 1st Marquess (1738–1805). The eldest son of the 1st Earl of Cornwallis, he was educated at Eton and Clare College, Cambridge, before entering the army. His career was marked by various high-ranking roles, including serving as governor of the Tower in 1770 and as a member of parliament for Eye.

Cornwallis is noted for his independence of character and inflexible integrity, often voting without regard to party. During the American War of Independence, he served as a major-general and commanded British forces in South Carolina starting in 1880, where he routed Gates at Camden. In 1781, he defeated Greene at Guilford Court House and conducted a destructive raid into Virginia. However, he was forced to capitulate on October 19, 1781, after being besieged at Yorktown by a French fleet and French and American armies.

Despite the fall of the English cause in the United States, Cornwallis escaped censure. In 1786, he was appointed commander-in-chief in Bengal and governor-general of India. His administration was focused on projecting reforms, though he was interrupted by conflict with Tippoo Sahib. Cornwallis personally conducted the war in 1791, capturing Bangalore, and in 1792, he besieged Seringapatam. This resulted in a treaty that stripped Tippoo Sahib of half his realm and required his two sons to be held as hostages by the English. Cornwallis returned to England in 1793, where he was granted a marquessate, a seat in the privy council, and the position of master-general of the ordnance with a place in the Cabinet.
