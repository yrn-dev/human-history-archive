# Banks of Issue and the History of Banking

The history of banking in leading nations and the United States is characterized by a progression from crude colonial experiments to the establishment of complex systems of note issue and commercial regulation. This evolution involved various attempts to secure circulating currency and manage the stability of financial institutions.

## Banking in the United States

Early banking in the American colonies consisted largely of projects for issuing paper money rather than the creation of commercial banks. Speculative banking was significantly curtailed by the Bubble Act (6 Geo. I. c. 18), an English law passed after the South Sea Bubble burst, which prohibited the formation of banking companies without a special charter; this act was extended to the colonies in 1740.

The serious history of American banking began with the Bank of Pennsylvania. This institution originated from a project by Philadelphia citizens to provide rations for the continental army. In 1780, the bank issued its first bills, which were interest-bearing notes payable at a future date. Shareholders' advances in continental money were secured by bills of exchange for £150,000 drawn on American envoys in Europe, though these were not intended for negotiation.

## New York Banking Systems

As New York became the chief commercial state of the Union, its city banks were generally sound, though they experimented with different systems to secure circulating notes:

*   **The Safety-Fund System:** Inaugurated in 1829, this system required each bank to contribute to a fund intended to cover the deficits of any contributing bank that failed with insufficient assets to meet its liabilities. While the act intended the fund to protect only bank-notes, it was used to pay all liabilities of failed banks. Consequently, the fund was exhausted following important failures during the panics of 1837 and 1857.
*   **The Free-Banking System:** Established by the act of 1838, this system allowed any group of persons who met legal requirements to form a bank and issue circulation secured by depositing various classes of public bonds. This model was imitated in other states and served as a partial model for the national banking system.

Prior to 1843, the state did not control the issue of notes, leading to several instances of illegal over-issues.

## Regional and National Developments in the U.S.

In other states, Indiana and Ohio developed successful state banks modeled on the European central bank plan. These institutions held exclusive charters for issuing notes within their states and maintained branches at important locations. Under the management of Hugh McCulloch, the bank of Indiana avoided suspending specie payments during the 1857 crisis and retired its circulation in 1862 when gold reached a premium.

Later financial instability occurred due to silver legislation enacted by Congress in 1878 and 1890, which led banks to discriminate against silver certificates and silver dollars. During the panic of 1893, leading bankers from Chicago, Baltimore, Philadelphia, Boston, and New York provided holdings to replenish government gold reserves. The panic of 1893 saw approximately 150 national banks suspend, though 84 eventually resumed business. Between June 1, 1893, and June 1, 1899, the number of banks decreased from 3,856 to 3,585, and aggregate capital fell from $698,454,665 to $610,028,895.

## European Banks of Issue

### The Bank of Amsterdam
Established January 31, 1609, the Amsterdamsche Wisselbank (Exchange Bank) was created to support the international commerce of Holland. It allowed deposits of money or bullion, which could be withdrawn at pleasure. By ordinance, all bills of 600 gulden (£50) or more—a limit lowered to 300 gulden (£25) in 1643—had to be paid through the bank via the transfer of credits, known as "bank money." The bank had no capital of its own and was intended to hold the full amount of specie for all outstanding bank money, though this was not strictly observed. After making loans to the Dutch East India Company and becoming a creditor to the city of Amsterdam, the bank ceased to exist in 1820.

### The Bank of England
A significant shift in the Bank of England occurred with the act of 1844, which divided the bank into two departments: one for the transaction of ordinary banking business and one for the issue of notes. Bullion was transferred to the issue department. Note issuance was restricted to £14,000,000 upon securities, with the remainder issued upon bullion and adjusted based on bullion stock fluctuations. The act also mandated weekly returns; previously, only a quarterly balance-sheet had been published monthly. The first weekly return was issued on September 7, 1844, separating the balance-sheets of the Banking and Issue Departments.
