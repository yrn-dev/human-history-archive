# Bank of England Notes

The notes of the Bank of England are produced within the bank using surface-plates. These notes are printed in black and do not feature color or special protection, with the exception of a water-mark embedded in the paper. To maintain the quality and security of the currency, the bank never reissues notes once they have been returned. Consequently, the average life of a note is very short—approximately six weeks—which ensures that worn or dirty notes are practically never seen in circulation.

## Security and Forgery Prevention
The Bank of England's issues have remained comparatively immune from forgery due to a combination of the bank's reissue policy and the difficulty of negotiating forged notes in England, as the lowest denomination is L5.

Historically, early bank-notes were printed forms where amounts were written by hand, typically for large sums of L40 and above. These were printed on water-marked paper, and while they lacked engraving precautions, forgeries were rare. However, when notes for smaller amounts (L1 and L2) were introduced at the end of the 18th century, forgery became rife; in one single year in England, 352 people were convicted of the crime.

This led to a continuous trial of skill between counterfeiters and note makers. To combat forgery, engravers introduced emblematical figures, views, and engine-turned ornaments, alongside special water-marks. The advent of photography provided counterfeiters with a dangerous tool to reproduce complicated vignettes and patterns. In 1856, Henry Bradbury introduced anti-photographic printing, where essential portions of the note were printed in one color with a second protective color placed over it. Because photographic advances eventually allowed these colors to be separated, it became necessary to introduce a third color and establish a specific photographic relation between the three to prevent separation.

## Legal Framework and Monopoly
The Bank of England was incorporated by the act of 1694. Subsequent legislation, including the acts of 1697 and 1708 (confirmed in 1800), restricted the establishment of joint-stock banks and the ability of partnerships exceeding six persons to borrow or take up money on notes payable on demand. In 1826, an act prohibited bank notes under L5.

The Bank Charter Acts of 1844 and 1845 were designed to concentrate note issues within the Bank of England to secure its monopoly as the sole issuer in England and Wales. These acts granted the Bank of England an absolute monopoly on note issue within the city of London and a 3-mile radius. Outside this radius, but within 65 miles of the city, a concurrent right to issue notes existed for banks established before 1844 that consisted of six or fewer persons (though some authorities suggest this could be increased to ten persons). Beyond the 65-mile radius, the privilege was available to all banks established and issuing notes before 1844, provided they had not lost the right through abandonment of business, bankruptcy, or temporary suspension of issue.

## Circulation and Economic Impact
By 1906, nearly all provincial banks in England had lost their right of issue. The gradual absorption of smaller, older concerns by larger joint-stock banks minimized the output of notes other than those issued by the Bank of England. 

The circulation of Bank of England notes increased significantly after 1844. In October 1844, the Bank of England's notes in circulation totaled L20,228,800. By 1906, the total resources—including reserves, capitals, and note circulation—reached a round figure of L177,500,000. This increase is attributed in part to the growth of banking offices; while there were 976 offices in England and Wales in 1844, there were more than 5,880 by 1906. Because each office must hold till-money, and Bank of England notes almost always form part of that money, a large portion of the increased circulation is likely held in bank tills rather than representing an increase in active circulation.

For a typical year ending April 5, 1904, the government received L184,930 2s. 2d. from the Bank of England's profits of issue, along with L120,768 18s. 6d. in composition for duties on the notes and bills of country bankers and the banks of Ireland and England.
