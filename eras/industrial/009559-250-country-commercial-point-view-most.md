# Commercial Geography and Infrastructure

The development of regional prosperity is frequently tied to the presence of navigable waterways, strategic urban locations, and the establishment of standardized financial systems. Across various global regions, the utility of specific rivers and the strategic placement of cities have historically served as primary drivers of commercial and industrial expansion.

## Navigable Waterways and Regional Trade

In several territories, the hydrographic structure determines the commercial viability of the region. In Bohemia, while the Elbe rises within the territory, the Moldau (Czech *Vltava*) is considered the principal river from every point of view. The Moldau serves as the country's principal commercial artery because it is navigable below Budweis, whereas the Upper-Elbe is not. The Moldau's basin covers 11,890 sq. m., which is twice the size of the Elbe's basin, and it possesses greater width and depth.

In western India, the Tapti river drains approximately 250 m. of country and is regarded as the most useful of the Gujarat rivers from a commercial perspective. Other significant rivers in the region include the Indus, which flows into the Arabian Sea through various mouths such as the Khudi, Pitiani, and Sir creeks, and the Nerbudda. The Nerbudda is navigable by small boats for about 100 m. from the sea at all seasons, and during the rainy season, it can accommodate vessels with a burden of 30 to 50 tons.

## Strategic Commercial Settlements

The location of cities often dictates their economic success. In Japan, Hideyoshi highlighted the superior advantages of Yedo over Odawara from both a commercial and strategical point of view. Consequently, when the Kwanto was conferred upon Ieyasu, Yedo was chosen as the capital, with an accompanying revenue of about two and a half million koku.

In China, Macao exists as a Portuguese settlement on a tongue of land 2½ m. in length and less than 1 m. in breadth. The Praya Grande, or Great Quay, runs along the east side of the peninsula and serves as the site for leading commercial establishments, as well as the governor's palace and administrative offices. Macao's commercial importance extended to other regions; for example, the annual visit of a large vessel from Macao to Japan was of great importance from a commercial point of view, facilitating not only trade but also the arrival of figures such as the Jesuit vice-general Valegnani.

## Financial Systems and Monetary Standards

The efficiency of commerce has historically relied on the stability of currency. In Hamburg, the Bank of Hamburg functioned as a representative of the free commercial cities of medieval Europe. It addressed the inconveniences faced by merchants regarding the uncertain value of foreign currencies by creating "bank money"—a money of account that could be transferred between holders. This international coinage provided a common standard of value, which was essential for a city of high commercial activity that was limited in territory.

On a global scale, the commercial world has increasingly moved toward a gold basis. The adoption of the gold standard by countries including India (1899), Japan (1897), Russia (1897), and Austria-Hungary (1892), combined with a great increase in gold output from South Africa and Australia, contributed to the expansion of trade and industry. By 1906, the yield from these mines reached £81,000,000, compared to less than £20,000,000 in 1883.

## Industrial and Colonial Expansion

Commercial prosperity is also linked to the acquisition of raw materials and the pursuit of colonial expansion. In Belgium, the Flemish cloth industry became dependent on England for raw materials after the annexation of Walloon Flanders by France. This relationship was managed by the Hansa of London, a powerful association of Flemish tradesmen who monopolized wool imports.

Under King Leopold II, Belgium shifted toward a policy of commercial and colonial expansion. Leopold II, who was made a member of the Belgian Senate on April 9, 1853, spent significant time touring the world to prepare for this task. His travels included visits to Egypt, Palestine, Greece, Italy, and Switzerland (1854–1855), Turkey and Asia Minor (1860), Spain, Algeria, Tunisia, Malta, and Egypt (1862–1863), and India (1864–1865). This pioneering effort in expansion resulted in the industrial and commercial prosperity of the country.
