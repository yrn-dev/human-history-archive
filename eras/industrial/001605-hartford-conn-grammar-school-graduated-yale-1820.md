# Leonard Bacon

## Early Life and Education
Leonard Bacon (1802–1881) was an American writer and Congregational preacher. He was born on February 19, 1802, in Detroit, Michigan. He was the son of David Bacon (1771–1817), who served as a missionary among the Indians in Michigan and founded the town of Tallmadge, Ohio. 

Bacon's academic preparation for college took place at the grammar school in Hartford, Connecticut. He subsequently attended Yale, where he graduated in 1820, and later graduated from the Andover Theological Seminary in 1823.

## Ministry and Pastoral Work
From 1825 until his death on December 24, 1881, Bacon served as the pastor of the First Church (Congregational) in New Haven, Connecticut. He occupied a pulpit that was considered one of the most conspicuous in New England, a position previously made famous by Nathaniel W. Taylor and Moses Stuart. Although he gave up the active pastorate in 1866, he was never dismissed from his connection with the church by a council.

## Editorial and Literary Contributions
Bacon was active in several editorial ventures throughout his career. From 1826 to 1838, he served as an editor of the *Christian Spectator* in New Haven. In 1843, he became one of the founders of the *New Englander*, a publication that later became the *Yale Review*. 

In 1848, Bacon founded *The Independent* alongside Joshua Leavitt, Dr. R. S. Storrs, Henry C. Bowen, and Dr. Joseph P. Thompson. The primary purpose of this publication was to combat the extension of slavery. Bacon remained an editor of *The Independent* until 1863.

## Academic Career and Theological Influence
In the later years of his life, Bacon transitioned into academic roles at Yale University. From 1866 to 1871, he served as the acting professor of didactic theology within the university's theological department. Following this, from 1871 until his death, he served as a lecturer on American church history and church polity.

Within his denomination, Bacon gained significant influence and was regarded as perhaps the most prominent Congregationalist of his era; some popularly referred to him as "The Congregational Pope of New England." He was known for advocating liberal orthodoxy and maintaining a broad-minded approach to his theological views. 

Bacon was a conspicuous figure in the heated theological controversies of his time, most notably the long and bitter dispute regarding the views of Dr. Horace Bushnell. In these conflicts, Bacon used his influence to seek harmony. He also demonstrated skill as a parliamentarian and debater in the councils of the Congregational churches, presiding as moderator over the Brooklyn councils of 1876 and 1874.
