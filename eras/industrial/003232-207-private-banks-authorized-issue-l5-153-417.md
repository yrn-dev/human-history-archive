# Private Banks and the Bank Act of 1844

## The Bank Act of 1844 and Issuing Privileges
Prior to the implementation of the Bank Act of 1844, nearly all provincial banks in Wales and England, in addition to the Bank of England, held the privilege of issuing notes. During the parliamentary discussions regarding the Bank Act, these banks continued their usual operations. However, once the arrangements of the act were made public, nine banks ceased issuing notes before the act officially came into operation on October 12, 1844. Of these nine, one was a joint-stock bank (the Western District Joint-Stock Banking Co., which was dissolved) and eight were private banks. One of the private banks closed, while the other seven began issuing Bank of England notes and were granted specific privileges for doing so.

## Authorized and Actual Circulation in England
The Bank Act of 1844 established a maximum circulation for English issuing banks, fixing it at the average circulation recorded during the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. Under these regulations, the banks allowed the privilege of circulation and their authorized issue amounts in England were as follows:
*   **Private banks:** 207 banks with an authorized issue of £5,153,417.
*   **Joint-stock banks:** 72 banks with an authorized issue of £3,478,230.

This resulted in a total authorized issue for these banks of £8,631,647.

By October 12, 1844, the actual circulation in England was recorded as £20,228,800 from the Bank of England, £4,674,162 from private banks, and £3,331,516 from joint-stock banks. When including Scotland (chartered, private, and joint-stock banks at £2,987,665) and Ireland (Bank of Ireland at £3,597,850 and private/joint-stock banks at £2,456,261), the total circulation was £37,276,254.

## Evolution of Private Banking Structures
The constitution of private banks was subject to specific legal restrictions regarding partnerships. Until 1862, partners in private banks were limited to six. While an act in 1862 increased this limit to ten for general private banks, this extension did not apply to private issuing banks, which remained restricted to six partners. 

The Bank Act of 1844 had an unexpected impact on the banking system. While it favored small banks for many years, it eventually facilitated the formation of large, powerful banks in England and Wales. The act prohibited the establishment of any new bank of issue. Existing private issuing banks with no more than six partners were permitted to remain or amalgamate with other private issuing banks to retain joint issues. However, if a private issuing bank joined or was formed into a joint-stock bank, its issue lapsed.

## Decline of Private Issuing Banks
By May 1907, the number of private banks and their issues had been significantly reduced. There were only 12 private banks remaining, with an authorized issue of £482,744 and an actual issue of £122,536. The actual circulation of these banks fell far below the authorized amount because their circulation was limited to very small areas. Notes from country banks became almost unknown outside the immediate vicinity of their issuance; although payable in London, they were often difficult to cash.

By 1906, the average circulation for private banks in England had dropped to £124,000. In Scotland, private banking never gained a significant hold; while eight private banks existed in 1819, they had all disappeared by 1844.

## Comparative Banking Statistics
The number of banking offices in England and Wales was estimated at 697 in 1840. By 1844, the *Banking Almanac* listed 336 private bank offices and 640 joint-stock offices in England and Wales. By 1906, the total number of banking offices in the United Kingdom had grown to 7,507, compared to 1,524 in 1844. This growth significantly reduced the number of inhabitants per office; in England and Wales, this figure dropped from 16,305 inhabitants per office in 1844 to 5,885 in 1906.
