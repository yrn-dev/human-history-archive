# Pressure Curves and Dynamics

In the study of ballistics and pneumatic systems, the behavior of pressure over time or distance is represented by a pressure-curve. The shape of this curve determines the resulting energy, velocity, and time curves of a projectile.

## Uniform and Linear Pressure Variations

When pressure is assumed to be uniform up the bore—a condition practically realizable in a Zalinski pneumatic dynamite gun—the pressure-curve is represented as a straight line (HK in fig. 3) parallel to AM. Under these specific conditions, the energy-curve (AQE) is also a straight line passing through A. The velocity-curve (AvV) takes the form of a parabola, as the ordinate v is proportional to the square root of the energy. Because the acceleration of the shot remains constant in this scenario, the time-curve (AtT) is also a similar parabola.

Variations in the linearity of the pressure-curve alter these results:

*   **Uniformly Falling Pressure:** If the pressure falls off uniformly, the pressure-curve is a straight line (PDF) that slopes downwards and cuts AM at point F. In this instance, the energy-curve becomes a parabola curving downwards. The velocity-curve is represented by a circle or ellipse with a radius of FA and a center at F, while the time-curve is a sinusoid.
*   **Uniformly Rising Pressure:** If the pressure-curve is a straight line (F'CP) sloping upwards and cutting AM behind A at point F', the energy-curve is a parabola curving upwards. The resulting velocity-curve is a hyperbola with its center at F'.

These theorems are utilized for preliminary calculations when the pressure-curve is nearly straight. However, when no observable law exists, the area of the pressure-curve must be calculated using Simpson's rule as an indicator diagram or read via a planimeter.

## Atmospheric and Osmotic Pressure

Pressure changes also manifest in natural atmospheric phenomena and chemical solutions.

### Atmospheric Pressure and Winds
Periodical changes in atmospheric pressure result in winds, which generally blow from areas of greatest pressure to areas of least pressure. These movements are modified by the earth's west-to-east revolution on its axis. For example, the south-west monsoon occurs north of the equator during the hot half of the year. This is caused by a great diminution of atmospheric pressure over Asia, which begins in April and May as temperatures rise, coinciding with relatively higher pressure over the equator and regions to the south. This pressure diminution reaches its maximum in July. As temperatures fall north of the equator and barometric pressure is equalized within the tropics after the October equinox, the south-west winds are succeeded by the north-east monsoon, driven by relatively greater atmospheric pressure over Asia during winter months.

### Osmotic Pressure
In salt solutions, the lowering of the freezing point (F.P.) with increased concentration can be calculated in terms of osmotic pressure by analogy with mechanical pressure. To separate 1 gramme of salt using a semi-permeable membrane requires work (PV) against the osmotic pressure P, where V is the diminution in the volume of the solution. In dilute solutions, the volume V is the reciprocal of the concentration C. The osmotic pressure P is equal to that of an equal number of molecules of gas in the same space and is deduced from the gas equation: $P = R[\theta] / VM = R[\theta]C / M$.

## Mechanical Pressure Regulation

Various apparatuses are designed to maintain constant pressure or utilize pressure for air compression.

### Gas Regulators
Regulators such as those designed by R. A. Buddicom and Moitessier manage gas flow based on pressure changes. In Buddicom's regulator, a metal bell is immersed in water and suspended from a balance arm poised by a weight. If gas pressure rises, the bell is raised, lowering the counter-poising weight and forcing mercury up a U-shaped tube. This diminishes the size of an oblique orifice, cutting off some gas to maintain constant pressure. If pressure falls, the reverse occurs, allowing more gas to pass.

Moitessier's regulator uses a closed cylinder filled with a two-to-one mixture of acid-free glycerine and distilled water. A bell within the cylinder supports a weighted dish. An inlet tube features a ball- or socket-valve supported by a thread. When pressure increases, the bell and valve are raised, reducing the aperture and cutting off gas. When pressure falls, the valve lowers, allowing more gas to pass. Both systems are typically adjusted in the morning when pressure is low.

### Air Compression
The "trompe" or water-blowing engine uses falling water in a vertical wooden pipe to draw in air through sloping holes. The resulting air pressure depends on the height of the water fall, obtaining somewhat less than half a pound per square inch for every foot of fall.
