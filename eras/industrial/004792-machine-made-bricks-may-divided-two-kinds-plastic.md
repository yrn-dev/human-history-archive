# Machine-Made Bricks

Bricks made of tempered clay can be produced either by hand or by machine. While hand-molding involves pressing clay into wooden or metal moulds—often using a "strike" to level the top and a "stock-board" to create a hollow "frog" in the bottom—machine production is more specialized. Machine-made bricks are divided into two primary categories: plastic and semi-plastic. Although the same type of machine is often utilized for both, the processes and materials differ.

## Plastic Bricks
Machine-made plastic bricks are produced from tempered clay. When harder shales or clays are used, machinery is employed for tempering and working the material. The preparation process typically involves grinding-mills and pug-mills. 

Grinding-mills may consist of a series of rollers with graduated spaces or a "mortar pan" type featuring a perforated or solid iron bottom where heavy rollers crush the shale or clay. For ordinary brick-clay in a plastic state, these mills are only necessary if pebbles larger than a quarter of an inch are present. Otherwise, the clay proceeds to the pug-mill. The pug-mill uses a central shaft with knives and cutters to knead the clay and force it through a delivery nose. 

The resulting continuous mass of clay is pushed along a smooth iron plate and cut into bricks using a wire cutting frame. This frame may be a skeleton cylinder with radial wires or metal disks. Bricks produced through this specific method are known as "wire-cuts."

## Semi-Plastic Bricks
Semi-plastic bricks are made from ground clay or shale that is damp enough to adhere under pressure. This method is particularly advantageous when using shales or certain types of clay-rock that would be impractical for the plastic method. A significant benefit of the semi-plastic process is that the "ageing," tempering, and weathering of the material may be entirely or largely dispensed with.

The production process involves crushing hard clay or shale under heavy rollers in an iron grinding-pan with a perforated bottom. The resulting powder is delivered via an elevator into a screen to remove coarser particles for regrinding. This coarse, damp powder is then fed into a press. While the presses are similar to those used for plastic clay, they are generally more heavily built to apply greater pressure.

## Comparison of Methods
The two methods offer different advantages and results:
*   **Strength and Quality:** The best plastic bricks are stronger and more perfect than semi-plastic bricks.
*   **Efficiency:** The semi-plastic method allows for a more compact brickyard and simpler processes. However, the plant required for semi-plastic production is more costly and heavier.
*   **Drying:** Plastic bricks shrink more, have a higher tendency to warp or twist, and take significantly longer to dry than semi-plastic bricks.

## Brick Presses and Equipment
Brick presses are generally made of iron, though other metals are occasionally used for dies and moulds. Most are adapted for belt-driving, though hydraulic presses have become more common in America and Germany. The essential components of a press include a moulding box or frame, a ram with a plunger or die to provide pressure, and a mechanism to eject the finished brick. In many modern presses, especially those for semi-plastic clay, a second ram works upward from beneath to press the brick from both the top and bottom simultaneously.

## Material Composition and Properties
The quality of bricks depends heavily on the composition of the clay. The strongest clays, possessing the highest tensile strength and plasticity, usually contain the highest percentage of hydrated aluminium silicates. All clays contain some amount of undecomposed felspar and free silica (sand). 

Oxide of iron is a critical ingredient, typically ranging from 2% to 10%, which determines the brick's hardness, durability, and color. A concentration of 5% to 8% generally produces a red brick. Higher temperatures or the presence of 3% to 4% alkalis can make the color darker or more purple, as the clay approaches its fusion point. Conversely, an excess of alumina tends to make the color brighter and lighter.

For specialized "fireclays" used in crucibles or blast furnaces, the most essential property is the ability to resist fusion at extremely high heats. This requires the clay to be free of metallic oxides like iron or lime. To prevent warping or cracking during drying, burnt material such as potsherds or old bricks is often added to the paste.
