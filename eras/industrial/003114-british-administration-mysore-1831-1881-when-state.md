## Geography and Administration
Mysore is described as a native state in India. Its capital is Bangalore, which serves as the largest British cantonment in southern India. The city is situated 3,113 feet above sea level and is located 219 meters west of Madras by rail. 

The state includes the District of Kolar, which occupies the Mysore table-land bordering the Eastern Ghats. This region is characterized by numerous hill ranges and a principal watershed around the hill of Nandidrug, which stands at 4,810 feet. The area is served by several rivers, including the North Pinakini, South Pinakini (or Pennar), Papagani, and Palar, all of which are used for irrigation via tanks and anicuts.

## Economic Development and Industry
During the latter part of the 19th century, Mysore saw the emergence of significant industrial activity, particularly in the District of Kolar. Gold prospecting began in this region in 1876, eventually establishing a secure industry. This led to the creation of the Kolar Gold Fields railway, a 10-mile branch of the Madras railway.

By the end of 1904, the gold industry—which included the Nandidrug, Ooregum, Champion Reef, and Mysore companies—had produced a total value of 21 million sterling. Of this, 9 million was paid in dividends and 1 million was paid as royalty to the Mysore state. The mines utilized electricity for motive power, sourced from the falls of the Cauvery, located 93 meters away. Other primary industries in the district include silk and cotton weaving, as well as the manufacture of sugar. In the town of Kolar, industries include the breeding of turkeys for export and the weaving of blankets.

## Historical Context of Key Sites
The archive notes several sites of historical interest within the region. The fort at Bangalore was strengthened by Hyder Ali, who was granted the area as a *jagir* (fief) by Nanjiraj, the raja's powerful minister, in 1758. This fort later served as a refuge for Hyder Ali in 1760 and was the site of the first captivity of Sir David Baird in 1780.

Additionally, the hill fort of Nandidrug is noted as a site of modern historical interest, having been stormed by the British in 1791 following a 21-day bombardment.
