# Private Banks

## Overview and Early Structure
In the early development of the English banking system, the Bank of England maintained a practical, though perhaps not entirely legal, monopoly on the issuance of notes within London. Surrounding this central institution was a circle of private banks, some of which possessed considerable power. Outside of London, nearly all provincial banks in England and Wales held the privilege of issuing their own notes.

For a significant period, the scale of private banking was limited by legal technicalities and partnership restrictions. Until 1862, private banks were restricted to a maximum of six partners. While an act in 1862 increased the allowed number of partners to ten for some private banks, this extension did not apply to private issuing banks, which remained restricted to six partners. Because of these constraints and other legal technicalities, it was practically impossible for large private banks to operate under ordinary circumstances. Consequently, banking did not assume its modern form until approximately the middle of the 19th century.

## The Bank Act of 1844
The Bank Act of 1844 introduced significant changes to the constitution of the banking system in England and Wales. Prior to the act's implementation on October 12, 1844, nine banks (eight private and one joint-stock) ceased issuing notes. Of these, one private bank closed and the Western District Joint-Stock Banking Co. was dissolved; the remaining seven began issuing Bank of England notes and received certain privileges for doing so.

The act fixed the maximum circulation for English issuing banks based on the average circulation from the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. At that time, the privilege of circulation was granted to 207 private banks, which had an authorized issue of £5,153,417.

The act also influenced the long-term structure of the industry. While it initially favored small banks, it eventually necessitated the formation of larger, more powerful banks. Under the act, no new banks of issue could be established. Private issuing banks with no more than six partners were permitted to remain or amalgamate with other private issuing banks to retain joint issues. However, if a private issuing bank joined or was formed into a joint-stock bank, its issue lapsed.

## Circulation and Statistics
The actual circulation of notes in October 1844 showed that private banks in England had £4,674,162 in circulation, compared to £20,228,800 for the Bank of England and £3,331,516 for joint-stock banks. In Scotland, the combined circulation of chartered, private, and joint-stock banks was £2,987,665.

By 1906, the role of private banks in England had diminished significantly. The average circulation for private banks in England fell to £124,000, while the Bank of England's circulation rose to £28,890,000.

In Ireland, the landscape shifted over time. By May 1907, there were 12 private banks with an authorized issue of £482,744, though the actual issue was much lower at £122,536. This discrepancy was attributed to the fact that the circulation of these country banks was limited to a very small area around the place of issue. Although these notes were payable in London, they were often difficult to cash.

## Regional Variations: Scotland
Private banking did not have a significant presence in Scotland. While eight private banks existed in 1819, all had disappeared by 1844. By 1906, the Scottish banking business was almost entirely conducted by ten banks of issue, which operated 1,180 branches.
