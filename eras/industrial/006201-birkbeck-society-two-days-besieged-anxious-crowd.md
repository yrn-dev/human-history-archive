# Birkbeck Society

The Birkbeck Society was a large building society that faced a significant financial crisis characterized by a sudden rush of depositors seeking to withdraw their funds. This event occurred within a broader climate of instability affecting building societies in the late 19th century.

## Context of Financial Instability
The instability of the period was precipitated by the failure of another entity known as the Liberator. This society had attracted over a million sterling from provident individuals across all classes and regions of the country through specious representations. Rather than utilizing these funds for the legitimate purposes of a building society, the active managers applied the capital to support other undertakings in which they held interests. 

By October 1892, this group of concerns became insolvent, resulting in the total loss of investments for the Liberator's shareholders and depositors. This collapse caused great distress and absolute ruin for many who lost their savings. Consequently, public confidence in building societies generally was weakened. This decline was evident in the capital of incorporated building societies, which dropped from a peak of nearly 54 million in 1887 to below 43 million by 1895. Several societies that had utilized the deposit system experienced "runs" and were unable to survive.

## The Siege of the Birkbeck Society
Amidst this general decline, the Birkbeck Society was besieged for two days by an anxious crowd of depositors who clamoured to withdraw their money. The society's ability to survive this crisis depended on its investment strategy; a very large portion of its funds had been placed in securities that were easily convertible.

Because of these liquid assets, the Birkbeck Society was able to obtain sufficient assistance from the Bank of England. This allowed the society to pay every depositor who requested their money without any hesitation. This decisive action firmly established the society's credit, to the extent that many people subsequently sought to deposit money into the institution.

## Impact on the Building Society Sector
The survival of the Birkbeck Society had a positive ripple effect on the wider financial sector. Given the size of the society, its failure would have been disastrous for the entire body of building societies. Instead, the energetic measures the society took to protect its own credit reacted in favor of building societies generally.

## Related Entities: Birkbeck Institution
Separate from the building society, the name Birkbeck is associated with the Birkbeck Institution (later known as Birkbeck College), founded in 1823 by George Birkbeck (1776–1841). George Birkbeck was an English philanthropist and physician born in Settle, Yorkshire. After graduating as a doctor of medicine, he served as the chair of natural philosophy at the Andersonian Institution of Glasgow, where he provided successful, gratuitous scientific lectures for the working classes.

Upon moving to London in 1804, he pursued philanthropic goals, eventually endowing the Mechanics' Institute with £3,700. He served as the director of the institute until his death on December 1, 1841. The institution's buildings were enlarged between 1883 and 1885, and the college provides classes in art, languages, literature, and various sciences. In later years, the college was associated with the University of London, employing lecturers such as Thomas Seccombe.
