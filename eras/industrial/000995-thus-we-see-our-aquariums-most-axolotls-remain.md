# Axolotl

The axolotl is a salamander of the genus *Amblystoma*, native to Mexico. Known to the Mexican people from the remotest times, it has been used as a wholesome and agreeable article of food brought from neighboring lakes to the Mexico market. Historically, it has been referred to by Francisco Hernandez (1514-1578) as *Gyrinus edulis*, *atolocatl*, *lusus aquarum*, *piscis ludicrus*, or *axolotl*.

## Physical Characteristics
The axolotl is a nearly exact image of British newt larvae, though it grows to a length of eleven inches. It possesses a plump, moderately long body with a low dorsal crest and a strongly compressed tail. Its head is large and thick, featuring a large pendent upper lip and small eyes that lack lids. 

The most characteristic feature of the axolotl is the presence of three large appendages on each side of the back of the head. These external gills are fringed with filaments resembling black ostrich feathers and allow the animal to breathe oxygen dissolved in the water. Its coloration is typically brownish grey, dark olive-grey, or blackish, often marked with round black dots or spots. The jaws contain small teeth in several rows, and the front of the palate features an elongate patch of teeth on each side.

## Habitat and Distribution
Axolotls are found in abundance in Lakes Chalco and Xochimilco near Mexico City. These lakes are described as a "paradise," situated approximately 10 feet higher than Lake Texcoco and separated from it by hills. Lake Xochimilco is characterized by powerful springs and dark, muddy water filled with decomposing vegetable matter, worms, *Daphniae*, larvae of insects, and fish.

The axolotls breed in early February, fastening their eggs to water plants. By June, the larvae grow into large creatures ready for market. While they may take to the rushes in late summer and become scarce in autumn, they have never been known to leave the water or metamorphose in their native environment. H. Gadow suggests that the abundance of food, stable water levels, and numerous hiding places—such as mud, reeds, and the roots of floating islands—induce the creatures to remain in this state, retaining larval features.

## Metamorphosis and Classification
The classification of the axolotl was a subject of scientific debate. In the early 19th century, Cuvier recognized that the axolotl was not related to permanent gill-breathers like *Proteus* and *Siren*, but was instead the larval form of an air-breathing salamander. However, this view was largely ignored for over half a century because axolotls were observed breeding in their branchiate (gilled) condition. This led systematists to place them in the genus *Siredon* among permanent gill-breathers.

It was later discovered that the axolotl can transform into *Amblystoma tigrinum*, a species of salamander that inhabits the United States and cooler parts of Mexico. The *Amblystoma tigrinum* is usually brown or blackish with yellow spots, rarely exceeds nine inches, and possesses movable eyelids and a straight upper lip. Unlike the axolotl, it leaves the water after metamorphosis, which is marked by the loss of the gills.

## History of Captivity and Breeding
In late 1863, thirty-four axolotls, including one albino, were sent to the Jardin d'Acclimatation in Paris by order of Marshal Forey. Six of these specimens (five males and one female) were given to Professor A. Dumeril at the Jardin des Plantes. While the original six specimens remained in the branchiate condition and bred frequently between 1865 and 1870, their second-generation offspring exhibited a different trait. Some individuals lost their gills and dorsal crest, developed movable eyelids, changed their dentition, and assumed yellow spots, effectively becoming *Amblystoma tigrinum*.

Between 1865 and 1870, twenty-nine such transformed salamanders were obtained, though they did not breed. It was not until 1876 that Professor E. Blanchard reported the first instance of an axolotl in its *Amblystoma* state spawning.

The Paris Museum distributed over a thousand examples to dealers and institutions across Europe. Many were kept in England starting in 1866, including specimens at the London Zoological Gardens in August 1864, though the London specimens did not appear to breed. In recent years, the number of specimens in England and France has decreased, likely due to close in-breeding without the infusion of new blood.

## Albino Varieties
"White" axolotls are albinos of a pale flesh color with red gills. These are believed to be descendants of a single albino male received by the Paris Museum in 1866. When paired with normal specimens in 1867 and 1868, they produced white offspring that became a permanent race. According to L. Vaillant, these albinos show no tendency toward reversion, and only two have ever been observed to transform into the perfect *Amblystoma* form.
