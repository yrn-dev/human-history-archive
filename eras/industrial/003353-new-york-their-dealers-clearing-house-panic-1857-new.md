# New York, their Dealers, the Clearing-House and the Panic of 1857

## Overview of New York Banking
As New York became the chief commercial state of the Union, the banks within New York City were generally considered sound. However, the region experimented with various systems to secure circulating notes. One such method was the "safety-fund system," which was inaugurated in 1829. Under this system, each bank contributed to a fund designed to cover the deficit of any contributing bank that failed with assets insufficient to meet its liabilities.

While the act intended for this fund to protect only bank-notes, it was instead treated as a fund for the payment of all liabilities of a failed bank. Consequently, the fund was exhausted following important failures during the panics of 1837 and 1857. Additionally, prior to 1843, the state did not control the issue of notes, leading to illegal over-issues in several instances.

## The New York Clearing-House
The system of clearing cheques in the United States has reached a level of development surpassed perhaps only by Great Britain. Clearing-houses operate in approximately 112 leading cities. The New York Clearing-House handles a significant portion of this business; in 1906, its clearings accounted for 67.2% of the total clearings across 55 of the larger cities. The volume of these clearings is subject to great fluctuation based on the business prosperity of the country and the volume of stock-exchange transactions.

The Clearing-House Committee exercises powerful influence over the banking situation. This influence is manifested in its ability to refuse emergency aid to banks that are unwisely conducted. For example, during the panic of 1907, this power was utilized to remove several speculative financial interests from the control of national banks.

## Membership and Reporting
At the time of the 1907 panic, membership in the Clearing-House was limited to the sub-Treasury, state banks, and national banks. The money-market and exchanges relied on the weekly reports of condition issued by these members every Saturday as an index of the market's state. 

However, this index was sometimes misleading and incomplete because trust companies did not provide regular weekly reports. To remedy this and create a more complete mirror of banking conditions in New York, the state superintendent of banking announced in early 1908 that he would use his legal power to require weekly reports from trust companies.

## Trust Companies and the Panic of 1907
Trust companies, particularly those in New York City, were a disturbing feature of the 1907 panic. While they were a small factor in New York finance during the panic of 1893—with total U.S. capitalization of $106,968,253 and individual deposits of $566,922,205 by 1897—their size grew significantly by 1907, reaching $276,146,081 in capital and $2,061,623,035 in deposits.

State law required these companies to maintain only 5% of their demand deposits in cash in their vaults. Although many held large deposits in national banks, these reserves were inadequate to support the vast mass of credit built upon them. Furthermore, less conservative companies engaged in speculative underwritings and locked funds into long-term enterprises rather than maintaining liquid assets through loans on first-class negotiable securities or commercial paper.

## The 1907 Financial Crisis
The panic was precipitated by over-speculation by a group of national banks, followed by the suspension of the Knickerbocker Trust Company on October 22, which held deposits of $48,000,000. This triggered runs on other companies. In the week of November 2, New York banks faced a deficit in required reserves of $38,838,825.

The crisis led to the following financial movements:
* Payment of cheques in currency was suspended in New York from October 28, 1907, until approximately the beginning of 1908.
* Arrangements were made to import foreign gold approaching $100,000,000.
* National bank circulation increased by about $77,000,000 during the autumn.
* The treasury transferred $72,000,000 to the banks.

In total, the amount of currency that disappeared or was added to circulation during a few weeks of the panic exceeded $275,000,000, representing nearly one-tenth of the country's usual circulation volume. By December 28, 1907, the total bank-note circulation had risen to an abnormal $687,340,835, which was reduced during the spring of 1908.
