# The Barrel-Organ

The barrel-organ is a small, portable musical instrument that is played mechanically. It is known by various names across different languages, including the "grinder-organ," "street-organ," "hand-organ," or "Dutch organ" in English; *orgue de Barbarie*, *orgue d'Allemagne*, *orgue mecanique*, *cabinet d'orgue*, and *serinette* in French; *Drehorgel* and *Leierkasten* in German; and *organetto a manovella* or *organo tedesco* in Italian.

## Mechanical Operation
The instrument derives its name from a cylinder, or barrel, which serves as the mechanism for storing music. Tunes are pricked onto this cylinder using staples and pins of various lengths, which are placed at definite intervals according to the requirements of the music. As the cylinder rotates, these pins and staples raise balanced keys. These keys are connected via a simple mechanism to the valves of the pipes, opening them mechanically to admit a stream of air from the wind-chest.

The rotation of the cylinder is controlled by a handle attached to a shaft. This handle sets the cylinder in slow rotation through the use of a worm that works in a fine-toothed gear located on the barrel-head. This same motion is used to operate the bellows by means of connecting rods and cranks located on the shaft. The resulting wind is forced into a reservoir and then passes into the wind-chest, where the pipes are grouped on the sides.

## Performance and Tuning
The barrel typically revolves slowly from back to front, with a single complete revolution generally playing one full tune. To change the tune, the performer uses a notch-pin in the barrel-head; this pin is furnished with as many notches as there are tunes, allowing the performer to shift the barrel.

Standard street barrel-organs typically possess a compass of 24 to 34 notes. These notes form a diatonic scale and include a few accidentals, generally C#, G#, and F#. These instruments usually feature two stops: one for closed wooden pipes and another for open metal pipes.

## Variations and Historical Use
While the street organ was common, more elaborate versions were constructed for use in chapels and churches during the early 19th and 18th centuries. These larger instruments were frequently used to play voluntaries, chants, and hymns. Some of these versions featured a circular revolving frame containing as many as four cylinders. Examples of such instruments include a barrel-organ built by Wright for Fulham church and a larger four-barrel instrument constructed by Bishop in 1820 for Northallerton church.
