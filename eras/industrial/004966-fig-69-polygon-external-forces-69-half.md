# Reciprocal Figures and Polygons of Forces

In the study of braced structures and frames, the graphic method of computing stresses on members utilizes the concept of reciprocal figures and polygons of forces. These geometric tools allow for the determination of the magnitude and direction of forces acting within a frame in equilibrium.

## Definition of Reciprocal Figures
As defined by J. Clerk Maxwell in 1864, two plane figures are considered reciprocal when they consist of an equal number of lines such that corresponding lines in the two figures are parallel. Furthermore, corresponding lines that converge to a point in one figure must form a closed polygon in the other. 

When a frame without redundant members and the external forces maintaining its equilibrium are represented by one of these plane figures, the reciprocal figure represents the forces between the joints of the frame. Consequently, the reciprocal figure provides a simple method for computing the stress on each member of the frame, representing both the direction and magnitude of those forces.

## Polygons of External Forces
External forces acting on a bridge or frame in equilibrium can be represented by a closed polygon. In this polygon, each side is parallel to one force and represents that force's magnitude and direction. The sides may be arranged in any order, provided that passing around the polygon in one direction corresponds to the direction of the forces represented.

If the sides of this polygon are arranged in the same order as the joints on which the forces act (for example, if joints are numbered 1, 2, 3, 4, etc., and the polygon sides follow that sequence), the polygon may be called the reciprocal figure of the external forces. Under this interpretation, the frame is considered as a single point in equilibrium under external forces.

In specific cases, such as girders where all forces are vertical, the polygon of external forces is reduced to two superimposed straight lines. In such a diagram, specific lengths represent specific loads and reactions (for example, length AX representing load AX and length YX representing reaction YX).

To compute stresses, the frame is analyzed at its joints. For a frame without redundant members, only two members abut at the point of support to balance the reaction. By drawing triangles where one side is the reaction and the other two sides are parallel to the members, a polygon of forces in equilibrium at the point of support is created. 

The direction of the forces determines the nature of the member:
*   **Struts:** Members that push (compression).
*   **Ties:** Members that pull (tension).

By moving from one joint to the next (e.g., from point YXE to point XEFA), the known forces—such as the downward load and the push from a strut—are used to complete the polygon of forces for that specific point. This process continues through the frame to determine the stresses on all members.

## Alternative Methods
While the method of reciprocal figures is a valid graphic approach, other techniques are often more convenient depending on the structure. The method of sections is frequently more convenient than the method of reciprocal figures, and the method of influence lines is often the readiest way to deal with braced girders.
