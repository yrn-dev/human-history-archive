# AUTHORITIES AND PARLIAMENTARY PROCEDURES

The administration of authority within governmental and parliamentary systems involves a combination of constitutional mandates, committee reports, and executive decision-making processes. These structures vary by institution, ranging from the naval administration of the Board of Admiralty to the legislative oversight of the House of Commons and the evolving parliamentary systems of Japan.

## Parliamentary Papers and Committee Reports
In the British House of Commons, significant authority and oversight are exercised through Select Committees and the publication of Parliamentary Papers. These documents serve as official records of investigation into financial and commercial matters. Notable reports include those concerning the High Price of Gold Bullion (June 8, 1810), the Bank of England Charter (1832), and various reports on Banks of Issue produced in 1840, 1841, and 1875.

Further legislative oversight is evidenced by the First and Second Reports of the Secret Committee on Commercial Distress (1848) and reports on Bank Acts issued in 1857 and 1858. The House of Lords also exercised authority through a Secret Committee report during the 1847-1848 session, which investigated the causes of distress among commercial classes and the impact of laws regulating the issue of bank notes payable on demand.

Additionally, the Board of Trade publishes annual reports as parliamentary papers. These include statistics and illustrations regarding the causes of current insolvency and the working of the bankruptcy system. For example, reports indicate that between December 31, 1895, and December 31, 1905, there were 43,141 insolvencies handled under bankruptcy acts, with estimated liabilities of £61,685,678 and assets of £26,001,417.

## Executive Authority in the Board of Admiralty
The constitutional authority of the Board of Admiralty has historically been exercised through a specific organizational structure. The Admiralty War Group typically consisted of the First Lord, the First Sea Lord, the Chief of the Staff, and the Secretary. On specific occasions, the Second Sea Lord and special advisers were added at the request of the First Sea Lord.

Decision-making within this group involved daily or twice-daily meetings to arrive at united decisions on matters of consequence. The roles were divided as follows:
* **Execution:** Confided to the Chief of the Staff.
* **Administration:** The Secretary was responsible for registering and recording actions and taking consequential action, separate from orders given by the War Staff.
* **Authorization:** Within agreed policies, the First Lord or First Sea Lord provided written authority for decisions and telegrams required by the Chief of the Staff.
* **Initiation:** The First Lord utilized minutes and memoranda to initiate action and discussion across all naval business, while papers from the First Sea Lord or other Lords were passed upward for decision by minute.

## Parliamentary Systems and Political Influence
The exercise of parliamentary authority is often tied to the influence of individual members and the stability of political parties. In the English Parliament, individuals such as Edward Baines (a member from 1834 to 1841) used their position to advocate for parliamentary reform and the separation of church and state. Similarly, Francis Horner exercised intellectual and moral influence over the House of Commons in the field of political economy. As chairman of the parliamentary committee investigating the depreciation of bank-notes in 1810, Horner contributed to the "Bullion Report," which helped check the paper-issue of English banks and reinstate gold and silver as circulating media.

In Japan, the parliamentary system has been characterized by a period of "long and deliberate experiment." The House of Peers once opposed Prince Ito due to his association with political parties, leading to his resignation as premier. He was succeeded by Marquis Saionji, to whom the Constitutionists yielded implicit obedience. The Japanese experience has shown a willingness to support Cabinets independent of parties, such as that of Prince Katsura (who served three years before stepping down in August 1911), provided the measures are conducive to the nation's good.
