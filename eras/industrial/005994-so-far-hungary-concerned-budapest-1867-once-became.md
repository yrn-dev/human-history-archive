# Budapest

Budapest is the capital and largest town of the kingdom of Hungary and the second largest town in the Austro-Hungarian monarchy. Situated on both banks of the Danube, the city was formed by the incorporation of the former towns of Buda and O-Buda on the right bank, and Pest and Köbánya on the left bank, which were merged into a single municipality in 1872. Geographically, the city is located where the Danube turns southward and where the Bakony Mountains meet the Carpathians. Its landscape is characterized by a contrast between the flat sandy plain of Pest and the steep hills of Buda, which are commanded by the Blocksberg and a fortress.

## Political and Social Position

Budapest serves as the seat of the Hungarian government, parliament, and all highest official authorities, including financial, judicial, military, and civil branches. Following the "Compromise" (or *Ausgleich*) concluded on February 8, 1867, which regulated relations between Austria and Hungary, Budapest immediately became the "favoured rival" of Vienna. Unlike Vienna, Budapest faced no competitors within its own sphere such as the Austrian provincial capitals. In 1892, it was elected as a royal residence on an equal footing with Vienna.

The city is the center of Hungary's political, social, and intellectual life, a trend that began largely in 1848 when it became the seat of the legislature. It is administered by an elected municipal council of 400 members and serves as the see of a Roman Catholic archbishop. The city's population reflects the heterogeneous elements of the Habsburg dominions. Furthermore, Budapest has been the primary site for the movement to Magyarize all institutions, resulting in the removal of German names from streets and buildings.

## Education and Culture

As the intellectual capital of Hungary, Budapest is home to several major educational and scientific institutions. The university of Budapest—the only one in Hungary proper—was established in 1635 at Tyrnau, moved to Buda in 1777, and transferred to Pest in 1783. Since 1867, it has been transformed into a national Hungarian seat of learning to encourage students to remain in the country rather than attending universities in Vienna. By 1900, the university had nearly 200 professors and lecturers and 4,983 students.

Other notable institutions include:
*   **The Polytechnic:** A center for scientific training with 1,816 students in 1900.
*   **Specialized Academies:** A veterinary academy, a commercial academy (which added an academy for oriental languages in 1899), military academies for Hungarian officers, a Protestant theological college, a Roman Catholic seminary, and a rabbinical institute.
*   **Scientific Societies:** The academy of sciences, founded in 1825 to encourage the study of various sciences and the Hungarian language (excluding theology).
*   **Cultural Institutions:** The national museum, founded in 1807 through donations from Count Stephan Széchényi, and the Kisfaludy society, founded in 1836 to cultivate the Hungarian language through literary prizes and the publication of folklore.

## Trade and Industry

Budapest is the most important town in Hungary for commerce and industry, ranking second only to Vienna in commerce within the Austro-Hungarian monarchy. Its commercial importance is largely due to its position on the Danube; the introduction of steamboats in 1830 provided a significant stimulus to the corn trade. The city is one of the world's greatest milling centers, utilizing local inventions in flour-milling machinery.

Principal industries include the manufacture of machinery, railway plants, chemicals, cutlery, gold and silver wares, carriages, bricks, jute, and distilling, as well as steam flour-milling. Major trade goods include corn, flour, wool, wood, hides, spirits, wines, cattle, and pigs. The Hungarian government's efforts to establish home industry have led to a remarkable increase in joint-stock companies: from 28 companies with £2,224,900 in capital in 1873, to 242 companies with £31,378,655 in capital by 1899.

## Infrastructure and Architecture

To prevent disastrous floods like those seen in 1838, the Hungarian government spent seven million sterling between 1867 and 1895 on river works. This included artificially blocking the Soroksar branch of the Danube so the river flows through Budapest in a single channel.

The city's architecture includes several significant religious sites:
*   **Buda:** The Matthias church, begun in the 13th century and restored between 1890 and 1896, served as a mosque during the Turkish occupation and was the site of Franz Joseph's coronation as king of Hungary in 1867. Other sites include the 13th-century Gothic garrison church and the Reformed church (finished 1898).
*   **Pest:** The Leopoldstadt Basilica (begun 1851) and the Franzstadt church (1874) are prominent Romanesque buildings. The parish church in the Eskü-Ter, built in 1500, is the oldest in Pest. The city also features synagogues in the Moorish style (1861) and Moorish-Byzantine style (1872).
*   **Turkish Influence:** A small octagonal Turkish mosque remains in Buda, preserved by a special article in the treaty of Karlowitz of 1699.
