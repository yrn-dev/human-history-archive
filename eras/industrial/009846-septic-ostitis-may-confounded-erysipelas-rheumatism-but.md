# Ostitis

Ostitis is the medical term for the inflammation of bone. Derived from the Greek *osteon* (bone), the condition is categorized as either acute or chronic.

## Acute Ostitis
Acute ostitis, also known as septic osteomyelitis, is a serious disease primarily affecting young people. The term septic osteomyelitis specifies both the cause and the exact location of the inflammation, which occurs in the marrow (*myelos*) and the delicate growing tissue of the bone. 

The inflammation is caused by the micro-organism *Staphylococcus pyogenes aureus*. These germs are characterized by a yellow tinge and are virulent, pus-producing organisms that collect in clusters resembling grapes. Typically, these germs enter the blood-stream through the gullet, the membrane lining the mouth, or other parts of the alimentary canal. In the era before antiseptic practices, these germs frequently entered sawn bone during limb amputations, often resulting in death or blood-poisoning. While staphylococci may circulate harmlessly in the blood of a healthy individual until they are consumed by white corpuscles, an injured bone provides an attractive focus for the germs.

### Symptoms and Progression
Initial signs often manifest as aches or pains near a joint, which are frequently misidentified as rheumatic. These symptoms may appear following exposure to cold, wet, unusual fatigue, injury, or during convalescence from typhoid or scarlet fever. The affected area becomes red, hot, swollen, and excessively tender; notably, this tenderness is located in the bone and the periosteum (the engorged membrane surrounding the bone) rather than the skin. 

Systemic symptoms may include violent delirium, shiverings, convulsions, and a temperature reaching 104 degrees. If the condition progresses, abscesses may form, and the patient may die from blood-poisoning, pericarditis, or septic pneumonia.

### Diagnosis and Treatment
Septic ostitis may be confounded with rheumatism and erysipelas; however, it can be distinguished by the presence of tenderness and central thickening.

Treatment requires prompt recognition and surgical intervention. An incision is made down to the bone to scrape out the affected area, which is then disinfected using a solution of corrosive sublimate. Because sub-periosteal abscesses may strip a considerable area of bone bare, necrosis is likely. Surgeons may need to open the shaft of the bone for much of its length to clear pus and germs. In severe cases, the diaphysis (the section of bone between the joint-ends) may be found loose within a large abscess cavity. Immediate amputation of the limb may be necessary to save the patient's life, or may be required later due to grave constitutional disturbance and long-continued suppuration. In some instances, inflammatory engorgement and devastating abscesses may cause multiple necrosis, where several bones are killed outright simultaneously.

## Chronic Ostitis
Chronic ostitis and periostitis are characterized by increased vascular supply and long-continued inflammation, typically found in the shafts of the bones. This condition may be caused by rheumatism, syphilis, or injury. Symptoms include a dull pain in the bone that worsens at night, as well as tenderness and thickening of the inflamed bone. This resulting lump is termed a "hard node," which is clearly visible via X-rays.

Treatment for chronic ostitis involves keeping the affected limb elevated and rested. Pain may be eased through the use of fomentations and leeches, while iodide of potassium is cited as the most useful medication.

## Tuberculous Ostitis and Caries
Chronic inflammation of tuberculous origin affects the spongy ends of long bones, the vertebrae, and the bones of the feet and hands. In this form of ostitis, bacilli in the spongy tissue cause colorless corpuscles from the blood to collect around them, forming small greyish-white heaps called tubercles. When these tubercles exist in large numbers and replace living tissue, it produces a "rarefying ostitis."

When these tubercles break down, they form tuberculous abscesses that slowly find escape to the surface. These should not be allowed to open spontaneously, as they may become infected with pus-producing germs, accelerating destruction. Treatment involves placing the part on a splint for absolute rest, providing fresh air, and prescribing iron and cod-liver oil. If an abscess advances toward the surface despite these measures, a surgeon should cut the area open, scrape out the foci, and use a strong antiseptic lotion. Tuberculin injections may also be considered.

When tubercles run together and break down the cancellous tissue, the condition is called caries (meaning decay or rottenness). While caries generally refers to tuberculous ostitis, the term has also been applied to syphilitic ulceration of bone.
