# Roebling Suspension Bridge

The Roebling suspension bridge, specifically the Niagara bridge constructed between 1852 and 1855 by J. A. Roebling, was considered one of the most remarkable bridges built using the suspension system. At the time of its construction, its span of 821 ft. was the largest of any railway bridge. The structure reached a height of 245 ft. above the river.

## Structural Design and Components
The bridge utilized four suspension cables, each measuring 10 in. in diameter. These cables were composed of seven strands, with each strand containing 520 parallel wires, totaling 3,640 wires per cable. To support these cables, each one was placed on a separate saddle equipped with rollers on each pier.

The bridge featured a stiffening girder that was primarily constructed of timber. This girder was box-shaped and braced, measuring 25 ft. wide and 18 ft. deep. The design allowed the bridge to carry a roadway within the girder and a railway on top.

## Modifications and Replacement
Over time, the bridge underwent various strengthenings and repairs. In 1880, the original timber girder was replaced with an iron one. However, by 1896-1897, the bridge was dismantled because it lacked the necessary strength to handle the increasing weight of railway traffic.

The Roebling suspension bridge was replaced by the Niagara Falls and Clifton steel arch. This successor bridge features a centre span designed as a two-hinged parabolic braced rib arch, with side spans measuring 210 ft. and 190 ft. The steel arch bridge carries two footways, two roadways, and two electric-car tracks. The main span of this replacement structure weighed 1,629 tons, while the side spans weighed 166 and 154 tons.

## Engineering Context
The replacement steel arch bridge was noted by Prof. Claxton Fidler as representing the furthest advance in its type of construction, particularly regarding the arrangement used to apply initial stress to the top chord. This method was employed to annul the bending action and excess compression in the upper member at the crown that typically occurs when a rib is erected on centering without initial stress.

The use of parallel wires in suspension cables was a preference of Roebling, who considered them to be 10% stronger than twisted wires. This preference is also evident in other major works, such as the Brooklyn bridge (begun in 1872), which utilized cables composed of 19 strands of 278 parallel steel wires.
