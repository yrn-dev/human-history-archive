# Frederick Augustus Porter Barnard

## Early Life and Education
Frederick Augustus Porter Barnard (1809–1889) was an American educationalist and scientist. He was born on May 5, 1809, in Sheffield, Massachusetts. Barnard attended Yale, where he graduated second on the honour list in 1828.

## Early Teaching Career
Following his graduation, Barnard served as a tutor at Yale. He then transitioned into specialized education, working as a teacher from 1831 to 1832 at the American Asylum for the Deaf and Dumb located in Hartford, Connecticut. He continued this work in a similar capacity from 1832 to 1838 as a teacher at the New York Institute for the Instruction of the Deaf and Dumb.

## Academic Appointments in the South
In 1838, Barnard entered university instruction at the University of Alabama. He served as the professor of natural philosophy and mathematics from 1838 to 1848. Subsequently, from 1848 to 1854, he held the position of professor of natural history and chemistry, and for a period of two years, he also filled the chair of English literature.

In 1854, Barnard moved to the University of Mississippi, where he was appointed professor of natural philosophy and mathematics. He rose to become the chancellor of the institution in 1856, a position he held until the start of the Civil War. Due to his sympathies with the North, he resigned his post and relocated to Washington.

## Public Service and Presidency of Columbia College
While in Washington, Barnard was in charge of the United States Coast Survey's chart and map department for a period of time. In 1864, he was appointed the tenth president of Columbia College in New York City (now known as Columbia University). He remained in this role until the year before his death, serving longer than any of his predecessors.

Under Barnard's leadership, Columbia College experienced rapid growth. His tenure saw the establishment of new departments, the extension of the elective system, and the creation of more adequate provisions for original research and graduate study. During this time, student enrolment increased from approximately 150 to more than 1,000.

## Advocacy and Legacy
Barnard was a proponent of expanding educational privileges to women as well as men. Following his death, Barnard College for women was established in his honour. 

Described as a versatile man of catholic training, Barnard was a chemist, physicist, mathematician, and a scholar of both English and classical studies. He was recognized as a vigorous, though sometimes prolix, writer on various topics and a capable public speaker; his annual reports to the Board of Trustees of Columbia were noted for being particular.

Barnard was also ordained as a deacon in the Protestant Episcopal Church in 1854. He died in New York City on April 27, 1889.
