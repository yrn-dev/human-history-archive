# Imperial Conference

The Imperial Conference was a series of high-level meetings between the British government and its colonies designed to seek and accept colonial opinion on critical matters of imperial organization and defence. Originally established as the Colonial Conferences, beginning with a meeting in 1887, the title of these gatherings was officially changed to "Imperial Conference" in 1907.

## Evolution of Imperial Consolidation
The development of these conferences occurred against a shifting backdrop of British colonial policy. Between 1845 and 1875, the "Manchester school" exerted a dominant influence on public affairs, promoting the fullest development of colonial self-government. During this era, it was generally accepted that self-governing institutions served as a preliminary to an inevitable separation from the empire. This period was marked by a general inclination to withdraw from imperial responsibilities, which provided foreign nations with opportunities to profit.

A reaction against this trend emerged as foreign enterprise increased pressure on British interests. Discussions regarding the value of an imperial federation for common interests began to surface, with New Zealand speaking of it as early as 1852 and the general association of Australian colonies putting forward a similar suggestion in London in 1857. The Royal Colonial Institution was founded in 1868 under the motto "United Empire." By 1872, Lord Beaconsfield repudiated the idea that self-government led to separation, instead describing colonial constitutions as part of a "great policy of imperial consolidation." In 1875, W. E. Forster advocated for imperial federation to replace dependence with association, later serving as the first president of the Imperial Federation League, founded in 1884.

## Imperial Defence and Naval Contributions
A primary focus of the Imperial Conferences was the question of defence, which was considered of sovereign importance to the empire's continued existence. The fundamental axiom of British defence was the "two-power standard," which required the British navy to maintain a 10% margin of strength over the joint strength of the two next most powerful navies.

Historically, the cost of this naval armament was borne exclusively by taxpayers in the United Kingdom. However, as the colonies recognized the fleet's value as insurance for British commerce, a desire emerged to contribute to a "truly imperial navy." This resulted in various subsidies:
* **Australasia:** Voted £126,000 per annum in 1895, which was increased to £240,000 in 1902.
* **Cape Colony:** Offered £30,000 a year in 1897, increased to £50,000 later.
* **Natal:** Voted £35,000 a year.
* **Newfoundland:** Voted £3,000.

Despite these contributions and the establishment of an Australian torpedo-boat flotilla in 1908-1909, the bulk of the imperial navy's cost remained with the British islands. The urgency of this issue was heightened in 1909 by revelations regarding the increase of the Austrian and German fleets, leading to a wave of colonial enthusiasm and offers of "Dreadnoughts" from New Zealand and other regions.

## Trade and Tariff Reform
The Imperial Conferences also served as forums for discussing commercial relations and "colonial preference." At the 1887 Colonial Conference in London, a South African delegate proposed a preferential system that would impose a 2% duty on all foreign goods to fund the imperial navy.

By 1894, at the Colonial Conference in Ottawa, it was expressed that injury to Great Britain's foreign trade in manufactured goods would necessarily react upon the colonies, as it would shrink the home markets for colonial raw materials. Canada led the way in this new imperial sentiment by introducing an imperial tariff in 1897, granting a 25% deduction on customs duties for British goods without requiring reciprocal advantages. This preferential treatment was later adopted by New Zealand, Australia, and the South African states.

In 1903, Mr. Chamberlain initiated a movement for tariff reform to protect imperial trade and give preference to colonial goods. While the Liberal government that took power in 1906 opposed measures inconsistent with free trade, the movement for colonial preference continued to gain ground. At the Imperial Conference of 1907, all colonial premiers advocated for this preference, citing the progress their own states had made in granting tariff preferences to British goods and to one another.
