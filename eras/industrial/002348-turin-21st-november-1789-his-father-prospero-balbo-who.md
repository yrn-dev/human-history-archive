# Cesare Balbo

## Early Life and Family
Cesare Balbo, a statesman and writer, was born in Turin on November 21, 1789. He was born into a noble Piedmontese family; his father, Prospero Balbo, held a high position within the Sardinian court and served as the mayor of the capital at the time of Cesare's birth. His mother belonged to the Azeglio family and passed away when Cesare was three years old. Following her death, he was raised in the home of his great-grandmother, the countess of Bugino.

## Career under the Napoleonic Empire
In 1798, Balbo joined his father in Paris. Between 1808 and 1814, he served the Napoleonic empire in various capacities across several locations, including Rome, Paris, Florence, and Illyria.

## Service to Piedmont and Political Exile
Following the fall of Napoleon, Balbo entered the service of his native country. During a period when his father was appointed as the minister of the interior, Cesare joined the army and carried out political missions to London and Paris. 

In 1821, a revolution broke out. Although Balbo disapproved of the revolution, he was suspected of sympathizing with it and was consequently forced into exile. While he was permitted to return to Piedmont shortly thereafter, he was denied all further public service.

## Literary Work and Political Ideals
Denied political appointments despite frequent efforts to obtain one, Balbo turned to literature as his sole remaining method for influencing the destiny of his country. This transition resulted in a body of literary work characterized by incompleteness and fitfulness, often containing temporary or practical elements even in his most elaborate writings.

The primary objective of Balbo's intellectual labors was to help secure Italian independence from foreign control. He did not desire or expect true Italian unity; instead, he was devoted to the house of Savoy, which he believed was destined to change Italy's fate. Balbo's genuine ideal was a confederation of separate states under the supremacy of the pope.

In his work *Speranze* (or *Hopes of Italy*), Balbo addressed what he considered the first essential of independence—a point he felt was neglected by Gioberti in the *Primato*. In this text, Balbo suggested that Austria should seek compensation in the Balkans to offset the inevitable loss of its Italian provinces. He emphasized the necessity of patience, alertness, and both moral and military preparation.
