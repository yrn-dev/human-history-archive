# Geodetic Triangulation and Exact Surveys in India

## Overview of Indian Geodetic Triangulation
In India, geodetic triangulation serves as the fundamental basis for the execution of exact surveys. By the close of the 19th century, the final links of the great geodetic triangulation of India were forged regarding the peninsula. These surveys extend eastward to the eastern boundaries of Burma, reaching a longitude of approximately 100 degrees East.

## Extensions and Geographical Impact
Beyond the primary triangulation, minor extensions have been implemented using systems of varying regularity. These extensions have pushed the boundaries of surveyed territory outwards in all directions:
* **West and South:** The surveys reach through Baluchistan and Afghanistan to the eastern districts of Persia, as well as along the coast of Makran to the coast of Arabia.
* **North and East:** The extensions have long included the farther mountain peaks of Nepal and have branched further into Siam and towards western China.

These far-reaching extensions provide the necessary basis for a vast amount of exploratory survey work of a strictly geographical nature. Consequently, they have played a significant role in raising the standard of accuracy for geographical surveys in Asia to a level that was considered unattainable fifty years prior.

## Connection to Global Systems
Despite the progress within the Indian peninsula, a geodetic connection with European systems has yet to be accomplished. To determine longitudes differentially with Greenwich, the telegraphic method has been employed. Since 1890, a more rigorous application of this method resulted in a slight correction of about 2" of arc to previous determinations. This specific determination was carried out through four distinct arcs:
1. Greenwich to Potsdam
2. Potsdam to Teheran
3. Teheran to Bushire
4. Bushire to Karachi

## Comparative Context in Asia
The state of exact surveys in India contrasts with other regions in Asia. In Russia, triangulation-based exact surveys extend as far east as Chinese Turkestan (approximately 75 degrees East of Greenwich). However, Russian surveys in Asia are separated from those in Europe by the Caspian Sea. Due to the arid nature of the trans-Caspian deserts, rigorous geodetic methods were hindered, leaving Russian geography in central Asia dependent on means other than direct measurement for coordinate values. While the Tashkent observatory serves as the starting point for the trans-Caspian triangulation, it is ranked as second-class and extends to the Pamir frontier beyond Osh.

While western Asia (excluding Arabia) was considered free from geographical perplexity by the end of the 19th century, other regions such as eastern Siberia, Mongolia, and China continued to have enormous areas where geographical knowledge remained in a primitive stage of nebulous uncertainty.
