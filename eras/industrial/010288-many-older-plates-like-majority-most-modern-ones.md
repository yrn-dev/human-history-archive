# Book-Plates: Styles and Evolution

Book-plates, also referred to as *ex-libris*, serve as private tokens of book-ownership. The study and collection of these items is a comparatively modern pursuit, having gained traction around 1875. Significant momentum was provided by the 1880 publication of the *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates* by Lord de Tabley, which established a general classification of styles.

## Historical Development in England
The English book-plate reflects the prevailing tastes in decorative art across different epochs, from the late Elizabethan to the late Victorian periods. The gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon is the oldest discovered English example, though a hand-painted armorial device belonging to Henry VIII is noted as not fitting the modern definition of a book-plate. The next oldest is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585.

Until the last quarter of the 17th century, authentic English plates were limited in number and characterized by a remarkable simplicity that lacked German elaborateness. These were typically plain and armorial, featuring symmetrical mantling and occasional wreaths or palms. Following the Restoration, book-plates became an established accessory for well-ordered libraries. While they maintained simple heraldic arrangements, they introduced two distinct changes: the use of tincture lines and dots (a method devised by Petra Sancta in the mid-century) and more elaborate mantling that resembled the periwigs of the era. This style was imported from France, and English decoration continued to follow French taste until the dawn of the French Revolution.

## Classification of Styles
According to the classification established by Lord de Tabley, book-plate styles are categorized as follows:
*   **Early Armorial:** Examples previous to the Restoration, such as the Nicholas Bacon plate.
*   **Jacobean:** A term including the heavy decorative manner of the early Georgian, Queen Anne, and Restoration days, exemplified by the Lansanor plate (1698).
*   **Chippendale:** A style described as rococo, represented by the plate of P.A. Convers (1762).
*   **Wreath and Ribbon:** A style belonging to the period of the urn.

## Pictorial and Modern Designs
Many of the older plates, similar to the majority of the most modern ones, were essentially pictorial. In these designs, the armorial element is secondary. Notable English pictorial genres include "book-piles" and "the library interior," as seen in the 1699 *ex-libris* of William Hewer. Other pictorial types include portrait-plates (such as those of John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys), landscapes by wood engravers of the Bewick school, and allegories engraved by George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth.

In recent years, the creation of personal book-tokens has been recognized as a minor branch of a higher art. Modern designs have shifted away from purely heraldic elements, allowing symbolic or allegorical elements to assert themselves more strongly. Prominent modern English artists in this field include Charles Ricketts, J.D. Batten, G.W. Eve, C.W. Sherborn, Robert Anning Bell, Erat Harrison, J. Forbes Nixon, John Vinycomb, John Leighton, and Warrington Hogg.

## Production and Accessibility
Historically, the design of book-plates was almost exclusively the work of heraldic stationers and was restricted to important collections or ancestral libraries. The advent of process work has popularized the medium by making the reproduction of elaborate designs cheaper and easier; consequently, the great majority of modern plates are reproduced by process. 

Despite this, some artists continue to use the graver. Notable engravers include G.W. Eve and C.W. Sherborn in England, and in America, E.D. French (New York City), K.W.F. Hopson (New Haven, Conn.), and J.W. Spenceley (Boston, Mass.).
