# Accountants in England and Wales

## The Institute of Chartered Accountants
The Institute of Chartered Accountants in England and Wales maintains a library that is of significant importance to the bibliography of book-keeping. This library is noted for likely containing the most complete collection of both modern and ancient works on accounting currently in existence, encompassing texts from both foreign and British sources.

## Notable Accounting Literature
Within the field of accounting and book-keeping, several works are identified as being of general interest. These include:
*   *Book-keeping* by G. van de Linde (1898)
*   *Book-keeping* (5th edition, 1906) and *Advanced Accounting* (2nd edition, 1905) by L.R. Dicksee
*   *Encyclopaedia of Accounting* (1903), edited by G. Lisle
*   *Accountants' Library* (1901), edited by the editor of *The Accountant*
*   *The Antiquity of Book-keeping* (1898) by J.W. Heaps
*   *History of Accounting and Accountants* (1905), edited by R. Brown

## Related Financial Infrastructure
The professional environment of accounting in England and Wales is linked to the broader banking system. The number of banking offices in these regions saw a significant increase between 1844 and 1906, growing from 976 offices to more than 5,880. By 1906, the banking business in England was primarily conducted by approximately sixty joint-stock banks and ten private banks, although some joint-stock organizations functioned as private firms.

The Bank Act of 1844 sought to establish a monopoly for the Bank of England as the sole issuer of notes in England and Wales. Consequently, by 1906, nearly all provincial banks in England had lost their right of issue.
