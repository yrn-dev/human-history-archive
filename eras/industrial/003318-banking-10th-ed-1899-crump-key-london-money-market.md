# Banking and the London Money Market

## Historical Development of Banking
English banking can be traced back to the 16th century, originating with the money dealings of London goldsmiths. However, the trajectory of banking in the United Kingdom was fundamentally shaped by the establishment of the Bank of England. Founded in 1694, the bank began as a loan to the government totaling £1,200,000, which was subscribed to between June 21 and July 2, 1694. Sir John Houblon was appointed governor on July 10, 1694, with Michael Godfrey serving as deputy-governor. At the time of its inception, some expressed fears that the bank might collaborate with William III to make the prince absolute and render parliaments useless.

On a broader scale, rudimentary money markets existed in ancient civilizations, including Egypt, Chaldea, republican Rome, and the Greek cities and Carthage. In Rome, there was a changing rate of interest and a specific code of law. However, highly organized markets only emerged more recently as Europe formed organized states. These developed in cities such as Venice, Genoa, Basel, Augsburg, the Hanse towns, London, and various cities in Portugal, Spain, and the Low Countries. These early markets were precarious because they relied on individuals possessing actual cash. This made them vulnerable to "necessitous potentates" who might pressure merchants for loans. In one instance, Charles II took stores of London goldsmiths that had been left in the Mint for safety. By the middle of the 17th century, a real market for money had established itself in many European cities.

## The London Money Market
In the 18th century, the London money market consisted of merchants, various banks, and the Bank of England, with no complete distinction between them. The modern form of the market began to emerge toward the end of the century with the appearance of bill brokers, who were dealers in credit. Further development was influenced by the reconstitution of the Bank of England via the Act of 1844 and the formation of discount companies and joint-stock banks.

In the daily terminology of the City, "the market" is often used in a special sense to refer specifically to discount houses and banks, or sometimes only to discount houses. This is evident in newspaper reports stating that "the market" has borrowed from or renewed loans with the Bank of England.

By the beginning of the 20th century, the working organs of the London money market were divided into two groups:
*   **Group A (Constantly Active):** The Bank of England; private and joint-stock banks (including great foreign banks); and bill-brokers and discount houses.
*   **Group B (Intermittently Active):** Certain finance houses and great merchants, as well as certain members of the Stock Exchange.

Many of the great merchant houses are registered as bankers, though they primarily focus on finance business, such as issuing capital for companies and making loans to foreign governments.

## Operations and Mechanisms
The "Bank Rate" is the term used in financial circles to describe the rate of discount charged by the leading bank or state in chief monetary centres, as opposed to the open-market rate. 

The Bank of England issues notes printed from surface-plates in black with water-marks for protection. These notes are not reissued once returned to the bank and have a short average life of approximately six weeks. Because the lowest denomination is £5 and forged notes are difficult to negotiate in England, the bank's issues remain comparatively immune to forgery.

The turnover of cheques is vast. In 1839, the London clearings totaled £954,401,600 across 29 banks. By 1899, this figure rose to £9,150,269,000 with 19 banks, and by 1906, it reached £12,711,334,000 with 18 banks. The total yearly turnover of cheques is estimated to be at least twice the amount registered by the London Clearing House when accounting for exchanges in Scotland, Ireland, large English towns, and internal bank settlements.

## Modern Market Examples
The handling of large war loans raised by Japan between 1904 and 1906 illustrates modern money market management. These loans were issued by three banks: a Japanese institution representing the government, a leading London bank, and the principal British bank operating in China. To minimize disturbance to the London market, "application money" withdrawn from other banks was lent back into the short loan market, often on the afternoon of the issue. 

The Japanese government managed its huge balances considerately to avoid embarrassing the London market. While the government promised not to withdraw the loans in gold, it transferred a portion of the deposits to the Bank of England.
