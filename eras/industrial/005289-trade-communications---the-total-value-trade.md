# Trade and Communications

The evolution of global trade and communications at the turn of the 20th century was characterized by the integration of new technologies, such as steam shipping, electricity, and railroads, which significantly accelerated the movement of goods and information.

## Technological Impacts on Global Commerce
The introduction of railroads and industrial machinery stimulated production and manufactures, while steam shipping and electricity transformed the movement of trade. In the 19th century, a journey to Australia took eight months, and business communications between London and Sydney often took a year for a response. By the beginning of the 20th century, the journey was reduced to thirty-one days, and telegraphic despatches allowed essential business to be transacted within twenty-four hours.

These advancements allowed a single ship to carry six times as many cargoes per year as was possible at the start of the 19th century. The ability to realize profits in two months rather than a year, combined with daily telegraphic communication, created a new element in imperial trade. This development enabled the exploitation of alternating seasons between the northern and southern hemispheres. Perishable commodities—including meat, poultry, eggs, butter, and fruit—now flow between hemispheres in increasing quantities. For example, the butter season in Australasia runs from October to March, while the season in northern Europe and Ireland runs from March to October. Following the introduction of ice-chambers in steamers, Victoria and New South Wales established a yearly butter trade with Great Britain valued at £1,000,000 without seriously impacting Danish and Irish markets.

## Regional Trade in Asia and the Middle East
In Assam, external trade is conducted via native boat, steamer, and rail. In the Brahmaputra valley, steamers handle 94% of imports and 86% of exports, while native boats carry approximately 43% of both in the Surma valley. For 1904-1905, exports were valued at 726 lakhs of rupees (chiefly tea, jute, coal, timber, tea-seed, oil-seeds, and rice in the husk), and imports were valued at 457 lakhs of rupees (including salt, sugar, iron and steel, tobacco, cotton twist, yarn, brass, copper, grain, pulse, rice not in the husk, and cotton piece-goods). Two-thirds of this trade is conducted with Calcutta. Trade through Chittagong is increasing due to the incorporation of the port into the province of Eastern Bengal and Assam and the opening of the Assam-Bengal railway's hill-section.

In Kuwet, total trade for 1904-1905 was valued at £160,000, with imports including coffee, sugar, rice, piece goods, and arms and ammunition, while exports included wool, dates, pearls, and horses. British India Steamship Company steamers call there fortnightly. In China, the town of Lungchow was opened in 1889 as a seat of frontier trade; its trade value rose from £20,000 in 1898 to £56,692 in 1904.

## African Trade and Infrastructure
In Lourenço Marques, the capital of Portuguese East Africa, the harbor is equipped with electric cranes, quays, and piers, allowing steamers to discharge cargo directly into railway trucks. The port serves as the nearest seaport to the Rand gold mines, with over 50% of Johannesburg's import trade passing through it. Chief imports include liquors, food-stuffs, live stock, timber, mineral oils, iron, and dyes. Most of these are forwarded to the Transvaal. Sea exports are limited primarily to coal from the Transvaal's Middelburg collieries, though a flourishing timber trade exists at Port Matolla.

In Uganda, exports—which comprise the larger part of external trade—include fibre, cotton, beeswax, chillies, ivory, rubber, potatoes, grains, skins, hides, and copra. Exports rose from £89,858 in 1900-1901 to £234,664 in 1904-1905. In 1906-1907, total trade volume was £1,194,352. The United States takes 33% of exports, and Great Britain takes 15%. Imports consist largely of manufactured goods, hardware, and textiles from Europe and India, with the latter two providing over 50% of total imports.

## Trade in Europe and South America
Bulgarian commerce has seen the decline of the primitive barter system and the diminished importance of great fairs (panaïri), such as those in Dobritch and Eski-Jumaia. In 1887, foreign commerce was valued at £4,419,589. Export seats include Baltchik, Burgas, and Varna on the Black Sea, and Vidin, Rakhovo, Silistria, Nikopolis, Rustchuk, and Svishtov on the Danube. New ports were opened at Burgas (1904) and Varna (1906).

In Bolivia, trade is limited by the poverty of the population and geographical constraints. In 1904, imports were £1,734,551 and exports were £1,851,758. However, 1903 export figures totaled £2,453,638, dominated by tin and its ores (£1,039,298) and silver and its ores (£636,743), followed by rubber, copper ores, coca, bismuth, and cinchona.
