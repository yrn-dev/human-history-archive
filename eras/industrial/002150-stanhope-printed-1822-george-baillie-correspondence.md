# George Baillie and the Baillie Family of Jerviswood

The history of the Baillie family of Jerviswood is characterized by political turmoil, patriotic struggle, and a legacy of literary and historical records. Central to this history are Robert Baillie of Jerviswood and his son, George Baillie.

## Robert Baillie of Jerviswood
Robert Baillie, known as Baillie of Jerviswood, was the son of George Baillie of St. John's Kirk in Lanarkshire. He was described by his cousin, Bishop Burnet, as a man of great virtue and piety, learned in languages, mathematics, and the law, and possessing Presbyterian principles.

Baillie's conflicts with the Scottish government began in June 1676 when he rescued his brother-in-law, a Presbyterian minister named Kirkton, who had been illegally confined by an informer named Carstairs. For this action, Baillie was imprisoned for four months and fined L500, half of which he paid to Carstairs to secure his liberation.

In 1683, distressed by the state of his country, Baillie considered emigrating to South Carolina, though this plan failed. That same year, he traveled to London to communicate with Russell, Monmouth, and their party to seek redress. Following the discovery of the Rye House Plot, Baillie was arrested. Although he denied knowledge of the conspiracy, he admitted to the king that he had been consulted regarding a potential insurrection in Scotland. Despite a lack of evidence connecting him to the plot, he was kept in close confinement and fined L6000. On December 23, 1684, while in a languishing state, he was charged with treason. He was found guilty the following day and hanged at the market cross in Edinburgh on December 24, 1684. His treatment is remembered as one of the worst crimes of the Stuart administration in Scotland.

## George Baillie and Lady Grizel Baillie
George Baillie, the son of Robert Baillie, took refuge in Holland following his father's execution. He eventually returned to Scotland with William III and was restored to his estates. In 1692, George married Lady Grizel Baillie.

Lady Grizel Baillie (1665–1746) was a Scottish song-writer and the eldest daughter of Sir Patrick Hume (later the Earl of Marchmont). Born at Redbraes Castle, Berwickshire, on December 25, 1665, she demonstrated early loyalty to the Baillie family; at age twelve, she delivered letters to Robert Baillie while he was imprisoned. During a period when the king's troops occupied Redbraes Castle, Grizel supplied her father with food while he remained in hiding in a churchyard. After Robert Baillie's execution in 1684, the Hume family fled to Holland, returning to Scotland at the Revolution.

Lady Grizel died on December 6, 1746. She is noted for her songs, including the famous "And werena my heart light I wad dee," which first appeared in *Orpheus Caledonius* (1725). Some of her work was also printed in Allan Ramsay's *Tea-Table Miscellany*.

## Literary Legacy and Correspondence
The lives of George and Grizel Baillie have been preserved through various publications and manuscripts. Their daughter, Lady Murray of Stanhope (who married Sir Alexander Murray of Stanhope), possessed a manuscript of her mother's prose and verse. Lady Murray also wrote the *Memoirs of the Lives and Characters of the Right Hon. George Baillie of Jerviswood and Lady Grisell Baillie*, which were printed in 1822.

Additionally, the *Correspondence* of George Baillie, covering the years 1702 to 1708, was edited by Lord Minto and published in 1842 for the Bannatyne Club. The family's history also influenced the work of Joanna Baillie, whose *Metrical Legends of Exalted Character* includes "The Legend of Lady Grizelda Baillie."
