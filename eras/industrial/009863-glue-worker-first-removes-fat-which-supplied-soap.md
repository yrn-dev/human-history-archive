# Industrial Utilization of Animal Bones

The industrial application of animal bones has been developed to a degree where every constituent of the bone—including mineral matter, fat, and gelatinous substances—is utilized in commerce. The primary components are processed into artificial manures, soaps, candles, glues, and various carved articles.

## Processing in Glue and Gelatin Works
In the glue-working process, the worker first removes the fat from the bones. This extracted fat is supplied to the candle and soap trades. Once the fat is removed, the bones are treated for the production of glue. The remaining residue is then processed for use as manure. These residues may be ground into a coarse or fine meal and supplied directly as fertilizer, or they may be treated with sulphuric acid to create superphosphates, which are more soluble and more readily assimilated by growing plants.

In certain regions, particularly South America, these residues are burned in a retort to produce a white "bone-ash." This commercial product contains approximately 70-80% tricalcium phosphate and is used both as a manure and in the manufacture of high-grade superphosphates.

In the gelatin industry, mineral matter is recovered from a solution of hydrochloric acid. The liquors are filtered to remove suspended matter and then mixed in vats with a neutralizer, such as milk of lime. This process first precipitates slightly soluble bicalcium phosphate ($\text{CaHPO}_4$), which, with additional lime, becomes ordinary tricalcium phosphate ($\text{Ca}_3(\text{PO}_4)_2$). The resulting cakes are filter-pressed and dried in heated chambers; this product serves as a valuable manure and is used in the manufacture of phosphorus.

## Manufacture of Bone Articles
While the majority of the world's bone supply is directed toward gelatin and glue works, specific bones—namely the knuckles and leg and thigh bones (termed "marrows")—are used to manufacture bone articles. The treatment for these bones differs from that used in glue-works:

1. **Preparation**: A saw is used to remove the ends of the bones.
2. **Steeping**: The bones are steeped in a 1% brine solution for three to four days to separate fibrous matter.
3. **Simmering**: The bones are heated with water and simmered for about six hours. This process removes gelatinous matter (which passes into solution) and fat (which rises as a scum). 

The gelatinous water is filtered and used to make size for cardboard boxes. The bones, now firm enough to be worked by a lathe, are scrubbed, dried, and transferred to the bone-worker. The skimmed fat is either bleached for use in fine soaps or, if unbleached, expressed as oil to be used as an adulterant for other oils. The solid matter, or stearine, is supplied to the candle-maker.

## Secondary Industrial Applications
Animal bones and other slaughtered animal parts contribute to various secondary enterprises. Small bones and hoofs provide the raw materials for glue, while large bones are carved into items such as knife-handles and buttons.

Beyond the primary processing of bones, animal fats are utilized in several other ways:
* **Food Substitutes**: Beef and other animal fats are used to create margarine (also known as butterine). One method involves heating minced beef suet with water, carbonate of potash, and fragments of fresh sheep's stomach. The pepsine in the stomach and the heat separate the fat from cellular tissue. This fat is then subjected to hydraulic pressure to separate stearin from oleomargarin, the latter of which is used for butter-making.
* **Lubricants**: Animal fats, such as tallow, are used in semi-solid lubricants due to their high degree of "greasiness." Lubricating greases are often produced by emulsifying vegetable and animal fats with water and soap. These are used for the bearings of slow-moving machinery and railway wagon axles.
* **Other By-products**: Dry distillation of bones that have only been partially stripped of gelatin produces a tarry distillate (yielding bone pitch and "bone oil") and a carbonaceous residue known as animal charcoal.

## Regional Industry Example: Illinois
In the United States, the state of Illinois became a leader in the wholesale slaughtering and packing of meats between 1870 and 1905. By 1900 and 1905, Illinois produced more than one-third of the total product of the United States in this industry. In 1900, this sector yielded 22.9% of the state's total manufactured product, and in 1905, it yielded 22.5%. This industry supported various secondary enterprises that ensured nearly all portions of the animal were used, including the conversion of fats into soap, lard, and butterine.
