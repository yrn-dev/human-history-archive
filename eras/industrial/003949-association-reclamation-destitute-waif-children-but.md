# Thomas John Barnardo and the Care of Destitute Children

## Origins and Motivation
Thomas John Barnardo (1845–1905) was an English philanthropist who dedicated his life to the establishment and direction of homes for destitute children. Born in Dublin, Ireland, to an English mother and a father of Spanish origin, Barnardo initially pursued a career in medicine. He studied at the London hospital, as well as in Edinburgh and Paris, eventually becoming a fellow of the Royal College of Surgeons. His original ambition was to qualify for medical missionary work in China.

However, his career path shifted in 1865 during an epidemic of cholera in the east end of London. While performing medical work, he became aware of the vast numbers of homeless and destitute children living in England's cities. With the encouragement and support of the first Earl Cairns and the seventh earl of Shaftesbury, Barnardo abandoned his plans for foreign missionary labor to focus on the welfare of these children.

## Establishment and Growth of the Homes
In 1867, Barnardo opened the first of "Dr Barnardo's Homes" in Stepney Causeway, London, which continued to serve as the institution's headquarters. The organization grew steadily over the following decades. By the time of Barnardo's death in 1905, the network had expanded to include 112 district "Homes" and various mission branches across the United Kingdom.

The primary objective of these institutions was to search for and receive "waifs and strays." Once admitted, the children were provided with food, clothing, and education. Where possible, the homes also provided industrial training tailored to the specific needs of each child.

## Admission Principles and Operational System
The institution operated on a principle of immediate and free admission. Barnardo maintained a policy of no restrictions regarding nationality, religion, sex, or age. The only requirement for admission was destitution; consequently, the homes received both the incurably diseased and those who were physically robust.

The care of the children was organized based on age and gender:
* **Infants and Young Children:** Younger boys and girls were primarily "boarded out" in rural districts.
* **Girls:** Those above fourteen years of age were sent to industrial training homes to learn useful domestic occupations.
* **Boys (13–17):** Boys in this age bracket were trained for various trades based on their physical or mental fitness.
* **Boys (17+):** Boys over seventeen were first tested in labour homes before being emigrated, sent to sea, or placed in domestic employment.
