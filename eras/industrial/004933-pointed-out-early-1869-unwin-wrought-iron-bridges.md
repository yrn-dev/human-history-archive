# Metallic Bridge Construction and Engineering

The evolution of bridge construction has transitioned from classical and medieval materials, such as timber, masonry, brick, and concrete, to the use of metals. Starting in the late 18th century, wrought iron began to be utilized, often in combination with cast iron or timber. The introduction of metal has enabled the construction of great spans ranging from 500 to 1800 feet.

## Materials and Their Evolution
Cast iron was initially used for arches and early railway bridge girders. In early braced bridges in America and England, cast iron—often in octagonal or circular tubes—was used for compression members, while wrought iron was used for tension members. By the middle of the 19th century, wrought iron was used on a large scale for suspension road bridges and entirely for great girder bridges, such as those at Saltash and the Menai Strait.

From 1880 onwards, mild steel, which possesses greater toughness and tenacity than wrought iron, began to supersede it for girders with spans exceeding 100 feet. More recently, ferro-concrete or concrete strengthened with steel bars has been introduced for arched bridges. Currently, cast iron is primarily used for arched bridges of moderate span.

## Bridge Types and Design
### Girder and Truss Bridges
Several bracing types for the web of girder bridges developed early on:
*   **Warren type:** Bracing bars form equilateral triangles.
*   **Whipple Murphy:** Features vertical struts and inclined ties.
*   **Lattice:** Both struts and ties are inclined, typically at 45° to the horizontal.

Specific examples include the Newark Dyke bridge (1851-1853), a pin-jointed Warren girder bridge with a span of 259 feet, which utilized cast iron hollow castings for the top flange and struts, and flat wrought iron links for the lower flange and ties. The Fink truss, an early American deck bridge type, used cast iron for compression and wrought iron for tension; however, this type disappeared because the use of long ties to transfer loads to the ends was uneconomical. In 1888, the Phoenix Bridge Company constructed the Ohio bridge on the Cincinnati & Covington railway, featuring a center span of 545 feet and a depth of 84 feet.

### Suspension and Arch Bridges
Suspension bridges have achieved significant spans. The Niagara bridge (1852-1855) had a span of 821 feet and four cables, each containing 3,640 parallel wires. The Clifton bridge over the Avon, completed in 1860 using chains from the Hungerford bridge, features three chains on each side supporting wrought iron stiffening girders.

Arch bridges have also utilized various metals. The Wearmouth bridge (1793-1796) used cast voussoirs to overcome the difficulty of casting heavy ribs. Other cast iron arch examples include the Southwark bridge (1814-1819) and the Austerlitz and Carrousel bridges in Paris. In 1858, M.C. Meigs erected an aqueduct bridge in Washington using two arched ribs formed by cast iron pipes.

## Engineering Principles and Stress Analysis
The calculation of working stress in metallic bars involves constants (n) varying from 1.3 to 2 depending on the quality of steel or iron. For mild steel or ductile iron, n is typically 1.5. The breaking stress diminishes as the range of variation in loading increases, a phenomenon referred to as "fatigue."

In 1869, Unwin proposed a rational method for fixing working stress based on the ratio of live load (B) to dead load (A). This method suggested that the factor of safety for live load stresses should be double that for dead load stresses. Unwin also provided a formula for the weight of main girders ($w_3$) based on total live load ($w_1$), platform weight ($w_2$), span ($l$), average stress ($s$), and the ratio of span to depth ($r$).

## Subaqueous Foundations
To reach hard strata through water-bearing or alluvial beds, engineers employ bottomless caissons sunk via excavation under compressed air. These structures feature a working chamber with a strong roof and an air-lock system to allow men and materials to enter and exit. Once a reliable stratum is reached, the chamber is filled with concrete. Notable applications of this method include:
*   **Brooklyn Suspension Bridge:** Timber caissons reaching rock at 78 and 45 feet below high-water.
*   **Forth Bridge:** Wrought iron caissons reaching rock between 64 and 89 feet below high-water.
*   **Eiffel Tower:** Wrought iron caissons sunk to a depth of 33 feet.
*   **Antwerp quay-walls:** Foundations constructed in the river Scheldt.
