# Artillery Brigades in Great Britain

In the context of military organization in Great Britain, an artillery "brigade"—which includes field, horse, and heavy varieties—is defined as a smaller unit compared to infantry or cavalry brigades. It is commanded by a lieutenant-colonel and consists of two or three batteries.

## General Brigade Organization
The term "brigade" (derived from French and German *brigade*, Italian *brigata*, and Spanish *brigada*) has been used in English since the early 17th century. Generally, a brigade is a military unit commanded by a colonel, brigadier-general, or major-general, and is composed of two or more regiments of artillery, cavalry, or infantry. 

While an artillery brigade in Great Britain is a relatively small command, other brigade types are larger:
*   **Infantry Brigades:** Typically consist of four battalions (approximately 4,000 bayonets) and include attached medical, transport, and supply units.
*   **Cavalry Brigades:** Comprised of two or three cavalry regiments.

The staff for infantry or cavalry brigades usually includes a brigade-major (a staff officer with duties between a general staff officer and an adjutant), an aide-de-camp, and the commanding brigadier.

## Logistics and Accommodation
The organization of horse and field artillery into batteries and brigades dictates the type of accommodation provided for them. This infrastructure is generally similar to standard military accommodations but includes specialized additions to meet the specific requirements of the unit, such as:
*   Vehicle sheds designed for ammunition wagons and guns.
*   Specialized shops for saddlers and wheelers.

## Related Military Units and Operations
Beyond the standard British army organization, other specialized brigades and artillery units have operated in various historical contexts:

### Marine Brigades and Artillery
The Royal Marine Artillery (R.M.A.) and marine infantry have served in several capacities. During the Crimean War, a marine infantry brigade occupied the heights of Balaclava, while R.M.A. personnel served with the Royal Artillery in the trenches before Sebastopol. In the China wars, brigades and batteries of the marine force played a prominent role. Other operations include:
*   **Indian Mutiny and Boer War:** Marine artillery and infantry served with the Naval Brigade.
*   **Egyptian and Sudan Campaigns (1881–1898):** R.M.A. gunners provided relief to the Royal Horse Artillery and, in one instance, constructed a functioning gun from captured enemy Krupp guns. In the final campaign, R.M.A. detachments assembled and fought gunboats that had been transported in pieces.

### Field and Heavy Artillery in Combat
Historical accounts highlight the distinction between different types of artillery fire. Field artillery is noted for its mobility and range, as seen in engagements where shells fell on roads and villages. Heavy artillery is often associated with more intense bombardments or close-range support. For example, during conflicts in Flanders, units faced "very heavy artillery fire," and in other engagements, "heavies" continued to fire on specific locations like Bouchavesnes after other guns had retired.
