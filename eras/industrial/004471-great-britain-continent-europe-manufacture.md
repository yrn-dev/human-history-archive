# Manufacture in Great Britain and Continental Europe

The industrial and manufacturing landscapes of Great Britain and the continent of Europe have been shaped by the utilization of diverse natural materials, the legacy of ancient techniques, and the pressures of industrial development.

## Wicker-work and Furniture Production
In the fabrication of wicker-work, various materials are utilized beyond the common use of willows. A significant material is the rattan of commerce, or the stem of *Calamus viminalis* and allied species, which is employed either whole or processed into skains. Since 1880, "cane-pith" or "cane-pulp"—the central pith of this material—has been used extensively across the continent of Europe and in Great Britain for the manufacture of finer classes of work and furniture. During this same period, enamelled skains of cane, as well as straw and plaited rush (which were often coloured), became common. These developments in basket-work eventually encroached upon the field of cabinet-making, as wood and nails began to be frequently used in the construction of tables, chairs, and other furniture.

## Willow Cultivation and Trade
Willow remains the primary source of materials for basket-making. While willows are grown extensively on the continent of Europe and exported in large quantities to the United States and Great Britain, English-grown rods are noted for their leathery and tough texture. The finest basket-making willows are cultivated in England, specifically in the valleys of the Trent and Thames, as well as in Nottinghamshire and Leicestershire. 

The British industry received early stimulation in the 19th century through premiums offered by the Society of Arts. Notable contributors to the field include Mr. William Scaling of Basford, Notts, who published pamphlets on cultivation, and Mr. W. P. Ellmore. Mr. Ellmore, a practical basket-maker, introduced valuable continental varieties and established a salicetum (willow plantation) near Leicester spanning approximately 100 acres.

## Ancient Manufacturing Legacies
The manufacture of bricks in Europe has roots in ancient practices. While the Greeks produced three kinds of bricks, they were not used to a great extent; the walls of Athens on the side towards Mount Hymettus are among the most important Greek brick-structures. The Romans, who likely acquired knowledge from the East during their occupation of Greece and Egypt, became masters of the brickmaker's art. Around the beginning of the Christian era, the Romans extended the manufacture of bricks, introducing kiln-burning methods and careful clay preparation. These methods were carried throughout western Europe, with abundant evidence of extensive brick-making in Britain and Germany.

## Industrial Development and Global Trade
The close of the nineteenth century was characterized by immense industrial developments, particularly in Germany, which was able to take full advantage of these advancements due to its predominance on the Continent. Meanwhile, progress in material science enabled Great Britain to spread a steamship empire across the world and contributed to the creation of a railway-and-steamboat republic in America.

In terms of external trade, Great Britain and India have been major suppliers of manufactured goods, hardware, and textiles to other regions. For example, in the trade of Uganda, Great Britain and India together supplied over 50% of total imports. In the export market for Uganda, Great Britain ranked second behind the United States, taking 15% of the exports.
