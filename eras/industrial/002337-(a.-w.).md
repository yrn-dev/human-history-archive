# Weight and Density Measurement

The measurement of weight and the determination of relative density (specific gravity) involve various mathematical formulas and physical methodologies, ranging from the engineering of bridge girders to the laboratory analysis of solids and liquids.

## Bridge Girder Weight Calculations
In bridge design, the weight of main girders ($W_g$) is calculated based on the live load ($W_l$) and the weight of the platform ($W_f$). The relationship is expressed as $W_g = (W_l + W_f)k / (1 - k)$. Because $W_l + W_f$ is known during the design phase, the value of $k(W_l + W_f)$ can be determined via a provisional design that neglects the weight of the girders. The final bridge members must then be scaled up from the provisional design by the ratio $k / (1 - k)$.

Empirical relations provided by Waddell allow for the comparison of girder weights per foot run ($w_1, w_2$) for different spans ($l_1, l_2$) and live loads ($p, p'$). For a live load $p$, the ratio is $w_2/w_1 = ½ [l_2/l_1 + (l_2/l_1)^2]$. When the live load changes to $p'$, the ratio becomes $w_2'/w_2 = 1/5(1 + 4p'/p)$.

Additionally, a partially rational approximate formula developed by Unwin (1869) determines the weight of main girders ($w_3$) using the total live load per foot run ($w_1$), the weight of the platform per foot run ($w_2$), the span ($l$), the average stress on the gross section of metal ($s$), and the depth of the girder at the center ($d$). The formula is expressed as $w_3 = (w_1 + w_2)l^2 / (Cds - l_2)$ or $w_3 = (w_1 + w_2)lr / (Cs - lr)$, where $r$ is the ratio of span to depth ($l/d$) and $C$ is a constant. For iron braced parallel or bowstring girders, $C$ varies from 1500 to 1800, while for steel girders, it ranges from 1200 to 1500. Another constant, $K$, ranges from 6000 to 7200 for iron and 7200 to 9000 for steel bridges.

## Hydrostatic Weighing and Relative Density
Hydrostatic weighing is a primary method for determining the relative density of a body. The process involves weighing a solid in air ($W$) and then in water ($W_1$). The difference ($W - W_1$) represents the weight of the water displaced. The relative density is thus the quotient $W / (W - W_1)$.

For solids lighter than water, a sinker is used to ensure the object sinks. In this case, if $w$ is the weight of the sinker in water and $W_1$ is the combined weight of the solid and sinker in water, the relative density is $W / (W + w - W_1)$. To ensure accuracy, air bubbles are removed with a camel-hair brush, and porous solids may be boiled in water to expel air from interstices.

When determining the relative density of two liquids, the weights of a solid body in air ($W$) and in the two liquids ($W_1$ and $W_2$) are compared. The relative density is the quotient $(W - W_1) / (W - W_2)$. Alternatively, a glass vessel can be weighed empty ($w$), filled with the liquid ($W$), and filled with a standard substance ($W_1$), resulting in a relative density of $(W - w) / (W_1 - w)$.

## Specialized Density Measurement Tools
For solids available only in small fragments that are insoluble in the standard liquid, a specific gravity bottle is used. This involves three steps: weighing the solid in air ($W$), weighing the bottle full of liquid ($W_1$), and weighing the bottle containing the solid filled with liquid ($W_2$). The relative density is calculated as $W / (W + W_1 - W_2)$.

Say's Stereometer is another tool used for volume determination. The volume of a body inserted into the cup ($v$) is found by first determining the volume of the cup ($u$) when it contains only air. The formula for $u$ is $al^1(h - k^1) / k^1$. This method is susceptible to errors caused by moisture (which disturbs Boyle's law) and variations in atmospheric pressure and temperature.

## Accuracy and Corrections
To achieve absolute density ($\Delta(0^\circ/4^\circ)$), corrections must be made for the buoyancy of brass weights, the density of water at the experiment's temperature, and the cubical expansion of the body. The high-accuracy formula is $\Delta(0^\circ/4^\circ) = (\rho\alpha W - \delta W_1) / (W - W_1)$, where $\rho$ is the density of the water, $\delta$ is the density of the air at temperature $t^\circ$ and pressure $p\text{ mm}$, and $\alpha$ is $(1 + \alpha t^\circ)$.

## Bending Moments and Shear in Bridge Loading
When concentrated loads ($W_1, W_2, W_3$) travel across a girder at fixed distances, they create varying bending moments and shear. The greatest bending moment occurs when a specific load is at the section and a specific condition regarding the sum of the loads is satisfied. As loads move, the bending moments describe parabolas, with middle ordinates such as $1/4 W_1l$.

The greatest shear at a section $C$ often occurs when $W_1$ is at $C$. However, if $W_1$ passes $C$, the shear may be greater when $W_2$ is at $C$, provided that $Ra/l + d > W_1$ (where $R$ is the resultant of the loads and $a$ is the distance between loads). If the load is distributed by flooring, the shear increases by moving $W_2$ to $C$ if $Ra/l > W_1$ (when $a > p$) or if $Ra/l > W_1a/p$ (when $a < p$).
