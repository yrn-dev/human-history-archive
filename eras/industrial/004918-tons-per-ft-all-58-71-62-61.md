# Locomotive and Structural Load Measurements

The measurement of weight and stress, often expressed in tons per foot or tons per square inch, is critical in the engineering of locomotives, railway bridges, and masonry structures. These measurements determine the capacity of infrastructure to support live loads and the structural integrity of materials under pressure.

## Locomotive Weights and Load Distribution
Locomotive weights are analyzed through various metrics, including total weight, tons per foot over all, and tons per foot of wheel base. Data for different engine types reveals varying load distributions:

*   **Passenger Engines:** Examples show total weights ranging from 84.35 to 98.90 tons, with "tons per ft. over all" values of 1.58, 1.71, 1.62, and 1.61. Their tons per foot of wheel base range from 1.92 to 2.04.
*   **Goods Engines:** These exhibit total weights between 75.65 and 78.90 tons. Their "tons per ft. over all" range from 1.50 to 1.54, while their tons per foot of wheel base range from 2.00 to 2.03.
*   **Tank Engines:** Total weights for these engines range from 47.00 to 60.80 tons. The "tons per ft. over all" vary from 1.55 to 1.70, and the tons per foot of wheel base range from 2.23 to 3.03.

In the United States, some locomotives are notably heavier. A consolidation engine may weigh 126 tons with a length of 57 ft. over buffers, resulting in an average load of 2.55 tons per ft. run. J.A.L. Waddell, in *De Pontibus* (1898), classified railways into seven classes based on expected live loads. For the lightest class, he cited a locomotive and tender of 93.5 tons (52 ft. between buffers) with an average load of 1.8 tons per ft. run. For the heaviest class, he cited a locomotive and tender of 144.5 tons (52 ft. between buffers) with an average load of 2.77 tons per ft. run.

## Railway Bridge Live Loads
The live load on a railway bridge is defined as the weight of the heaviest train capable of crossing it. Historically, girder bridges used a uniform load of 1 ton per foot run for each line of way, as locomotives on 4 ft. 8½ in. gauge railways weighed between 35 and 45 tons and did not exceed 1 ton per foot run. Heavily loaded wagon trains at that time did not exceed three-quarters of a ton per foot run.

Modern traffic requires greater strength due to increased engine and wagon weights and the straining action caused by axle load concentration. W.B. Farr determined equivalent uniform loads for forty heavy locomotives across different spans. For a 5.0 ft. span, the equivalent load is 7.6 tons per ft. run; this decreases as the span increases, reaching 1.97 tons per ft. run for a 100.0 ft. span.

Regarding wagons, Waddell's classifications assume weights of 1.3 tons per ft. run for the lightest class and 1.9 tons for the heaviest. Long ore wagons may weigh two tons per ft. run when loaded.

## Structural Stress and Material Limits
Bridge-builders apply various limits of working stress depending on the member's function and the bridge's design:

*   **Dufferin Bridge (Steel):** Working stress was 6.5 tons per sq. in. in diagonals and bottom booms, 6.0 tons in top booms, and 5.0 tons in long compression members and verticals.
*   **Stanley Bridge (Brisbane):** Limits included 7.0 tons per sq. in. in tension booms, 6.5 tons in compression booms, diagonal ties, and cross and rail girders, 5.0 tons in vertical struts, and 8.0 tons in wind bracing.
*   **Tay Bridge:** The general limit is 5 tons per sq. in., reducing to 4 tons per sq. in. for members where stress changes sign.
*   **Forth Bridge:** Limits varied based on frequency of stress change: 5.0 tons per sq. in. for frequent variations from 0 to maximum, and 5.6 tons per sq. in. for rare variations. For frequent alternations of thrust and tension, the limit was 3.3 tons per sq. in., or 5 tons per sq. in. if infrequent.

Steel qualities are further classified by tenacity: soft steel (22½ to 26 tons per sq. in.), medium steel (26 to 34 tons per sq. in.), moderately hard steel (34 to 37 tons per sq. in.), and hard steel (37 to 40 tons per sq. in.).

## Masonry and Pressure Limits
The crushing strength of brickwork is measured in tons per sq. ft. and varies by material and mortar:

*   **Staffordshire blue brick:** 114.34 tons (lime mortar) to 135.43 tons (cement mortar).
*   **Leicester red brick:** 45.36 tons (lime mortar) to 83.36 tons (cement mortar).
*   **Fletton brick:** 30.68 tons (lime mortar) to 56.25 tons (cement mortar).
*   **Gault brick:** 31.14 tons (lime mortar) to 51.34 tons (cement mortar).
*   **Stock brick:** 18.63 tons (lime mortar) to 39.29 tons (cement mortar).
