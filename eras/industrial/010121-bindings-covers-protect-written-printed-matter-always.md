# History of Book Bindings and Protective Covers

Protective covers for written or printed matter have historically evolved to match the shape of the materials being preserved. Early inscriptions on wood or rock required no such protection. The earliest known protective covers appeared around the 8th century B.C. among the Assyrians, who used outer shells of the same shape as their cuneiform tablets to enclose records of loans, slave sales, and other small matters. These shells were often impressed with a short title.

## Ancient and Classical Forms
In Egypt, papyrus rolls were typically kept in roll form, bound with papyrus tape and frequently sealed with Nile mud; these were sometimes stored in rectangular hollows carved from wood. Tree bark was another early writing material; bark books, often containing medical receipts or magical formulae, generally took the form of rolls folded back and forth like a concertina.

In Rome, the *volumen* (roll wound around sticks) was the predominant form while papyrus was used. However, as parchment began to supersede papyrus, the *codex* gained popularity. The *codex*—a term originally referring to a block of wood or tree stump—influenced the layout of early texts. For example, the 4th-century Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus featured three and four columns per page, respectively. By the 6th century, the Codex Bezae utilized a single column, a format that persisted in various forms until the invention of printing.

Other early prototypes of the modern book include the diptychs found at Pompeii in 1875. These consisted of wooden leaves with hollowed inner sides filled with blackened wax, hinged with leather thongs. One such diptych contains a record of payment to Umbricia Januaria from A.D. 55, marking the earliest known Latin manuscript. Between the 1st and 6th centuries, Roman consuls presented ornamental ivory diptychs to great personages.

## Medieval and Early Printing Developments
Medieval "half-bindings" emerged from the need to protect the projecting bands at the back of sewn quires. Strips of leather were fastened across the back, and strong wooden boards were added to each side to prevent vellum leaves from curling. These boards were typically held closed by front clasps. Over time, the leather strip expanded to cover the entire board, creating the "whole" binding.

The introduction of printing to Europe in the mid-15th century initially led to a reaction against the precious covers of illuminated manuscripts. Early printed books were often "ugly" and cheap, bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf and decorated with blind panel stamps. However, the art of gold tooling on leather soon emerged, likely brought to Venice from the East. In England, Thomas Berthelet, the Royal Binder to Henry VIII, was the first to practice this Italian-influenced art, working until approximately 1558.

## Evolution of English and French Binding
In the 17th century, English prayer-books and small New Testaments and Bibles were frequently covered in gold or silver thread and gay embroideries of coloured silks. Samuel Mearne, imitating the French binder Le Gascon, produced some of the daintiest leather bindings in England during the latter half of the century. Trade bindings of the era primarily used sheepskin and rough calf, and the practice of lettering the book's spine became more common.

By the late 18th century, Roger Payne revitalized English bookbinding by introducing ornamental "doublures" (inside linings), richly gold-tooled corner-pieces, and "straight grain" morocco. In 1785, John Edwards of Halifax patented a method to make vellum transparent for use as a cover over delicate paintings. Edwards and John Whitaker both utilized "Etruscan" styles, characterized by classical ornaments and borders; Whitaker specifically used strong acids and engraved metal plates to ornament calf bindings.

## Cultural Perspectives and Modern Production
In China, written characters are viewed as sacred. The Chinese believe that paper with written or printed words possesses a spiritual essence and must not be trampled or lightly tossed. Instead, such paper should be reverently destroyed by fire. Under the Taoist faith, using printed matter to strengthen book covers or as soles for shoes is considered criminal. In the sixth Court of Purgatory, sinners are punished for showing no respect for written paper or for failing to destroy blasphemous or obscene books.

Modern industrial blocking presses, largely of German, English, or Scottish make, are used to apply designs to covers. For gold work, a block is heated by gas jets and pressed against a cover overlaid with gold leaf, which is fastened to the leather or cloth using egg albumen. Colour work follows a similar process but uses inked rollers instead of heat, requiring a separate printing for every colour.
