# The Treaty of 1818 and the State of Banswara

## Overview of Banswara
Banswara, which literally translates to "the forest country," is a Rajput feudatory state located in Rajputana, India. The state's geography is defined by its borders with Gujarat and several native states: it is bounded by Udaipur (also known as Mewar) and Dungarpur to the north, Partabgarh to the east and northeast, the state of Jabua and the dominions of Holkar to the south, and the state of Rewa Kantha to the west. 

The state measures approximately 45 miles in length from north to south and 33 miles in breadth from east to west, covering a total area of 1,946 square miles. The Mahi is the only river within the state, and the region suffers from a great scarcity of water during the dry season. As of 1901, the population of the state was 165,350.

## Political History and British Protection
The Banswara chief is a member of the family of Udaipur. Historically, Banswara served as one of the dependencies of the Delhi empire during its period of vigour. Following the decline of the Delhi empire, the state passed under the control of the Mahrattas.

Due to the oppressions of the Mahrattas, the chief of Banswara petitioned for English protection in 1812. This request was made on the condition that the state would become tributary once the Mahrattas were expelled. This arrangement was formally given effect by the treaty of 1818. Under the terms of this treaty, Britain guaranteed the prince against refractory chiefs and external enemies. In exchange, the prince pledged that he would be guided by the British representative in the administration of his state.

## Administration and Economy
The administration of the state is managed by the chief, who is assisted by a minister known as a *hamdar*. The estimated gross revenue of the state is £17,000, with a tribute of £2,500.

The chief town of the state is Banswara. Located about 8 miles west of the Mahi river, the town is characterized by an old, disused rampart and various Hindu temples. The battlements of the chief's palace overlook the town, which had a population of 7,038 in 1901. Additionally, the petty state of Kushalgarh exists as a feudatory to Banswara.

## Social Conditions and Customs
While the custom of suttee (widow-burning) has long been abolished within Banswara, the population has maintained superstitions regarding sorcery and witches. An example of this occurred as late as 1870 in Kushalgarh, where a Bhil woman of approximately eighty years of age was swung to death following an accusation of witchcraft. Although the perpetrators of this crime were sentenced to five years of rigorous imprisonment, they maintained the sympathy of the local people.
