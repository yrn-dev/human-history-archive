# Panegyricus (1831) and Related Philological Works

The *Panegyricus* (1831) is an edition of the work of Isocrates produced by the Swiss philologist and textual critic Johann Georg Baiter. This publication was part of a broader career dedicated to the application of textual criticism, primarily focused on the Attic orators and Cicero.

## Johann Georg Baiter
Johann Georg Baiter (1801–1877) was born in Zurich on May 31, 1801. After receiving his early education in his hometown, he attended the University of Tuebingen in 1818, though a lack of funds forced his return to Zurich, where he worked as a private tutor for several years. Between 1824 and 1829, Baiter pursued further studies under several scholars: Friedrich Thiersch at Munich, Georg Dissen at Goettingen, and Christian Lobeck at Koenigsberg.

From 1833 until 1876, Baiter served as an *Oberlehrer* at the gymnasium in Zurich, the city where he died on October 10, 1877. He was recognized for his accuracy in collations and his skill in locating the best manuscript authorities.

## Collaborative Works and Editions
Baiter frequently collaborated with other scholars, most notably Orelli, who considered Baiter his "right-hand man." Their joint efforts included:
*   **Isocrates**: In addition to the *Panegyricus* (1831), Baiter edited Isocrates for the Didot collection of classics in 1846.
*   **Lycurgus**: In 1834, Baiter and Sauppe produced an edition of *Leocratea*.
*   **Attic Orators**: Baiter worked on the *Oratores Attici* from 1838 to 1850.
*   **Plato**: Between 1839 and 1842, Baiter, Orelli, and Winckelmann produced a critical edition of Plato that utilized two new manuscripts.
*   **Babrius**: He collaborated with Orelli on *Fabellae Iambicae nuper repertae* in 1845.

Baiter's association with Orelli extended to a major work on Cicero, contributing to the *Ciceronis Scholiastae* (1833) and the *Onomasticon Tullianum* (1836–1838). He was solely responsible for the *Triumphales* and *Fasti Consulares*. Later in his career, he assisted in the second edition of Cicero with Orelli and Halm, and edited the author for the Tauchnitz series (1860–1869) alongside Kayser. He also produced new editions of Horace and Tacitus by Orelli. Outside of textual editing, Baiter and Sauppe translated Leake's *Topography of Athens*.

## Lycurgus in Historical and Philological Context
The name Lycurgus appears in the archive in two distinct contexts: as a subject of philological study and as a historical figure of Sparta.

### Philological Study
Beyond Baiter's 1834 edition of *Leocratea*, the works of Lycurgus have been edited by various scholars. These include the *editio princeps* (Aldine, 1513), F. G. Kiessling (1847), C. Rehdantz (1876), T. Thalheim (1880), C. Scheibe (1885), F. Blass (major edition 1889; minor edition 1902), and E. Sofer (1905). Additionally, L. L. Forman provided an index to Lycurgus, Andocides, and Dinarchus in 1897, and F. Dürrbach wrote an exhaustive treatise, *L'Orateur Lycurgue* (1890).

### The Spartan Lawgiver
In Greek history, Lycurgus is the reputed founder of the Spartan constitution. Historical accounts of his life are contradictory; Plutarch noted that his genealogy, travels, death, and legislative activities are variously recorded. While some scholars suggest he may have been a god or hero—citing a temple and cult at Sparta and a Delphic oracle that questioned if he was a god or a man—most modern historians accept him as a historical person.

Regarding his reforms, some traditions suggest Lycurgus derived his ideas from Cretan institutions, while others attribute them to the Delphic oracle. Ephorus attempted to unite these views, suggesting Lycurgus used the oracle to give religious sanction to a system he had actually derived from Crete.

The dating of Lycurgus's legislation is highly disputed:
*   **10th Century B.C.**: Suggested by a tradition recorded by Xenophon.
*   **9th Century B.C.**: Thucydides dates reforms to shortly before 804; Isocrates and Ephorus suggest 869; other chronographers suggest 821, 828, or 834.
*   **7th Century B.C.**: B. Niese assigns him to the first half of this century.
*   **Other**: Aristotle noted his name on a discus at Olympia regarding the sacred truce, though the date of the discus (possibly 776 B.C. or earlier) remains uncertain.
