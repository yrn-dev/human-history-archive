# Breaking Strength and Bridge Engineering

## Statical and Dynamic Breaking Strength
In the study of materials used for bridge design, the breaking stress of a bar is not a fixed quantity. According to experiments conducted by A. Wöhler and repeated by others, including Sir B. Baker and Johann Bauschinger, the breaking stress depends on the range of variation of stress to which a bar is subjected when that variation is repeated a very large number of times.

The "statical breaking strength" (denoted as $t$ or $K$) is defined as the breaking strength of a bar per unit of section when it is loaded once gradually up to fracture. This is calculated by dividing the breaking load by the original area of the section.

Other forms of strength include:
*   **Primitive Strength ($u$):** The breaking strength of a bar that is loaded and unloaded an indefinitely great number of times, with the stress varying alternately from $u$ to 0.
*   **Vibration Strength ($s$):** The breaking strength of a bar subjected to an indefinitely great number of repetitions of stresses that are equal and opposite in sign (tension and thrust), ranging alternately from $s$ to $-s$.

## Wöhler's Law and Material Fatigue
Wöhler's law provides an empirical expression for determining when a bar will ultimately break based on the range of stress ($\Delta$), where $\Delta = f_{max.} - f_{min.}$. For a bar subjected to stresses varying from $k_{max.}$ to $k_{min.}$, the range is $k_{max.} - k_{min.}$ if the stresses are of the same kind, and $k_{max.} + k_{min.}$ if they are of opposite kinds.

The rule is expressed as $k_{max.} = ½\Delta + \sqrt{K^2 - n\Delta K}$, where $n$ is a constant. This constant varies between 1.3 and 2 for different qualities of steel and iron; for mild steel or ductile iron, it is typically 1.5. 

Specific outcomes of this law include:
*   **Statical load:** When the range of stress is nil ($\Delta = 0$), $k_{max.} = K$.
*   **Alternating load and removal:** When $\Delta = k_{max.}$, then $k_{max.} = 0.6 K$.
*   **Alternate tension and compression:** When $\Delta = 2 f_{max.}$, then $k_{max.} = 0.33 K$.

The term "fatigue" is used to describe the phenomenon where the breaking stress under repeated loading diminishes as the range of variation increases. To determine safe working stress, $k_{max.}$ is divided by a factor of safety.

## Bridge Construction and Straining Actions
Practical engineering experience indicates that while 6½ tons per sq. in. for steel or 5 tons per sq. in. for iron is safe for long bridges with a large ratio of dead to live load, it is not safe for short bridges where stresses are mainly due to live load. In 1869, Unwin suggested that working stress should depend on the ratio of live load ($B$) to dead load ($A$), such that the factor of safety for live load stresses is double that for dead load stresses.

Certain bridge types are built out from abutments, using the completed sections as erecting stages for lifting appliances. In these cases, material must be added to resist erecting stresses. Examples include:
*   **St Louis Bridge:** Half arches were built from each pier to balance the load, supported by skeleton towers and chains.
*   **Douro Bridge:** Girders of side spans overhung the great span by 105 ft. to form a platform for suspending arch parts, with dwarf towers providing additional support.
*   **Cantilever Bridges:** These are specifically adapted for this "building out" method of erection.

## Arch and Bracing Systems
Arch stability depends on the position of the line of pressure relative to the extrados and intrados of the arch ring. While masonry and brickwork bridges were traditionally treated as rigid blockwork, the introduction of hinges simplifies stress calculations. Three arch types are identified:
1.  **Rigid:** Rigid at both the crown and springings.
2.  **Two-hinged:** Hinges located at the springings.
3.  **Three-hinged:** Hinges at both the crown and springings.

The Viaur viaduct in south France is the largest three-hinged-arch bridge, featuring a central span of 721 ft. 9 in. The Alexander III bridge over the Seine utilizes fifteen steel ribs hinged at the crown and springings, with a span of 353 ft. between hinge centres.

Regarding web bracing, three early types developed:
*   **Warren type:** Bracing bars form equilateral triangles.
*   **Whipple Murphy:** Vertical struts with inclined ties.
*   **Lattice:** Both struts and ties are inclined, usually at 45° to the horizontal.

## Notable Bridge Examples
*   **Lansdowne Bridge (Sukkur):** Completed in 1889 over the Indus with a clear span of 790 ft. Its back guys are the most strained parts, designed for 1200 tons of stress due to wind pressure, the cantilever weight, the half weight of the centre girder, and rolling loads.
*   **Niagara Bridge:** Completed in 1883 with a total length of 910 ft. and a clear span between towers of 495 ft.
*   **Red Rock Cantilever Bridge:** Located over the Colorado river with a centre span of 660 ft.
*   **Vienna Danube Canal Bridge:** A 264 ft. span bridge erected in 1860 using parallel chains connected by bracing to form a stiff inverted arch. It was removed in 1884 due to calculation errors.
