# Seals and Ichthyology of the Baikal Region

The biological and geographical study of Lake Baikal and its surrounding waters has been documented through various scientific explorations, focusing particularly on the lake's unique fauna, its river systems, and the specific species of seals and fish that inhabit the region.

## Geography and Hydrography of Lake Baikal
Lake Baikal is characterized by a complex system of inflows and a single outflow. The lake receives more than 300 streams, the majority of which are short mountain torrents. Significant inflows include the Selenga to the south-east, the Barguzin to the east, and the Upper Angara at the north-east extremity. The lake's only outflow is the lower Angara, which exits through a rocky cleft on the western shore. While the Irkut river once reached the lake, it now diverts northwards through a mountain gap to join the Angara at Irkutsk.

The lake is largely encircled by lofty mountains, including the Barguzin range to the east and the Khamar-daban border-ridge to the south, where a peak reaches 5,300 feet above the lake. Other significant formations include the Ulanburgasu mountains, which intrude into the Selenga delta, and the Onot and Baikal (Primorskiy) ridges along the north-west shore.

## Fauna of Lake Baikal
The fauna of Lake Baikal, explored by researchers such as Godlewski, Dybowski, and later Korotnev (1900-2), is noted for its original character and richness. While theories suggesting a direct communication between the lake and the Arctic Ocean during the Tertiary or Post-Tertiary ages remain unproven, the lake hosts several marine-like species. These include polychaetes, marine sponges, a marine mollusc known as *ancilodoris*, and certain marine gammarids. The gammarids are particularly diverse, with as many as 300 species; those found at depths of 330 to 380 fathoms exhibit abyssal characters similar to deep-sea ocean fauna.

## Seals of the Region
Seals are aquatic animals that spend most of their time swimming and diving, feeding primarily on crustaceans, fish, and other marine animals. While they progress with difficulty on land, they must come ashore to give birth. They are widely distributed in cold and temperate zones and can be found in oceans, large rivers, and inland seas or lakes, such as the Caspian and Baikal.

In Lake Baikal, a specific seal exists (*Phoca vitulina* or *Phoca baikalensis*, as named by Dybowski) which is considered quite akin to the seals found in Spitsbergen. In other regions, such as Krasnovodsk Bay in the Caspian, seals are also hunted.

## Ichthyology and Fisheries
The waters of Lake Baikal are described as swarming with fish, specifically *salmonidae* and sturgeons. The chief product of the lake's fisheries is the herring (*Salmo omul*), although the volume of these catches has notably decreased over the last forty to fifty years. Additionally, the small Lake Frolikha, located near the northern extremity of Lake Baikal and connected to it by a river of the same name, is home to a peculiar species of trout, *Salmo erythreas*, which is not found elsewhere.

Scientific contributions to the study of the region's fish include Yakovlev's work on the "Fishes of Angara," published in a periodical between 1890 and 1893. Other related research includes reports on "Fishing in Lake Baikal and its Tributaries" published between 1886 and 1890.
