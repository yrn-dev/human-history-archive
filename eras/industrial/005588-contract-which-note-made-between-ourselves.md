# Contract Notes and Brokerage Practices

In the context of commercial trade, particularly within English law and specialized exchanges, a contract note serves as a written memorandum of a sale. These documents are essential for ensuring that contracts are legally enforceable, especially under specific statutory requirements.

## Statutory Requirements for Enforceability
Under section 4 of the Sale of Goods Act 1893 (which re-enacted section 17 of the Statute of Frauds), contracts for the sale of goods valued at ten pounds or more are not enforceable by action unless certain conditions are met. The buyer must either accept and receive part of the goods as sold, provide something in earnest or part payment to bind the contract, or there must be a note or memorandum of the contract in writing. This memorandum must be signed by the party to be charged or their agent in that behalf.

## The Role of the Broker
Historically, from the reign of James I until 1884, London brokers were regulated by statute and licensed by the corporation. It was common for a single broker to act as an intermediary and agent for both the buyer and the seller. To comply with the Statute of Frauds, such brokers would record a memorandum of each contract in a formal book and sign it on behalf of both parties. The broker would then issue a "bought note" to the buyer and a "sold note" to the seller.

In modern practice, brokers are no longer regulated by statute in London or elsewhere and do not maintain formal books. Instead, the written memorandum is typically found in a "contract note." The sufficiency of a contract note as a memorandum depends on several factors, including whether one or two brokers were involved and whether the notes were exchanged between brokers or sent individually to clients.

## Brokerage in Specialized Exchanges
In certain markets, such as the Liverpool Cotton Association, Limited, the rules of the exchange modify ordinary law regarding the rights and liabilities of members. A primary modification is that all members are treated as principals; no agents are recognized on the market. Consequently, a broker employed by a non-member to buy on the market is treated as buying for himself and is personally liable for the contract.

If the contract involves futures, the broker must follow weekly settlement rules. Should a principal fail to take delivery, the broker is required to pay the seller any difference between the contract price and the market price at the date of delivery.

## The Principal Clause and Contract Notes
Exchanges often use printed contract forms to alter the relationship between a broker and their principal. For example, a "Delivery Contract Note" for American Cotton specifies that the contract is made between the parties signing it and not with any other person, regardless of whose instructions were followed or who benefits from the transaction.

The "principal clause" in these forms allows a broker to buy from or sell to their principal on their own account rather than acting as an agent to secure a contract with a third party. By using these printed forms, a principal binds himself to the rules of the exchange, such as those of the Liverpool Cotton Association.

## Liability and Agency
The legal liability of a broker depends on how the contract note is signed:
* **As Broker:** A broker who signs on behalf of a principal is not personally liable to the third party.
* **As Party to the Contract:** If the broker makes himself a party to the contract, the third party may sue either the broker or the principal. However, electing to treat one as the party to the contract may preclude the third party from suing the other.

Additionally, brokers may be liable for damages in tort for the conversion of goods if they negotiate a sale for a principal who does not hold the title to those goods. While brokers generally lack authority to receive payment, if it is customary in a specific trade and a buyer pays a seller's broker who then becomes insolvent or absconds, the buyer may use that payment as a defense against an action by the seller.

## Other Forms of Contract
Outside of commercial goods, other types of contracts exist under English law. Marriage may be the subject of an ordinary contract, and such promises do not need to be in writing nor name a specific time. However, certain agreements are void, including contracts in restraint of marriage (preventing a person from marrying anyone) and "marriage brocage contracts," which are undertakings for reward to procure a marriage between two people.
