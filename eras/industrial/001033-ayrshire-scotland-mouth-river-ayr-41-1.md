# Ayr

Ayr is a royal, municipal, and police burgh and seaport located in Scotland at the mouth of the river Ayr. It serves as the county town of Ayrshire and is situated 41-1/2 miles south-south-west of Glasgow, accessible via the Glasgow & South-Western railway. The town is positioned on a fine bay characterized by beautiful sands that attract thousands of visitors during the summer.

## Geography and Infrastructure
The town of Ayr proper is located on the south bank of the river Ayr. The river is crossed by a railway viaduct and three bridges: the Victoria Bridge, erected in 1898; the New Brig, built in 1788 through the efforts of Provost Ballantyne; and the "Twa Brigs" of Burns, including the Auld Brig, which is said to date from the reign of Alexander III (who died in 1286). The New Brig was rebuilt in 1879 after yielding to floods in 1877, and the Auld Brig was closed for public safety in 1904.

The town has expanded significantly on the southern side of the stream, particularly toward the racecourse, where numerous fine villas are located. Notable architectural features include the county buildings, which were designed after the temple of Isis in Rome to house various local authorities and provincial and circuit courts. The town buildings feature a spire reaching 226 feet in height and contain reading and assembly rooms.

## Public Institutions and Culture
Ayr's educational history includes a burgh grammar school existing in the 13th century, which was superseded in 1764 by the Academy (rebuilt in 1880). The town also hosts a Carnegie free library, established in 1893. Charitable and medical institutions in the area include a district asylum, a county hospital, St John's refuge, a deaf and dumb home, the Kyle combination poor-house, and industrial schools for both girls and boys.

The town's High Street features the Gothic Wallace Tower, which stands on the site of an older building of the same name demolished in 1835; the tower incorporates the bells and clock of the Dungeon steeple. A statue of the Scottish hero Sir William Wallace, created by the self-taught sculptor James Thorn (1802-1850), is located in a niche in front of the tower. Other statues in the town commemorate General Smith Neill, the 13th earl of Eglinton, and Burns.

The *Ayr Advertiser*, first published on August 5, 1803, was the earliest newspaper produced in Ayrshire.

## Regional Context: Ayrshire
Ayr is the administrative center of Ayrshire, a south-western county bounded by the Firth of Clyde to the west, Renfrewshire to the north, Lanarkshire and Dumfriesshire to the east, Kirkcudbrightshire to the south-east, and Wigtownshire to the south. The county covers 724,523 acres (1142 sq. m.) with a 70-mile coastline. Off the coast lie Horse Island, Lady Island, and the rock of Ailsa Craig.

Historically, the shire was divided into the districts of Carrick (south of the Doon), Kyle (between the Irvine and the Doon), and Cunninghame (north of the Irvine). Kyle was further split by the river Ayr into Kyle Stewart and King's Kyle. Politically, the county is divided into North Ayrshire (Cunninghame) and South Ayrshire (Kyle and Carrick).

The river Ayr is the longest of the county's six most noted rivers. It rises at Glenbuck on the Lanarkshire border and flows for approximately 38 miles before entering the Firth of Clyde at Ayr.

## Economy and Industry
Ayr is one of the leading ports of the county, alongside Troon, Irvine, Girvan, and Ardrossan. The town is a center for shipbuilding and extensive engineering works. Other local industries include the manufacture of woollens, blankets, carpets, and boot and shoe factories.

On a broader county level, Ayrshire is the principal mining county in Scotland, possessing the second largest coalfield. The region also produces fire-clay, pig iron, and iron ore. Other significant industries in the surrounding area include chemical trades in Irvine, explosives manufacturing near Stevenston, and lace curtain factories in Darvel, Newmilns, and Galston.
