# Note-Issuing Banks and Currency Regulation

The history of note-issuing banks involves the transition from private issuance to centralized control, with varying policies implemented across Europe and the British Isles during the 18th and 19th centuries.

## Evolution of Note Issuance in England
Bank notes in England began as goldsmiths' notes, which served as receipts for deposited moneys payable on demand. Until approximately 1781, London bankers provided these notes or deposit-receipts to customers before transitioning to the use of cheque-books. Because the issuance of notes was once viewed as the primary feature of banking, prohibitions on issuance were used to prevent the formation of banks. For example, the act of 6 Anne, c. 50 (1707) and the Bank of England Act 1708 prohibited more than six persons from issuing promissory notes, which effectively prevented the creation of joint-stock banks.

## The Bank Act of 1844 and its Impact
The Bank Charter Acts of 1844 and 1845 significantly restructured the English monetary system. Sir Robert Peel outlined a plan to divide the Bank of England into two separate departments: one for the transaction of ordinary banking business and one for the issue of notes. The issue department was tasked with managing bullion and was restricted to an issue of £14,000,000 upon securities, with the remainder governed by bullion fluctuations. By March 29, 1901, the issue on securities had increased to £18,450,000, meaning the bank held £4,450,000 less gold than it would have under the original 1844 arrangements.

The 1844 act also established geographical monopolies and restrictions:
* **City of London:** The Bank of England held an absolute monopoly within the city and a 3-mile radius.
* **3 to 65-mile radius:** Concurrent rights were granted to banks established before 1844 that issued notes at that time and consisted of six or fewer persons.
* **Beyond 65 miles:** The privilege was extended to all banks established and issuing notes before 1844, provided they had not lost the right through abandonment of business, bankruptcy, or temporary suspension.

Prior to the act, nearly all provincial banks in England and Wales had the privilege of issue. Following the act's announcement, nine banks (eight private and one joint-stock) ceased issuing notes before October 12, 1844. One private bank closed and the Western District Joint-Stock Banking Co. was dissolved; the other seven began issuing Bank of England notes.

## Circulation and Bank Operations
In October 1844, the actual circulation in England included £20,228,800 from the Bank of England, £4,674,162 from private banks, and £3,331,516 from joint-stock banks. In Scotland, chartered, private, and joint-stock banks had a circulation of £2,987,665.

The Bank of England's notes are printed in black from surface-plates with water-marks for protection. They are not reissued once returned to the bank and have a short average life of about six weeks. Forgery is mitigated by these practices and the fact that the lowest denomination is £5.

Over time, the importance of provincial note issuance diminished. Data from 1844 to 1875 showed that the proportion of circulation relative to banking deposits fell sharply. In some instances, deposits increased between three and twelve and a half times, while the circulation ratio dropped from as high as one-third of deposits to as low as 1.5% or 9%.

## Regional Variations in the British Isles
Sir Robert Peel applied different policies in Scotland and Ireland compared to England. Banks in these regions were permitted to exceed their authorized issues if they held specie to cover the excess. Additionally, no restrictions were placed on bank amalgamations. Notes for amounts less than £5 remained permitted in Scotland and Ireland. Consequently, ten large Scottish banks and six of the nine Irish banks maintained the power to issue notes, which aided their extensive local branch networks.

## Note Issuance in France and Germany
In France, the first bank of issue, the *Banque Generale*, was established in 1716 by John Law. It became the *Banque Royale* in 1718 with guarantees from King Louis XV, but ended in 1721. After several unsuccessful attempts at reconstruction, Napoleon I and his minister Mollien founded the Bank of France in 1800 to support industry and trade. While departmental banks with issuance privileges existed under an 1803 law, they were absorbed into the Bank of France by 1848. The Bank of France has since held an exclusive privilege of issue, including the acquisition of the Bank of Savoy in 1863.

In Germany, the *Reichsbank* (Bank of Germany) possesses an automatic power to augment note issue on securities to provide banking accommodation and prevent panic. This is generally charged at a rate of 5%, though it reached 7% briefly in 1899. Between 1881 and 1906, the duty on extra issues totaled £839,052. In contrast, proposals to grant the Bank of England similar powers failed; for instance, a 1873 proposal by Mr. Lowe to charge 12% was rejected as such a high rate was associated with business risks and panic.

Regarding current German regulations, it remains to be seen if note-issuing banks will find certain conditions too onerous and give up their right to issue notes. These enactments aim to protect the *Reichsbank's* specie reserve, though the practical effect is questioned given that other major German banks are not bound by similar conditions.
