# British Military Stations and Infrastructure

The establishment and maintenance of British military stations involve a variety of land and sea-based infrastructures, ranging from permanent barracks in colonial territories to strategic naval stations and temporary depots. These installations are designed to support troop housing, training, logistics, and international strategic cooperation.

## Land-Based Barracks and Facilities
In India, barracks for British troops are constructed by Royal Engineer officers with the assistance of military foremen—who are graduates of civil engineering colleges—and a native subordinate staff. These facilities are governed by Indian army regulations, which provide more liberal accommodation for private soldiers than the home government allows for colonial stations. 

The architectural design of these barracks features lofty, airy rooms with clerestory windows and surrounding verandahs, typically utilizing double tiling for roofs. Each man is allocated 90 square feet of space in rooms 16 feet high, with an adjoining day room used as a sitting-room or for meals. To support the well-being of the troops, these stations include:
* **Recreation:** Plunge baths, cricket grounds, fives courts, and bowling and skittle alleys.
* **Family Support:** Separate blocks for married quarters and schools for children.
* **Health:** Hospital accommodation on a scale higher than that found at home, supplemented by hill sanatoria that provide a change of air during hot weather.
* **Utilities:** Piped water supplies, improved sanitary arrangements, and updated cooking facilities.

General station infrastructure also includes engineer offices with workshops and building yards for the upkeep of War Department property. Large stations, such as Aldershot, may require specialized works for electricity, gas, and sewage disposal. Administration is handled through district and brigade offices, as well as quarters for headquarters staff and the general officer commanding.

## Specialized Army Installations
Beyond standard barracks, the British military utilizes several specialized facilities:
* **Army Schools:** Located between barracks and married quarters, these provide instruction for men seeking educational certificates and education for children of married soldiers.
* **Garrison Churches:** Built when troops cannot easily attend divine services at nearby places of worship.
* **Detention and Prisons:** Ordinary sentences are served in detention barracks or branch detention barracks attached to military districts. Only two military prisons remain—Dover and Curragh—which are reserved for soldiers discharged with ignominy.
* **Logistics:** All barracks require expense stores for bedding and utensils provided by the Army Service Corps. Large stations may also include a supply depot for provisions, featuring an attached bakery and abattoir.

## Depots and Training
A depot is defined as a place for storing things, such as forage or furniture, or the accumulation of military stores within a theatre of operations. In a military context, depots are typically situated in the center of a regiment's recruiting district. Here, recruits receive preliminary training before joining active troops. In peace time, these depots maintain oversea or distant garrisons. In armies using compulsory service, regiments are stationed in their own districts; when they move to war, a small "nucleus" of depot troops remains to form and train drafts for later deployment.

## Naval Stations and Strategic Deployment
British naval strategy emphasizes a policy of vigorous offense. The first duty of British warships is to hunt down enemy warships wherever they are stationed, rather than remaining in a defensive expectancy. This approach is intended to protect British merchant ships by ensuring the superiority of the British Navy.

The British Admiralty maintains general direction over naval warfare, including the management of various naval stations. For example, officers commanding the China and Mediterranean Stations have been permitted to communicate with French Senior Officers on their respective stations. In the Mediterranean, while the British Admiral serves as the junior officer, the French hold the direction of allied fleets. British naval bases are placed at the disposal of the French as part of these cooperative arrangements.

## International Treaties and Troop Stationing
The stationing of British troops can be governed by diplomatic treaties. In 1854, under the governor-generalship of the Marquess of Dalhousie, General John Jacob arranged a treaty between the British government and the Kalat state under Nasir Khan. This treaty annulled a previous 1841 agreement and established that:
* Nasir Khan and his successors were bound to oppose enemies of the British government and act in subordinate co-operation.
* The Baluch chief was required to prevent plundering by his subjects near or within British territory.
* If deemed necessary, British troops could be stationed in any part of the Kalat territory, occupying positions as advised by British authorities.
