# Cleansing in the English Top Fermentation System

In the process of brewing in the United Kingdom, the top fermentation system is employed to convert wort into beer. This process involves seven main stages: grinding, mashing, boiling, cooling, fermenting, cleansing, and racking and storing. Cleansing is specifically defined as the separation of the yeast from the liquid.

## The Fermentation Process
Before cleansing begins, wort—generally at a temperature of approximately 60° F.—is "pitched" with liquid yeast, also known as "barm," at a rate of 1 to 4 lb per barrel. As fermentation progresses, a froth or scum appears on the surface, developing into a light curly mass called a "curly head" or "cauliflower." This evolves into a "rocky head," which eventually shrinks into a compact "yeasty head" that emits gas bubbles with a hissing sound. At this stage, the cleansing of the beer has fairly commenced.

To prevent the temperature from exceeding a maximum of 70-75° F. during fermentation, vessels are equipped with "attemperators," which are systems of pipes through which cold water is run.

## The Cleansing System
The methods used in England to apply the top fermentation system for cleansing are classified into two primary categories: the Cleansing System and the Yorkshire Stone Square System.

### The Cleansing System
The Cleansing System is further divided into three distinct methods:

*   **The Skimming System:** In this method, fermentation occurs from start to finish in wooden vessels called "rounds" or "squares." These vessels are fitted with an attemperator and a skimming device, such as a parachute, used to remove or "skim" the yeast once fermentation is complete.
*   **The Dropping System:** Under this principle, the main fermentation occurs in a "square" or "round," after which the beer is dropped into second vessels to complete fermentation and cleansing. This is subdivided into:
    *   **The Ponto System:** An older method where beer is discharged into vat-like vessels with specially shaped overflow lips. Yeast flows over the lip into a gutter for collection. These pontos are kept full by a vessel positioned at a higher level.
    *   **The Ordinary Dropping System:** Partly fermented beer is let down into large vessels called skimming or dropping "backs." These are equipped with attemperators and parachutes (which usually cover the full width of the back) for yeast removal.
*   **The Burton Union System:** An improved version of the ponto system, this method utilizes a series of casks that can rotate axially. These casks are supplied with beer at the cleansing stage from a feed vessel. Each cask features an attemperator, a swan neck fitting leading to a common gutter, and a pipe and cock at the base to remove "bottoms" and finished beer. This system is considered essential by many Burton brewers for achieving the "Burton character."

### The Yorkshire Stone Square System
Used exclusively in the north of England, this system employs two superimposed square vessels built of stone and kept very cool. The fermenting wort is pumped between these two vessels via a valve and a man-hole. Once fermentation is complete, the man-hole is closed and the yeast is removed from the top square. Unlike other systems, the temperature in the Stone Square System is higher than the standard 60° F.

## Racking and Storing
Once fermentation and cleansing are finished, the beer is racked off into trade casks or storage vessels, sometimes after spending a few hours in a settling tank. The duration of storage varies by beer type:
*   **Stock and Pale Ales:** These finest ales are stored for three months to six weeks.
*   **Running Beers (Mild Ales, etc.):** These are often sent out within ten days or a week of mashing.

For many higher-quality beers, "dry hopping" (adding hops in the cask) is practiced. Running beers or beers that have become flat are "primed" by adding a small quantity of sugar solution to the cask, which ferments rapidly to produce "condition."

## Alternative Bottling Methods
While some brewers still use a special beer matured by storage and secondary fermentation for bottling, modern preferences for bright, light beer have led to the "chilling" and "carbonating" system. In this process, beer is chilled to a very low temperature to cause the immediate deposition of matter. It is then filtered for brightness and "carbonated" by being impregnated with carbon dioxide (carbonic acid gas) under pressure to create immediate condition.
