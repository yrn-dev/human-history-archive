# The Electric Pulse: The Telegraph and the Shrinking World

## Overview
During the Industrial Revolution, the speed of information was limited by the speed of physical travel—whether by horse, rail, or steamship. The invention of the electrical telegraph in the early 19th century fundamentally decoupled communication from transportation, ushering in the era of "instantaneous" data transfer. While early systems were terrestrial, the ultimate industrial triumph was the laying of transatlantic cables, which bridged the gap between the Old and New Worlds.

The telegraph did more than speed up mail; it revolutionized global commerce, diplomacy, and warfare. For the first time, commodity prices in London could be known in New York within minutes rather than weeks, creating the first truly globalized financial markets. However, this leap was not without struggle. The effort to bridge the Atlantic was one of the greatest engineering challenges of the 19th century, characterized by repeated cable snaps and signal degradation.

## Key Facts
*   **The Morse Code:** While many systems existed, Samuel Morse and Alfred Vail’s binary-style code of dots and dashes became the global standard due to its simplicity and efficiency.
*   **The Great Eastern:** The massive iron steamship *Great Eastern* was essential for the 1866 success; it was the only ship of the era capable of carrying the immense weight of the armored cable across the ocean floor.
*   **Diplomatic Shifts:** The telegraph altered the nature of diplomacy. Previously, ambassadors had significant autonomy because instructions from home took weeks to arrive. With the telegraph, central governments could micromanage foreign policy in real-time.
*   **Historical Debate:** Historians often debate the "determinism" of the telegraph. Some argue it was the primary driver of 19th-century imperialism, allowing empires to control distant colonies more tightly. Others suggest it was merely a tool that accelerated existing political trends rather than creating them.

## Did You Know?
1. **The "Victorian Internet":** Historians often call the telegraph the "Victorian Internet" because it created the first global network of information and led to the first instances of "information overload."
2. **Signal Decay:** Early transatlantic cables suffered from "retardation," where electrical pulses smeared together, making messages incredibly slow to transmit—sometimes only a few words per minute.
3. **The 1858 Failure:** The first 1858 cable failed largely because the chief electrician, Wildman Whitehouse, applied excessive voltage (thousands of volts) to the line to force a signal through, effectively frying the insulation.
