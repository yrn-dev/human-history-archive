# The Fall of Louis Philippe and the Revolutions of 1848

The fall of Louis Philippe in February 1848 served as a catalyst for widespread political upheaval across Europe, transforming smouldering revolutionary tensions into an active blaze. This event triggered a series of conflagrations, most notably within the Austrian Empire, where existing materials for unrest had already been prepared.

## Impact on the Austrian Empire
Following the events in France, the revolutionary spirit spread to Austria. On March 3, 1848, Kossuth delivered a famous speech in the diet at Pressburg, which functioned as a declaration of war by Hungarian Liberalism against the Austrian system. Kossuth described the "Vienna cabinet" as a "charnel-house" from which a "pestilential air" breathed, claiming it paralyzed the spirit and dulled the nerves of the people.

The vision presented by Hungarian Liberalism was for a liberated Hungary to serve as the center of freedom for all races under the Austrian crown, leading to a "fraternization of the Austrian peoples." However, this ideal of a "fraternal union" overlooked the critical question of how conflicting nationalities would be positioned. 

Historically, the Austrian system had relied on "Germanism" as a common, unnational mediating element to manage contradictory racial tendencies. However, as the idea of German unity grew, Germanism evolved into a new ideal centered outside the boundaries of the Austrian monarchy. This new sentiment of a united Germany became antagonistic to the aspirations of other races. Ultimately, the doctrine of fraternization conflicted with traditional German ascendancy, and it was upon this conflict that the revolution suffered shipwreck in both Germany and Austria.

## The Reign of Louis Philippe
Louis Philippe served as the constitutional king of France for eighteen years, from 1830 to 1848. He ascended to the throne after Paris rose against Charles X, who had attempted to restore absolute government and destroy the liberty of universities and the press. 

Initially, Louis Philippe adopted a middle-class disguise to conciliate revolutionary passions for equality. He removed royal lilies from his carriages and kept the Palais Royal open to the public, allowing citizens to shake hands with the head of state. This pose was intended to keep the democrats of the capital in a good temper while he consolidated his throne and convinced other European sovereigns that he represented a conservative rather than a revolutionary force.

As his position stabilized, Louis Philippe demonstrated a tenacious grip on personal power. The migration of the royal family to the Tuileries symbolized his belief in the right to rule, not just reign. He positioned himself as the defender of middle-class interests, who constituted the *pays légal* represented in Parliament. His policy became increasingly reactionary and purely dynastic, supported by a majority in a house of representatives elected through a narrow and corrupt franchise. He further sought to strengthen his standing in Europe through family alliances, including the marriage of his daughter, Louise of Orléans, to King Leopold I of the Belgians.

## Political Opposition and the Transition to Republic
Opposition to Louis Philippe existed within the French legislature. Lazare Hippolyte Carnot, elected deputy for Paris in March 1839 after Louis Philippe dissolved the chamber, became a leader of the party hostile to the king within the Radical Left. On February 24, 1848, Carnot pronounced in favor of the republic.

Following the fall of the monarchy, Carnot was appointed minister of education in the provisional government by Lamartine. In this role, he proposed laws for free and obligatory primary instruction and secondary education for girls. However, Carnot's opposition to purely secular schools—arguing that the schoolmaster and the minister were the two columns supporting the republic—led to his resignation on July 5, 1848, after alienating both the Right and the Extreme Left Republicans.

Other figures associated with the era also retreated from public life following the February collapse. Baron de Barante, a statesman and historian who had remained a supporter of the government throughout Louis Philippe's reign, withdrew from political affairs and retired to his country seat in Auvergne after the monarchy fell.
