# The Brontë Sisters and the Clergy Daughters' School

The Brontë sisters—Charlotte, Emily, and Anne—were the daughters of Patrick Brontë, a clergyman of the Church of England who served as the perpetual incumbent of the parish of Haworth in the West Riding of Yorkshire for the final forty-one years of his life. Patrick Brontë, born in Ireland in 1777, had previously worked as a weaver and schoolmaster before attending St John's College, Cambridge, in 1802. In 1812, he married Maria Branwell, a Cornishwoman. Together they had six children: Maria, Elizabeth, Charlotte, Patrick Branwell, Emily Jane, and Anne.

## Education at Cowan Bridge
Following the death of Mrs. Brontë, Patrick Brontë invited his sister-in-law, Elizabeth Branwell, to move from Penzance to Haworth to reside with the family. Miss Branwell provided conscientious care for her nephew and five nieces. In 1824, four of the daughters—Maria, Elizabeth, Charlotte, and Emily—were sent to the Clergy Daughters' school at Cowan Bridge. 

The experience at the school was marked by drastic discipline and bad food. The tragedy of the institution was felt most acutely by the eldest two sisters, Maria and Elizabeth, who returned home in 1825 and subsequently died. While congenital disease is considered a probable cause for their deaths, the school's environment left a lasting impression on Charlotte. Years later, she depicted the school under the pseudonym "Lowood" in her novel *Jane Eyre*, with the school's principal, the Rev. William Carus Wilson (1792-1859), serving as the counterpart to the character Mr. Naomi Brocklehurst. Charlotte and Emily escaped the fate of their older sisters and returned to a home life at Haworth in 1825.

## Early Literary Development and Later Education
At Haworth, the four surviving children engaged in study under the guidance of their aunt, Elizabeth Branwell. They developed precocious literary aspirations, producing essays, verses, and stories in tiny booklets. 

Charlotte's formal education continued in 1831 when, at age sixteen, she became a pupil at Miss Margaret Wooler's school at Roe Head, Dewsbury. After leaving in 1832, she helped educate her younger sisters, utilizing her proficiency in composition, French, and drawing. Charlotte later returned to Roe Head in 1835 as a governess; during this time, Emily attended as a pupil for three months before being replaced by Anne. In February 1842, Charlotte and Emily traveled to Brussels to study languages at the Pensionnat Héger. They were admired by the principal teacher, M. Héger, but were recalled to England before the year ended due to the critical illness of their aunt, Miss Branwell, who died on October 29, 1842.

## Literary Careers and Pseudonyms
The three sisters—Charlotte, Emily, and Anne—attained literary distinction, often utilizing pseudonyms to disguise their identities. In 1846, they published a joint volume of verse titled *Poems, by Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell*. The volume contained nineteen poems by Charlotte, twenty-one by Emily, and twenty-one by Anne. Despite costing the sisters approximately £50, only two copies were sold. While the volume was not recognized at the time, it is now accepted that Emily's verse revealed true poetic genius.

The sisters later transitioned to novels. Charlotte's first effort, *The Master* (later retitled *The Professor*), was deemed too short for the required three-volume format by the firm Smith, Elder & Co. In response, she wrote and published *Jane Eyre* in October 1847, which received wide acclaim. Emily wrote *Wuthering Heights*, a novel described as a monument of intensity and grim melancholy, while Anne wrote *Agnes Gray* and *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall*. Because the publisher T. C. Newby attempted to persuade the public that Emily and Anne's works were written by the author of *Jane Eyre*, Charlotte and Anne visited London in the summer of 1848 to establish their separate identities.

## Final Years and Legacy
The later years of the sisters were marked by personal loss. Their only brother, Patrick Branwell, who had struggled with alcoholism after failed attempts at art and tutoring, died on September 24, 1848. Emily died shortly after at Haworth on December 19, 1848. Anne fell ill and died in Scarborough on May 28, 1849. Within eight months, Charlotte lost all three of her closest companions.

Charlotte continued to write, completing *Shirley* (published October 1849) and *Villette* (published January 1853). She married Arthur Bell Nicholls, her father's curate, on June 29, 1854. Charlotte died in 1855. The sisters' lives were later chronicled by Mrs. Gaskell in *The Life of Charlotte Brontë* (1857), a work that sparked controversy regarding the identity of the Cowan Bridge school as the inspiration for "Lowood."
