# Tons per Foot of Wheel Base

In the study of locomotive engineering and bridge design, the measurement of tons per foot of wheel base is used to determine the distribution of weight and the resulting straining action on tracks and structures. This metric varies significantly depending on the type of engine and its intended use.

## Locomotive Weight Distribution

Data regarding different classes of locomotives reveals varying levels of weight concentration over the wheel base. For certain engines, the tons per foot of wheel base are recorded at 1.92, 2.04, 1.97, and 1.95. These correspond to total weights of 84.35, 98.90, 91.90, and 85.48 tons, respectively.

Goods engines exhibit a different distribution pattern, with tons per foot of wheel base measured at 2.02, 2.02, 2.03, and 2.00. The total weights for these specific goods engines are 77.90, 78.80, 76.46, and 75.65 tons.

Tank engines generally show a higher concentration of weight over the wheel base compared to goods or other engines. Recorded values for tank engines include 2.45, 2.52, 2.23, and 3.03 tons per foot of wheel base, with total weights ranging from 47.00 to 60.80 tons.

## Bending Moments and Equivalent Loads

To analyze the impact of heavy locomotives on different spans, Farr developed diagrams of bending moments for forty different heavy locomotives. This research determined a uniform load that would produce a bending moment equal to the actual wheel loads. The equivalent uniform load per foot run for each track varies by the span:
*   **5.0 ft span:** 7.6 tons
*   **10.0 ft span:** 4.85 tons
*   **20.0 ft span:** 3.20 tons
*   **30.0 ft span:** 2.63 tons
*   **50.0 ft span:** 2.24 tons
*   **100.0 ft span:** 1.97 tons

## Road Bridge Live Loads

The weight of wagons and traction engines passing over road bridges often produces greater straining action than dense crowds of people. In some manufacturing districts and near large towns, road bridges must be designed to accommodate loads of 30 tons.

In Switzerland, road classifications dictate specific load prescriptions for designers:
*   **Main Roads:** A wagon load of 10 tons with a 13 ft. wheel base.
*   **Secondary Roads:** A wagon load of 6 tons with a 10 ft. wheel base.
*   **Other Roads:** A wagon load of 3 tons with an 8 ft. wheel base.

## American Locomotive and Wagon Loads

Certain American locomotives are noted for being exceptionally heavy. A consolidation engine may weigh 126 tons with a length of 57 ft. over buffers, which results in an average load of 2.55 tons per foot run. Long ore wagons are also utilized, weighing two tons per foot run when loaded.

J.A.L. Waddell proposed a seven-class system for railways based on expected live loads. In this system:
*   **Lightest Class:** A locomotive and tender weighing 93.5 tons with 52 ft. between buffers (average load of 1.8 tons per foot run), and wagons weighing 1.3 tons per foot run.
*   **Heaviest Class:** A locomotive and tender weighing 144.5 tons with 52 ft. between buffers (average load of 2.77 tons per foot run), and wagons weighing 1.9 tons per foot run.

Waddell's calculations utilize short tons of 2000 lb.
