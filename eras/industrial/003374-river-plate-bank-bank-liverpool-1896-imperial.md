# River Plate Bank v. Bank of Liverpool [1896] and Imperial Bank of Canada v. Bank of Hamilton [1903]

## Legal Context of Banking Losses
In instances where a banker suffers a loss due to fraud or forgery, and such circumstances are not covered by statutory protection, the right to relief is determined by general principles. Generally, a banker is unable to charge a customer for payments made based on a forgery of the customer's signature. This holds true regardless of whether the banker is presumed to be familiar with the signature or if the payment is considered unauthorized.

However, there are exceptions to this rule. A customer may be estopped from disputing a banker's right to debit their account if the customer has accredited the forgery. Similarly, if the customer had knowledge, or reasonable grounds to believe, that a forgery had been committed but failed to warn the banker—resulting in loss or prejudice to the banker—the customer is held responsible. This principle is supported by cases such as *Ewing v. Dominion Bank* [1904], *McKenzie v. British Linen Co.*, and *Vagliano v. Bank of England* [1891].

## Negotiability and Mistake of Fact
The legal framework also addresses payments made to wrongful possessors of order bills through the doctrine of the fictitious person as payee, which may exonerate a banker. When a payment is made on a forgery to a holder who is innocent, it is classified as a payment under a mistake of fact. 

While a payor typically has the right to recover money paid under a mistake, in these instances, that right is subordinated to the necessity of maintaining the characteristics of negotiability.

## Judicial Divergence in Recovery
There is a lack of consensus regarding the specific conditions under which the recovery of such funds is precluded. Legal views differ on whether recovery is prevented only when the opportunity to provide a notice of dishonour is lost or prejudiced due to a delay in reclaiming the payment. Alternatively, some views suggest that the mere possibility of damage is sufficient to preclude recovery. This specific point of legal contention is highlighted in the cases of *London & River Plate Bank v. Bank of Liverpool* [1896], 1 Q.B. 7, and *Imperial Bank of Canada v. Bank of Hamilton* [1903], A.C. 49.
