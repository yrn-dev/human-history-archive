# Book-Plates

Book-plates, also known as *ex-libris*, are private tokens of book-ownership. While they have long existed, the taste for collecting them and the formal study of the medium are comparatively modern developments, hardly dating back further than 1875. A significant impetus for the study of book-plates was provided in 1880 with the publication of the *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates* by Lord de Tabley (then the Hon. Leicester Warren).

## Classification of Styles
The general classification of book-plate styles was established by Lord de Tabley and includes the following categories:

*   **Early Armorial:** This style refers to plates created previous to the Restoration, such as the plate of Nicholas Bacon.
*   **Jacobean:** Though the term is considered somewhat misleading, it is used to describe the heavy decorative manner associated with the early Georgian days, Queen Anne, and the Restoration. An example of this style is the Lansanor plate.
*   **Chippendale:** This style is identified with the rococo, as seen in the French plate of Convers.
*   **Wreath and Ribbon:** This style belongs to the period characterized by the use of the urn.

## English Book-Plates
English book-plates from the late Elizabethan to the late Victorian period reflect the prevailing tastes in decorative art of their respective epochs. The gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon is the oldest discovered English example; however, a hand-painted armorial device belonging to Henry VIII (currently in the King's library at the British Museum) is not considered a book-plate in the modern sense. Another early example is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585.

Until the final quarter of the 17th century, authentic English plates were limited in number and characterized by a simple composition that lacked German elaborateness. These were typically plain armorials with symmetrical mantling and occasional wreaths or palms. Following the Restoration, book-plates became common accessories for well-ordered libraries. These plates introduced tincture lines and dots—a method devised in the middle of the century by Petra Sancta in *Tesserae Gentilitiae*—and featured elaborate mantling surrounding the shield that resembled the periwigs of the era. This style was imported from France, and until the French Revolution, English decorative modes generally followed French tastes with a delay of several years.

## Composition and Genres
Beyond general styles, certain compositions were prevalent during specific periods. While many older and modern plates are essentially pictorial, several distinct English genera exist:

*   **Library Interiors and Book-piles:** Examples include the *ex-libris* of William Hewer, who served as secretary to Samuel Pepys.
*   **Portrait-plates:** Notable examples include those of the architect John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys.
*   **Allegories:** These were engraved by artists such as George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth.
*   **Landscape-plates:** These were produced by wood engravers of the Bewick school.

In these pictorial genres, the armorial element typically plays a secondary role.

## Continental Developments
In France, the "movable *ex-libris*" did not become common until the 17th century. Prior to this, book-owners preferred the more luxurious practice of stamping a personal device directly onto the cover. By the middle of the 17th century, the *ex-libris* became naturalized and was often very handsome. The term *ex-libris*, used as a substantive, originated in France and became the recognized term for book-plates across the continent, appearing in the tokens of other nationalities only after it had become a standard French inscription.

## Professional Societies and Literature
As interest in the subject grew, societies of collectors were established in England, France, Germany, and the United States. Many of these societies publish archives or journals, including the quarterly *Ex-libris Zeitschrift* in Berlin and the monthly publications *The Journal of the Ex-libris Society* in London and the *Archives de la societe francaise de collectionneurs d'ex-libris* in Paris.
