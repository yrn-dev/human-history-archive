# Iron and Steel Girder Bridges

Iron and steel girder bridges are structures where the main supporting members consist of two or more horizontal beams, trusses, or girders. These bridges are characterized by a superstructure that is approximately half in tension and half in compression. Unlike arches or suspension chains, a girder bridge imposes only a vertical load on its abutments and piers, avoiding horizontal thrust, and is generally easier to erect.

## Design and Classification
Girder bridges are classified based on the position of the platform or floor they carry. In "deck" bridges, the floor is located on top of the girders; in "through" bridges, the floor is positioned near the bottom. For railway applications, the platform typically consists of cross girders attached to or resting on the main girders, with longitudinal rail girders (stringers) supporting the rails and sleepers.

A fundamental distinction in these bridges is the mode of support:
*   **Independent or Discontinuous:** The main girders are supported only at the ends.
*   **Continuous:** A main girder is supported at two or more points across multiple spans. While continuity allows for an economy of weight—providing a theoretical advantage of about 16% for live loads and 49% for dead loads in three-span bridges—it carries the risk that pier settlement may alter stress distribution and render the bridge unsafe.
*   **Cantilever and Suspended Girders:** These are composed of cantilevers and suspended girders, acting virtually as a continuous girder hinged at points of contrary flexure to eliminate ambiguity regarding stresses.

To manage temperature-induced expansion in spans exceeding 75 ft., one end of each chain girder is placed on rollers between the bed-plate on the pier or abutment and the bearing-plate on the girder.

## Materials and Evolution
Historically, bridges were constructed from masonry, timber, brick, or concrete. Wrought iron began to be used in the late 18th century, often in combination with cast iron or timber. In early braced bridges in America and England, cast iron (often in circular or octagonal tubes) was used for compression members, while wrought iron was used for tension members.

By 1880, mild steel—which possesses greater toughness and tenacity than wrought iron—began to supersede wrought iron for girders with spans greater than 100 ft. In some regions, such as the Pacific coast, "combination bridges" remained in use; these utilize timber for compression members and steel for tension members due to the availability of timber and the distance to steel works.

## Notable Bridge Types and Examples
### Truss and Girder Systems
*   **Warren Girder:** A system that provides convenient support for floor girders, sometimes utilizing intermediate verticals or two sets of bracing bars. An example was built in Louisville in 1869 with a 390 ft. span. The Newark Dyke bridge (1851-1853) was a pin-jointed Warren girder bridge with a span of 259 ft.
*   **Fink Truss:** An early American deck bridge type using cast iron for compression and wrought iron for tension. This type eventually disappeared because the use of long ties to transfer loads to the bridge ends was uneconomical.
*   **Polygonal Girders:** The Ohio bridge on the Cincinnati & Covington railway (1888) features independent polygonal girders. Its center span is 550 ft. and side spans are 490 ft. The center girder is 545 ft. long and 84 ft. deep, carrying two railway lines, two footways, and two carriageways.

### Suspension and Stiffening Girders
Metal construction enabled great spans between 500 and 1800 ft.
*   **Brooklyn Bridge (begun 1872):** Features a center span of 1595½ ft. and stiffening girders that are 40 ft. deep and 67 ft. apart.
*   **Williamsburg Bridge (opened 1903):** Has a span of 1600 ft. and two main trusses or stiffening girders supported by cables over the center span.
*   **Niagara Bridge (1852-1855):** Originally featured a box-shaped braced timber stiffening girder 18 ft. deep and 25 ft. wide, which was replaced by an iron girder in 1880 before the entire structure was replaced by a steel arch in 1896-1897.
*   **Clifton Bridge:** Utilizes wrought iron stiffening girders supported by three chains on each side.

### Specialized Structures
Bascule bridges utilize girders for their construction. For example, certain bascules use four main lattice girders over the river, with transverse girders spaced 12 ft. apart. These structures may be worked by hydraulic machinery, rotating through an angle of 82° on steel shafts.
