# The Bombardon

The bombardon is a brass wind instrument characterized by a wide conical bore, which allows for the production of fundamental sounds with immense power and a rich, full quality. This design distinguishes the bombardon and other tubas from saxhorns, which possess a narrower bore. In Germany and Austria, this distinction led to the classification of brass winds as "whole" instruments (*Ganze Instrumente*)—those with the wide conical bore—and "half" instruments (*Halbe Instrumente*), such as the saxhorns.

## Construction and Design
The modern bombardon exists in two primary forms. The upright model is utilized for stationary band music, while the circular model, known as the helicon, is designed to be worn around the body. In the helicon model, the large bell rests on the left shoulder, a design reminiscent of the Roman *cornu*, which facilitates carrying the heavy instrument during marches.

The instrument is an evolution of the bugle family, resulting from the application of valves. Historically, brass instruments with cup-shaped mouthpieces required tubing twice as long as an organ pipe of the same pitch to produce a note; for instance, a French horn sounding an 8 ft. C required a 16 ft. tube, making the fundamental unobtainable. However, the adoption of the bugle's proportions allowed baritone and bass instruments to produce the fundamental clearly. Consequently, the tubes of tubas are now half the length of older instruments, corresponding directly to the length of an organ pipe of the same pitch.

## Technical Specifications and Compass
The bombardon possesses a chromatic compass ranging from 3-1/2 to 4 octaves, with a harmonic series consisting of the 1st through the 8th harmonics. To achieve a complete chromatic scale, the instrument requires seven different lengths of tubing, each with its own harmonic series. These lengths are managed through a combination of pistons or valves.

Typically, bombardons feature three or four pistons that lower the pitch by 1, 1/2, 1-1/2, and 2-1/2 tones. In Belgium, the pistons lower the pitch by 1, 1/2, 2, and 3 tones. Specific combinations of these pistons further alter the pitch:
*   Pistons 2 and 3 combined lower the pitch by two tones.
*   Pistons 1, 2, and 3 combined lower the pitch by three tones.
*   Pistons 1, 2, 3, and 4 combined lower the pitch by five and a half tones.

In Germany and Austria, a "cylinder action" is frequently used, replacing the piston or pump with a four-way brass cock operated by a key and a series of cranks.

## Performance and Intonation
The lowest notes produced by the valves are noted for being very difficult to obtain. This is because the lips rarely have enough power to vibrate a column of air of such immense length at a rate slow enough to synchronize with such deep pitches. Furthermore, these lowest valve notes are often barely audible unless another bombardon doubles them an octave higher.

A known defect of the valve system is that combinations of pistons often fail to provide absolutely correct intonation because the length of the tubing opened is not the theoretical length required. Various inventors have attempted to remedy this:
*   **Adolphe Sax** developed a six-valve independent system.
*   **Besson** introduced the *Registre* (eight independent positions) and the compensating system *Transpositeur*.
*   **D.J. Blaikley** invented the Boosey automatic compensating piston.
*   **V. Mahillon** created automatic regulating pistons.
*   **Rudall, Carte & Company** implemented Klussmann's patent bore, which is conical throughout the open tube and additional lengths.
*   **Besson** also produced an enharmonic valve system featuring three pistons and six independent tuning slides.

## Notation and Usage
In most regions, bombardons are treated as non-transposing instruments, meaning the music is written as it is sounded. The exception to this rule is found in Belgium and France, where transposition is the usual practice.
