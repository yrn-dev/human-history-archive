# Banking in the United Kingdom

## Origins and the Bank of England
English banking can be traced back to the 16th century, originating from the money dealings of London goldsmiths. However, the development of banking in the United Kingdom was profoundly shaped by the Bank of England and associated acts of parliament. The Bank of England was established in 1694, arising from a government loan of £1,200,000. This loan was subscribed to in just over ten days, between June 21 and July 2, 1694.

On July 10, 1694, the subscribers appointed Michael Godfrey as deputy-governor and Sir John Houblon as governor. Michael Godfrey authored a pamphlet detailing the bank's purposes and its utility to the country. This document also addressed contemporary fears that the bank might collaborate with William III to make the prince absolute, thereby rendering parliaments useless.

## Evolution of Banking Operations
In the early 19th century and late 18th century, note circulation was a primary component of banking business. Over time, deposits became the more significant element. For much of its history, the banking sector was characterized by a reluctance to publish balance sheets, making trustworthy statistics difficult to obtain for more than forty or fifty years. Scottish banks generally did not publish accounts until 1865, and Irish joint-stock bank figures were published collectively starting in 1840 by Dr. Neilson Hancock.

The earliest basis for a trustworthy estimate of deposits was provided in 1851 by W. Newmarch in the *Journal of the Statistical Society*. He estimated total deposits across Ireland, Scotland, England, and Wales (including the Bank of England) to be between £250,000,000 and £360,000,000. By 1872, Palgrave's *Notes on Banking* estimated this figure at £430,000,000 to £450,000,000, excluding bank capitals and deposits in discount houses. By the close of 1906, deposits (including acceptances) reached approximately £997,000,000.

## Regional Resources and Note Circulation
By 1906, total resources—including reserves, capitals, and a note circulation of approximately £177,500,000—were distributed as follows:
*   **England and Wales:** £922,297,000 (Bank of England and other banks)
*   **Scotland:** £135,042,000
*   **Ireland:** £73,707,000
*   **Isle of Man:** £898,000

The total resources for the United Kingdom amounted to £1,131,944,000. Regarding average circulation in 1906, the Bank of England accounted for £28,890,000, while English private banks and joint-stock banks accounted for £124,000 and £429,000 respectively. Scotland's circulation was £7,477,000 and Ireland's was £6,452,000, bringing the United Kingdom total to £43,372,000.

In Ireland, the Bank of Ireland had an authorized issue of £3,597,850, while private and joint-stock banks had a combined authorized issue of £2,456,261. However, actual issues were significantly lower; by May 1907, 12 private banks had an actual issue of £122,536 against an authorized £482,744, and 17 joint-stock banks had an actual issue of £437,693 against an authorized £1,084,836. This discrepancy existed because country bank circulation was often limited to the immediate neighborhood of the issuing office.

## Joint-Stock Banks and Liability
The legal framework for joint-stock banks evolved through several key acts. A prohibition on such banks was modified in 1826 and removed in 1833, though only the Bank of England was permitted limited liability. Consequently, shareholders in joint-stock banks were liable for the full extent of their means until the act of 1862 permitted "limited liability." 

The danger of unlimited liability was highlighted by the failures of the Western Bank of Scotland in 1858 and the City of Glasgow Bank in 1878. The latter failure led to the Companies Act of 1879, which allowed unlimited companies to adopt limited liability. Under this act, shareholders who paid the nominal amount of their holding were not liable for further amounts, unless the company issued bank notes, in which case they remained liable as if the company were unlimited.

## Banking Infrastructure and Settlement
The number of banking offices grew significantly between 1844 and 1906. In 1844, there were 1,524 offices in the United Kingdom (976 in England and Wales, 368 in Scotland, and 180 in Ireland). By 1906, this number rose to 7,507 (5,527 in England and Wales, 1,180 in Scotland, 777 in Ireland, and 23 in the Isle of Man). This growth reduced the number of inhabitants per office from 17,526 in 1844 to 5,530 in 1906.

Modern transactions in the United Kingdom are primarily settled by cheque via ledger transfers, with the London Clearing House facilitating a large proportion of these. This system is supported by the fact that every bank in the United Kingdom maintains an agent in London. The annual circulation of the London Clearing House exceeds £12,000,000,000.

## Bank Holidays
Bank holidays are designated days when banks in England, Scotland, and Ireland are closed, as established by the Bank Holidays Act 1871. Prior to 1834, the Bank of England closed for approximately thirty-three days for anniversaries and saints' days, which was reduced to four days in 1834.

Under the 1871 Act, holidays in England and Ireland include Easter Monday, the Monday in Whitsun week, the first Monday of August, and December 26 (if it falls on a weekday). The Bank Holiday (Ireland) Act 1903 added March 17 as a special holiday for Ireland. In Scotland, holidays include New Year's Day, Christmas Day, Good Friday, and the first Mondays of May and August. If Christmas or New Year's Day falls on a Sunday, the following Monday is observed. The sovereign may also appoint additional bank holidays by proclamation.
