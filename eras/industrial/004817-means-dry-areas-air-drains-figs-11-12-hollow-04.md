# Dry Areas and Drainage Systems

In the context of building construction and land management, the prevention of moisture accumulation is critical for structural integrity and agricultural productivity. Methods for managing water include the use of dry areas in architecture, the implementation of hollow walls, and various subterranean drainage systems for land.

## Dry Areas in Building Construction
Dry areas, also referred to as air drains, are used to protect the portions of building walls situated below the ground. These are constructed to form a hollow space, measuring 9 inches or more in width, around the walls. The primary objective of this design is to prevent the main walls from coming into contact with the brickwork, thereby stopping moisture from being imparted to the building. 

To maintain the effectiveness of dry areas, specific arrangements must be made for draining and ventilating the space, as well as keeping it clear of vermin. Due to the fact that dry areas are considered far from sanitary, they are seldom used in modern practice and are being superseded by the application of cement or asphalt to the face of the wall.

## Prevention of Dampness in Walls
Walls are susceptible to dampness from three primary sources: rain driven onto the face by wind, water soaking down from the top of the wall, and wet rising from the earth. To combat these, several techniques are employed:

*   **Rising Damp:** This is prevented through the construction of dry areas or the introduction of damp-proof courses.
*   **Soaking from the Top:** To prevent water from soaking down, a coping of impervious material is used. This may consist of stone, cement, non-porous coping bricks, or cast-iron. Another method involves using ordinary bricks set on edge in cement with a double course of tiles immediately below, known as "creasing." These materials are often sloped or "weathered" to throw off rain.
*   **Driven Rain:** Protection against wind-driven rain includes coating the exterior surface with rough cast or cement, covering the walls with tiles or slates fixed on battens, or the adoption of hollow walls.

## Hollow Walls
Buildings in exposed positions often utilize cavity-walls, which consist of a main inner wall and an outer skin (usually half a brick thick) separated by a cavity of 2 or 3 inches. These walls are connected by iron or stoneware ties, approximately 8 inches long and ¾ inch wide, placed at 12 to 18 inch vertical intervals and 3 feet apart horizontally. These ties feature a bend or twist in the center to prevent water from transmitting to the inner wall.

Water that penetrates the outer shell drops to the base of the cavity—which is taken a course or two below the damp-proof course—and exits through gratings located a few inches above ground level. While hollow walls help maintain an equable internal temperature and keep the building dry, they can harbor vermin. To prevent this, the top of the wall is typically sealed with brickwork, though air gratings are included to allow circulation and facilitate drying after rain.

## Agricultural Drainage Systems
The practice of keeping arable lands dry dates back to the Romans, who used covered drains filled with twigs or stones, or open trenches. In the 17th century, Walter Blith suggested using channels filled with stones or faggots to free barren land from stagnant water.

In the late 18th century, Joseph Elkington developed "sink-hole drainage" for sloping ground drowned by springs. This method involves cutting a deep drain through clay—sometimes aided by auger holes or wells—to tap into a subjacent bed of gravel or sand where water is pent up, conveying it to a stream or ditch.

Around 1823, James Smith of Deanston revolutionized the art of draining by implementing a system of parallel underground channels. Rather than using a few cuts to dry extensive areas, Smith insisted on a complete system of parallel channels running in the line of the greatest slope of the ground. These drains were placed 10 to 40 feet apart, depending on the retentiveness of the ground, and were set at a depth of 30 inches. These channels were filled with stones small enough to pass through a 3-inch ring. A main receiving drain was placed at the lowest part of the ground, with sub-drains located in every subordinate hollow. Josiah Parkes, an engineer to the Royal Agricultural Society, later advocated for a depth of at least 4 feet to ensure subterranean water was reached.

## Specialized Drainage in Aviaries
In the construction of aviaries, specifically those with multiple compartments and a back passage, drainage is integrated into the water supply for birds. Shallow water-basins, supplied by an underground pipe and an external cock, must be connected to a properly laid drain via an overflow pipe and a waste plug.
