# 19th Century Book Production and Appearance

The 19th century witnessed a significant transformation in the physical appearance and production of books, driven by changes in materials, printing technologies, and the economic structure of the publishing trade.

## Evolution of Book Covers and Bindings
A great change in the appearance of books occurred through the introduction of new materials for book cases issued by publishers. Around 1820, glazed calico began to be used, followed by the use of cloth around 1830. Initially, lettering on these covers was printed on paper labels; however, this was soon replaced by gilt stamping directly onto the cloth. By the final quarter of the century, English and American books featured many beautifully designed covers.

Regarding leather bindings, designs were primarily imitations of older works for many years, though greater originality began to emerge toward the end of the 1880s.

## Illustration and Printing Techniques
Book illustrations underwent various phases during the century. In the first half of the 19th century, publishers utilized lithographs, line engravings, etchings, and colour-prints as subsidiary methods. The primary reliance, however, was on wood-engraving, a field in which extraordinary technical skill was developed. During the 1860s and the years immediately surrounding that decade, many of the leading English artists provided drawings for engravers.

By the last decade of the century, wood-engraving was practically eliminated due to the perfection of photographic reproduction methods. The most popular of these photographic processes utilized paper that was heavily coated with china clay.

## Printing Standards and Market Trends
Trade-printing in America and England improved steadily throughout the century. Between 1891 and 1896, William Morris and other amateur printers who imitated him established a new standard for the richness of general effect, ornament, and beauty of type at the Kelmscott Press.

Simultaneously, the immense extension of the reading public created a demand for cheap reprints of famous works. This was met by the production of numerous "flimsy" but pretty editions priced at 1s., 1s. 6d., or even less. The challenge of producing well-sewn books on good paper at moderate prices remained unresolved until the 20th century. In England, around 1894, the availability of medium-priced books increased when single-volume novels priced at 6s. (subject to discount) replaced three-volume editions that cost 31s. 6d. The high price of 10s. 6d. per volume had previously been adopted during the initial popularity of the *Waverly Novels*.

## The Publishing Trade and Periodicals
The retail bookseller served as a third partner in the publishing industry. To protect retail interests, a London booksellers' society was established in 1890 to control trade details, arrange prices, and restrict discounts to 25%. This organization later expanded its operations to cover the entire United Kingdom, becoming "The Associated Booksellers of Great Britain and Ireland."

A separate class of business existed in the trade of second-hand or old books, which required bibliographic knowledge and focused on individual books rather than mass copies. Dealers in this sector occasionally acquired "remainders"—books that had stopped selling with the publisher—to offer as bargains.

The periodical trade also grew during the 19th century, beginning with publications such as *Chambers's Journal* and the *Penny Magazine*. This growth was further promoted by the introduction of attractive illustrations, the abolition of the duty upon paper and the newspaper stamp, and the ability for customers to purchase books by instalments.

## Academic and Literary Shifts
By 1895, English literature had become a subject of regular instruction for special degrees at most universities in America and England. This academic shift led to research and historical treatments of the evolution of literary types, though it also resulted in an excessive focus on etymology, prosody, and grammar. 

General educational agencies of the time also impacted the volume of writing; while one man could write competently on a subject in the middle of the 19th century, by 1910 there were fifty. This led to a multiplication of books and reading apparatus, characterized by a tendency to repeat existing works in the same manner.
