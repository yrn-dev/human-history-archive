# Reciprocal Figures

Reciprocal figures are a geometric concept defined by J. Clerk Maxwell in 1864 (published in *Phil. Mag.*) to describe a specific relationship between two plane figures. According to Maxwell, two plane figures are considered reciprocal when they consist of an equal number of lines, satisfying two primary conditions: first, that corresponding lines in the two figures are parallel; and second, that corresponding lines which converge to a point in one figure form a closed polygon in the other.

## Application in Structural Engineering
Reciprocal figures provide a simple method for computing the stresses on the members of a frame. In this application, a frame—provided it does not have redundant members—and the external forces required to keep that frame in equilibrium are represented by a diagram that constitutes one of the two plane figures. The corresponding reciprocal figure then represents the forces between the joints of the frame. Specifically, the lines in the reciprocal figure indicate both the direction and the magnitude of these forces, which consequently reveals the stress on each individual member.

## The Polygon of Forces
The use of reciprocal figures is based on a well-known proposition in statics, which states that external forces acting on a bridge or frame in equilibrium can be represented by a closed polygon. In such a polygon, each side is parallel to one of the forces and represents that force's magnitude and direction. These sides may be arranged in any order, provided that when passing around the polygon in one direction, the direction of each side corresponds to the direction of the force it represents.

By extending the definition of reciprocal figures, this polygon of forces can be specifically called the reciprocal figure of the external forces if the sides are arranged according to the order of the joints on which the forces act. For example, if joints and forces are numbered 1, 2, 3, 4, and so on, while passing around the outside of the frame in one direction and returning to joint 1, the polygon should be drawn so that the side representing force 2 is next to the side representing force 1, followed by the side representing force 3, and so forth. Under these conditions, if the frame is considered as a single point in equilibrium under external forces, the polygon falls under Maxwell's definition of a reciprocal figure.

## Practical Implementation and Alternatives
Reciprocal figures are easily drawn by following definite rules. An example of this application can be seen in the analysis of a Warren girder loaded at the top and bottom joints. In such a case, the polygon of external forces and the reciprocal figure (or half of the reciprocal figure) can be used to determine the stresses.

While the method of reciprocal figures is available for these calculations, other techniques may be more convenient depending on the circumstances. The method of sections is often more convenient, and the method of influence lines is frequently the readiest way to deal with braced girders.
