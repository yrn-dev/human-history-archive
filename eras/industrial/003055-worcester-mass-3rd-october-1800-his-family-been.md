# George Bancroft

## Early Life and Education
George Bancroft was an American statesman and historian born on October 3, 1800, in Worcester, Massachusetts. His family had a long history in America, having arrived in 1632. His father, Aaron Bancroft, was an author, clergyman, and a distinguished soldier during the revolutionary period.

Bancroft's education began at Phillips Academy in Exeter and continued at Harvard University. He furthered his studies in Europe at the universities of Berlin, Goettingen, and Heidelberg. While at Goettingen, he studied natural science with Blumenbach, New Testament Greek with Eichhorn, and Plato with Heeren. Bancroft was particularly influenced by Heeren, whom he considered the greatest living historical critic and a forerunner of the modern school; from Heeren, Bancroft acquired his enthusiasm for minute and painstaking erudition.

## European Travels and Early Career
Bancroft concluded his period of preparation with a tour of Europe. During this time, he received kind attention from numerous distinguished figures in the fields of art, science, and letters, including Hegel, Byron, Goethe, Humboldt, Savigny, Constant, Manzoni, Bunsen, Schleiermacher, and Niebuhr.

Upon returning from Europe in 1822, Bancroft attempted to enter the ministry, as his father was a Unitarian who had devoted him to that work. However, his initial experiments at preaching were unsatisfactory, as the theological teaching of that era had replaced faith with literature and criticism. He subsequently took a position as a tutor at Harvard.

## Intellectual and Social Challenges
During his time at Harvard, Bancroft found himself at odds with the institution's narrow curriculum and the pedantic spirit associated with the pursuit of classical studies. As a humanist, he struggled to fit into a society that was highly satisfied with itself, especially since he had adopted a new manner in Europe characterized by the affections of ardent youth. This caused him to be subjected to considerable ridicule.

In 1823, he published a small volume consisting of original pieces and translations of poetry, though the work did not bring him fame. Over time, as he became more familiar with his surroundings, his literary and personal style came to be recognized as a symbol of an enormous underlying energy and a firm resolve to maintain a philosophic calm. This energy was spent in labor described as "ohne Hast, aber auch ohne Rast."
