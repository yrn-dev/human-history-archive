# Benjamin Helm Bristow

Benjamin Helm Bristow (1832–1896) was an American politician and lawyer who served in various high-ranking legal and financial capacities for the United States government, most notably as the secretary of the treasury.

## Early Life and Education
Born on June 20, 1832, in Elkton, Kentucky, Bristow was the son of Francis Marion Bristow (1804–1864), who served as a Whig member of Congress from 1854–1855 and 1859–1861. Bristow graduated from Jefferson College in Canonsburg, Pennsylvania, in 1851. Following his graduation, he studied law under his father and was admitted to the Kentucky bar in 1853.

## Military Service
At the start of the American Civil War, Bristow entered military service as a lieutenant-colonel of the 25th Kentucky Infantry, during which time he was severely wounded at Shiloh. He later served as lieutenant-colonel and eventually colonel of the 8th Kentucky Cavalry, which he helped recruit. In July 1863, he assisted in the capture of John H. Morgan.

## Legal and Political Career
Following his military service, Bristow held several legislative and legal positions:
* **State Senate:** He served as a state senator from 1863 to 1865.
* **District Attorney:** Between 1865 and 1866, he served as an assistant United States district-attorney. He subsequently served as the district-attorney for the Louisville district from 1866 to 1870.
* **Solicitor-General:** After practicing law for a few months with John M. Harlan, Bristow became the first appointed solicitor-general of the United States, serving from 1870 to 1872.

In 1873, President Grant nominated Bristow for the position of attorney-general of the United States. However, this appointment was contingent upon the confirmation of George H. Williams as chief justice of the United States, a condition that did not occur.

## Secretary of the Treasury and the "Whisky Ring"
Bristow served as the secretary of the treasury from 1874 to 1876. During his tenure, he vigorously prosecuted the "Whisky Ring," a fraudulent operation headquartered in St. Louis that had begun in 1870 or 1871. The ring defrauded the Federal government of a significant portion of its rightful revenue from whisky distillation.

The conspiracy involved revenue officers and distillers in cities including Cincinnati, Milwaukee, and St. Louis. In St. Louis alone, illicit gains were estimated to exceed $2,500,000 over the six years between 1870 and 1876. These funds were divided between the officers and distillers, with assessments levied on distillers under the guise of a Republican campaign fund intended to support the re-election of President Grant.

Bristow's prosecution efforts led to the indictment of more than 200 men, some of whom were convicted. However, these individuals were pardoned after several months of imprisonment. The prosecution was complicated by the personal friendship between President Grant and Orville E. Babcock, the president's private secretary and an alleged accomplice of the ring, which led Grant to interfere indiscreetly in the proceedings.
