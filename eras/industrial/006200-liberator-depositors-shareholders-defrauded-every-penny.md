# The Liberator Building Society

The Liberator was a building society that attracted funds exceeding one million sterling from provident individuals across all social classes and regions of the country. These funds were acquired through the use of specious representations.

## Mismanagement and Insolvency
Although the society was presented as a building society, the active managers did not apply the collected funds to legitimate building society purposes. Instead, the money was used to support other undertakings in which those same managers were involved. This diversion of funds led to the insolvency of the entire group of concerns in October 1892.

As a result of this collapse, the shareholders and depositors of the Liberator were defrauded of every penny of their investments. This loss caused great distress for many who lost their savings, and in some instances, led to absolute ruin.

## Impact on Building Societies
The failure of the Liberator significantly weakened public confidence in building societies in general. This decline was particularly evident in the capital of incorporated building societies, which fell from a peak of nearly 54 million in 1887 to below 43 million by 1895.

The loss of confidence triggered runs on several societies that had utilized the deposit system, leaving some unable to withstand the withdrawals. One such instance occurred at the Birkbeck Society, where an anxious crowd of depositors besieged the institution for two days demanding their money. 

The Birkbeck Society avoided collapse because a very large portion of its funds were invested in securities that were easily convertible. This allowed the society to obtain sufficient assistance from the Bank of England to pay every depositor who requested their money without hesitation. This action firmly established the credit of the Birkbeck Society and encouraged others to deposit money. Because this large society did not succumb, the disastrous results that would have affected the entire body of building societies were avoided.

## General Context of Building and Loan Associations
Building and loan associations typically operate within small communities, such as a single county, though some national associations operate on a larger scale. National associations sell shares regardless of residence and provide loans on property anywhere. 

In local associations, profits are distributed based on the total dues paid by shareholders. In national associations, only a portion of the dues is considered for profit distribution; for example, if dues are 60 cents a share per month, 8 or 10 cents may be allocated to an expense fund, with the remainder credited to the loan fund. The creation of these expense funds has occasionally been a source of disaster.

The security of these associations depends primarily on the integrity of their conduct rather than their organizational form. While some states—including California, Illinois, New Jersey, New York, Massachusetts, and Ohio—place these associations under the same legal supervision as savings banks, others only require formalities of incorporation. Generally, these associations are managed by individuals not trained as bankers. When unsuccessful associations disband, great losses are usually avoided because the business consists of loans made to the shareholders themselves, who hold the securities.

## Share Value and Withdrawal
Prior to the maturing of a share, two distinct values exist:
*   **Book Value (or Holding Value):** The actual value of a share at a specific time, calculated by adding accrued profits to all paid dues.
*   **Withdrawal Value:** The amount the association is willing to pay a shareholder who wishes to sever their connection before the share matures.

Some associations prohibit withdrawal before maturity, meaning a shareholder can only realize the value of their shares by selling them to another person at an obtainable price. There are twelve or more different plans for the withdrawal of funds, with each association maintaining its own regulations.
