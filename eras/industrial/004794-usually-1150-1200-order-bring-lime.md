# Lime in Industrial and Construction Applications

Lime is a versatile chemical substance utilized extensively in the production of building materials, the dyeing of textiles, and the formulation of various mortars. Its application varies depending on whether it is used in its raw, slaked, or combined form.

## Use in Brick Production
In the manufacture of burnt bricks, the chemical composition of the clay determines the firing process. Clays are generally divided into two types: those with a small percentage of carbonate of lime and those containing a considerable percentage, known as "marls." Marls typically contain between 15% and 30% calcium carbonate, though some may exceed 40%. These lime-clays are distinctive because they burn to a sulphur-yellow colour.

The firing process is critical for the strength and durability of the brick. While ordinary bricks are typically fired at around 1050° C (with a range of 900° C to 1250° C), lime-clays require a higher finishing temperature, usually between 1150° C and 1200° C. This higher heat is necessary to bring the lime into chemical combination with other substances present in the clay. During the preparation of these marls, it is important to remove limestone pebbles; if left in the clay, these pebbles convert into quicklime during firing, which can cause the brick to shatter when exposed to weather.

Additionally, "sand-lime bricks" are produced using a different process patented by Dr. Michaelis of Berlin in the early 1880s. These are made by mixing dry sand with approximately one-tenth of its weight in powdered slaked lime. The mixture is moistened, pressed under high pressure (about 60 tons per sq. in.), and then subjected to high-pressure steam (120 lb to 150 lb per sq. in.) for ten to fifteen hours in steel cylinders. This process creates a bond of hydrated calcium silicate, making the sand-lime brick stronger than ordinary mortar-bricks.

## Mortars and Cements
Lime is a primary component in various mortars used for construction. The type of lime used affects the setting time and durability:
*   **Fat Limes:** Pure limes that are slow-setting and unsuitable for mortar because they may remain soft for months if air access is limited.
*   **Grey Stone Lime:** Feebly hydraulic and suitable for ordinary work, though it decays in weather and should be pointed with cement or blue ash mortar. It is not suitable for foundations or wet areas.
*   **Lias Lime:** A hydraulic lime that sets firm under water and is used for high-class work.

Common mortar proportions include:
*   **Grey lime mortar:** One part lime to two or three parts sand.
*   **Lias lime mortar:** Similar proportions, though equal parts lime and sand are used for work below ground.
*   **Selenitic lime mortar:** One part lime to four or five parts sand, mixed by grinding the lime in water first.
*   **Blue or black mortar:** Equal parts lime and foundry ashes.
*   **Chimney flues:** A mixture of one part lime and three parts cow dung (free from straw or litter) is used for pargetting.

Lime is also used to enhance the strength of Portland cement mortars. For example, a fortified mortar may consist of one part slaked lime, one part Portland cement, and seven parts sand.

## Application in Dyeing
Lime is utilized in several types of vats for dyeing cotton and wool. In the "lime and copperas vat," quicklime, ferrous sulphate, and finely ground indigo are used; the lime decomposes the ferrous sulphate to precipitate ferrous hydrate, which reduces indigo to indigo white. In the "zinc powder vat," lime and indigo are combined with zinc powder, which liberates the hydrogen necessary to reduce the indigo.

In the "hydrosulphite vat," lime is used to neutralize a solution of hydrosulphite of soda (prepared from zinc powder and bisulphite of soda) before it is used with indigo. This specific vat is advantageous because it is easily prepared, produces little sediment, and can be used for both wool and cotton. For wool specifically, a "woad vat" may be used, which includes lime along with indigo, woad, bran, and madder.
