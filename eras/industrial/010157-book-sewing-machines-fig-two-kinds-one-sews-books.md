# Book-Sewing Machines

Book-sewing machines are a primary component of commercial machine-binding, a field largely developed through German or American ingenuity. These machines are designed to execute tasks that were previously performed laboriously by hand, providing a significant saving in time and labor. While high-class trade binders may still utilize older manual methods and private binders generally avoid machines entirely, larger trade binders employ them extensively.

## Types of Sewing Machines

There are two primary kinds of book-sewing machines. The first type sews books on bands, which may be either round or flat. These band-working machines pull the return thread through the upper and lower edges of the back of each section. This process weakens the sections to some extent, though this effect can be partially neutralized through careful head-banding.

The second type of machine replaces bands with a kind of chain stitch. In this system, the return thread is brought back inside each section. While this avoids cutting the edges of the back, it results in a flattening of the book's back, which creates difficulties during the subsequent covering process.

## Mechanical Operation

In these machines, sections are sewn continuously in a long line and are later cut apart. The operator places each section one by one onto a metal saddle. The machine operates continuously, though the action can be controlled or cut off using a pedal or foot-lever. The process involves threads catching into hooked needles and being drawn through holes created by piercers set at specific distances. A shuttle, similar to those found in ordinary sewing machines, sews the inner thread back and forth. Although the inner threads may be too bulky to be entirely satisfactory, this is considered a minor issue compared to the cutting of the back edges found in band-working machines.

## The Martini Machine

A significant improvement in this technology is the Martini book-sewing machine. Unlike other models, the Martini machine sews books on tape without cutting the head or tail of the book. It is also capable of forming complete Kettle stitches. This machine can accommodate books of any size up to 18 inches, utilizing straight needles and simple adjustments for various book sizes.

## Comparison with Hand Binding

While machine-binding is efficient for large editions of a single book, it has limitations compared to hand labor. Modern machines are usually power-driven and must be accurately set to a specific book size, making them unsuitable for books of varying sizes. 

Furthermore, the old methods of sewing and rounding backs by hand are considered safer and stronger, and they are less likely to bruise or injure the paper. A specific difference exists in the fastening of the cover: in hand-sewn books on bands, the loose ends of the bands are drawn through the boards and strongly fastened through their substance. Machine-bound "cased" books, by contrast, typically have the loose ends of the tapes pasted to the inner surfaces of the boards. Consequently, a cased book is more likely to slip out of its cover once the paste perishes.

## Related Technical Processes

The sewing process is part of a broader technical development in bookbinding. Other machine-executed processes include folding printed sheets, rounding the backs of sewn books, making outer cases, stamping designs, and covering books with leather or cloth. 

For the final assembly, the Smyth casing-in machine is used to paste the sides of a book and attach the cover. It utilizes rollers and regulated pressure to fix the cover, including a "jointing-in" device to align the joints of the cover with those of the book. This machine can handle books from 4 to 22 inches in length and 1/4 to 3 inches in thickness, processing between 10 and 15 books per minute.
