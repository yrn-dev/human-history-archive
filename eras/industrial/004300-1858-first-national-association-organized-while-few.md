# The First National Association of Base-Ball

## Origins and Early Development
Base-ball is the national summer sport of the United States, and it is also popular in Japan and throughout Canada. The origins of the game are obscure, with different authorities offering conflicting theories. Some suggest it is derived from rounders, an old English game with several variations played in America during the colonial period. Others argue that the resemblance to rounders is a coincidence and that the game originated in the United States, specifically in Cooperstown, New York, in 1839, where it is said Abner Doubleday, later a general in the U.S. army, devised a scheme for playing it.

At the start of the 19th century, schoolboys in the North Atlantic states played a popular game known as "One Old Cat." This version involved three boys who took turns batting and fielding; a run was scored if the batsman could run to a single base and back without being put out. This game was later modified into "Two Old Cat," "Three Old Cat," and "Four Old Cat," which featured four, six, and eight players, respectively.

## Formalization and Club Organization
A development of the "Old Cat" games resulted in "town-ball," leading to the organization of the Olympic Town-Ball Club of Philadelphia in 1833. The first matches between organized base-ball clubs took place in the neighborhood of New York, where the Washington Baseball Club was founded in 1843.

In 1845, the Knickerbocker Baseball Club drew up the first regular code of rules. These rules were utilized in matches involving several clubs, including the Empire and Gotham Eagle clubs of New York, as well as the Eckford, Atlantic, Putnam, and Excelsior clubs of Brooklyn.

## The First National Association
In 1858, the first National Association was organized. While the laws of this association were few and simple—and generally similar to the rules of the present code—there were distinct differences in play. The ball used during this era was "livelier" and larger than the modern ball. Additionally, the pitcher was required to deliver the ball with a full toss, and no approach to a throw was permitted.

## Expansion and Professionalism
The game's popularity grew rapidly, leading to the creation of many famous clubs. These included the Lowell and Beacon clubs of Boston, the Maple Leaf of Guelph, the Forest City of Cleveland, and the Red Stockings of Cincinnati. However, the sharp rivalry between the leading teams eventually led to the introduction of semi-professionalism.
