# Christian Mission, the Christian Mission and (in 1878) the Salvation Army

The history of Christian missions encompasses a wide range of activities, from the early attempts to establish the faith in Asia and the Pacific to the development of specialized urban ministries in the 19th century. These efforts have varied in their methods, ranging from diplomatic envoys and educational initiatives to quasi-military social organizations.

## The Salvation Army and William Booth
William Booth (1829– ), born in Nottingham, was the founder and "general" of the Salvation Army. His religious development began at age fifteen under the influence of Wesleyan Methodists, where he served as a local preacher. After moving to London in 1849, Booth's passion for open-air preaching led to his departure from the Wesleyans. He subsequently joined the Methodist New Connexion and was ordained a minister, though he left in 1861 to pursue active "travelling evangelization."

Alongside his wife, Catherine Mumford, Booth preached in Cardiff and Walsall. In Walsall, he organized a "Hallelujah band" consisting of converted criminals and others who provided public testimony of their conversion. Upon returning to London in 1864, Booth conducted services in tents and the open air. He founded an organization that underwent several name changes: it was first known as the East London Revival Society, then the East London Christian Mission, then the Christian Mission, and finally, in 1878, the Salvation Army.

The Salvation Army is a quasi-military organization with "Orders and Regulations" modeled on the British army. Its operational methods include:
* Holding outdoor meetings and processions.
* Visiting prisons, private houses, and public-houses.
* Conducting meetings in factories, theatres, and other unusual buildings.
* Utilizing the language of everyday life and popular song-tunes.
* Encouraging every convert to act as a daily witness for Christ in private and public.

Early campaigns faced violent opposition from a "Skeleton Army" organized to disrupt meetings, resulting in fines and imprisonment for Booth's followers until approximately 1889. The Army expanded to the United States in 1880, Australia in 1881, and subsequently to India, Ceylon, the European continent, and other regions.

## Missions in Asia
Christian missions in Asia have seen varying degrees of success and different strategic approaches.

### China and Mongolia
Early missions to the Mongol Empire and China were often feeble. While Innocent IV sent Dominicans to Karakorum and Saint Louis of France sent missionaries and relics via Persia, many legates and bishops were lost before reaching China. Mangu Khan had Nestorian Christians at his court, and a papal legate reached Pekin in 1346. However, these efforts often failed because they were perceived as attempts to advance the claims of the Pope to world dominion rather than focusing on the teachings of Jesus. In 1269, Kublai Khan requested a hundred men of learning from the Pope to establish an understanding, but the response was two Dominican friars who abandoned the expedition.

With the fall of the Yuan dynasty in 1368, the nationalist Ming dynasty emerged, which was initially hostile to foreigners, potentially leading to a massacre of Christian missions. Christianity remained largely absent until the later Ming period (up to 1644), when Jesuits made a more successful attempt to propagate Catholic Christianity by arriving via the sea.

### India
In India, Protestant missions initially found success only among outcaste and low-caste peoples, particularly in south Travancore and Tinevelly. Alexander Duff (1806–1878), a Scottish missionary who arrived in Calcutta in 1830, shifted the strategy toward an educational mission to reach high-caste Hindus and Mahommedan communities. Duff established an English school centered on the Bible, teaching secular knowledge up to a university standard. This approach influenced the British government's 1835 decision to promote European science and literature in higher education.

Other missions adapted to local social structures. Jesuits Xavier and Fra dei Nobili adopted Brahman customs—including wearing yellow or cavy dress, performing frequent ablutions, and using sandalwood paste—to convert southern India. Pope Gregory XV eventually published a bull sanctioning caste regulations within Indian Christian churches. Similarly, the German mission of Schwarz and the Danish mission of Tranquebar permitted followers to retain their caste. Despite these efforts, native converts rose from 1,246,000 in 1872 to 2,664,000 in 1901, though the faith remained largely limited to out-castes.

### Korea
The first serious attempt to convert Korea was made by the French *Société des Missions Étrangères* in 1835. Despite persecutions—including the martyrdom of French priests and the beheading or imprisonment of thousands of native Christians in 1866—the mission grew to 40,000 converts by 1900. Protestant missionaries from the American Methodist Episcopal and Presbyterian Churches entered in 1884 and 1885. By 1890, English clergy, doctors, and nursing sisters arrived, establishing schools, hospitals, and a college in Seoul. They also established tri-lingual printing presses and translated the Bible into *En-mun*.

## Missions in the Pacific and Americas
In the Loyalty Islands, Christianity was introduced to Maré by native teachers from Samoa and Rarotonga. The London Missionary Society established settlements at Maré (1854), Lifu (1859), and Uea (1865). These Protestant missions faced violence from a French commandant, but intervention by Lord Shaftesbury and the emperor Napoleon secured liberty of worship. Further persecution in Uea in 1875 prompted a protest from the British government.

In the United States, Mark Hopkins (1802–1887), a professor and president of Williams College, maintained a lifelong interest in missions. From 1857 until his death, he served as president of the American Board of Commissioners for Foreign Missions (the American Congregational Mission Board).
