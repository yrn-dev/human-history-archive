# Commercial and Political Developments of 1873

The year 1873 was marked by significant commercial shifts, infrastructural completions, and political transitions across various global regions, ranging from the Austro-Hungarian Empire to the United States.

## The Commercial Crisis and Political Shift in Austria-Hungary
In the Austro-Hungarian Empire, the German Liberals held a period of predominance, utilizing an increased majority in the Reichsrath following a reform bill. This majority allowed them to pass several important laws concerning ecclesiastical affairs. However, the rule of the German Liberals was eventually overthrown, and the government was forced to seek support from the Clericals and Federalists within four years.

The influence of the German Liberals was indirectly affected by a great commercial crisis in 1873, which followed several years of active speculations. This economic instability coincided with a broader shift in economic thought. After 1873, Austrian manufacturers—who were facing competition from Belgian, English, and German goods—began a strong movement in favor of protective duties. This represented a reaction against the doctrines of free trade.

## Evolution of the Austro-Hungarian Customs Union
The commercial policy of the dual state was governed by the Customs Union established in 1867. Initially, the customs tariff relied on commercial treaties with nations such as Great Britain, Italy, France, and Germany. These treaties included "most favoured nation" clauses and specified maximum duties, resulting in a system that nearly lacked customs duties entirely.

Because these treaties prevented tariff revisions through legislation, the shift toward protectionism only materialized after the treaties expired after 1875. While Hungary initially favored free trade to ensure a market for its agricultural produce, it eventually gave notice to terminate the commercial union with Austria. A new treaty was reached in 1877. Under this agreement:
* Raw materials continued to be imported free of duty.
* Low duties were placed on corn and textile goods.
* Excises on brandy and sugar were increased.
* All duties were required to be paid in gold, which caused a considerable increase.

Ultimately, treaties with France and Great Britain were not renewed, and agreements with Germany failed due to the policy changes adopted by Bismarck. This allowed Austria-Hungary to introduce tariffs based simply on legislation.

## American Commercial and Industrial Milestones
In the United States, 1873 saw the completion of the International bridge from Squaw Island, which cost $1,500,000 and connected Buffalo with the Canadian shore. This contributed to Buffalo's status as a major industrial and commercial center, supported by the Erie Canal (completed in 1825) and extensive lake commerce.

Additionally, 1873 was a pivotal year for American capitalism in the West. John William Mackay, along with William S. O'Brien, James C. Flood, and James G. Fair, discovered the great Bonanza vein in the Comstock lode. Located more than 1,200 feet deep, the vein yielded as much as $632 per ton in March 1873 and totaled nearly $19,000,000 by 1877. This venture led to the establishment of the Bank of Nevada in San Francisco.

## Czech Political Divisions
During this era, the Czechs were weakened by internal conflicts between two primary factions: the Old Czechs and the Young Czechs (also known as Radicals). The Old Czechs, led by Rieger, maintained a policy of passive opposition and an alliance with the clergy and Feudalists. 

In contrast, the Young Czechs recalled Hussite traditions and sought universal suffrage, rejecting alliances with the clergy and aristocracy. They aimed to join Germans in political reform and take their seats in the diet. This tension led twenty-seven members of the diet, including Stadkowsky and Gregr, to resign their seats after being outvoted in the Czech Club. Although the Young Czechs were defeated in subsequent elections, the two parties spent the following four years opposing each other as much as they opposed the Germans.
