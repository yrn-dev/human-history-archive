# The Colonial Empire

The colonial empire is a vast political entity comprising over fifty distinct governments. For the purposes of political administration, the broader empire is divided into three primary sections: the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland (including the dependencies of the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands), the Indian empire (consisting of British India and feudatory native states), and the colonial empire, which encompasses all other colonies and dependencies.

## Classification of Colonies
The colonial empire is divided into dependencies and colonies of three specific classes, which are sometimes associated into federated groups for administrative purposes:

*   **Crown Colonies:** In these territories, the crown maintains entire control over legislation, and public officers are controlled by the home government. Laws are typically made by the governor with the concurrence of a crown-nominated council. In colonies acquired by settlement, the council is created under imperial or local laws (such as in Jamaica), whereas in those acquired by cession or conquest, the council's authority rests wholly on the crown (such as in Ceylon). Exceptions include St Helena and Gibraltar, where the governor alone may make laws.
*   **Representative Colonies:** These possess representative institutions but lack responsible government. The home government retains control of public officers, and the crown holds only a veto on legislation.
*   **Responsible Colonies:** These possess both representative institutions and responsible government. While the crown retains a veto on legislation, the home government has no control over any public officer except for the governor.

Other administrative arrangements include partially self-governing communities with appointed executives and elected legislatures, such as Bermuda, the Bahamas, Jamaica, and Malta. Additionally, there are nominal protectorates—often tropical raw-product areas—administered either through a chartered company (as in Rhodesia) or a High Commissioner overseeing native chiefs (as in Basutoland).

## Federated Groups and Unification
To manage matters of common interest, separate governments may mutually consent to the authority of a common government. In self-governing colonies, these federations are created voluntarily. The two primary self-governing federated groups are the Commonwealth of Australia and the Dominion of Canada. 

Other examples of federation include the federated Malay States and the Leeward Island group of the West Indies. In India, the associated provinces are under the control of a central government. In South Africa, the self-governing colonies chose unification over federation, merging into the Union of South Africa in 1910.

## Evolution of Self-Government and Imperial Policy
The development of colonial self-government reached its fullest extent between 1845 and 1875, a period dominated by the "Manchester school." During this time, self-governing institutions were generally viewed as a preliminary step toward inevitable separation. This inclination to withdraw from imperial responsibilities provided opportunities for foreign nations and eventually led to a reaction, culminating in the scramble for Africa.

By 1872, Lord Beaconsfield repudiated the idea that self-government led to separation, instead describing colonial constitutions as part of a "great policy of imperial consolidation." In 1875, W. E. Forster advocated for imperial federation to replace dependence with association. This movement progressed with the 1884 foundation of the Imperial Federation League. Subsequent Colonial Conferences (renamed Imperial Conferences in 1907) sought colonial opinion on imperial organization and defence.

## Administration and Territorial Growth
The crown receives advice on colonial administration from the secretaries of state for India and the colonies. This structure followed the 1782 suppression of the "Council of Trade and Plantations." While there was a period where the government attempted to treat colonies simply as dependencies, representative institutions were granted to Canada in 1791, and later to South Africa and Australia.

Most territorial acquisitions occurred in the 17th century and later. Earlier exceptions include the Channel Islands (part of William the Conqueror's Norman inheritance) and the Isle of Man. The Isle of Man was held by Edward I, sold in 1393 to Sir William Scrope, and later granted by Henry IV in 1406 to Sir John Stanley. It passed through the earls of Derby and the dukes of Atholl before being sold to the crown for £70,000 and an annuity of £2000. Sir Humphrey Gilbert also took nominal possession of Newfoundland in 1583.

## Imperial Defence and Finance
Historically, land defence was the responsibility of the colonies; for example, the early American colonies and the East India Company in India provided their own defence. However, during the French wars, the North American colonies could not sustain the cost, leading Great Britain to intervene with troops under General Braddock in 1755. To fund this, the imperial parliament taxed the colonies and passed the 1765 Quartering Act to station 10,000 imperial troops there.

Following the American War, the United Kingdom taxpayers bore the cost of imperial defence (including the navy) for most of the empire, except for the Hudson Bay territories and India, where trading companies paid their own expenses. This arrangement lasted until the end of the Napoleonic Wars. By the mid-19th century, fiscal changes gave self-governing colonies command of their own resources, leading to the expectation that they provide for their own governing expenses, including defence.
