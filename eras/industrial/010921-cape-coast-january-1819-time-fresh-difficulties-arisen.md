# Cape Coast in January 1819

## Diplomatic Missions and the Arrival of Joseph Dupuis
In January 1819, Mr. Joseph Dupuis arrived at Cape Coast. He had been appointed as consul to Kumasi by the government at home, which, despite some demurs regarding the course previously pursued, recognized the wisdom of maintaining and cultivating intercourse with the powerful African kingdom of Ashanti. Prior to this appointment, Mr. Dupuis had served as the consul at Mogador.

## Regional Conflict and the Treaty of Kumasi
Upon his arrival, Dupuis found that fresh difficulties had arisen between the Ashanti and the coast natives, the latter of whom were supported by the British. Following his arrival at Cape Coast, Dupuis set out on February 9, 1820, reaching Kumasi on February 28 of that year. 

After conducting several meetings with the king, a treaty was established. This agreement acknowledged the sovereignty of the Ashanti over the territory of the Fanti and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of their enemies.

## British Administrative Response
The treaty was disowned by Mr. J. Hope Smith, the governor of Cape Coast, who viewed the agreement as a betrayal of the interests of the natives under British protection. Mr. Hope Smith's position was supported by the government in London. Consequently, in 1821, the British government assumed direct control of the British settlements.

The crown appointed Sir Charles M'Carthy as the first governor. Sir Charles espoused the cause of the Fanti, which led to a military confrontation with the Ashanti.

## The Battle of Essamako and Its Aftermath
On January 21, 1824, at a location beyond the Prah called Essamako, Sir Charles M'Carthy was defeated in battle by the Ashanti. The Ashanti forces numbered 10,000 men, while Sir Charles commanded only 500. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed during the engagement. Following the battle, the governor's skull was taken to Kumasi and used as a royal drinking-cup.

There were assertions that the defeat occurred because the ordnance-keeper brought up kegs of vermicelli instead of ammunition; however, records indicate that this mistake, if it occurred, only hastened an inevitable catastrophe.

## Transition of Power and Subsequent Warfare
The day of the defeat at Essamako coincided with the death of Osai Tutu Quamina, who was succeeded by Osai Okoto. This period initiated a state of chronic warfare. This conflict continued until August 7, 1826, when the Ashanti suffered a signal defeat at Dodowah.

Following the battle of Dodowah, the power of the Ashanti over the coast tribes began to wane, and the payment of ground rent for the forts held by the British ceased. By 1831, the king of Ashanti was forced to purchase peace from Mr. George Maclean, the administrator of the Gold Coast. The price for this peace was 600 oz. of gold and the delivery of the king's son as a hostage to Cape Coast.
