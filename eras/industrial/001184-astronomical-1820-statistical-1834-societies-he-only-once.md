# Astronomical and Statistical Societies

The Astronomical Society (founded 1820) and the Statistical Society (founded 1834) were scientific organizations established in England during the early 19th century. Both societies were linked to the efforts of prominent mathematicians and astronomers of the era to systematize data and improve the precision of scientific calculation.

## The Astronomical Society
Founded in 1820, the Royal Astronomical Society was established with the involvement of several key figures in English science. Charles Babbage, an English mathematician and mechanician, was instrumental in the founding of the society. Babbage had been drawn early in his career to the importance of errors introduced into astronomical calculations caused by inaccuracies in the computation of tables.

Francis Baily, an English astronomer who retired from the London Stock Exchange in 1825 to devote himself entirely to astronomy, also took a leading part in the foundation of the society in 1820. Baily's contributions to the field were extensive; in 1827, he was awarded the society's gold medal for his preparation of the Astronomical Society's Catalogue of 2881 stars. Baily further contributed to the field by revising the catalogues of several historical astronomers, including Ptolemy, Tycho Brahe, Ulugh Beg, Hevelius, and Edmund Halley.

## The Statistical Society
The Statistical Society was founded in 1834. Charles Babbage was instrumental in its establishment. The society's work intersected with the interests of various public figures and scientists who sought to apply statistical analysis to governance and science. 

John Lubbock, 1st Baron Avebury—an English banker, naturalist, and politician—was a prominent supporter of the Statistical Society. Lubbock utilized his association with the society to take an active role in criticizing the increase of municipal debt and the encroachment of municipal trading.

## Relation to Mathematical and Stellar Science
The development of these societies occurred alongside a broader movement to improve mathematical instruction and computation in England. Charles Babbage, along with George Peacock and Sir John Herschel, worked to raise mathematical standards, specifically attempting to replace Newtonian notation with Leibnitzian notation in infinitesimal calculus. Babbage's interest in the reduction of errors in tables led him to develop the principles of a calculating engine, a project to which he devoted many years and a portion of his private fortune.

The application of statistical methods eventually expanded into the field of astronomy itself, leading to the emergence of a branch of science known as Stellar Statistics. This field treats the vast number of stars as a unified whole rather than a collection of individual bodies. In this discipline, catalogues detailing the proper motions, position, magnitude, and spectra of stars are considered as essential to the stellar statistician as census tables are to those who study humanity.
