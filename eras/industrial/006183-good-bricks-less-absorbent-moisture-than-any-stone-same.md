# Brick and Stone in Construction

## Material Properties and Moisture Absorption
In general construction, stone and brick serve as the primary materials and are held in combination by gravity. While the materials themselves are wholly incompressible, the masses created from them are compressible until the setting medium, such as cement or mortar, has indurated to a state of hardness.

A critical factor in the selection of these materials is their relationship with moisture. The most suitable stone for general construction is that which is free to work and least absorbent of moisture, as absorbent stone disintegrates rapidly when exposed to weather. However, non-absorbent stone is often so hard that it is not always economical to use. Consequently, builders may use harder stone for the main body of a structure or in areas exposed to weather, while protecting softer stone.

Good bricks possess a distinct advantage over stone regarding moisture: they are less absorbent of moisture than any stone of the same degree of hardness. Additionally, bricks are better non-conductors of heat than stone. The absorbent properties of bricks vary by type; for example, high-quality ordinary London stock should not absorb more than one-fifth of its bulk after twenty-four hours of soaking, whereas inferior bricks may absorb as much as one-third.

## Structural Stability and Bonding
Brickwork is considered more reliable than stone in the form of rubble as a basis for a stable structure, provided the constituents and setting materials are the same. This is because the shaped form of the brick allows it to seat itself truly and produce a more perfectly combined mass through bonding. In contrast, dressed rubble consists of variously sized and imperfectly shaped stones that cannot bond or bed truly. This requires the use of mortar to compensate for inequalities in form and the introduction of different sized stones to account for irregularity.

The highest level of stability is achieved using accurately bonded and wrought blocks of stone. In such cases, mortar is used only as necessary to prevent the disintegrating action of wind and water. However, when a structure must resist the force of water, mortar is essential to ensure every block maintains its full weight and the support of collateral and superimposed stones to resist loosening.

## Historical Use and Development
The Romans were prolific users of both sun-dried and burnt bricks, introducing the practice to England. They frequently used large, thin bricks as a bond for rubble masonry. Although the art of brickmaking fell into disuse following the decline of the Roman Empire, it was revived centuries later, as seen in the terra-cotta and brick work of ancient Italy.

In the Low Countries, specifically the Netherlands, a brick architecture developed due to the scarcity of stone. In England, brickmaking reached a state of great perfection during the reigns of the early Tudor kings, producing some of the finest ornamental brickwork. The Great Fire of London in 1666 further accelerated the industry, as most new buildings were constructed of brick. This led to the passing of a statute regulating the thickness of walls in various rates of dwelling-houses. In other regions, such as the Castilian landscape of Spain, villages are characterized by the use of sunburnt unbaked bricks that mirror the brownish-grey tint of the soil.

## Moisture Prevention and Regulations
To protect buildings from moisture, various techniques and regulations are employed. By-laws from 1891 issued by the London County Council require every wall of a building to have a damp course of moisture-impervious materials at least 6 inches below the lowest floor. External walls abutting earth must also be protected by impervious materials.

To prevent water from soaking down from the top of a wall, copings are used. These may consist of:
* Hard, well-burnt bricks set on edge in cement.
* Specially made non-porous coping bricks.
* Stone, cement, or cast-iron that is "weathered" or sloped to shed rain.
* Ordinary bricks set on edge in cement with a "creasing" of two courses of tiles below.

Other protections include coating exterior walls with rough cast or cement, or covering them with tiles or slates on battens. Historically, "dry areas" or air drains—hollow spaces at least 9 inches wide—were used below ground to prevent moisture from reaching the main walls, though these have largely been superseded by the application of cement or asphalt.

## Mortar and Manufacturing
The quality of mortar depends heavily on the sand used. Pit sand is preferred because it is sharp and angular, though it must be washed to remove clay and screened to remove gravel. River sand is less effective because attrition rubs the particles smooth. Sea sand is considered objectionable because it contains a saline taint that attracts moisture, which can keep brickwork permanently damp. For external pointing, blue or black mortar is often used for its durability, utilizing smith's ashes or foundry sand. Other substitutes for sand include fine stone grit, clinkers, cinders, or ground old bricks.

In the manufacturing process, the drying of bricks is now typically conducted in heated sheds. Proper ventilation is required to ensure a dry atmosphere; if the air is too moist, soluble salts are carried to the surface as water, creating a "scum." In a dry atmosphere, moisture escapes as vapour, leaving salts distributed through the mass and preventing scum.
