# BAIRD, SIR DAVID (1757-1829)

Sir David Baird was a British general born in December 1757 at Newbyth in Aberdeenshire. His military career spanned several decades, characterized by extensive service in India and other colonial territories.

## Early Career and Captivity in India
Baird entered the British army in 1773. In 1779, while serving as a captain in the 73rd Highlanders (which later became the 71st), he was sent to India. Upon his arrival, he was attached to a force under the command of Sir Hector Munro. This force was dispatched to assist Colonel Baillie's detachment, which was under threat from Hyder Ali. 

The resulting action led to the destruction of the entire force. Baird was severely wounded during the conflict and was captured by the Mysore chief. He and other prisoners were treated barbarously and remained in captivity for over four years. During this time, Baird remained in fetters; it is noted that his mother, upon hearing of this, remarked, "God help the chiel chained to poor Davie." The bullet from his wound was not extracted until he was released.

## Rise Through the Ranks
Following his release, Baird's career progressed steadily. He attained the rank of major in 1787 and visited England in 1789. In 1790, he purchased a lieutenant-colonelcy and returned to India the following year. During the war against Tippoo, he held a brigade command and served under Cornwallis during the Seringapatam operations of 1792. He was promoted to colonel in 1795.

Baird's service extended beyond India; he served as a brigadier-general at the Cape of Good Hope. He returned to India as a major-general in 1798.

## The Capture of Seringapatam
During the final war against Tippoo in 1799, Baird was appointed to the senior brigade command in the army. During the successful assault of Seringapatam, Baird led the storming party, eventually becoming the master of the stronghold where he had previously been held as a prisoner.

Despite his success, Baird felt he had been treated with disrespect and injustice. He was disappointed that the command of the nizam's large contingent had been given to Colonel Arthur Wellesley, and he felt further slighted when Wellesley was appointed governor after the fortress was captured.

## Later Recognition and Commands
For his gallant bearing during the assault on Seringapatam, Baird received the thanks of the East India Company and the British parliament. Although the East India Company offered him a pension, he declined it, apparently in the hope that the government would grant him the order of the Bath.

In 1801, General Baird commanded the Indian army that was sent to co-operate with Abercromby to expel the French.
