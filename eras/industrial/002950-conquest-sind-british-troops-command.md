# The British Conquest and Administration of Sind

## Conquest and Initial Annexation
The British conquest of Sind was achieved in 1843 under the command of General Sir Charles Napier. This expansion was part of a broader pattern of the East India Company's rise to supremacy in India, during which its power spread to regions including Oudh, Assam, and Sind. The process of annexation was facilitated by military engagements, specifically the battles of Meeanee and Daba, which placed the entirety of Sind at the disposal of the British.

Following the conquest, the British government prohibited the transit trade in opium between Karachi and China, which had previously been carried on at Damaun in the early 19th century, effectively ending that trade in 1837.

## The State of Khairpur
During the period of conquest, the state of Khairpur remained a notable exception. The mirs of Khairpur belonged to the Talpur, a Baluch family that had risen following the fall of the Kalhora dynasty of Sind. In 1832, the British government recognized the individuality of the Khairpur state through a treaty that secured the roads of Sind and the use of the river Indus. 

Because the mir of Khairpur, Ali Murad, provided cordial support for British policy during the first Kabul expedition, Khairpur was the only state permitted to retain its political existence under the protection of the paramount power after the battles of Meeanee and Daba. One of its later enlightened rulers was the chief mir, Faiz Mahommed Khan, G.C.I.E., who died in 1909.

## Relations with Baluch States and Tribes
Following the 1843 conquest, there was no diplomatic intercourse of note between the British and the Baluch states until 1854. During the governor-generalship of the marquess of Dalhousie, General John Jacob—then the commandant and political superintendent on the Sind frontier—was tasked with arranging a treaty with the Kalat state, which was then under the chieftainship of Nasir Khan.

Executed on May 14, 1854, this treaty annulled a previous 1841 offensive and defensive treaty made by Major Outram. Under the new terms, Nasir Khan II and his successors were bound to:
* Oppose all enemies of the British government to the utmost.
* Act in subordinate co-operation with the British government.
* Refrain from negotiating with other states without British consent.
* Allow British troops to occupy any positions deemed advisable within Kalat territory.
* Prevent their subjects from plundering within or near British territory.

Simultaneously, the British dealt with the Marri, a Baluch tribe on the Dera Ghazi Khan border of Baluchistan. Relations began in 1840 with Marri attacks on the communications of Sir John Keane's army. An early British attempt to punish the tribe failed disastrously when Major Clibborn was repulsed at the Naffusak Pass, resulting in 179 killed and 92 wounded out of 650 men. While the Marris joined the British against the Bugtis in 1845, they caused significant trouble after the 1843 annexation of Sind until they were pacified by Sir Robert Sandeman and General John Jacob. In 1880, during the second Afghan War, the tribe raided British communications and plundered a treasure convoy. This led to a campaign by Brigadier-General Macgregor with 3,070 troops, after which the tribe submitted, provided hostages, and paid 1¼ lakh (£12,500) from a fine of 2 lakhs (£20,000).

## Administrative Integration
Sind was integrated into the Bombay Presidency, a province consisting of both British districts and native states. The presidency was divided into four divisions: the southern or Carnatic, the central or Deccan, the northern or Gujarat, and Sind. Within the Sind division, the British established twenty-six districts across the presidency, including Karachi, Hyderabad, Shikarpur, Thar and Parkar, and the Upper Sind frontier.

By 1904, under the re-arrangement of the Indian army by Lord Kitchener, the old Bombay command was replaced by the Western army corps. The 4th division of this corps, headquartered at Quetta, was given responsibility for the troops in the Sind and Quetta districts.

## Transition to Crown Rule
The administration of these territories shifted in 1859 following a serious mutiny of native troops. The empire of the East India Company was annexed to the British Crown via "An Act for the Better Government of India." This act transformed the Governor-General into a Viceroy representing the Sovereign and replaced the Company with a Secretary of State for India, who was responsible to the British Parliament. This administrative structure was further solidified in 1877 when Queen Victoria was proclaimed Empress of India.
