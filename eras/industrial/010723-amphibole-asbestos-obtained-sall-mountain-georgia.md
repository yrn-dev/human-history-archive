# Asbestos

Asbestos is a widely distributed mineral found in various localities across the globe. While it occurs in many regions, it is only found in a few areas in sufficient purity and abundance to be worked commercially.

## Varieties and Mineralogy
Asbestos is primarily categorized into two mineralogical types: amphibole-asbestos (also known as hornblende-asbestos) and serpentine-asbestos.

Amphibole-asbestos is typically grey or white in color. It is characterized by fibers that can reach exceptional lengths—some Italian varieties reaching 5 or 6 feet—though the material is often brittle and harsh. Common fibrous forms of amphibole include anthophyllite and tremolite. A specific blue fibrous variety of amphibole called crocidolite is worked as "Cape asbestos" from the Asbestos Mountains in Griqualand West, Cape Colony, where it occurs in veins in slaty rocks associated with quartzites and jaspers rich in brown iron-ore and magnetite. In Dauphiny, a blue or green fibrous amphibole is known as asbestoid or byssolite.

Serpentine-asbestos, known mineralogically as chrysotile, has become the most common variety on the market in recent years. Its fibers are shorter, typically 2 or 3 inches in length, but possess great elasticity, tensile strength, and a delicate silky lustre, appearing in greenish or yellowish hues.

The character of the fibers leads to various descriptive names. Silky, flexible fibers are sometimes called "mountain flax," while finer kinds are termed amianthus. When fibers form a naturally interwoven felted mass, the mineral is referred to by names such as mountain paper, mountain cork, or mountain leather.

## Global Distribution and Extraction
In the United States, asbestos is worked to a limited extent. Amphibole-asbestos is obtained from Sall Mountain, Georgia, and the mineral has also been worked in the serpentine of Vermont. Other occurrences in the U.S. include Virginia, South Carolina, Arizona, and Massachusetts.

Canada possesses the most industrially important asbestos, found in a small belt of serpentine in Quebec, particularly near Thetford and Black Lake, where its commercial value was recognized around 1877. The extraction process involves quarrying the rock, hand-cobbing, drying, and crushing it in rock-breakers. It is then passed through rollers and "fiberizers" to reach a finer division before being sorted on a shaking screen.

Historically, Italy and Corsica were the almost exclusive sources of asbestos, and Italian workings continue to yield large quantities, mostly of the amphibole variety. In some instances, this is associated with green garnet nodules called "seeds" (*Semenze dell' amianto*). Other global occurrences include:
* **Europe:** Russia, Hungary, Tirol, and the British Isles (specifically parts of Ireland and the old rocks of North Wales).
* **Oceania:** New Zealand, New South Wales, and Queensland.
* **Asia:** Asbestos is found in the Tartar country of Tangu, specifically in the region of Chingintalas.

## Industrial and Historical Uses
The economic value of asbestos is derived from its low thermal conductivity, its ability to withstand high temperatures, and its partial resistance to acids. These properties make it suitable for:
* **Industrial Engineering:** Jacketing steam-pipes and boilers, serving as an electric insulator, and acting as a filtering medium for corrosive liquids.
* **Machinery:** Packing for stopcocks, glands, and joints, as well as the production of millboard, felt, and yarn.
* **Construction:** Fire-proof paints, plasters, and cements; fire-proof sheathing and felt for roofing and flooring.
* **Specialized Equipment:** Packing for safes and the creation of fire-clay balls for gas-stoves.

Historically, asbestos was a rare curiosity woven into fabrics. Charlemagne reportedly owned an asbestos tablecloth that could be purified by throwing it into a fire. In 1676, a merchant from China presented a handkerchief of *linum asbesti*, or "salamander's wool," to the Royal Society. The Eskimos of Labrador have utilized asbestos as lamp-wicks, a practice also seen in certain sacred lamps of antiquity.

## Related Minerals and Geological Processes
Dr. G.P. Merrill has indicated that some asbestos is the result of a shearing process within rocks. Asbestos is also found in association with other minerals; for example, in the Untersulzbachthal in Salzburg, it occurs in cavities in epidote-schist alongside apatite, calcite, adularia, and dark green crystals of epidote.
