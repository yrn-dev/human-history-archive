# Brigandage in Catalonia

## Overview of Spanish Brigandage
Brigandage in Spain has historically been endemic in areas south of the Sierra Morena. In the northern regions, it typically flourished during periods of weak government, following civil wars, or after foreign invasions. However, such activity has generally been suppressed effectively by capable administrations.

## Development in Catalonia
Brigandage reached its greatest heights in Catalonia. The phenomenon originated from conflicts between peasants and the feudal exactions imposed by landlords. A traditional hero associated with this period is Roque Guinart, who is featured in the second part of *Don Quixote*.

The activity of Catalan brigands received significant stimulus from two major historical events: the 1640 revolt against the house of Austria and the War of the Succession, which took place between 1700 and 1714.

## Suppression and the Escuadra de Cataluna
The suppression of brigandage in Catalonia was characterized by methods without precedent in Italy. Pedro Veciana, a country gentleman and the hereditary *balio* (civil and military lieutenant) of the archbishop of Tarragona in the town of Valls, resisted brigand attacks by arming his own farm-servants. Veciana and other neighboring country gentlemen formed a powerful band known as the Mozos (Boys) of Veciana. When brigands attempted to eliminate Veciana by attacking the town of Valls, they were repulsed with heavy losses.

Following this, the government of Philip V commissioned Veciana to establish a special police corps called the "escuadra de Cataluna." For five generations, the colonel of this corps was a member of the Veciana family. The escuadra de Cataluna continues to exist.

## Later Developments and the 1874 Incident
In central and northern Spain, the rural population has historically supported police forces when the government acted with firmness. Brigandage was further kept under control following the organization of "La Guardia Civil" around 1844 by the duke of Ahumada.

At the conclusion of the Carlist War in 1874, a few bands continued to infest Catalonia. One of the worst of these bands was surprised and destroyed on the ruins of the castle of San Martin de Centellas, where all its members were beaten to death with boxwood cudgels by a gang of charcoal-burners.

## Regional Variations
There is a greater level of sympathy for "bandoleros" in the south of Spain, where they find equivalents to the Italian "manuténgoli." In this region, a lawless class is maintained by tobacco smuggling from Gibraltar, which frequently devolves into pure brigandage.
