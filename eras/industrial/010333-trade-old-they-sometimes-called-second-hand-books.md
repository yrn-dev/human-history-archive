# The Trade in Old and Second-Hand Books

The trade in old or second-hand books is a specialized branch of the broader bookselling industry. Within the professional hierarchy of the book trade, it is regarded as a higher class of business than standard retail. This distinction arises because the trade in second-hand books requires a specific knowledge of bibliography, and its transactions focus on individual books rather than the sale of numerous copies of a single title.

## Market Operations and Stock Acquisition
Dealers in old and second-hand books employ various methods to maintain and replenish their inventories. While they deal primarily in individual volumes, they occasionally acquire "remainders." These are books that have ceased to sell with the original publisher for various reasons; the second-hand dealer purchases these remainders and offers them to the public as bargains.

## Position Within the Bookselling Industry
By the 19th century, a fundamental distinction emerged between the publisher—acting as a literary promoter—and the bookseller, who functioned as a shopkeeper. Booksellers generally operated within one of four specific trades:
*   Wholesale bookselling
*   Retail bookselling
*   The periodical trade
*   The old or second-hand book trade

The retail and second-hand sectors are supported by wholesale booksellers, who act as distributing agencies. Because it is impossible for a retail bookseller to stock half of all published works, they rely on wholesalers for miscellaneous supplies. In London, Paternoster Row served as the center of this industry for over a hundred years, where collectors gathered books ordered by the public. Some leading houses in this department maintain constant stocks of over 1,000,000 books and serve as the primary point where publishers "subscribe" new books to determine their potential fate.

## Historical Context of Bookselling
The foundations of the bookselling trade are traced back to the 12th century in Bologna and Paris, driven by the needs of universities and lawyers. Early dealers were known as *stationarii*, a term possibly derived from the open stalls (*statio*) where they conducted business. According to the statutes of the universities of Paris and Bologna, these dealers sold books on commission. 

These early *stationarii* are sometimes distinguished from *librarii*, a term that originally referred to copyists before being applied to those who traded in books. Beyond the sale of books, these dealers sold writing materials—which retained the name "stationery"—and provided services in decorating and binding. They also likely employed professional copyists, a profession that existed in large cities and university centers.

## Professional Organization
To protect the interests of retail booksellers, a London booksellers' society was established in 1890. The initial goals of this society were to control the details of the trade, arrange general prices, and restrict discounts to 25%. A few years after its founding, the society expanded its operations to cover the entire United Kingdom and Ireland, renaming itself "The Associated Booksellers of Great Britain and Ireland."
