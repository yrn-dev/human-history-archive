# The Imperial Bank of Germany (Reichsbank)

The Imperial Bank of Germany, also known as the "Reichsbank," is the most powerful banking institution in Germany. It operates within a national banking system that includes approximately 140 other banks across the states of the German empire. These industrial and credit banks, alongside the Reichsbank, have exerted an immense influence on the country's industrial development.

## Management and Governance
The Reichsbank is managed by a bank directory appointed by the chancellor of the empire, who exercises complete powers of control over the institution. Shareholders participate in management through a committee; to be a member of this committee, an individual must hold at least three shares. The bank's influence extends across all separate kingdoms of the empire via its branches, a reach made possible by the uniformity of coinage introduced by laws between 1871 and 1873.

## Note Issue and Circulation
The Reichsbank is one of five banks in Germany possessing the right of uncovered note-issue. The Imperial Bank holds the largest such right, valued at L23,641,450. Other banks with this right include the Bank of Bavaria (L1,600,000), the Bank of Saxony (L838,500), the Bank of Baden (L500,000), and the Bank of Wuerttemberg (L500,000).

The note circulation is governed by the law of 1875 and an amending law of 1899. The uncovered limit for the Reichsbank began at L12,500,000, rose to L14,811,450 as other banks' issues lapsed, and was further extended by the act of June 5, 1902, to L23,641,450. In 1906, the average notes issued for the year were L69,000,000.

Under a 1906 law, the bank issues notes (_Reichsbanknoten_) in denominations of 20 marks (L1) and 50 marks (L2, 10s.), adding to the existing 5, 10, 100, and 1000 mark notes. The introduction of these notes led to the withdrawal of treasury notes (_Reichskassenscheine_) of 20 and 50 marks from circulation.

## Reserves and Redemption
The Reichsbank is legally bound to redeem its notes in current German money, which may consist of silver thalers, gold coin, or bar-gold. The gold rate is 1392 marks per pound fine of gold (reckoned as 20 marks to L1, or L69, 12s. per pound fine). While the importance of gold payment is admitted, the bank has not always provided facilities for these obligations in practice.

To secure notes not represented by specie, the bank must hold discounted bills (maturing within three months and usually possessing three, but never fewer than two, good indorsements), treasury notes, or notes from other issuing banks. Additionally, at least one-third of notes in circulation must be covered by gold in bullion, foreign coin, money, current German notes, or imperial treasury notes. As of December 30, 1906, the gold held was L24,069,000. The balance sheet for December 31, 1906, showed round numbers of L24,000,000 in gold and L9,000,000 in silver.

## Statutory Powers and Financials
A distinct feature of the German banking act is the automatic statutory power to exceed the legal limit of note circulation if business conditions require it. This is permitted upon the payment of a 5% surplus fee (though the rate was 7% for a short time in 1899). This arrangement is intended to provide necessary banking accommodation and prevent panic. Between 1881 and the end of 1906, the duty paid on these extra issues totaled L839,052, providing funds for the relief of taxation.

Financially, the capital of the Reichsbank was raised to L9,000,000 by a bill in 1899. Its reserve fund, built from surplus profits, reached L3,240,000, exceeding the L3,000,000 requirement set by the 1899 act. The 1899 amending act also increased the government's share of profits while diminishing the dividends for stockholders. In the 1906 balance sheet, loans and advances amounted to L14,200,000, and bills held totaled L67,000,000.

## Role in German Business
The Imperial Bank supports German business primarily through two mechanisms: the development of the clearing system (_Giro-Verkehr_) and the facilities provided by its note circulation. While the bank receives deposits and allows cheques to be drawn against them, notes remain the primary method of payment for ordinary business in Germany.
