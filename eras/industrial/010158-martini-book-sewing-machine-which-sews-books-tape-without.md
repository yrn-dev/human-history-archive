# The Martini Book-Sewing Machine

The Martini book-sewing machine is a specialized piece of equipment used in the commercial binding process. It is designed to sew books on tape, featuring a significant improvement in that it performs this task without cutting up the head or tail of the book. Additionally, the machine is capable of forming complete Kettle stitches.

## Technical Capabilities and Operation
The Martini machine is versatile in terms of the dimensions it can handle, as it is capable of sewing books of any size up to 18 inches. The machine utilizes straight needles, and the adjustments required to accommodate various book sizes are described as very simple. The machine's functions also extend to the processes of backing and rounding.

## Context of Machine Binding
The Martini machine exists within a broader landscape of commercial binding machinery, much of which is the result of German or American ingenuity. Most modern bookbinding machines are driven by power. Because many of these machines must be accurately set to a specific book size, they are often unsuitable for binding books of varying sizes; consequently, they are most advantageous when used for large editions of a single title.

In the general process of machine sewing, there are two primary methods:
* **Band-working machines:** These sew books on bands (either round or flat). These machines pull the return thread through the upper and lower edges of the back of each section, which can weaken the section, though this may be neutralized by careful head-banding.
* **Chain stitch machines:** These replace bands with a chain stitch that brings the return thread inside each section. While this avoids cutting the edges, it can result in a flattening of the book's back, which creates difficulties during the subsequent covering operation.

In these automated processes, sections are sewn continuously in a long line and later cut apart. The operator places each section onto a metal saddle one by one. The machine operates continuously, though it can be controlled or cut off using a pedal or foot-lever. Threads are drawn through holes made by piercers and caught by hooked needles, while a shuttle—similar to that found in an ordinary sewing machine—sews the inner thread back and forth.

## Comparison with Manual Binding
The development of ingenious machinery has allowed for the efficient execution of tasks that were previously done laboriously by hand, including the folding of printed sheets, sewing on bands, making outer cases, stamping designs, and covering books in leather or cloth. This has resulted in a great saving of time and labor.

However, the archive notes that older manual methods of sewing and rounding the backs of books were safer and stronger. Hand-binding is also less likely to bruise or injure the paper. Due to these factors, while larger trade binders utilize machines extensively, high-class trade binders often do not depart from older methods, and private binders generally have no reason to use machines.

## Structural Considerations in Binding
A key distinction between machine and hand binding involves the fastening of the book to its cover. In books sewn by hand on bands, the loose ends of the bands are drawn through the boards and strongly fastened through their substance. In contrast, most machines for covering sewn books simply paste the loose ends of the tapes to the inner surfaces of the boards. As a result, a "cased" book is more likely to slip out of its cover once the paste perishes, whereas hand-sewn bands provide a more effective attachment.
