# English Book-plates

The English book-plate serves as a reflection of the prevailing tastes in decorative art across different epochs, spanning from the late Elizabethan to the late Victorian periods. These private tokens of book-ownership provide a visual history of stylistic evolution, moving from simple heraldic devices to complex artistic compositions.

## Early History and Development
The earliest discovered English example is the gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon. While a gorgeous, hand-painted armorial device associated with a folio once belonging to Henry VIII exists in the British Museum's King's library, it is not considered a book-plate in the modern sense. Following Bacon's, the next known example is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585.

Until the final quarter of the 17th century, authentic English plates were limited in number. These early examples were characterized by a remarkable simplicity that avoided the elaborateness found in German plates. They were typically plain and armorial, with decoration usually restricted to wreaths, palms, or a symmetrical arrangement of mantling.

## Post-Restoration and French Influence
Following the Restoration, book-plates became established accessories for most well-ordered libraries. While they maintained the heraldic simplicity of the previous era, their "physiognomy" changed. Two primary characteristics emerged:
1. The adoption of tincture lines and dots, a method devised in the middle of the century by Petra Sancta, author of *Tesserae Gentilitiae*.
2. The introduction of more elaborate mantling surrounding the shield, which resembled the periwig style of the period.

This style was imported from France. From this point until the dawn of the French Revolution, English book-plate decoration generally followed ruling French tastes, though often with a delay of several years.

## Classification of Styles
The general classification of styles was established in 1880 by Lord de Tabley (then the Hon. Leicester Warren) in his work, *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates*. The accepted categories include:
*   **Early Armorial:** Examples previous to the Restoration, such as the Nicholas Bacon plate.
*   **Jacobean:** A term used to describe the heavy decorative manner of the early Georgian, Queen Anne, and Restoration days (exemplified by the 1698 Lansanor plate).
*   **Chippendale:** A style described as rococo, represented by the plate of P.A. Convers (1762).
*   **Wreath and Ribbon:** A style belonging to the period associated with the urn.

## Composition and Genres
While heraldic elements were dominant, many plates were essentially pictorial. Notable English genres include:
*   **Library Interiors and Book-piles:** Exemplified by the 1699 *ex-libris* of William Hewer, secretary to Samuel Pepys.
*   **Portrait-plates:** Notable examples include those of the architect John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys.
*   **Allegories:** Engraved by artists such as George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth.
*   **Landscape-plates:** Produced by wood engravers of the Bewick school.

In these pictorial styles, the armorial element typically played a secondary role.

## Modern Evolution and Collection
The taste for collecting and studying book-plates is a relatively modern phenomenon, dating back hardly further than 1875. This interest led to the formation of collectors' societies in the United States, France, Germany, and England. These societies produced various publications, including the quarterly *Ex-libris Zeitschrift* in Berlin and the monthly *Journal of the Ex-libris Society* in London and *Archives de la societe francaise de collectionneurs d'ex-libris* in Paris.

In recent years, the creation of book-plates has shifted from the routine skill of heraldic stationers to being recognized as a minor branch of higher art. Modern designs often emphasize symbolic or allegorical elements over purely heraldic ones. The popularization of the book-plate—which was once restricted to important collections or ancestral libraries—has been aided by process work, which made the reproduction of elaborate designs cheaper and easier.

Modern English artists noted for their book-plate designs include Charles Ricketts, J. Forbes Nixon, Robert Anning Bell, Erat Harrison, J.D. Batten, John Leighton, Warrington Hogg, John Vinycomb, G.W. Eve, and C.W. Sherborn. While most modern plates are reproduced by process, a few artists continue to use the graver, including G.W. Eve and C.W. Sherborn in England, and E.D. French (New York), K.W.F. Hopson (New Haven), and J.W. Spenceley (Boston) in America.
