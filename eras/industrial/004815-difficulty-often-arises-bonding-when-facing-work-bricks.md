# Bonding Difficulties in Faced Brickwork

In the construction of brick walls, bonding is the arrangement used to ensure that bricks in every course cover the joints of the course below. This process allows the mass of bricks to act dependently, distributing weight over an increasing area with every course. While bonding provides longitudinal stability, a combination of stretchers and headers is required to produce a transverse bond, preventing a thick wall from acting as several independent, thinner walls.

## Facing and Backing Bricks
A specific difficulty often arises when a wall is constructed using "facing" work—the external layer of bricks—and "backing," which refers to the interior brickwork. This problem occurs when the facing bricks are of a slightly different size than those used for the backing. 

Because it is necessary to maintain all brickwork in properly levelled courses, any difference in brick size forces a corresponding difference in the thickness of the mortar joints. This discrepancy is detrimental to the stability of the wall and is likely to produce cracking and unequal settlement. Additionally, this practice increases the amount of labour required. To avoid these issues, great care must be taken to ensure that both backing and facing bricks are of equal size.

## Material Discrepancies and Settlement
Similar issues of stability occur in "brick-ashlar" constructions, where walls are faced with ashlar stonework and backed with brickwork. These structures are particularly susceptible to unequal settling. The outer face consists of unyielding stone with very thin joints—sometimes occupying no more than a hundredth part of the stone's height. In contrast, the brick backing contains mortar joints that occupy about one-eighth of the height. Because mortar naturally yields to pressure and shrinks during the drying process, the wall may fail or the face may bulge.

To mitigate this, builders are advised to:
* Use a stiff mortar composed of sand and a large proportion of Portland cement.
* Ensure the height of the stones equals an exact number of brick courses.

A similar difficulty is found when using Kentish rag rubble for facing. Due to the irregularity of the stones, achieving a proper bond between the stone and the brick backing is difficult. Stability is sought by frequently building in headers or bonding stones and using cement mortar.

## Improper Bonding Practices
Certain "dishonest" practices by bricklayers further compromise the integrity of faced walls. When using superior quality bricks for the face and inferior bricks for the interior, some may use "snapped headers." This involves cutting heading bricks in half so that one brick serves the appearance of two. This is considered a pernicious practice because it creates a skin of brickwork 4½ in. thick with a straight mortar joint behind it, which destroys the proper bonding between the face and the back of the wall.

## Structural Reinforcement and Alternatives
To obtain additional longitudinal tie in brickwork, hoop-iron may be used. This iron is typically 1/16 in. thick and 1½ in. wide, and is either galvanized or sanded and tarred to prevent rusting. The standard practice is to use one strip of iron for every half-brick of wall thickness. These strips are hooked together at the length and angles. Some available patent stabbed irons are perforated to provide a key for the mortar.

In terms of bonding patterns, different methods are used depending on the desired strength and appearance:
* **English Bond:** Considered the most perfect in use, utilizing alternate courses of headers and stretchers. It is preferred for engineering works and manufacturing buildings where compactness and strength are of highest importance.
* **Flemish Bond:** Places headers and stretchers alternately in the same course. While it offers a neater appearance for domestic external walls, it is not as strong as English bond due to a weaker transverse tie and a higher number of joints.
* **Single Flemish Bond:** A compromise where the face is Flemish bond and the backing is English bond, which removes the need for bats in the interior.
