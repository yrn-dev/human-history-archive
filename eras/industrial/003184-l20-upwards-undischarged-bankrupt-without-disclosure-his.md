# Obtaining Credit by an Undischarged Bankrupt

## Criminal Liability and Disclosure
Under the Bankruptcy Act of 1883, an undischarged bankrupt is guilty of a misdemeanour if they obtain credit for more than £20 from any person without informing that person of their status as an undischarged bankrupt. 

The official receiver is tasked with reporting such facts to the court. If the court determines there is a reasonable probability of conviction, it is required to order a prosecution. This legal process is then conducted by the director of public prosecutions.

## Application in Scotland
The Bankruptcy Frauds and Disabilities (Scotland) Act 1884 extends specific criminal provisions to Scotland, specifically applying section 31 of the English Bankruptcy Act 1883. This application concerns the obtaining of credit for £20 and upwards by an undischarged bankrupt without disclosing their position. This act also aligns the law regarding disqualifications for such bankrupts with the English act, applying these provisions to decrees of cessio and sequestrations.

## General Obligations of the Bankrupt
Beyond the specific prohibition on obtaining undisclosed credit, a bankrupt is bound to assist the trustee in the administration of their estate. Failure to deliver any part of their property wilfully constitutes contempt of court. 

Under the Debtors Act, a bankrupt may face criminal prosecution if they act with intent to defraud through several specific actions:
* Concealing or removing property valued at £10 or upwards.
* Failing to deliver all property, documents, or books to the trustee.
* Knowingly allowing false debts to be proved on the estate without disclosure.
* Destroying, falsifying, or mutilating accounts or books.
* Attempting to explain property through fictitious losses.
* Obtaining property on credit via false representation within the four months preceding a bankruptcy petition.
* Disposing of or pledging unpaid property outside the ordinary course of trade.
* Using misrepresentation to obtain creditor assent for agreements regarding their affairs.

## Discharge and Trading
A bankrupt may apply for a discharge, which releases them from most obligations, excluding debts incurred by fraud, debts due to the crown, and other public obligations requiring Treasury consent. However, the court may refuse or suspend a discharge if the bankrupt has traded after knowing they were insolvent or contracted debts without a reasonable probability of payment. 

In the United Kingdom, bankrupts are under no obligation to apply for their discharge, which has resulted in a population of 70,000 undischarged bankrupts. In contrast, the systems in New Zealand, Victoria, and New South Wales require a bankrupt to apply for discharge within a fixed period, failing which they are guilty of contempt of court.

## Disqualifications
Until five years after their discharge, or during the period of bankruptcy, a bankrupt is disqualified from holding several public offices unless the bankruptcy is annulled or the court certifies that the bankruptcy resulted from misfortune without misconduct. These prohibited roles include:
* Member of the legislature.
* Justice of the peace, mayor, alderman, or councillor.
* Guardian or overseer of the poor.
* Member of a burial, school, highway, or sanitary board.
* Member of a select vestry in the United Kingdom.
