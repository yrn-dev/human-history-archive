# Roberts's Printers' Marks and Silvestre's Marques

## Overview of Printers' Marks Literature
Within the study of printing history, specific works have been devoted to the documentation of printers' marks through the use of facsimiles. Among these specialized texts are *Printers' Marks*, published in London in 1893 by W. Roberts, and *Marques typographiques*, a French work published in Paris between 1853 and 1867 by Silvestre. 

These publications serve as resources for identifying the devices used by early printers. They exist alongside other similar scholarly efforts, such as Berjeau's *Early Dutch, German and English Printers' Marks* (1866), as well as *Marques typographiques des imprimeurs et libraires qui ont exerce dans les Pays-Bas* (1894). Other comprehensive collections include *Die Buchermarken oder Buchdrucker und Verlegerzeichen* (1892-1898), which contains devices utilized in Cologne, Mainz, Frankfort, Basel, Italy, and Alsace.

## Related Bibliographic Studies
The study of printers' marks is closely related to the broader field of book-plate composition and possession marks. Various authorities have contributed to this area of research, including:
* Henri Bouchot, who wrote *Les Ex-libris et les marques de possession du livre* (1891).
* Egerton Castle, author of *English Book-plates* (1892).
* Walter Hamilton, who produced *French Book-plates* (1892) and *Dated Book-plates* (1895).
* Friedrich Warnecke, author of *Die deutschen Bucherzeichen* (1890).
* The Hon. J. Leicester Warren (Lord de Tabley), who wrote *A Guide to the Study of Book-plates* (1880).
* Sir A.W. Franks, who produced *Notes on Book-plates*, 1574-1800 (1887).
* A. Poulet-Malassis, author of *Les Ex-libris francais* (1875).
* H.W. Fincham, who wrote *Artists and Engravers of British and American Book-plates* (1897).
* Count K.E. zu Leiningen-Westerburg, whose *German Book-plates* was translated by G.R. Denis in 1901.

Additionally, the Ex-libris Series published in London provides further treatment of book-plate composition.

## Etymology of the Term Mark
The term "mark" (and its variations such as the French *marque* or Italian *marca*) has evolved through several linguistic stages. In Old English, the word was *mearc*, and it is found across all Teutonic languages, including the German *Mark* (boundary) and *marke* (impression or sign), and the Dutch *merk*. 

The principal meanings of the word, in order of their probable development, are:
1. A boundary or an object set up to indicate a position or boundary.
2. A token, sign, trace, or impression.

Cognate forms exist in other languages, such as the Persian *marz* (boundary) and the Latin *margo* (margin). Other possible origins include the Sanskrit *marga* (trace of hunted game) or the Lithuanian *margas* (parti-coloured or striped). While the use of "mark" to describe a tract of country near a frontier is now largely obsolete in English—having been replaced by "march"—the term persists in historical contexts, such as the mark of Brandenburg or the Italian *marca* of Ancona. It also refers to the German *mark*, which is a tract of land held in common by a village community.
