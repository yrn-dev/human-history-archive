# Beer Fermentation Systems and the United Kingdom

The production of beer involves the process of fermentation, where living yeast cells act upon the sugar contained in the wort to split it into carbonic acid and alcohol. This process is categorized into two primary systems: top fermentation and bottom fermentation.

## The Top Fermentation System
The top fermentation system is the method traditionally employed in the United Kingdom for the production of British-type ales. In this system, wort is generally kept at a temperature of approximately 60° F. and "pitched" with liquid yeast, also known as "barm," at a rate of 1 to 4 lb per barrel, depending on the beer's strength and type.

The fermentation process manifests visually on the surface of the liquid. After a few hours, a slight scum or froth appears, which develops into a light curly mass called a "curly head" or "cauliflower." This evolves into a "rocky head" and eventually shrinks into a compact "yeasty head" that emits hissing bubbles of gas. This stage marks the beginning of "cleansing," the separation of yeast from the liquid. To prevent temperatures from exceeding a maximum of 70-75° F., fermenting vessels are equipped with "attemperators," which are pipes used to run cold water.

In England, the application of top fermentation is classified into two main categories:
*   **The Cleansing System:** This includes the Skimming System (using wooden vessels with skimming devices), the Dropping System (including the old-fashioned ponto system and the ordinary dropping system using "backs"), and the Burton Union System (a series of rotating casks used by some Burton brewers to achieve a specific character).
*   **The Yorkshire Stone Square System:** Used exclusively in the north of England, this involves pumping wort between two superimposed stone square vessels kept very cool.

## The Bottom Fermentation System
The bottom fermentation system is used to produce "lager" type beers and is widely employed on the continent of Europe. This system differs significantly from the English infusion and top fermentation method, often being paired with the decoction system of brewing.

In bottom fermentation, the "pitching" and fermentation occur at very low temperatures in relatively small vessels. Fermenting cellars are maintained at approximately 37-38° F., and the wort temperature does not rise above 50° F. Unlike the top fermentation system, the yeast remains at the bottom of the fermenting tun. Primary fermentation lasts about eleven to twelve days—significantly longer than the three days required for the English system. Following this, the beer is moved into store (lager) casks at temperatures approaching the freezing point of water.

## Global Production and Comparison
Germany is the largest beer-producing nation by liquid bulk, followed by the United States and then the United Kingdom. However, the consumption per head is slightly higher in the United Kingdom than in Germany and considerably higher than in the United States.

Compositionally, bottom fermentation beers (lager type) from America are very similar to German beers. In contrast, British-type ales produced via top fermentation are generally heavier than the corresponding British products found in the United States. In America, the common brewing system combines bottom fermentation with infusion mashing, though the mashing process differs from the English method by using a very low initial heat of about 100° F.

## Bottom Fermentation in Great Britain
Although the United Kingdom traditionally uses top fermentation, modern lager brewing has taken root in Great Britain. An example of this is the process introduced by Messrs Allsopp. In this modern adaptation:
*   Wort is prepared on infusion lines and cooled with refrigerated brine.
*   Fermentation occurs in huge, glass-enamelled sheet-steel cylindrical vessels under reduced pressure.
*   A vacuum pump removes carbonic acid, which is replaced by cool sterilized air.
*   The fermenting cellars are kept at 40° F. using a pure culture bottom yeast.
*   Due to the constant supply of fresh air and removal of metabolism products, fermentation is accelerated, finishing in about six days.
*   The beer undergoes a secondary fermentation in store tanks for three weeks, where it is charged with carbonic acid under pressure.
*   The entire process, from the copper to bottling, is conducted under counter pressure to ensure the beer does not contact outer air.
