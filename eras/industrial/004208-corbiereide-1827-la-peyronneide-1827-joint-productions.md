# Corbiereide and La Peyronneide

The *Corbiereide* and *La Peyronneide* are political satires published in 1827. These works were the joint productions of the French satirical poet Auguste Marseille Barthelemy and his friend and compatriot, J. P. A. Mery.

## Authorship and Collaboration
Auguste Marseille Barthelemy was born in Marseilles in 1796. He maintained a collaboration with J. P. A. Mery (1798–1866) that was so intimate that it is considered impossible to distinguish their individual personalities within their joint works. 

Barthelemy initially established a local reputation as a poet before moving to Paris. One of his first efforts, *Le Sacre de Charles X* (1825), earned him the favor of the court. However, his energies were soon redirected toward the service of the opposition party.

## Political Satires of 1827
Following the publication of the clever political satire *Les Sidiennes* in 1825, Barthelemy and Mery produced a series of works in 1827. In addition to *La Corbiereide* and *La Peyronneide*, the authors jointly produced *La Villeliade ou la prise du chateau de Rivoli*. 

The success of these works was immediate and pronounced. Specifically, *La Villeliade* was in such demand that fifteen editions were called for within the year.

## Subsequent Works and Career
Following the success of their 1827 productions, Barthelemy and Mery released a rapid succession of satires and political squibs. Notable among these were *Biographie des quarante de l'academie francaise* (1826) and *Napoleon en Egypte* (1828), the latter of which saw nearly a dozen editions in a single year.

The authors' political activities eventually led to legal consequences for Barthelemy. In 1829, he was fined 1000 francs and imprisoned for the publication of *Fils de l'homme*, a poem concerning Napoleon's son, the duke of Reichstadt.

Barthelemy was liberated by the Revolution of 1830. Together with Mery, he celebrated the triumph of the people in a work titled *L'Insurrection*. Between March 1831 and April 1832, the pair produced *Nemesis*, a series of weekly verse satires that attacked the ministers and government of Louis Philippe.

## Later Years
During the publication of *Nemesis*, the small pension Barthelemy received was stopped. When the publication ended, suspicions arose that Barthelemy had received payment in exchange for his silence. In 1832, he published an anonymous poem that supported certain government acts that were particularly obnoxious to the Liberal party. This shift in political alignment destroyed his influence, and his subsequent writings went unnoticed. For several years following this period, Barthelemy refrained from satirical writing and received a handsome pension from the government.
