# Bathing and Light Technologies

## Historical and Cultural Practices of Bathing
Bathing practices have varied significantly across different civilizations and social strata. In ancient Egypt, bathing was tied to religious mandates; the Egyptians were the first to establish a religious rule prohibiting lying with women in temples or entering temples after being with women without first bathing. This distinguished them and the Hellenes from other peoples, who viewed such actions as no different from the behavior of beasts and birds in sacred enclosures.

In the country of Cathay, the use of hot baths was widespread. The general population typically maintained such baths at least three times a week, and daily during the winter. Noblemen and wealthy individuals possessed private baths for their own use. To sustain these baths and other needs, the people of Cathay utilized "black stones" dug from mountains, which served as a superior and more cost-effective fuel than wood.

In other historical contexts, bathing served as a mark of victory and luxury. Following a decisive victory over the Persians, Alexander the Great sought to "wash off the sweat of the battle" in the bath of Darius. This bath, located within the royal pavilion, featured vessels for water, boxes of unguents, and the bath itself made of pure gold, accompanied by rich perfumes.

## Public and Municipal Bathing Facilities
In more recent municipal developments, cities have integrated bathing into public infrastructure. The city of Buffalo maintains several well-equipped public baths and manages its own water-supply system, drawing water from Lake Erie. In Cordoba, located in the Argentine Republic, the city features tiled baths supplied with running water, which were established during the period when the Jesuits ruled the city.

## Light and Energy Systems
The transition between different lighting technologies is evident in various urban settings. In Cordoba, streets are illuminated by both electricity and gas. Similarly, Buffalo utilizes manufactured gas and electricity generated by the water power of Niagara Falls for lighting; gas from the Canadian (Welland) and Ohio-Pennsylvania natural gas fields is also used for heating.

Observations of urban environments have highlighted the contrast between these technologies. In some areas, streets relying on gas-lighting remained "pitch-dark" at night, while main streets utilizing electrical lighting remained bright. This electrical lighting also extended to shop windows, particularly those of tobacconists.

## Bactericidal Effects of Light and Radiant Energy
Scientific investigations between 1880 and 1890 focused on the reactions of bacteria to heat, while the subsequent decade explored other forms of radiant energy. Research involving Arloing, Buchner, and Chmelewski proved that rays of light alone could kill organisms. 

Marshall Ward's experiments from 1892-1893 further demonstrated that reflected light rays could kill the spores of *B. anthracis* at temperatures that were not injurious and were sometimes even favorable to growth. This bactericidal action occurs without food materials, indicating it is not a poisoning effect of the medium. The most effective rays for this destruction are those of highest refrangibility, specifically the ultra-violet and blue-violet rays of the spectrum.

These findings explain the natural destruction of pathogenic bacteria, such as typhoid and anthrax, in the atmosphere and rivers. While the electric arc is rich in these bactericidal rays, mechanical difficulties have hindered its use for hospital disinfection. However, this discovery has been applied to the treatment of lupus and is considered important for the ventilation and lighting of dwellings and the purification of streams.
