# Bristol

The name Bristol refers to several distinct locations across England and the United States, as well as various historical records and figures.

## Bristol, England
Bristol is an English city with a long history of trade and municipal development. Historically, the city maintained a close connection with Ireland, which supported the growth of its wool trade; many Bristol men settled in Dublin, a city whose charters were almost duplicates of those granted to Bristol. The city developed a wine trade with southern France and exported wool to the Baltic, while tanning and soap-making flourished as local industries.

### Governance and Legal History
Originally organized manorially, Bristol's chief courts were the court leet (held three times annually) and the weekly hundred court, both presided over by a reeve appointed by the earl of Gloucester. The town transitioned away from the feudal yoke after the marriage of Earl John to the heiress of Earl William of Gloucester, which made Bristol part of the royal demesne.

A significant epoch occurred with the charter granted by John in 1190. This charter established that no non-burgess could sell corn, wool, cloth, leather, or wine within Bristol, and that no burgess could be impleaded outside the city walls. It also stipulated that all should hold by burgage tenure and that corn did not need to be ground at the lord's mill. Following this, a commune was established based on a French model, and in 1200, Robert FitzNichol became the first mayor. Unlike the reeve, the mayor was chosen by the merchants of the merchant gild. Documentary evidence of this gild dates to 1242.

The mayor was assisted by officials who were called provosts until 1267, stewards from 1267 to 1311, and bailiffs after 1311.

### Guilds and Institutions
Beyond the merchant gild, at least twenty-six craft gilds existed, with the most prominent being the fullers, tuckers, and weavers. The Gild of the Kalendars of Bristol focused on social, educational, and religious work.

The city's public architecture includes the council house (dating from 1827 and enlarged in 1894), which houses the city archives and portraits by Kneller and Van Dyck, and a modern Gothic Guildhall. The exchange, used as a corn-market, was designed in 1743 by John Wood. Colston Hall, used for meetings and concerts, commemorates Edward Colston (d. 1721), a benefactor of the city. Other notable institutions include the city free library (1613), a bank that claims to be the original of the first English savings-bank (1812), and an art gallery opened in 1905. In 1906, a central library was opened following a £50,000 bequest from Vincent Stuckey Lean (d. 1899).

## Bristol, United States
Several townships and cities in the United States bear the name Bristol.

### Rhode Island
Located on a peninsula between Mount Hope Bay and Narragansett Bay, this Bristol is the shire-township of Bristol county. It was awarded to Plymouth Colony in 1676 after King Philip's War. The village was laid out in 1680 and named Bristol in 1681 after the English town. It became a shire-township in 1685, was under Massachusetts jurisdiction in 1692, and was annexed to Rhode Island in 1747. During the War of Independence, British forces bombarded the village on October 7, 1775, and partially destroyed it on May 25, 1778. The area is known for agriculture (specifically onions) and yacht-building; the Herreshoff yards built "Reliance" and "Columbia," defenders of the America's Cup.

### Connecticut
Located in Hartford county, this Bristol consists of the borough of Bristol (incorporated 1893) and the village of Forestville. Settled around 1727 as part of Farmington, it became an independent corporation with the formation of the first church in 1742. During the War of Independence, the local Protestant Episcopal Church was associated with the Loyalist party; its rector, Rev. James Nichols, was tarred and feathered by Whigs, and member Moses Dunbar was hanged for treason. A local landmark, "The Tories' Den," is a cave at Chippen's Hill. The borough is known for manufacturing hardware, brass ware, silverplate, iron castings, bells, woollen goods, and clocks.

### Pennsylvania
A borough in Bucks county on the Delaware river, this Bristol was one of the first Pennsylvania settlements after 1681. It served as the county seat of government until 1725. Laid out in 1697 and incorporated in 1720, it currently operates under a charter from 1851. Its industries include rolling mills, machine shops, a planing mill, and factories producing patent leather, wallpaper, carpets, and hosiery, yarn, and worsted goods. The area is also known for the medicinal chalybeate waters of Bath Springs.

### Tennessee and Virginia
This Bristol is a railway centre divided by the state line between Sullivan county, Tennessee, and Washington county, Virginia. Settled around 1835, the Tennessee side was incorporated in 1856, while the Virginia side (formerly known as Goodson) was chartered as a city in 1890. The city is a distribution point for coke, coal, and iron, and contains lumber mills, iron furnaces, packing houses, furniture factories, and tobacco factories. Educational institutions include the Virginia Institute for Women (1884), Sullins College for women (1870), and a normal college for negroes in Tennessee.
