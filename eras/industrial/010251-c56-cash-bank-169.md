# Cash at Bank

In commercial and bookkeeping contexts, "cash at bank" refers to the funds a business maintains within a banking institution. This is distinguished from "office cash," though both may be recorded in a single separate book known as the cash book. In a cash book, parallel columns are used to track the movements of office cash and cash at the bank. Modern business practices often involve paying all received moneys into a bank without deduction and settling accounts via cheque, which frequently eliminates the need for an office cash account, except for petty payments recorded in a petty cash book.

## Bookkeeping and Financial Reporting

In the double-entry record system, "cash at bank" is categorized as an asset on a balance sheet. For example, in a trial balance and balance sheet dated December 31, 1906, "Cash at bank" is listed as an asset with a value of L4,169 5 5. 

In such financial statements, cash at bank is grouped with other assets—such as stock on hand, trade debtors, bills receivable, and fixtures and furniture—to determine the total assets of a business. These assets are weighed against liabilities, such as trade creditors and bills payable, to calculate the excess of assets over liabilities, which is referred to as "Capital" at that specific date.

## Definitions and Usage of Cash

The term "cash" originates from the Old French *casse* (modern *caisse*), meaning a box or chest. While it originally referred to the box used to keep money, it is now commonly applied to coin or ready money. 

In banking and commercial usage, "cash" may be specifically confined to specie. In opposition to securities, drafts, or bills, it is also applied to bank-notes. Consequently, the act of "cashing" refers to the conversion of negotiable instruments, such as cheques, into coin. In bookkeeping, this significance extends to terms such as "cash-payment" (ready-money payment) as opposed to "credit," regardless of whether the payment is made via notes, coin, or cheque.

## Banking Reserves and Cash Resources

Banking institutions are subject to requirements regarding the maintenance of reserves against deposits. In "reserve cities," reserves are required to the amount of 25%, while "country banks" outside these cities require 15%. These reserves are not required to be kept entirely in cash. 

In the United States, New York, Chicago, and St Louis are identified as the three central reserve cities where cash is required with only trifling deductions. In other reserve cities (which numbered forty in 1908), banks are permitted to deposit half of their cash in national banks located in central reserve cities. Country banks are permitted to deposit three-fifths of their cash in any reserve city. The importance of these resources was highlighted during the panic of 1907, which placed a severe strain on the cash resources of banks in New York City.

## Institutional Examples of Cash Holdings

Various historical and international banking entities have maintained significant cash reserves:

*   **The Austro-Hungarian Bank:** Founded in 1816, this joint-stock company's notes of issue must be covered by legal specie (current silver and gold) in reserve to the extent of two-fifths. At the end of 1900, the bank reported cash holdings of L50,754,000. By 1907, its cash holdings increased to L60,036,625.
*   **The Bank of North America:** Authorized on May 26, 1781, this institution helped restore order to Federation finances. It loaned $1,249,975 to Robert Morris, the government superintendent of finance, of which $996,581 was repaid in cash.
*   **Imperial Treasury (Germany):** The imperial treasury is obligated to pay state notes in cash upon request, although no independent fund of cash has been set apart for this specific purpose.

## Regional Variations and Currency

In certain historical contexts, "cash" refers to a unit of currency rather than a general term for ready money. In China, specifically in cities like Fuchau, private and local promissory notes were used as currency. These bills varied in amount from 100 cash to 1,000 dollars. The value of these units fluctuated; for instance, in 1858 at Fuchau, the dollar was worth between 18,000 and 20,000 cash in Government Bills. In Peking, Dr. Rennie noted the dollar was valued at 15,000 and later 25,000 paper cash.
