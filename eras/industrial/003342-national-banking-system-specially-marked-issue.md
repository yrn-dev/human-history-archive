# The National Banking System of the United States

The national banking system was a financial structure established in the United States primarily to address the financial necessities of the Federal government during the Civil War. It was specifically marked by the issuance of circulating notes secured by United States bonds.

## Origins and Establishment
The system was devised by Mr. Chase, the secretary of the treasury, because the government found it difficult to float government bonds at profitable rates. To create a compulsory market for these bonds, Chase offered special privileges to banks organized under Federal charters. These banks were permitted to issue circulating notes, provided they were secured by the deposit of government bonds.

This plan was authorized by the act of February 25, 1863, and was later supplemented by the act of June 3, 1864. The national banking system was influenced in part by the "free-banking system" inaugurated in New York by the act of 1838, which allowed bodies of persons to form banks and issue circulation secured by various public bonds.

## Competition with State Banks
Initially, the national banks did not hold predominance because state banking systems in older states were firmly entrenched in the confidence of the commercial community. To address this, a 10% tax was imposed on the face-value of state bank notes in circulation after July 1, 1866. This measure drove state banks out of the note-issuing business; some state banks converted into national banks, while others continued commercial operations under state laws without the privilege of issuing notes.

Prior to the uniform system of 1864, state note-issues suffered from a lack of uniformity and inconvenience, as notes issued outside a specific state required careful scrutiny regarding the responsibility of the issuers. While systems in Louisiana, New England, Ohio, and Indiana were successful, loose banking laws in other states fostered speculation and discredited state banks.

## Mechanics of Note Issue and Capital
National banks desiring to issue notes were required by law to deposit United States bonds with the United States treasurer. The amount of bonds deposited could not exceed the bank's capital stock, and the bank received circulation equal to 90% of the bonds' par-value.

The system had specific capital and tax requirements:
* **Investment Requirements:** Originally, no bank could be established without investing one-third of its capital in bonds. In 1874, this was reduced to 25%, with a maximum mandatory requirement of $50,000.
* **Taxation:** Notes were taxed at a rate of 1% per annum.
* **Denominations:** The lowest authorized denomination for national bank-notes was $5, and no more than one-third of a bank's issues could be of this denomination. (The government issued the $1 and $2 notes).

Critics described the banks' position as receiving "double interest," as they were credited with interest on bonds held by the treasury while simultaneously lending their notes to the public. However, profits were reduced by the 1% tax, the fact that notes could not be issued to full par-value, and the necessity of setting aside reserves in gold or other lawful money for redemption.

## Growth and Evolution
The national banking system experienced remarkable growth in its early years. In 1864, there were 453 national banks with an aggregate capital of $79,366,950. By 1865, this grew to 1,014 banks with an aggregate capital of $242,542,982.

An extension of the system occurred via the act of 1900, which allowed the incorporation of national banks in places with populations not exceeding 3,000 with capital as low as $25,000 (down from a previous minimum of $50,000). By October 31, 1907, this provision led to the incorporation of 2,389 national banks with capitals under $50,000, including 1,365 new institutions, 752 reorganizations, and 272 conversions of private and state institutions.

## Currency and Redemption
Following the suspension of specie payments at the close of 1861 and the issuance of government paper-money, gold ceased to be a medium of exchange (except in California). Consequently, new banks redeemed their notes in government paper. The gold-value of bank-notes fluctuated alongside government notes until the national treasury resumed payments in specie on January 1, 1879.

As of October 31, 1907, total outstanding issues were $609,905,441, with $10 bills constituting the largest amount of a single denomination ($249,946,530). Of the total circulation, $562,727,614 was secured by bonds, while $47,252,852 was covered by lawful money in the government treasury for redemption and retirement.

Over time, the exclusive privilege of issuing notes diminished in importance as other methods of transferring credit developed. This allowed for the continued existence of trust companies, private banks, and state banks, which, despite lacking note-issuing privileges, obtained other privileges that were sometimes greater than those of national banks.
