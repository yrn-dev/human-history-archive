# French Plate of Convers and the Wreath and Ribbon Style

The French plate of Convers and the "wreath and ribbon" style are classifications within the study of book-plates, which are private tokens of book-ownership. The systematic study and collection of these items is a relatively modern pursuit, having gained traction around 1875. A significant catalyst for this interest was the 1880 publication of the *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates* by Lord de Tabley (then known as the Hon. Leicester Warren).

## Classification of Book-Plate Styles

Lord de Tabley's work established a general classification of styles used to categorize book-plates. These styles serve as the primary criteria for dating old examples when other data is unavailable. The established categories include:

*   **Early Armorial:** This style refers to plates created previous to the Restoration, with the Nicholas Bacon plate serving as an example.
*   **Jacobean:** Though described as a somewhat misleading term, it is used to encompass the heavy decorative manner characteristic of the early Georgian days, Queen Anne, and the Restoration. The Lansanor plate is cited as a typical example of this style.
*   **Chippendale:** This style is identified with the rococo manner. The French plate of Convers is noted as a representative example of the Chippendale style.
*   **Wreath and Ribbon:** This specific style belongs to the period characterized as that of the urn.

## Development of the Study of Book-Plates

Following the influence of Lord de Tabley, literature regarding book-plates expanded. This led to the formation of collectors' societies in several countries, including France, Germany, the United States, and England. These societies established archives and journals to document their findings, such as the quarterly *Ex-libris Zeitschrift* in Berlin, and the monthly publications *The Journal of the Ex-libris Society* in London and the *Archives de la societe francaise de collectionneurs d'ex-libris* in Paris.

## English Book-Plate Evolution

The English book-plate is noted for reflecting the prevailing tastes in decorative art across different epochs, from the late Elizabethan to the late Victorian periods. 

### Early English Examples
The gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon is considered the oldest discovered English example, as other earlier devices—such as the hand-painted armorial device once attached to a folio belonging to Henry VIII—do not fit the modern definition of a book-plate. Another early example is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585. Until the final quarter of the 17th century, authentic English plates were limited in number and characterized by a simple composition that lacked German elaborateness. These were typically plain armorials with decoration limited to wreaths or a symmetrical arrangement of mantling.

### Post-Restoration and French Influence
After the Restoration, book-plates became common accessories for well-ordered libraries. While they maintained simple heraldic arrangements, their appearance changed. They began to invariably use dots and tincture lines, a method devised in the middle of the century by Petra Sancta in *Tesserae Gentilitiae* and adopted across Europe. Additionally, the mantling became more elaborate, resembling the periwigs of the era. This specific style was imported from France. Until the start of the French Revolution, English decorative modes for book-plates generally followed the ruling French taste, though often with a delay of several years.

## Other Compositional Styles

Beyond the general stylistic periods, certain compositional genres were prevalent:

*   **Pictorial Plates:** Many older and modern plates are essentially pictorial. In England, this includes "book-piles" (exemplified by the *ex-libris* of William Hewer) and "the library interior."
*   **Portrait-Plates:** Notable examples include plates of the architect John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys.
*   **Allegories:** These were engraved by artists such as George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth.
*   **Landscape-Plates:** These were produced by wood engravers of the Bewick school.

In these pictorial and allegorical styles, the armorial element typically plays a secondary role.
