# Bridge Design and Impact Stress Analysis

The analysis of stresses in bridge construction involves the calculation of both dead and live loads, with particular attention paid to the effects of "impact." Impact refers to the increased stress and deformation that occur when vertical loads are imposed on a structure with velocity or suddenness, rather than resting quietly.

## Mechanics of Impact Stress
When a vertical load is applied suddenly but without velocity, the resulting deformation and stress are momentarily double those of the same load at rest. In practical bridge applications, loads are rarely applied in this exact manner. Instead, stresses are increased by several factors:
*   **Speed and Deflection:** When loads are applied such that deflection increases with speed, stresses exceed those of gradually applied loads, creating vibrations around a mean position.
*   **Rail Conditions:** Because rails are not perfectly smooth or straight, lurching and centrifugal actions alter how loading is distributed.
*   **Mechanical Forces:** Unbalanced moving parts within an engine create rapidly changing vertical forces.
*   **Structural Inequalities:** Shocks are produced by inequalities of level at the ends of rails.

These factors ensure that live load stresses are always greater than those of an equivalent resting load. This increment is more pronounced on flooring girders than on main girders, and more significant on short main girders than on long ones.

## Calculation of Working Stress
To determine the working stress for combined dead and live loads, a specific rule is employed where the factor of safety for the live load is twice as great as that for the dead load. This is calculated using the formula:
$f = f_1(1+[\rho])/(1+2[\rho])$

In this formula, $f_1$ is assumed to be 7½ tons per sq. in. for iron and 9 tons per sq. in. for steel. The resulting working stress ($f$) varies based on the ratio of the loads:
*   **All Dead Load:** For a ratio of 0, the stress is 7.5 tons per sq. in. for iron and 9.0 for mild steel.
*   **Live Load = Dead Load:** At a ratio of 1.00, the stress drops to 4.9 tons per sq. in. for iron and 5.9 for mild steel.
*   **All Live Load:** At an infinite ratio, the stress is 3.7 tons per sq. in. for iron and 4.5 for mild steel.

Bridge sections designed using this rule are noted to differ very little from those designed using formulae based on Wöhler's experiments. This method has seen a revival and increasing reliance in American bridge design.

## Empirical Observations and Vibrations
E.H. Stone analyzed measurements of deflection from official trials of Indian bridges, concluding that the increment of deflection due to impact depends on the ratio of dead to live load. Stone further inferred that the augmentation of stress for any member—due to causes included in impact allowance—would follow the same percentage for the same ratios of live to dead load stresses.

Further measurements regarding the deformations of tension members and girders caused by moving trains were conducted by S.W. Robinson and F.E. Turneaure. Turneaure utilized two recording extensometers and a recording deflectometer. His findings highlighted several key conclusions:
1.  **Vibration Sources:** Locomotive balance weights have a significant effect on causing vibration. In some instances, vibrations are cumulative, exceeding the value caused by any single impact action.
2.  **Speed Thresholds:** Little vibration is observed at speeds below 25 miles per hour.
3.  **Span Length:** For girder spans under 50 feet, the increase in deflection due to impact at 40 or 50 miles per hour can reach 40% to 50%. This percentage decreases for longer spans, falling to approximately 25% for 75-foot spans.
4.  **Stress Distribution:** The percentage increase in boom stresses due to impact is roughly equal to the increase in deflection, though the increase in web bracing bars is somewhat greater.
5.  **Mean Deflection:** The speed of the train affects the magnitude of vibrations but does not produce an effect on the mean deflection.
