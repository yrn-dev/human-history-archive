# Economic

In the context of financial associations and political study, the term "economic" relates to the management of savings, the provision of loans for property, and the scientific study of political economy.

## Building Societies and Economic Terminology
In Scotland, building societies are frequently referred to as "Economic" or "Property Investment." These organizations are designed to raise a fund through member subscriptions to provide advances for mortgage purposes on leasehold, copyhold, or freehold estates. While the act of 1836 utilized the term "Benefit Building Society," this did not become the sole distinctive name for such societies in England until 1849. 

The term "Building Society" is noted as an incomplete description of these entities, as they often perform the function of investing small savings and may not undertake actual building operations. Instead, they advance money to members to purchase land or dwelling-houses. Under the act of 1836, members who did not wish to purchase or erect a house could still receive the value of their shares, including interest paid by those who had received advances.

## Structure and Regulation of Building Societies
Building societies are categorized as either "permanent" or "terminating." A terminating society concludes at a fixed date or upon reaching a specific result defined in its rules; a permanent society has no such fixed end date.

In the United Kingdom, the Friendly Societies Act of 1834 extended benefits to associations for frugal investment. A subsequent act in 1836 granted building societies privileges including exemption from stamp duty, simplicity in conveyance forms, and exemption from usury laws. An act in 1874 further modified these regulations by:
* Making future societies incorporated bodies and allowing existing ones to apply for incorporation.
* Relieving members from the need to trace titles through successive trustees.
* Limiting member liability to arrears due at the time of winding up or the amount secured by mortgage deeds.
* Granting the power to borrow money, provided the limitation of liability was printed on the loan acknowledgment and the borrowed amount did not exceed one year's income from subscriptions (for terminating societies) or two-thirds of the amount secured by member mortgages.

## Financial Operations and Risks
Building and loan associations provide members with the benefits of a savings bank and accruing compound interest. They enable members to borrow for building purposes, though the economy of this method compared to ordinary savings banks is debated. In some cases, premiums paid to secure a loan can make this method more expensive than ordinary borrowing. Premiums may be paid as a number of advance interest payments or an amount exceeding legal interest.

The growth of these societies has faced challenges and risks, including:
* The practice of receiving deposits repayable at call while funds were invested in long-term loans.
* The emergence of "mushroom societies" that primarily served to pay fees to promoters.
* The "vicious system" of trafficking in advances awarded by ballot.
* The failure of large societies, such as the Liberator, which had ceased genuine building society business.

## International Variations and Rivals
In the Dominion of Canada, these entities are sometimes called "loan companies." While they can invest in areas other than real estate, approximately 90% of their advances are secured by such property. As of the end of 1904, their liabilities to the public were £21,000,000, with liabilities to stockholders exceeding £13,000,000 and uncalled capital of £5,000,000. In Australia, the movement progressed in several colonies, though a crisis occurred in Victoria where many societies suffered severely.

Various rivals to building societies have emerged, including industrial and provident land and building societies, bond and investment companies under the Companies Acts, and local authorities authorized to lend money to the working classes. Additionally, friendly societies have begun investing surplus funds in loans to members based on the building society principle.

## Political Economy as a Science
The study of economics as a science is represented by the work of British political economist John Elliott Cairnes (1823-1875). Cairnes, who held the chair of political economy at Dublin, published *Character and Logical Method of Political Economy* in 1857. This work expanded upon J.S. Mill's treatment of unsettled questions in political economy and provided a clear exposition of the meaning of the term "law" within the field. Cairnes' work is characterized by precision of language, logical exactness, and a firm grasp of economic facts.
