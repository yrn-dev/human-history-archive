# Cranz, Compendium der Ballistik (1898) and The Official Text-Book of Gunnery (1902)

The *Compendium der Ballistik* (1898) by Cranz and *The Official Text-Book of Gunnery* (1902) are recognized authorities in the field of ballistics and gunnery. These works are situated within a broader historical lineage of scientific study regarding the motion of projectiles and the mechanics of ordnance.

## Context within Ballistic Literature
The study of ballistics has been informed by a wide array of historical and international authorities. Early foundational works include the *Nova Scientia* (1537) by Tartaglia and the writings of Galileo (1638). Later developments in the field were advanced by Robins in *New Principles of Gunnery* (1743) and Euler in *The True Principles of Gunnery* (1777).

The discipline has seen significant contributions from various national traditions, including:
* **French:** Works by Didion, Helie, Hugoniot, Vallier, and Baills, as well as Charbonnier's *Balistique* (1905).
* **Italian:** Siacci's *Balistica*.
* **Russian:** *Balistique* by Mayevski and Zabudski.
* **Spanish:** *Balistica* by La Llave, Ollero, and Mata.

More recent technical advancements leading up to the early 20th century include Bashforth's *The Motion of Projectiles* (1872) and *The Bashforth Chronograph* (1890), as well as the works of Lissak (*Ordnance and Gunnery*, 1907), Bruff (*Ordnance and Gunnery*), and Ingalls (*Exterior and Interior Ballistics, Handbook of Problems in Direct and Indirect Fire*).

## Related Technical Concepts in Ordnance
The study of gunnery involves the analysis of explosive propellants and the resulting physical forces. In the British service, explosive cordite is utilized. This substance is named for its appearance as short lengths of cord, produced by squeezing the composition in a viscous state through a die. 

The size of the cordite is designated by the diameter of the hole in the die, measured in hundredths of an inch. For example, cordite size 30 is produced using a hole with a diameter of 0.30 inches. 

The combustion of these explosives produces gas, the volume, temperature, and pressure of which are assigned an upper limit based on the thermochemical properties of the constituents. However, determining these limits requires extensive experimentation, such as the results published by Sir Andrew Noble in the *Phil. Trans.* from 1905-1906 and subsequent years.
