# Bronchitis

Bronchitis is defined as the inflammation of the mucous membrane of the bronchial tubes. The condition is categorized into two main varieties: specific and non-specific. Specific bronchitis occurs in infectious disorders such as pneumonia, measles, influenza, and diphtheria, and is caused by the micro-organisms associated with those diseases. Non-specific bronchitis results from mechanical or chemical irritation, or from extension from above. For clinical description, the disease is divided into acute and chronic forms.

## Acute Bronchitis
Acute bronchitis generally arises from a sudden change from a heated to a cool atmosphere or from exposure to cold, particularly when accompanied by dampness. The disease often begins as a common cold or catarrh, but is distinguished by general constitutional disturbance and feverishness. 

Early symptoms include a dry, painful, and short cough, wheezing and rapid respiration, and a feeling of tightness or oppression throughout the chest. Patients may also experience pain behind the breast bone and rawness in the throat. In some instances, symptoms of bronchitic asthma may be superadded, increasing the patient's suffering.

Acute bronchitis is regarded as a severe and serious ailment. It is particularly dangerous at the extremes of life—infants and the aged—and is one of the most fatal diseases for these groups. This is due to the fragility of their frames and the tendency of the disease to assume the capillary form. It is also frequently severe in individuals addicted to intemperance. Furthermore, it acts as an alarming complication for those with existing respiratory or heart diseases, as well as children who have suffered from whooping-cough or measles, which cause bronchial irritation.

## Capillary Bronchitis and Broncho-pneumonia
When inflammation spreads to the minute ramifications of the bronchial tubes near the air-cells of the lungs, the condition is known as broncho-pneumonia or capillary bronchitis. This form is characterized by intensified symptoms: the cough becomes incessant, respiration is laboured and extremely rapid, and the nostrils dilate with each effort. 

The patient may appear dusky or pale with livid lips, experiencing suffocative paroxysms that make it impossible to lie down. If the chest is not cleared through expectoration and coughing, the patient may succumb to delirium and somnolence. In very young patients, this form can be fatal within forty-eight hours.

## Chronic Bronchitis
Chronic bronchitis may develop independently or as a result of repeated acute attacks. While it can affect any age, it is more frequent in those advanced in life. It is often referred to as "winter cough" because it typically recurs during colder seasons and disappears in summer, though in advanced cases, the cough is seldom entirely absent.

The symptoms are similar to the acute form, but pain and febrile disturbance are less marked. The cough is usually more troublesome in the morning and is accompanied by copious expectoration; when this is particularly abundant, it is termed bronchorrhoea.

Chronic bronchitis leads to structural changes, including the ulceration or thickening of the mucous membrane. Long-standing cases may result in impaired lung nutrition and emphysema (dilatation of the air-tubes), leading to constant breathlessness. It can also occur as a serious complication of syphilis, gout, heart disease, or Bright's disease of the kidneys.

Occupational factors also contribute to the disease. The inhalation of vegetable dust and the growth of organisms carried by that dust make millers, grain-shovellers, weavers, and cotton factory workers especially liable to the condition.

## Treatment and Complications
Treatment for chronic bronchitis depends on the patient's age, the severity of the case, and any complications. Prime importance is placed on general health, particularly for those exposed by their work. Cod-liver oil tonics are considered highly advantageous. Protective measures include using a respirator in damp or cold weather, or, in aggravated cases, removing the patient to a genial climate or confining them to a warm room during winter.

Bronchitis can be a complication of other severe medical events. For example, during the second period of a severe burn (between the passing of shock and the separation of the slough), bronchitis has been recorded as a potential complication, likely resulting from septic absorption. Additionally, the inhalation of gas pumped from cylinders—as seen in warfare—can produce acute bronchitis, leaving victims gasping for breath and, in some cases, temporarily blind.

Finally, both acute and chronic bronchitis, along with other lung diseases like phthisis or pneumonia, may leave behind bronchiectasis. This is a condition involving the dilatation of the bronchi, typically affecting the medium and smaller tubes in one lung.
