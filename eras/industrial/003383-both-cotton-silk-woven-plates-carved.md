# Textile and Carving Industries

The production of woven fabrics and carved objects represents a significant portion of industrial and artisanal activity across various global regions, ranging from large-scale steam mills to primitive household occupations.

## Cotton and Silk Production

Cotton and silk are primary materials for weaving in several regions, though the methods of production vary from industrial to manual.

### India
In Bombay, the modern industrial landscape is defined by the growth and manufacture of cotton. Large steam mills have developed rapidly in Bombay City, Ahmedabad, and Khandesh. By 1905, there were 432 factories in the presidency, the majority of which were dedicated to cotton. Bombay City and Island serve as the center of this industry, housing nearly two-thirds of the mills. Despite a period of depression between 1891 and 1901 caused by famine and widespread plague, the trade has expanded and the quality of goods has improved. Additionally, as of 1901, there were 178,000 hand-loom weavers in the province who specialize in weaving designs into cloth.

Silk goods are manufactured in Bombay, Thana, Nasik, Yeola, Surat, and Ahmedabad. These materials are frequently decorated with woven or printed designs, though competition from European goods has caused most branches of this industry to decline. In the Bankura district of Bengal, both silk and cotton are woven, and tussur silk is gathered in the hilly tracts.

### Burma and Japan
In Burma, silk-weaving and dyeing are chief occupations, often performed by cultivators during the by-season. While the introduction of silk fabrics and cheap cottons has impacted hand-weaving, the industry persists in rural areas. Weaving is generally carried out by women and girls when they are unoccupied by domestic duties, though in Amarapura, many people follow silk-weaving as their sole livelihood.

In early Japan, cloth was primarily woven from mulberry bark and hemp. While silken fabrics were manufactured, they were not highly favored for raiment because the process of boiling cocoons had not been invented; instead, cocoons were held in the mouth for spinning, resulting in coarse, uneven threads. Consequently, silk was used chiefly for cushions, while cotton, hemp, or mulberry bark were preferred for clothing.

### Bolivia and Europe
In Bolivia, spinning and weaving are household occupations using rough Spanish looms of colonial patterns. While coarse cloth is made from llama fleece, the people also manufacture cotton and woollen goods. Finer fabrics are woven from vicuna and alpaca wool, which are sometimes mixed with lamb's wool or silk. In the Chiquitos and Mojos districts, Jesuit missionaries taught the natives to weave an excellent cotton cloth.

In Europe, patterned silks were known in the classical age and reached great development during the Byzantine era. Egypt served as a chief center for the production of linen and silk textiles, with fabrics dating back to the 3rd century. In the 6th century, silks featured repeated figures, such as Samson and the lion. Byzantine altar curtains for St Sophia were described as having crimson grounds with gold embroidery and woven images of St Paul, St Peter, and Christ.

## Specialized Woven Fabrics

Certain weaving techniques produce highly decorative or functional materials. Brocade is a class of richly decorative shuttle-woven fabrics, often made with gold and silver threads and coloured silks. These fabrics create a low-relief effect on the face, while the back shows clipped or loose floating weft threads. An example of 11th or 12th-century Siculo-Saracenic brocade features Kufic inscriptions and animals such as geese and seated animals within a trellis framework.

Tapestry refers to hangings and curtains woven in a special way, though in later medieval times the term was used for seat and floor coverings. In ancient Egypt, these were often linen inwoven with coloured wools.

## Carving and Artisanal Work

Carving is practiced across various materials, including wood, stone, and metal.

### Wood-Carving
In India, Ahmedabad and Surat are famous for carved wood-work; many houses in Ahmedabad feature elaborate carving, with further examples found in Yeola, Nasik, Surat, Baroda, and Broach. In Burma, wood-carving is a chief art, noted for its spontaneity and freedom. Teak wood is used for fixtures, while *yamane* clogwood is preferred for other works. Artisans use a mallet, gouge, and chisel, tracing designs with charcoal before finishing them with fine tools.

### Stone and Other Materials
In the Bankura district of British India, plates and other items are carved from soap-stone. In ancient Assyria, marble slabs were carved in low relief to copy the geometrical patterns and lotus borders of floor covers or rugs.

Other artisanal activities include silver work in Burma, which primarily consists of cups, betel boxes, and bowls. In India, goldsmiths are employed to create gold and silver ornaments, typically using metal supplied by the customer.
