# Victorian London: Life in the Industrial Capital

## Overview
During the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), London evolved into the undisputed center of the global economy, serving as the heart of the British Empire and the vanguard of the Industrial Revolution. This era was characterized by a jarring dichotomy: unprecedented wealth and technological progress existed alongside systemic poverty and environmental degradation.

The city experienced an explosive population boom, growing from roughly 2 million in 1840 to over 6 million by the century's end. This rapid urbanization outpaced infrastructure, leading to the creation of notorious slums in the East End. While the West End showcased the opulence of the rising middle class and the aristocracy, the working class faced grueling labor in factories and docks. 

Historians continue to debate the "Standard of Living" during this period. Some argue that real wages rose and quality of life improved toward the end of the century; others contend that these gains were offset by the psychological toll of urban alienation, pollution, and the precariousness of employment.

## Key Facts
*   **The Great Stink:** In the summer of 1858, the smell of untreated human waste in the River Thames became so unbearable that Parliament was nearly forced to relocate. This crisis prompted Joseph Bazalgette to engineer a massive, sophisticated sewer network that effectively ended cholera outbreaks in the city.
*   **Social Stratification:** The city was geographically divided by class. The "West End" was the domain of government and wealth, while the "East End" became a hub for immigrants and the industrial poor, characterized by overcrowded tenements.
*   **Transport Revolution:** The introduction of the hansom cab and the "Tube" revolutionized urban mobility, allowing the city to expand outward and creating the first modern commuter culture.
*   **Public Health:** The era saw the birth of modern epidemiology. John Snow’s 1854 discovery that cholera was waterborne (rather than caused by "miasma" or bad air) fundamentally changed urban planning and medicine.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Fog was Chemical:** The famous "pea-soupers" were not natural fogs but "smogs"—a combination of fog and thick sulfurous smoke from millions of coal-burning fireplaces and factories.
2. **The Mudlarks:** Impoverished children and adults, known as "mudlarks," would scavenge the banks of the Thames at low tide for scrap metal, coal, or jewelry to sell for food.
3. **The First Underground:** When the Metropolitan Railway opened in 1863, it used steam locomotives, meaning the tunnels were often filled with sulfurous smoke despite the ventilation shafts.
