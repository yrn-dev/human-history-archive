# BARROW-IN-FURNESS

Barrow-in-Furness is a seaport and municipal, county, and parliamentary borough located in Lancashire, England. It is situated 264-1/2 miles north-west by north from London and is served by the Furness railway. The town is positioned on the seaward side of a hammer-shaped peninsula within the district of Furness, lying between Morecambe Bay and the estuary of the Duddon. A narrow channel separates the mainland from Walney, a long, low island. In 1904, the War Office undertook the construction of a strong fort on Walney, and a bridge connecting the island to the mainland was started in 1905. Within the channel lies Barrow Island, which has been connected to the mainland through reclamation efforts that left only a narrow channel, which now serves as docks.

## Growth and Industrial Development
Barrow is characterized by remarkably rapid and modern growth, which was primarily driven by the presence of pure haematite iron ore veins in the Furness district. This ore had been known and smelted since early times by the Romans, the British, and the monks of Conishead Priory and Furness Abbey. 

Initially, Barrow served as a port for exporting this ore to the midlands and the furnaces of South Wales. At the start of the 19th century, these exports were limited to a few thousand tons. By 1847, exports had risen to approximately 50,000 tons, though the population of the town remained small at only 325 people. The opening of the first section of the Furness railway in 1846, which linked Barrow to mines near Dalton, facilitated a great increase in trade.

Industrialization accelerated with the institution of the iron works of Messrs Schneider & Hannay in 1859. In 1866, this company was absorbed by the Barrow Haematite Steel Company, leading to a large output of steel produced via the Bessemer process. This process was particularly suited to the non-phosphoric ore found in the region. While the haematite is also worked at Dalton, Askam, and Ulverston, local furnaces have since become partly dependent on ore imported from Spain.

While steel was foundational, shipbuilding eventually surpassed it in importance. The firm of Vickers, Sons & Maxim maintains a yard in the town where they construct various vessels, including numerous warships. Other local industries include jute works, a paper-pulp factory, a petroleum storage establishment, and wagon and engineering works.

## Urban Layout and Public Institutions
The town is organized in a rectangular layout and features several public buildings, including an exchange, municipal buildings, and handsome churches. The center and outskirts are connected by an electric tramway service. 

The town's rise in the middle of the 19th century is attributed in large part to the dukes of Buccleuch and Devonshire, as well as Sir James Ramsden, the first mayor of Barrow and managing director of the Furness railway. A statue of Sir James Ramsden (who died in 1896) and a statue of Lord Frederick Cavendish (assassinated in Dublin in 1882) stand in front of the town hall. Additionally, the cottage where the painter George Romney lived from 1742 to 1755 has been preserved as a memorial.

Educational facilities in the town include a technical school, a girls' high school, and a school of art and science. In religious administration, Barrow serves as a suffragan bishopric within the diocese of Carlisle.

## Administration and Demographics
Barrow was incorporated in 1867 and achieved the status of a county borough in 1888. Its corporation is composed of a mayor, 24 councillors, and 8 aldermen. The borough covers an area of 11,023 acres. As a parliamentary borough established in 1885, it returns one member and falls within the North Lonsdale division of the county.

The population of the town has seen steady growth, recorded at 51,712 in 1891 and increasing to 57,586 by 1901.
