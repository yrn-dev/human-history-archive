# Ashland and Related Industrial Manufactures

The name Ashland refers to several distinct locations within the United States, including a city in Kentucky, a city in Wisconsin, and a village in Virginia. While these locations vary in their primary economic drivers, certain industrial products—specifically furniture, leather, and brick—are common to several of these regions and other industrial centers.

## Ashland, Kentucky
Ashland, Kentucky, is a city located in Boyd County on the Ohio River, approximately 130 miles east by north of Frankfort. Settled in 1854 and chartered as a city in 1870, it serves as a distributing center for the surrounding country, which is rich in timber, oil, clay, stone, iron ore, and coal. The city is connected by the Norfolk & Western and the Chesapeake & Ohio railways, and maintains an electric line connection to Huntington, West Virginia.

The city's manufacturing sector is diverse, producing wire rods, sheet steel, nails, and pig iron. It is also noted for the production of leather, furniture, fire brick, and dressed lumber, with a particular emphasis on poplar. For recreation, the city features a 30-acre natural park called Central Park and the 75-acre Clyffeside Park. The latter is a pleasure resort and meeting ground for the Tri-State "Chautauqua," featuring a casino capable of seating 3,000 people.

## Other Locations Named Ashland
In Wisconsin, Ashland is a city and the county-seat of Ashland County, situated at the head of Chequamegon Bay on Lake Superior. Settled around 1854, it was incorporated as a village in 1863 and became a city in 1887. It serves as a major port for the shipment of iron ore from the Gogebic Range, with annual shipments valued at $12,000,000. Its industries include iron and steel rolling mills, foundries, large saw-mills, and the manufacture of charcoal, wood-alcohol, sulphite fibre, and dynamite.

In Virginia, Ashland is a village in Hanover County, located 17 miles northwest of Richmond. Settled in 1845 and incorporated in 1856, it is known as the site of Randolph-Macon College. The college, chartered in 1830 and moved to Ashland in 1868, is part of a system of colleges and academies.

### Brick Production and Firing
Bricks are a type of artificial stone made from burnt clay. The strength and durability of a brick depend on the firing process, which involves chemical decompositions and re-combinations. For ordinary bricks, the finishing temperature is typically around 1050° C, though it can range from 900° C to 1250° C. Lime-clays require higher temperatures, usually between 1150° C and 1200° C.

There are two primary methods of firing:
*   **Permanent Kilns:** Considered the best method, these have been used since the Roman era.
*   **Clamps:** A slower, less economical method used in smaller brickfields. Unfired bricks are arranged in rectangular stacks with coal spread in horizontal layers. Fires are lit in "fire-mouths" at the bottom. Once cooled, the bricks are sorted, and under-fired bricks are reserved for the next clamp.

### Leather and Timber
Leather production often involves tanning processes. In Russia, the bark of the common birch is used for tanning. Specifically, the bark yields an empyreumatic oil known as *diogott* through dry distillation, which provides Russia leather with its characteristic pleasant odor. 

Timber is also a vital industrial resource. In Russia, the birch tree is highly valued for its toughness, used by turners, upholsterers, and carriage-builders. In the United States, cities like Ashland, Kentucky, specialize in the production of dressed lumber, particularly poplar.
