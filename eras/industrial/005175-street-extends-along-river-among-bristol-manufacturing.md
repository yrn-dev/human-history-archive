# Bristol

The name Bristol refers to several distinct locations across the United States and the United Kingdom, including a borough in Pennsylvania, a township in Connecticut, and a city in England.

## Bristol, Pennsylvania
Located in Bucks county, Pennsylvania, this borough is situated on the Delaware river, 20 miles northeast of Philadelphia and opposite Burlington, New Jersey. The borough is built on level ground elevated several feet above the river within an attractive farming country. It is served by the Pennsylvania railway.

The layout of the borough features Mill Street as the location for principal business houses, while Radcliffe Street extends along the river. Manufacturing establishments in Bristol include rolling mills, machine shops, a planing mill, worsted, hosiery, and yarn mills, as well as factories producing patent leather, wall paper, and carpets. Just outside the borough limits are the Bath Springs, which are known for the medicinal properties of their chalybeate waters, though they were more famous in the early 18th century.

Settled after William Penn received his province charter in 1681, Bristol served as the county seat of government until 1725. It was laid out in 1697 and incorporated as a borough in 1720, though its present charter dates from 1851. Population records show 6,553 residents in 1890, 7,104 in 1900 (including 1,134 foreign-born), and 9,256 in 1910.

## Bristol, Connecticut
This township in Hartford county, Connecticut, is located in the central part of the state, approximately 16 miles southwest of Hartford. It covers an area of 27 square miles and includes the borough of Bristol (incorporated in 1893) and the village of Forestville. Both are situated on the Pequabuck river. Transportation is provided by electric railway to New Britain, Terryville, and Hartford, as well as the western branch of the midland division of the New York, New Haven & Hartford railway.

The borough of Bristol is known for various manufactures, including brass ware, silverplate, hardware, iron castings, woollen goods, bells, and clocks. Bristol clocks have been widely known since they were first manufactured shortly after the War of Independence.

Originally part of the township of Farmington, the area was first settled around 1727. It became an independent corporation in 1742 with the formation of the first church, known as the New Cambridge Society after 1744. A Protestant Episcopal Church was organized in 1748; its members were associated with the Loyalist party during the War of Independence. During this time, the church's rector, Rev. James Nichols, was feathered and tarred by the Whigs, and member Moses Dunbar was hanged for treason by Connecticut authorities. Local Loyalists frequently gathered at Chippen's Hill, where a cave called "The Tories' Den" remains a landmark. In 1785, West Britain and New Cambridge were incorporated as the township of Bristol, though they were divided into the present townships of Burlington and Bristol in 1806. Population figures indicate 7,382 residents in 1890, 9,643 in 1900 (with 6,268 in the borough), and 13,502 in 1910 (with 9,527 in the borough).

## Bristol, England
The nucleus of this city lies north of the river. Its business center is located in a district where Corn Street, Wine Street, High Street, and Broad Street radiate from a center near the Floating Harbour. To the south, connected by Bristol Bridge, is an island formed between the New Course of the Avon and the Floating Harbour. This area contains public buildings, the general hospital, two major churches (St Mary Redcliffe and the Temple), and Temple Meads station above Victoria Street. The river Frome joins the Avon immediately above the bridge. The city's inner streets are generally narrow and irregular, featuring picturesque ancient gabled houses.

### History and Governance
Bristol's history includes a long struggle to absorb the manor of Bedminster. The western half, Redcliffe, was sold by Earl Robert of Gloucester to Robert FitzHardinge, while the eastern half was held by the Templars. In 1247, a stone bridge was built over the Avon and a new course was cut for the river Frome to improve the harbour. A significant charter in 1256 gave burgesses the right to choose coroners. Later, in 1312, a great insurrection occurred due to the exclusiveness of the merchant gild, leading to a royal siege in 1313.

A charter in 1373 made Bristol a county in itself, independent of county courts, with a council of forty and an elected sheriff. The town was divided into five wards, each with an alderman. This period saw great prosperity, including the voyages of Sebastian and John Cabot and the influence of William Canyng (1399-1474), a five-time mayor who rebuilt St Mary Redcliffe and conducted a large cloth trade with the Baltic. By 1500, the Society of Merchant Venturers, whose records began in 1467, directed all foreign trade.

In the 16th century, Bristol traded with the Canaries, Spain, and Spanish colonies in America, and began a trade in African slaves that flourished in the 17th century. The city was besieged three times during the Civil War. In 1710, Queen Anne granted a charter, and the city later became a center for the Methodist revival of Wesley and Whitefield.

### Industrial Development and Decline
During the reign of Edward III, cloth manufacture developed, with Thomas Blanket setting up looms in 1337. By 1353, Bristol was a Staple town. However, by the early 19th century, prosperity diminished due to the abolition of the slave trade (which ruined the West Indian sugar trade), the rise of Liverpool, and high rates levied by the Bristol Dock Company. A decline was eventually checked by the Municipal Reform Act of 1835 and the efforts of the Bristol chamber of commerce (founded 1823). The corporation bought the docks in 1848 and later acquired docks at Portishead and Avonmouth (made between 1877-1880) in 1884.

The city's principal industries include:
*   Chemical works, glass works, and potteries.
*   Leather works, tanneries, and shoe factories.
*   Shipbuilding, ropewalks, anchors, sail-cloth, and chain cables.
*   Tobacco mills, sugar refineries, and chocolate factories.
*   Iron works, machine works, copper, lead, and shot works.
*   Saw mills, breweries, and soaperies.
*   Stained-paper works and buttons.

A coalfield extending 16 miles southeast to Radstock supports these manufactures. As of 1904, the city area was 17,004 acres. The government consists of a lord mayor, 66 councillors, and 22 aldermen.
