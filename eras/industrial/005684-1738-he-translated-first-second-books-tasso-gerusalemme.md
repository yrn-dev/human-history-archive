# Henry Brooke

Henry Brooke (c. 1703–1783) was an Irish author and legal student known for his poetic works, translations, and political involvement during the mid-18th century.

## Early Life and Education
Born around 1703 at Rantavan, County Cavan, Brooke was the son of William Brooke, who served as the rector of Killinkere in the same county. His mother was the daughter of Simon Digby, the bishop of Elphin. Brooke's early education included instruction from Dr. Thomas Sheridan. In 1720, he entered Trinity College, Dublin, and subsequently moved to London in 1724 to pursue the study of law.

## Literary Works and Translations
Brooke's literary output spanned poetry, translation, and drama. In 1735, he published a philosophical poem in six books titled *Universal Beauty*. In 1738, he translated the first and second books of Tasso's *Gerusalemme liberata*. 

The following year, in 1739, Brooke produced a tragedy titled *Gustavas Vasa, the Deliverer of his Country*. Although the play underwent rehearsals at Drury Lane for five weeks, the performance was forbidden. This prohibition was attributed to a suspected portrait of Sir Robert Walpole in the role of Trollio, as well as a general dislike by the government for the play's spirit of fervent patriotism. Despite the ban on performance, the piece was printed and sold extensively, and it was later staged in Ireland under the title *The Patriot*. This controversy led Samuel Johnson to write a satirical pamphlet in 1739 titled "A Complete Vindication of the Licensers of the Stage from the malicious and scandalous Aspersions of Mr Brooke."

## Political Activity and Later Life
Brooke aligned himself with the party of the prince of Wales and resided in a small house at Twickenham, located near Alexander Pope. Due to his connections with the political opposition, his wife, Catherine Meares (his cousin and ward, whom he married before she was fourteen), encouraged him to return to Ireland.

Once back in Ireland, Brooke developed an interest in the nation's literature and history. He planned a history of Ireland from the earliest times and a collection of Irish stories, though both projects were eventually abandoned due to disputes regarding the ownership of the materials.

During the Jacobite rebellion of 1745, Brooke wrote *Farmer's Six Letters to the Protestants of Ireland* (collected in 1746), a work influenced by the form of Swift's *Drapier's Letters*. He also authored other pamphlets supporting the Protestant side and served as the secretary to an association. In recognition of his services, the government appointed him as the barrack-master at Mullingar, a position he held until his death in 1783.
