# Metal Sheathing and Related Costs

## Maritime Repair and Metal Sheathing
In the adjustment of claims for General Average (G.A.), specific rules govern the deductions made for "new for old" repairs. For wooden or composite ships, no "new for old" deduction is applied if the ship is under one year old from the date of its original register at the time of the accident. After one year, a deduction of one-third is generally applied, though there are specific exceptions.

Metal sheathing is handled by allowing in full the cost of a weight equal to the gross weight of the metal sheathing that was stripped off, minus the proceeds obtained from the old metal. Other related materials, such as felt, nails, and labour metalling, are subject to a one-third deduction.

## General Ship Repair Allowances
Certain expenses in ship repairs are allowed in full regardless of the specific ship type. These include the expense of replacing and taking out bent ironwork to straighten it, as well as the labour involved. Additionally, graving dock dues are allowed in full, including the use of stages, shears, graving dock materials, and expenses related to cartages and removals.

For iron or steel ships, all repairs are allowed in full if the ship is up to one year old from the date of original register, with the exception of the coating or painting of the bottom, which incurs a one-third deduction. In general, painting of the bottom is not allowed if it had not been painted within the six months prior to the accident. Deductions are regulated by the age of the ship rather than the age of the specific part being repaired, except in the case of machinery, boilers, provisions, and stores. No deduction is made for provisions and stores not in use, or for old material that is repaired without being replaced by new material.

## Other Maritime and Cargo Costs
When a ship is detained in a port for repairs, the cost of maintenance and wages for the crew, officers, and master during the extra period of detention is admitted as G.A. until the ship is ready to proceed. However, if the voyage is abandoned or the ship is condemned, wages and maintenance incurred after that date are not admitted as G.A. Furthermore, loss or damage to cargo caused by storing, reloading, stowing, or discharging is made good as G.A. only when the cost of those specific measures is admitted as G.A.

## Construction and Infrastructure Materials
In the context of building and infrastructure, the choice of materials is often dictated by cost and durability. For bridges, masonry and concrete are more durable than metal, and metal is more durable than timber. While brick, concrete, or masonry are used for moderate spans without excessive cost, steel is more economical for longer spans and imperative for very long spans.

Regarding building codes, the New York acts provide specific prescriptions for materials that are not as extensively detailed in the London Building Act. These include requirements that bricks be well-burned, hard, and good, and that mortar sand be clean, sharp, grit sand free from dirt or loam. The New York code also defines the strengths of cast-iron and steel, as well as tests for new materials. Additionally, excavations must be sheath-piled where necessary to prevent adjoining earth from caving in.

## Legal Costs and Recoveries
Historically, in courts of common law, costs were not granted to plaintiffs or defendants. The Statute of Gloucester (1277) first allowed demandants to recover the costs of their writ alongside damages. Costs for successful defendants were first introduced by the Statute of Marlbridge (1267) for wardship in chivalry, and were not generally granted until 1531. By 1606, these laws were extended to other actions.

Under the current rules of the Supreme Court of Judicature, costs are generally at the discretion of the judge or court. However, if a case is tried with a jury, costs typically follow the event unless the court orders otherwise for good cause.
