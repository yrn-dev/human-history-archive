# Spencer Fullerton Baird

## Early Life and Education
Spencer Fullerton Baird was an American naturalist born on February 3, 1823, in Reading, Pennsylvania. He pursued his higher education at Dickinson College in Carlisle, Pennsylvania, from which he graduated in 1840. 

## Early Scientific Pursuits
In 1841, the year following his graduation, Baird undertook an ornithological excursion through the mountains of Pennsylvania. According to one of his biographers, this journey involved walking 60 miles on the final day and a total of 400 miles over the course of twenty-one days. 

Baird's focus on ornithology was significantly influenced by a meeting with J. J. Audubon in 1838. Following this encounter, his studies became largely ornithological, aided by Audubon, who provided him with a portion of his own bird collection.

## Academic Career
After spending a period of time studying medicine, Baird returned to Dickinson College in 1845 as a professor of natural history. During his tenure there, he also took on the responsibilities of the chair of chemistry and provided instruction in mathematics and physiology. This diverse range of academic duties in a small college environment fostered a breadth of scientific interest that defined his professional life and established him as perhaps the most representative general man of science in America.

## The Smithsonian Institution and Government Service
Baird held a long-term position at the Smithsonian Institution in Washington, serving as assistant-secretary from 1850 until 1878. Upon the death of Joseph Henry, he ascended to the role of secretary. While serving as an officer of the Smithsonian, Baird supervised workers across a wide variety of disciplines.

In addition to his institutional roles, Baird served as the U.S. Commissioner of Fish and Fisheries from 1871 until his death in 1887. Between 1850 and 1860, he provided general superintendence for several government expeditions tasked with the scientific exploration of the western territories of the United States. To support these efforts, he prepared a manual titled *Instructions to Collectors*.

## Scientific Contributions and Publications
Baird's published works and studies spanned a vast array of scientific fields, including:
* General zoology and ornithology
* Botany
* Geology and mineralogy
* Anthropology
* Iconography

For several years, he edited an annual volume that summarized progress across all lines of scientific investigation. A bibliography compiled by G. Brown Goode covers Baird's publications from 1843 to the end of 1882, listing 1,063 entries. The majority of these—775 entries—were short articles written for his *Annual Record*. Among his most significant volumes was *Birds*, which was part of a series of reports regarding explorations and surveys for a railway route from the Mississippi.
