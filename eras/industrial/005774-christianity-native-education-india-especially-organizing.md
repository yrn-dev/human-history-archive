# Christianity and Native Education in India

The intersection of Christian missions and native education in India was characterized by a shift from traditional evangelizing methods toward organized educational systems. This transition was driven by various religious orders and individual missionaries who sought to integrate secular knowledge with religious instruction to reach diverse segments of the Indian population.

## Early Missionary and Educational Efforts
In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, figures such as Claudius Buchanan contributed to the advancement of both Christianity and native education. Serving as the Calcutta chaplain and vice-principal of the college of Fort William between 1797 and 1799, Buchanan focused on the organization of systematic translations of the Scriptures as a primary means of promoting these goals.

During the same era, French Catholic efforts were represented by Jean Antoine Dubois. Arriving in India in 1792, Dubois operated in the southern districts of the Madras Presidency and later reorganized the Christian community in Mysore after the fall of Seringapatam in 1799. Dubois believed that missionary work required a thorough understanding of the native population's character and innermost life. To achieve this, he adopted native clothing and abjured European society, which allowed him to gain welcome among people of all conditions and castes. His observations on the caste system and Brahminical life provided a foundation for understanding the difficulties of applying Western notions of progress and civilization to the administration of the empire.

## The Educational Mission of Alexander Duff
A significant shift in strategy occurred through the work of the Scottish missionary Alexander Duff, who arrived in Calcutta in May 1830. Duff observed that prior Protestant missions had been successful primarily among outcaste and low-caste peoples, particularly in south Travancore and Tinevelly, while the Mahommedan and Hindu communities remained largely untouched. 

Duff concluded that educational methods must replace evangelizing methods to reach these communities. He established an English school where the Bible served as the center of school work, while secular knowledge was taught from basic rudiments up to a university standard. Duff utilized the English language as a tool to provide Hindus with the "key of Western knowledge" and as the primary instrument for higher education. 

The success of Duff's educational mission had three primary results:
1. It altered the education policy of the government of India.
2. It led Christian churches in home countries to recognize education as a missionary agency.
3. It facilitated the introduction of Christian ideas to high-caste Hindus.

This influence culminated in a government minute adopted on March 7, 1835, which stated that the British government's objective in higher education should be the promotion of European literature and science among the natives of India, and that funds for education should be employed for English education alone.

## Religious Orders and Global Influence
The broader framework of Christian education in India was also influenced by international religious organizations. The Order of Jesuits, founded by Ignatius of Loyola, created a powerful missionary and educational organization that served as a primary vehicle for carrying Christianity to India. The Jesuits were noted for raising the standard of Catholic education, with their schools regarded for a long time as the best in Christendom.

Additionally, the Brethren of the Christian Schools (also known as the Ignorantines), founded by Jean Baptiste de la Salle in 1680, focused on providing free education, particularly in religion, to the children of the poor. This order, which required its members to serve without remuneration, eventually expanded its reach into Asia, Africa, and America.

## Comparative Educational Contexts
The organization of education under British and Christian influence stood in contrast to earlier periods. While the Emperor Akbar established various Hindu and Moslem schools, he did not implement a general educational scheme or a university system. The archive notes that Moslem teachers of that era often acted as conservators of bigotry rather than educators, and the Brahmins, who held a monopoly on Hindu teaching, were characterized by the slackness of hereditary privilege.

Under British rule, native schools were established in all settled communities. While systems of instruction in the "white portions" of the empire aimed to provide a basis for liberal education, native instruction was governed by local requirements.
