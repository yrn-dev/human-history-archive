# Designes, Death, and Maximes

The concepts of design, death, and the use of maxims appear across various historical contexts in the archive, ranging from political biographies and ancient legalities to philosophical traditions and industrial engineering.

## Design and Intent
In historical accounts, "design" often refers to the intent or purpose behind an action, which can be either benevolent or malicious. In the biography of Sir Philip Sidney written by Fulke Greville, the author details Sidney's "Actions, Counsels, Designes, and Death," specifically focusing on his efforts to suppress the power of Spain and the interests of England.

Conversely, the term is used to describe the absence of malice or the presence of ambition. In the records of the temple of Ceres, two Acarnanians were put to death without mercy after entering the temple by mistake and "with no ill design." Similarly, Joseph, the husband of Salome, revealed a secret injunction from King Herod to Herod's wife "without any ill design," intending only to prove the king's love for her. In a more predatory sense, Clearchus, a senator of Heraclea, utilized the city's internal commotions to further his "design of subjecting it to his own power," eventually establishing himself as a tyrant.

In a technical context, design refers to structural engineering. The "Woodeson" boiler, produced by Messrs. Clarke, Chapman & Co., features a design consisting of three upper drums and three smaller lower drums connected by tubes and enclosed in firebrick casing. This specific design allows for the inspection of all tubes and the renewal of any single tube without displacing other parts of the boiler.

## Death and Capital Punishment
Death is recorded both as a result of political conflict and as a penalty for violating sacred or state laws. Robert Greville, the 2nd Lord Brooke, was killed on March 2, 1643, at Lichfield while commanding forces in the midland counties during the Civil War. In Heraclea, the tyrant Clearchus was slain by two young citizens who were disciples of Plato.

Death also served as a tool for maintaining secrecy. At the temple of Ceres, it was a capital crime to divulge the mysteries of the feast. This led to the proscription of Diagoras the Melian and nearly cost the poet Æschylus his life.

## Maximes and Philosophical Sayings
Maxims are presented as tools for governance, moral guidance, and practical discernment.

### Ancient Greece and Japan
In ancient Greece, the Seven Wise Men—including Solon the Athenian, Thalês the Milesian, Pittakus the Mitylenean, and Bias the Prienean—were known for pithy repartees and distinctive mottoes. These maxims, such as "Know thyself," "Nothing too much," "Know thy opportunity," and "Suretyship is the precursor of ruin," were later inscribed in the Delphian temple. Dikæarchus noted that these men were not philosophers in the modern sense but possessed practical discernment regarding society.

In the tradition of Bushido, knowledge was not pursued as an end in itself but as a means to wisdom. Those who could produce maxims and poems without practical application were viewed as mere "convenient machines." This aligned with the doctrine of the Chinese philosopher Wan Yang Ming, who stated, "To know and to act are one and the same."

### Political and Legal Maxims
Maxims have also been used to define statesmanship and law. The *Letters to Sir Hercules Langrishe*, written by the father of a Catholic Association adviser, contain maxims of moral and political prudence regarding the admission of Irish Catholics to the franchise. In the Roman Empire, the edict of Milan's acknowledgement of a species of landed property pertaining to churches was received in tribunals as a "maxim of civil law."

## Religious and National Maxims
The archive notes a "grand maxim" regarding the people of Israel (Bani Israel). According to the history found in the Bible and the writings of Josephus, it is asserted that the sons of Israel could not be destroyed or hurt except by being drawn into sin against God. This is supported by accounts of the perversion of the Israelites by Midianite women, as preserved in the Samaritan Chronicle and other writings.
