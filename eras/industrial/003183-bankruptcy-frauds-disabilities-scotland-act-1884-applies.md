# The Bankruptcy Frauds and Disabilities (Scotland) Act 1884

The Bankruptcy Frauds and Disabilities (Scotland) Act 1884 is a piece of legislation governing specific aspects of insolvency and bankrupt behavior within the Scottish legal system. Its primary application and purpose are to align certain criminal and disqualification standards in Scotland with those found in English law.

## Scope and Application
The 1884 Act applies specifically to decrees of cessio and sequestrations. In the context of Scottish law, sequestration may be awarded by the sheriff or the court of session. It can be granted for a living debtor subject to the jurisdiction of Scottish courts—either via their own petition with the concurrence of qualified creditors or via a petition from qualified creditors provided the debtor is a notour bankrupt and has maintained a place of business or dwelling-house in Scotland within the previous year. Sequestration may also be awarded for a deceased debtor subject to the court's jurisdiction at the time of death, upon petition by qualified creditors or the debtor's mandatory.

## Criminal Provisions and Credit
A significant feature of the Bankruptcy Frauds and Disabilities (Scotland) Act 1884 is its adoption of criminal provisions found in section 31 of the English Bankruptcy Act 1883. These provisions specifically address the act of obtaining credit of £20 or more by an undischarged bankrupt who fails to disclose their bankrupt position. By incorporating these rules, the Act seeks to penalize the non-disclosure of insolvency when seeking significant credit.

## Disqualifications
Beyond criminal penalties, the 1884 Act addresses the legal disabilities and disqualifications that attach to bankrupt individuals. It places the law regarding these disqualifications on a similar footing to the English act, ensuring a consistent approach to the restrictions placed upon those who have not yet been discharged from bankruptcy.

## Context within Scottish Bankruptcy Law
The 1884 Act exists within a broader history of Scottish legislation designed to remedy the frauds of insolvent debtors. Early laws, such as an act of the Scottish parliament from 1621, established that debtors could not fraudulently diminish funds belonging to creditors; for instance, a deed of assignment executed in favor of a confidential friend or near relation after contracting debt was presumed to be fraudulent dealing. Later, the act of 1696 defined a "notour bankrupt" as a debtor who, while under diligence by caption or horning, fled, absconded, retired to a privileged place (such as an abbey), was imprisoned, or defended their person by force, and was subsequently found insolvent by the lords of session.

While the Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act of 1856 remains the main regulatory statute for bankruptcy in Scotland, the 1884 Act serves as a targeted measure to address fraud and disability. Other related measures passed since 1883 include the Bankruptcy Appeals (County Courts) Act 1884, the Bankruptcy (Discharge and Closure) Act 1887, and the Preferential Payments in Bankruptcy Act 1888.

## Related Administrative Changes
The legal landscape surrounding these acts was further modified by the Judicial Factors Act of 1889. This act abolished the office of accountant in bankruptcy (originally established in 1856), merging its duties into the office of accountant of the court of session. The 1889 Act also introduced measures to prevent excessive administration costs by requiring that the remuneration of a trustee under a sequestration be intimated to the accountant of court and the creditors before being acted upon, allowing the accountant of court to modify the rate if deemed expedient.

Notably, while England and Ireland utilize the Deeds of Arrangement Act 1887, this specific act does not apply to Scotland. Consequently, Scotland lacks an analogous requirement for the registration of private deeds of assignment for the benefit of creditors as a condition for their validity.
