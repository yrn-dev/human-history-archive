# Britannia Bridge

The Britannia bridge is a railway structure crossing the Menai Straits, designed to carry the Chester and Holyhead railway. Completed in 1850, the bridge was the result of efforts by engineers Robert Stephenson and William Fairbairn, with assistance from Eaton Hodgkinson, who contributed to the experimental tests and the formulation of the theory available at the time.

## Design and Construction
The bridge was conceived during a period when the admiralty imposed specific conditions to protect navigation. While there was an initial proposal to combine a girder with suspension chains—and tower piers were built to accommodate such chains—the theory for a combined structure could not be formulated. Consequently, experiments proved that a simple tubular girder made of wrought iron provided sufficient strength for the railway.

The bridge consists of a pair of tubular girders, with one line of rails passing through each tube. It features two spans of 460 feet and two spans of 230 feet, situated 104 feet above high water. Each girder is 1511 feet long and weighs 4,680 tons. In cross section, the girders are 15 feet wide, with a depth that varies from 23 feet at the ends to 30 feet at the center.

Because scaffolding could not be used for the center spans, the girders were constructed on shore, floated out to their positions between the piers using pontoons, and then raised into place by hydraulic presses. Although each girder is continuous over the four spans, it is regarded as an imperfectly continuous girder because its proportions over the piers do not strictly match those of a continuous girder.

## Structural Composition and Engineering
The tubular girders feature solid plate sides stiffened by angle irons. To facilitate the assembly of a large mass of plates and to counteract the tendency to buckle under compression, the top and bottom sections were designed to be cellular. These cells were sized to allow passage for painting. For the large-span girders, the total area of the cellular top flange is 648 square inches, while the bottom flange is 585 square inches.

In the design of the Britannia bridge, the ratio of depth to span was 1/16. This design reflects a broader engineering understanding of girder functions:
*   **Flanges:** The top and bottom flanges resist horizontal tension and compression. The stress to be resisted by the flanges varies inversely as the depth of the girder.
*   **Web:** The vertical web connects the flanges and resists forces equivalent to shear on vertical and horizontal planes.

While increasing the depth of a girder can be economical for the flanges, it requires a heavier web. For plate webs, the ratio of depth to span cannot be economically increased beyond 1/12 to 1/15 due to the need for extra material to reduce corrosion and prevent buckling.

## Historical Context of Girder Development
The construction of the Britannia and Conway bridges contributed to the understanding of the vertical web's function in girders. Following these projects, steel plate and wrought iron web girders became common for English railway bridges. 

Early English railways utilized cast iron girder bridges for spans between 20 and 66 feet, sometimes trussed with wrought iron. Over time, it became apparent that braced, lattice, or open web girders were more economical regarding material than solid web girders, except in cases of small spans. Consequently, plate web girders are now only used for spans of less than 100 feet.
