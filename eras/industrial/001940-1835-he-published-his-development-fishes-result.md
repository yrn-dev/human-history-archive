# Karl Ernst von Baer

Karl Ernst von Baer (1792-1876) was a German biologist whose research in organic development and the life-history of organisms established his pre-eminence in the field of science.

## Early Life and Education
Born on February 29, 1792, at Piep, in Esthonia, von Baer was the son of a small landowner. He attended school at Reval, which he left at the age of eighteen to pursue medical studies at Dorpat University. During his time there, the lectures provided by K. F. Burdach (1776-1847) inspired von Baer to conduct research in the broader field of life-history. 

Seeking greater encouragement and better facilities for scientific work, von Baer moved to Wuerzburg. There, he studied under J. I. J. Doellinger (1770-1841), a professor of anatomy. Doellinger provided the direction for von Baer's studies that would later secure his standing in the science of organic development.

## Professional Career and Research
Von Baer began his research by collaborating with C. H. Pander (1794-1865) on the evolution of the chick. These initial results were first published in a treatise on physiology written by Burdach. Following this, von Baer conducted independent investigations into the evolution of organisms in general. After spending time in Berlin, he was invited in 1817 by his former teacher, Burdach—who had become the professor of anatomy at Koenigsberg—to serve as the chief of the new zoological museum and as a prosector.

## Major Scientific Discoveries
Von Baer is noted for several significant contributions to biology and embryology:

**The Human Ovum**
In his 1827 work, *Epistola de Ovo Mammalium et Hominis Genesi*, von Baer detailed his discovery of the human ovum. He demonstrated that the Graafian follicles in the ovary are not the actual eggs. Instead, they contain a spherical vesicle—the true ovum—measuring approximately one one-hundred and twentieth of an inch in diameter. Von Baer asserted that this body contains the properties that transmit physical and mental characteristics from parents, grandparents, or more remote ancestors.

**Vertebrate Development**
In his *History of the Evolution of Animals* (*Ueber die Entwickelungsgeschichte der Thiere*), published in two parts in 1828 and 1837, von Baer presented further findings on vertebrate development. He showed that in all vertebrates, the primary stage of cleavage of the fertilized egg is followed by a modification into leaf-like germ layers. These layers—consisting of mucous, vascular, muscular, and skin—are the source from which the various organs of the body arise through differentiation. Additionally, he discovered the cylindrical, gelatinous cord.
