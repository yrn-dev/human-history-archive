# Bridge Substructure

In bridge engineering, a clear distinction is made between the superstructure and the substructure. While the superstructure consists of the main supporting members—such as trusses, beams, girders, suspension ropes, or arch rings—the substructure provides the essential support and stability required to maintain the bridge's integrity.

## Components of the Substructure
The substructure of a bridge comprises three primary elements: the piers, the abutments, and the foundations.

*   **Piers:** These are intermediate supports that generally sustain vertical loads. In certain bridge types, such as stiffened suspension bridges, the action of a pier is analogous to that of a pier situated between two arches.
*   **Abutments:** These are the end piers of the bridge. Unlike intermediate piers, abutments must resist more than just vertical loads; they are designed to resist the pull of suspension chains, the oblique thrust of an arch, or the thrust of an embankment. In the case of suspension bridges, the abutment acting as the anchorage must be thoroughly stable to withstand the maximum pull exerted by the chains.
*   **Foundations:** These are the portions of the substructure located below ground level. Foundations are often the most costly and difficult parts of the construction because the required position of a bridge may be dictated by conditions that are not naturally suited for supporting a heavy structure.

## Materials and Construction
Substructures are typically constructed from various forms of masonry, which include concrete, brickwork, and stone masonry. In some instances, woodwork or metal work may be utilized for intermediate piers.

Specialized construction techniques are employed for challenging environments. For example, at the St Louis bridge over the Mississippi, the east abutment and east pier were established on rock at a depth of 136 feet below high water. This was achieved using air locks and caissons with air chambers. In the case of the Tower Bridge in London, the main towers—which function as part of the support system—consist of a steel skeleton enclosed in brickwork and a facing of Portland stone and granite.

## Structural Function and Stability
The design of the substructure is heavily dependent on the type of superstructure it supports. When the superstructure consists of girders, the pressure on the abutments and piers is vertical, and dimensions are regulated by the need to bear this specific load. However, when arches are used, the abutment must be designed to distribute the resultant thrust to the foundation in a safe direction to prevent any part from being unduly compressed. Intermediate piers in arched bridges must possess considerable stability to counterbalance the thrust that occurs when one arch is loaded while an adjacent one is not.

The relationship between these components is critical for stability. For instance, a masonry arch is not a complete piece of construction on its own; it requires abutments to prevent it from falling to pieces. In a series of arches, each arch may serve as an abutment for the next, but the entire series remains dependent upon the ultimate abutments of the bridge.

## Substructure in Movable Bridges
The substructure plays a specialized role in movable bridge designs:
*   **Bascule Bridges:** In these designs, a leaf or bascule may turn around a horizontal hinge at one abutment. When the bridge opens, the prolongation of the bascule sinks into the piers.
*   **Rolling Bridges:** The bridge is designed so that the center of gravity remains over the abutment when the bridge is rolled forward.
*   **Swing Bridges:** These may revolve around a vertical axis located on a pier at the center of the river (equal-armed) or on one abutment (unequal-armed).

## Economic Considerations
The cost of the substructure significantly influences the "economic span" of a bridge. While the cost of bridge flooring and abutments is largely independent of the span length, the cost of a pier depends primarily on the height of the bridge and the character of the foundations. 

Generally, the cost of the superstructure increases as the span increases. However, if the cost of the substructure is higher, a larger span becomes more economical. The most cost-effective arrangement is broadly one where the cost of a single pier and its foundation is equal to the cost of the superstructure of one span.
