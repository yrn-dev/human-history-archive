# The History of Book Pricing and Production

The evolution of book pricing and production from the 18th through the 19th centuries reflects broader changes in printing technology, binding materials, and the expansion of the reading public. While early efforts to regulate prices through legislation existed, the market was largely driven by publishing formats and the costs of materials.

## 18th Century Production and Pricing
During the 18th century, France and England saw notable improvements in presswork, type, and paper, with Germany and Italy following toward the end of the century. Books of this era became generally elegant and neat. In terms of binding, the use of calf and sheepskin became less common in trade bindings, with most books being cased in paper boards.

Pricing varied significantly by format. Poetry was often published by subscription at very high prices, a practice followed by Pope and Prior. However, single poems by Pope were sold for 1s. or 1s. 6d. Novels were typically published in several volumes. At the start of the century, the price was mostly 1s. 6d. per volume, remaining steady for many years before rising toward the end of the century. For example, Miss Burney's *Evelina* (1778), consisting of three volumes, sold for 7s. 5d., while her *Camilla* (1796), consisting of five volumes, sold for L1:1s. Other high-value works included Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755), which cost L4:4s. in sheets and L4:15s. in boards.

## 19th Century Material Changes
The 19th century brought a great change to the appearance of books. Around 1820, glazed calico began to be used for book cases, followed by the use of cloth around 1830. Initially, lettering was printed on paper labels, but this transitioned to gilt stamping on the cloth. By the final quarter of the century, English and American books featured many beautiful cover designs. While leather bindings initially imitated older works, greater originality appeared toward the end of the 1880s.

Illustration methods also evolved. Wood-engraving was the primary method for much of the century, reaching a high level of technical skill, particularly in the 1860s when chief English artists provided drawings to engravers. Other subsidiary methods included etchings, lithographs, line engravings, and colour-prints. By the last decade of the century, photographic reproduction methods—specifically those using paper heavily coated with china clay—practically eliminated wood-engraving.

## Market Shifts and the Novel
The extension of the reading public created a demand for cheap reprints of famous works. This was met by the production of flimsy but pretty editions priced at 1s., 1s. 6d., or even less. However, the industry struggled to produce books that were well sewn and printed on good paper at moderate prices, a problem that remained unsettled until the 20th century.

A significant shift in the pricing of novels occurred around 1894 in England. The previous standard had been the three-volume edition, which cost 31s. 6d. This pricing structure included a "preposterous" price of 10s. 6d. per volume, a rate adopted during the initial popularity of the *Waverly Novels*. This was eventually substituted by single-volume novels priced at 6s. each (subject to discount), which greatly increased the number of medium-priced books available.

## Trade Printing and Standards
Trade-printing in America and England improved steadily throughout the century. A new standard for richness of general effect, ornament, and beauty of type was established between 1891 and 1896 through the work of William Morris at his Kelmscott Press, as well as by amateur printers who imitated his style.

## Legal Regulations
Early attempts to regulate the cost of books can be found in the first English Copyright Act of 1709. This act allowed any person who believed the published price of a book was unreasonably high to complain to the archbishop of Canterbury and other named individuals. If the complaint was found to be well founded, the price would be reduced. Booksellers who charged more than the fixed price faced a fine of L5 for every copy sold. However, this enactment apparently remained a dead letter.
