# The Reed Boiler

The Reed boiler is a specific type of water-tube boiler. In the general classification of boilers, water-tube boilers are those in which the water is primarily contained within numerous comparatively small tubes, with the fire typically positioned beneath these tubes to form the majority of the heating surface.

## Design and Construction
The Reed boiler is characterized by tubes that are longer and more curved than those found in the Normand boiler. A distinguishing feature of its construction is the method of tube attachment; rather than being expanded into the tube plate, each tube is fitted at both ends with specially designed nut and screw connections. This design allows the tubes to be quickly replaced or removed if necessary.

To optimize the use of space, the tubes are reduced in diameter at their lower ends, which permits the use of smaller chambers than would otherwise be required.

## Operation and Combustion
Unlike the Normand boiler, the Reed boiler does not utilize "water-walls." In the Reed design, the products of combustion pass directly from the fire-grate, moving amongst all the tubes, and proceed straight to the chimney.

## Maintenance and Access
The boiler is designed with specific access points for maintenance:
* **Lower Tube Ends:** Access is provided through numerous doors located in the water-chambers.
* **Top Tube Ends:** Access is obtained via the steam-chest.

## Comparison with Related Boilers
The Reed boiler belongs to a broader category of water-tube boilers that includes several other designs:
* **Normand Boiler:** This boiler utilizes three chambers connected by a large number of bent tubes and large outside circulating tubes. It employs "water-walls" created by arranging the outer rows of heating tubes to touch one another for nearly their entire length, protecting the outer casing. Its inner tubes are also bent to form a water-wall for much of the boiler's length, forcing combustion products to follow a definite course.
* **Blechynden and White-Foster Boilers:** Like the Normand, these use three chambers connected by bent tubes. The Blechynden boiler allows tubes to be removed through small doors in the upper part of the steam-chest, while the White-Foster boiler allows removal through a manhole at the end of the steam-chest.
* **Yarrow Boiler:** This design also uses three chambers. In its "express" version—used in cruisers and torpedo-boat destroyers for maximum power relative to weight—the tubes are smaller in diameter and more closely arranged to facilitate a high rate of combustion via "forced draught."
