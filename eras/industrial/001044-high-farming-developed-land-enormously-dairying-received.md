# Dairy Farming and High Farming

Dairy management, which encompasses the production and treatment of milk, butter, and cheese, is a critical branch of husbandry. The term "dairy" originates from the Middle English *deieris* (from *dey*, a maid-servant), referring to the area of farm buildings where the *dey* worked. While different global regions utilize various animals for milk—such as reindeer in Lapland, mares among the roving Tatars, camels by the Bedouin, and buffaloes in tropical swamps and jungles—the cow is now the sole animal used for dairy purposes in Great Britain, with the exception of a few goats and asses kept for invalids or infants.

## High Farming and Land Development
High farming has significantly developed the land, particularly in regions where proximity to large towns provides farmers with ready markets and access to good manure. In certain districts, such as those near the Clyde and Leven, this stimulus has allowed dairying on a considerable scale to prosper. 

Historically, agricultural progress in some areas was hindered by heavy soil and moist climates, requiring drainage for the successful growth of green crops. While lime was used as an auxiliary in the early 19th century, thorough draining—accelerated by Sir Robert Peel's drainage loan—provided a greater impetus for growth. The combination of green-crop husbandry and the liberal use of guano and other manures resulted in an immense increase in produce.

## Evolution of Dairy Farm Management
The dairying industry underwent a revolution during the closing quarter of the 19th century. Prior to the growth of the urban trade in country milk, dairy farms were largely self-sustaining regarding feeding-stuffs and manures, with cropping governed by routine. Modern dairy farms, however, often operate at "high pressure," utilizing purchased materials such as cake, corn, and manure. This allows land to be cropped independently of traditional courses, focusing on what is most needed at the time.

To supplement failing grasslands in the fall, farmers use root crops (especially mangel) and "soiling" crops—a North American term for green crops consisting of cabbages, green maize, or vetches and tares held up by oat stalks. Winter feeding is supported by meadow, clover hay, and root crops, supplemented by concentrated foods like oilcake, corn, and bulky foods such as brewers' grains. This shift toward exporting enormous quantities of milk has likely improved soil fertility in the British Isles by importing the fertility of corn-growing soils from distant countries.

## Technological and Organizational Advancements
Two primary factors revolutionized the industry: the introduction of the centrifugal cream-separator around 1880, which replaced the slow process of raising cream in pans, and the invention of a quick method to determine milk fat content approximately ten years later. 

In the United Kingdom, the movement toward increased production of cream, butter, and cheese intensified around 1875. The first London dairy show was held on October 24, 1876, leading to the foundation of the British Dairy Farmers' Association by Professor J. Prince Sheldon.

In the United States, the industry shifted toward a factory system of concentrated or co-operative manufacture. In northern Vermont, for example, the first creamery in Franklin county was built in 1880; by 1890, there were 15. A single creamery company at St Albans eventually operated over 50 separating stations, processing milk from more than 30,000 cows. This system allows for the production of standard quality "extra creamery" butter on a massive scale, with a single churning place serving an entire county.

## International Perspectives
In Denmark, agriculture is the main source of wealth, employing about two-fifths of the population. Dairy farming was greatly developed during the last forty years of the 19th century through scientific methods, machinery, and joint dairies. The Danish government supported this via inspection systems to prevent adulteration and grants for experiments. Co-operative systems are central to Danish poultry-farming, swine rearing, and butter-making.

In Scotland, dairying has received particular attention. The Ayrshire breed of cows is noted for the excellence and quantity of its milk. While Dunlop cheese was once common, it was often unequal in character and unsaleable in English markets. To remedy this, the Ayrshire Agricultural Association brought a Somerset farmer and his wife in 1855 to teach the Cheddar method, leading to the production of first-rate Cheddar cheese in Ayrshire.

## Utilization of By-products
Recent years have seen an increase in the economical use of dairy by-products. For every pound of butter, there are approximately 15 to 20 lb. of skim-milk and 3 lb. of butter-milk; every pound of cheese yields nearly 9 lb. of whey. While farm dairies use these to feed animals, factories have developed commercial products. Whey is used to make sugar-of-milk, and the albumin of skim-milk is used in arts and food products. Casein from skim-milk is used as a basis for enamel paint, a substitute for glue in paper-sizing, a baking substitute for eggs, and has been proposed for use in making electrical insulators, combs, and buttons.
