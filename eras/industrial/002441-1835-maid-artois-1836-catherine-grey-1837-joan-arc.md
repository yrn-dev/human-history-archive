# Joan of Arc and Related Historical Records

## Joan of Arc
Joan of Arc, also known as the Maid of Orleans, was born in Domremy-la-Pucelle, a village in the department of Vosges in eastern France. The cottage of her birth still stands, featuring an inscription from 1481 that reads "Vive labeur; vive le roi Louys" and the arms of France and Joan of Arc above the door. A modern basilica stands on a nearby hill where it is said she heard the voices that led her to take up the sword. The villagers continue to celebrate her story annually through a play.

Historical records and local traditions associate her with various locations. In the neighborhood of the village of Bellinglise, local tradition suggests that the old Château de Beauvoir provided shelter to Joan of Arc for one night while she was a prisoner. However, a tablet in the church at Élincourt claiming she made a pilgrimage to St. Marguerite and communicated in the church in 1430 is not supported by history, as she could not have visited Élincourt while it was occupied by the English.

In later periods, Joan of Arc has been commemorated through art and monuments. A plaster statue of the seventeen-year-old Maid of Orleans, depicted in shining armour and holding a white standard adorned with fleurs-de-lys, is located in a church choir. Additionally, a small silvered statuette of her is located on the porch of Notre Dame de Bonne Garde.

## Musical Works and The Maid of Artois
Michael William Balfe (1808–1870) was an Irish musical composer and operatic singer born in Dublin. After early instruction from his father and a musician named C. E. Horn, Balfe pursued a career that took him to Rome and Italy, where he composed several operas and a ballet, *La Perouse*. 

Following his return to England in 1833, Balfe achieved significant success at Drury Lane. On October 29, 1835, his work *Siege of Rochelle* was produced. This success led to the production of *The Maid of Artois* on May 27, 1836, an opera whose success was further confirmed by the singing of Malibran.

## Events of 1835, 1836, and 1837

### Political and Financial Developments in Illinois
In the state of Illinois, the year 1835 saw the chartering of a second state bank, following the failure of a previous bank. This second bank suspended payment two years later. In February 1835, Stephen Arnold Douglas was elected public prosecutor of the first judicial circuit. During that same year, Douglas was among several Democrats in Morgan county who supported a state Democratic convention to elect delegates for the 1836 national convention. 

By December 1836, Douglas became a member of the state legislature. In 1837, President Van Buren appointed him as the registrar of the land office at Springfield. Financially, the state of Illinois granted a loan for the construction of the Illinois and Michigan canal in 1836 and appropriated $10,000,000 for railroads and other improvements in 1837.

### Abolitionism and Women's Rights
In the United States, Sarah and Angelina Grimke became active in the abolitionist movement. On August 30, 1835 (recorded as "8th Month, 30th"), Angelina wrote a sympathetic letter to W. L. Garrison. In 1836, Angelina published *Appeal to the Christian Women of the South*, followed by Sarah's *Epistle to the Clergy of the Southern States* at the end of that year. 

By 1837, the sisters began giving public talks on slavery in Massachusetts, requiring the use of large halls. This prompted some members of the General Association of Congregational Ministers of Massachusetts to call for the clergy to close churches to women exhorters. In response to *Miss Beecher on the Slave Question* (1837), Angelina wrote letters in the *Liberator*.

### Academic Developments in Germany
Johann Heinrich Joseph Duntzer (1813–1901), a German philologist and historian of literature, studied ancient classics and Sanskrit at Berlin and Bonn between 1830 and 1835. In 1836, he published *Goethes Faust in seiner Einheit und Ganzheit*, and in 1837, he published *Goethe als Dramatiker*. In 1837, Duntzer established himself as a *Privat docent* for classical literature at Bonn, advocating for a critical method of interpreting German classics similar to the treatment of ancient classics.
