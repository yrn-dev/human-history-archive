# Exploration and Administration of Burma and China

## Geographical Exploration of the Far East
Since 1874, there has been rapid progress in geographical research regarding the route connections between China and Burma. This period of investigation began with Augustus Margary, who lost his life in 1874. Following his efforts, a growing number of experts and explorers traveled between the east and west, driven by a desire to unlock doors to commercial and economic development in China.

Among the notable explorers of this era was Captain William Gill of the Indian survey, who traveled across China to Burma and eastern Tibet, later documenting his experiences in the story of the *River of Golden Sand*. In 1877-1878, E.C. Baber further clarified the geographic mysteries of the western provinces of the Celestial empire. In 1881, Archibald Colquhoun wandered from Peking to Talifu. Later, in 1887, Mark Bell crossed the continent, illustrating ancient trade routes by following the steps of Colquhoun.

## Surveys of Burma and Neighboring Regions
The acquisition of Burma and the subsequent demarcation of boundaries facilitated the extension of geographical surveys into previously untraversed areas. Woodthorpe was among those who entered Burmese fields, and Prince Henri d'Orleans was one of the earliest travelers to explore the mountains that conceal the sources of the Mekong, the Salween, and the Irrawaddy.

While Burma was rapidly brought under survey, other regions were also being mapped. James M'Carthy handled the map-making for Siam, and knowledge of its coast districts was expanded by Warrington Smyth and Curzon. In the early 20th century, between 1900 and 1904, C.C. Manifold explored and mapped the upper basin of the Yang-tsze river, providing a valuable illustration of western Chinese configuration. Additionally, the Russo-Japanese war, which ended in 1905, advanced geographical knowledge of the north-east Russo-Chinese borderland.

## British Administration and Law in Burma
The British government administered law in Burma using principles identical to those adopted in other British dominions in India. Anglo-Indian law was generally applicable, although it did not operate in certain districts inhabited by tribes in a backward state of civilization. Acts of the British parliament relating to India were considered applicable to all crown dominions in India, including Burma, regardless of whether they were passed before or after annexation.

The Burma Laws Act of 1898 declared that acts of the governor-general in council passed for India generally were applicable to Upper Burma, as they had been for Lower Burma. This act also applied English law and court-based rules of law introduced into India. However, the British government did not disturb a large field of native law. According to the 1898 act, the following laws were administered regarding marriage, caste, inheritance, succession, or religious institutions:
*   **Buddhist law** for Buddhists, which is contained in sacred books known as *Dhammathats*.
*   **Mahommedan law** for Mahommedans.
*   **Hindu law** for Hindus.

The Buddhist laws are derived from collections attributed by Hindus to Manu, though they now differ widely from ancient Hindu law.

## Historical and Cultural Notes
Historical records indicate varying relations between China and Burma. In one era, Burma was forced to pay tribute to China. In later centuries, diplomatic missions occurred, such as a Portuguese embassy reaching Peking in 1750, followed by a Dutch mission and Lord Macartney's famous mission in 1793.

Cultural similarities have also been noted in architecture; the style of palaces featuring a masonry basement with a timber superstructure (often gilded and carved) is found in China, Burma, Siam, and Java. 

The region is also noted for its large bells. In Upper Burma, a pagoda houses a bell weighing approximately 80 tons with a diameter of 16 feet. For comparison, the great bell at Peking weighs 53 tons.
