# Barometers and the Measurement of Atmospheric Pressure

A barometer is an instrument used to measure the weight or pressure of the atmosphere. The term is derived from the Greek words *baros* (pressure) and *metron* (measure). While some devices, known as baroscopes, simply indicate variations in atmospheric pressure without providing quantitative data, barometers provide specific measurements.

## Historical Development
Prior to the work of Galileo, philosophers attempted to explain the function of suction pumps by suggesting that "Nature abhorred a vacuum." Galileo observed that a common suction pump could not raise water higher than approximately 32 feet, leading him to suggest that this "abhorrence" was limited to that height. His pupil, Evangelista Torricelli, hypothesized that this phenomenon occurred because the atmosphere had weight, and the pressure it exerted was equal to a 32-foot column of water.

To prove this, Torricelli reasoned that if the atmosphere could support 32 feet of water, it should support a column of mercury approximately 2-1/2 feet high, as mercury is about 13-1/2 times heavier than water. In his experiment, Torricelli used a hermetically sealed glass tube, 4 feet long and a quarter of an inch in diameter, filled with mercury and inverted into a basin of mercury. The liquid sank to nearly 30 inches above the basin's surface, creating a vacuum at the top of the tube now known as the Torricellian vacuum.

Further confirmation came from observing barometric columns at different elevations. Rene Descartes and Blaise Pascal predicted that the height of the mercury column would fall when carried up a mountain due to diminished atmospheric pressure. This was experimentally verified by Florin Perier on the Puy de Dome and by Pascal at various towers in Paris, including St Jacques de la Boucherie and Notre Dame.

## Types of Barometers
### Mercurial Barometers
The ordinary mercurial barometer consists of a mercury-filled tube about 36 inches long, closed at the upper end.
*   **Cistern Barometer:** The tube's open end is placed in a basin of mercury; pressure is measured by the difference in height between the mercury in the tube and the cistern.
*   **Siphon Barometer:** The cistern is removed, and the tube is bent upon itself at the lower end; the reading is the difference in levels between the two limbs.
*   **Specialized Forms:** Bernardo Ramazzini and Sir Samuel Morland suggested the diagonal barometer, where the upper tube is inclined. Dr. R. Hooke developed the wheel barometer and the steel-yard barometer, which use floats to magnify the mercury column's oscillation. G. Amontons invented the pendant barometer in 1695, using a vertical funnel-shaped tube, though it was unstable if jarred.

### Aneroid Barometers
The aneroid barometer (from the Greek *a-*, privative, and *neros*, wet) uses no liquid. Instead, it relies on the volume changes of an exhausted metallic chamber. Lucien Vidie patented an instrument in 1845, though Nicolas Jacques Conte had devised a similar instrument in 1799 for measuring war-balloon altitudes during the Egyptian campaign. Commercial manufacture grew after E. Bourdon's 1849 patent of the metallic manometer.

Internally, an aneroid consists of a flat, circular metallic box with corrugated surfaces to increase elasticity. This partially exhausted chamber acts as a spring. A principal lever is attached to a projection on the chamber's surface; this lever, supported by a spiral spring to compensate for temperature changes, transmits motion through a second lever and a chain to a drum and hand. This mechanism multiplies the slight movement of the box 657 times, meaning a movement of 1/220th of an inch in the box moves the hand three inches on the dial.

### Other Instruments
The sympiezometer, invented in 1818 by Adie of Edinburgh, is a revived form of Hooke's marine barometer. It uses a glass tube containing air at the top and glycerin in the lower part and cistern. It includes a thermometer and sliding-scale to correct for temperature-induced pressure errors. While sensitive and useful for travel or sea use, it has been largely superseded by the more handy aneroid.

## Barographs and Registration
Barographs are barometers that automatically record pressure variations. Aneroid-based barographs use a lever attached to a collapsible chamber that ends in an ink-fed stylus, recording pressure on a moving ribbon. Mercurial barometers can be made self-registering using a lens to concentrate light on the top of the mercury column. The light passes through the vacuum above the mercury to a sheet of sensitized paper on a rotating drum, creating a continuous photograph of the column's height. To ensure accuracy, these registering barometers must be checked four to eight times daily against a mercury standard barometer.

## Standards of Measurement
In gasometry, measurements are reduced to a standard pressure. The common standard is a column of pure mercury 76 cm (29.922 in.) high at 0°C. Because gravity varies by location, pressure can be expressed in dynes. At Greenwich (where $g = 981.17$), the standard pressure at 0°C is 1,013,800 dynes; at Paris, it is 1,013,600 dynes. Some writers use a mega-dyne per square centimetre (one million dynes) as a standard for convenience and independence of locality.

It is important to note that atmospheric pressure is expressed by the perpendicular height of the mercury column, not by the weight of the mercury. For example, a reading of 30 inches means the pressure can sustain a column of that height, rather than referring to the weight of the mercury filling a square-inch section of the tube.
