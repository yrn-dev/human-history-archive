# Effect on Bankrupt

The legal status and obligations of a bankrupt individual involve a transition of property control to a trustee and the imposition of strict behavioral requirements to avoid criminal or civil penalties. These processes vary by jurisdiction, with specific regulations governing the English and Italian systems.

## Obligations and Criminal Liability in England
In the English system, a bankrupt is legally bound to assist the trustee in the administration of their estate. Failure to deliver any part of their property willfully constitutes contempt of court. Under the Debtors Act, a bankrupt faces criminal prosecution if they act with the intent to defraud. Specific prohibited actions include:
* Concealing or removing property valued at £10 or more.
* Failing to deliver books, documents, or property to the trustee.
* Knowingly allowing false debts to be proved on the estate without disclosure.
* Destroying, falsifying, or mutilating accounts and books.
* Attempting to explain property losses through fictitious means.
* Obtaining property on credit via false representation within four months prior to the bankruptcy petition.
* Disposing of unpaid property outside the ordinary course of trade or obtaining creditor assent to agreements through misrepresentation.

Additionally, under the act of 1883, it is a misdemeanour for an undischarged bankrupt to obtain credit exceeding £20 from any person without disclosing their status. The official receiver is tasked with reporting these facts to the court; if a reasonable probability of conviction exists, the court orders a prosecution conducted by the director of public prosecutions.

## Property and the Role of the Trustee
The trustee takes immediate possession of the bankrupt's property, including accounts, books, and deeds. The term "property" encompasses not only assets the bankrupt truly owns but also those in their possession or disposition for trade or business where they are the reputed owner. However, the doctrine of reputed ownership is restricted when trade customs, such as "hire and purchase" contracts, are proven.

The trustee's title to property relates back to the date of the first act of bankruptcy committed within three months before the petition. This allows the trustee to invalidate and recover payments or assignments made to creditors during that period if the creditor had knowledge of the act of bankruptcy. Similarly, any payment made to give a creditor preference over others within three months of the petition is void. 

Settlements of property are also subject to scrutiny:
* Settlements within two years are void unless made in good faith for valuable consideration or in consideration of marriage.
* Settlements within ten years are void unless the settlor was solvent at the date of settlement and interest passed to the trustees upon execution of the deed.

## Discharge and Recovery
A court may grant a bankrupt a discharge, either absolutely or under specific conditions, following a public examination. A discharge releases the debtor from most obligations, excluding debts incurred by fraud, debts due to the crown (unless the Treasury consents), and certain public obligations.

The court may refuse or suspend a discharge for at least two years, or qualify it by requiring payment from future earnings, if the bankrupt:
* Committed a bankruptcy-related criminal offence.
* Failed to keep proper books or traded after knowing they were insolvent.
* Contributed to bankruptcy through gambling, rash speculation, culpable neglect, or unjustifiable expenses.
* Contracted debts without a reasonable probability of payment.
* Provided an undue preference within three months of the receiving order.
* Increased liabilities to make assets equal to ten shillings in the pound.
* Failed to provide assets equal to ten shillings in the pound of unsecured liabilities (unless not responsible).

An English bankrupt may plead their discharge as a defence in a colonial court because the English act vests all their property—whether in the colonies or the United Kingdom—in the trustee.

## Italian Bankruptcy Law
Under the Commercial Code of 1883, bankruptcy in Italy is restricted to merchants, defined as those who engage in commercial business as a habitual profession, including merchant companies. Proceedings can be initiated by the debtor, a creditor for a commercial debt, or the court. Bankruptcy requires proof of insolvency; the amount of debt is immaterial.

Once adjudicated, the bankrupt loses the right to administer their affairs. A trustee, supervised by a judge and a commission of creditors, sells all movable and immovable property by auction for distribution. Bankruptcy may also be closed via an arrangement if creditors representing three-fourths of the indebtedness assent. Italian law does not recognize composition before bankruptcy. Bankrupts may face criminal proceedings for offences such as failing to keep books as prescribed by law.

## Compositions and Schemes
A debtor may submit a proposal for liquidation or a composition payment to the official receiver. If approved by a majority in number and three-fourths in value of creditors who proved their debts, and subsequently approved by the court, the scheme becomes binding. Such a proposal can be made before or after adjudication.

Approval of a scheme does not exempt the debtor from criminal liabilities or the requirement of a public examination. The court must be satisfied that the proposal is reasonable, benefits creditors, and that no criminal offences were committed. If the debtor defaults or the scheme is found to be unjust, the court may adjudicate the debtor bankrupt, voiding the scheme except for past transactions.
