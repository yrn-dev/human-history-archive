# The History and Implementation of the Ballot

The ballot, as a system of secret voting, has been adopted across various nations and contexts to manage elections and administrative processes. While its implementation has varied significantly by region and era, its primary objective has often been the reduction of undue influence and the protection of the voter.

## Development in Great Britain
In Great Britain, the concept of the ballot was suggested for parliamentary use during the time of Charles II. It was utilized by the Scots parliament in 1662 regarding the Billeting Act; in this instance, members wrote names of suspected persons in a disguised hand on papers, which were placed in a sealed bag, opened in the exchequer chamber, and then burned to conceal the names of those ostracized. In 1705, Fletcher of Saltoun proposed secret voting to protect members from the influence of the court.

Demand for the ballot in parliamentary elections grew during the early 19th century. The Benthamites advocated for the system in 1817, and banners requesting the ballot appeared at the Peterloo Massacre in 1819. Despite ridicule from Sydney Smith—who referred to it as a "mouse-trap" and described voters in sedan-chairs with closely-drawn curtains—support for the ballot increased in the House of Commons between 1833 and 1839.

The Ballot Act, introduced by W. E. Forster in 1872, established the ballot for municipal and parliamentary elections, excluding university parliamentary elections. This act abolished public nominations of candidates and addressed election expenses and the offence of personation. The act also created several new offences, including the forging, fraudulent defacing, or destruction of papers and official marks, and the unauthorized removal of papers from a station. Officers and clerks committing these crimes faced up to two years of imprisonment, with or without hard labour, while others faced six months. While some feared "the confessional," it was noted that Catholic penitents are not bound to confess their vote, and confessors committing the crime of divulging such a vote would be liable.

## Implementation in the United States
Early American elections utilized *viva voce* voting, though several colonies adopted written or printed ballots early on. By 1775, ballots were used in Pennsylvania, Delaware, North Carolina, South Carolina, and the New England states. New Jersey followed in 1776 and New York in 1778. By the time of the U.S. Constitution's adoption, only Georgia, Virginia, and Maryland still used *viva voce* voting. Other states transitioned later: Maryland, Georgia, and Illinois (1848) adopted the ballot in the first half of the 19th century, and Arkansas did so in 1846. In Missouri, general adoption occurred in 1863, and Virginia provided for the ballot in 1869.

Initially, ballots were written by voters or provided by candidates and political committees. Because voters received these tickets within a few feet of the polls, secrecy was nearly impossible, making bribery and intimidation frequent.

Reform movements gained momentum after the 1888 presidential election, where allegations of large-scale vote purchasing created deep alarm. Massachusetts enacted the first complete law in 1888, providing state-printed alphabetical ballots. This "Massachusetts plan" was adopted by Washington, Wyoming, Mississippi, and Vermont (though Vermont later changed its system). In 1891, seventeen states and two territories adopted the Australian ballot system. This included the "blanket ballot," though arrangements differed: some used party-groups (such as Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Colorado), while others used the Massachusetts arrangement (such as Texas, Kentucky, and Nebraska). California added a list of party names to allow a single mark to cast a vote for all candidates of a party. Later adoptions of the Australian system included Iowa (1892), Alabama and Kansas (1893), Virginia (1894), Florida (1895), and Louisiana and Utah (1896). New York adopted the blanket ballot in 1895. Missouri is noted as the only state to abandon the blanket ballot after adoption, returning to separate ballots in 1897 without secret booths.

## European Applications
In Prussia, the ballot was introduced for municipal assembly elections in 1808 via Stein's *Staedteordnung*. Under the German constitution of 1867 and the constitution of January 1, 1871, Reichstag elections were conducted via universal suffrage under the ballot, following the electoral law of May 31, 1869.

Hungary's history with the ballot was distinct. Secret voting was unknown there before 1848. The electoral law of 1848 left regulations to town and county councils, few of which used the ballot. The primitive method involved candidates having large, color-coded boxes; voters entered alone and placed a rod (4 to 6 feet long to prevent concealment of unofficial rods) through a slit in the lid. By 1874, the ballot was abolished for parliamentary elections but made obligatory for county and town council elections.

## Other Uses of the Ballot
The term "ballot" was also applied to the allocation of advances in building societies. A "vicious system" of trafficking in these ballot-awarded advances, described as being akin to gambling, was one of several signs of weakness in these societies. This contributed to a broader crisis and the failure of the Liberator, a large society incorporated under the act of 1874.
