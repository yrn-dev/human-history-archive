# Sindhia and the Bombay Presidency

## Territorial Relations and the Lease of 1853
The relationship between the British administration in India and Sindhia involved various territorial transfers and agreements over several decades. In 1782, the treaty of Salbai resulted in the ceding of Salsette to the British, while Broach was handed over to Sindhia. Later, in 1783, the British ceded Broach to Sindhia in recognition of specific services. However, by 1803, Broach was stormed by a detachment under Colonel Woodington and was eventually ceded to the East India Company by Sindhia under the treaty of Sarji Anjangaom.

Further territorial interactions occurred in Burhanpur. The town was transferred to Sindhia by the peshwa in 1778. Although the army of General Wellesley took Burhanpur in 1803 during the Mahratta War, a treaty that same year restored it to Sindhia. Burhanpur remained part of Sindhia's dominions until 1860-1861, when territorial arrangements led to the town and its surrounding estates being ceded to the British government. In 1853, the Bombay presidency expanded further through the lease of the Panch Mahals from Sindhia.

## Military Development under Mahadji Sindhia
During the late 18th century, Mahadji Sindhia sought to modernize his military forces. In 1784, he was joined by Benoit de Boigne, a French military adventurer, who was tasked with training Sindhia's troops in European methods of warfare. Boigne's battalions proved effective at the battles of Lalsot and Chaksana, maintaining the field after the rest of the Mahratta army had been defeated by the Rajputs.

In 1788, Boigne restored the fortunes of the Mahrattas at the battle of Agra, which established Mahadji Sindhia as the undisputed master of Hindostan. Following this success, Boigne commanded a brigade of ten infantry battalions, winning victories at Merta and Patan in 1790. He was subsequently appointed commander-in-chief of Sindhia's army and defeated Holkar's army at the battle of Lakhairi in 1793. After the death of Mahadji Sindhia in 1794, Boigne remained loyal to Daulat Rao Sindhia until his resignation in 1795 due to failing health.

## Administration of the Bombay Presidency
The framework of the Bombay presidency evolved significantly between 1803 and 1827. By 1803, the presidency consisted of Surat, Bankot, Salsette, and the harbour islands. It expanded to include the Gujarat districts (taken over in 1805 and enlarged in 1818) and saw settlement measures in Mahi Kantha and Kathiawar between 1807 and 1820. Following the defeat and capture of Baji Rao, the last of the peshwas, in 1817-1818, large portions of his dominions—including Nasik, Poona, Belgaum, Sholapur, Ahmednagar, Dharwar, and Kaladgi—were incorporated into the presidency.

The settlement of these areas was completed by Mountstuart Elphinstone, who served as governor from 1819 to 1827. Elphinstone's administrative policy focused on ruling according to native lines and avoiding changes for which the population was not yet prepared. Despite this, he codified laws, established schools and courts, and stopped the grosser abuses of the previous regime to pacify the country.

## Impact of Orderly Administration
The subsequent establishment of an orderly administration brought mixed results for the population. One specific outcome was a general fall in prices. This economic shift made the newly introduced regularity of tax collection "doubly unwelcome," leading to feelings of resentment and misgiving among the people.

Despite these tensions, the general population remained prosperous and contented. Under the leadership of Lord Elphinstone (1853-1860), the presidency navigated the crisis of the Mutiny without a general rising. While there were local and isolated rebellions in the southern Mahratta country, among the Bhils, and in Gujarat, as well as outbreaks among troops in Kolhapur, Ahmedabad, and Karachi (which resulted in the disbanding of two regiments), these were quickly suppressed.

Agricultural prosperity later peaked under Sir Bartle Frere (1862-1867). This was driven by the American Civil War, which created an enormous demand for Indian cotton in Europe and led to an epidemic of speculation due to the influx of money.
