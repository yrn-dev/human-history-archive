# The Habits and Instincts of Animals with reference to Natural Theology

## Overview of Natural Theology and Animal Study
The study of animal habits and instincts has been a subject of scientific and theological inquiry, notably within the framework of Natural Theology. A prominent example of this is found in the *Bridgewater Treatises*, a series of works published between 1833 and 1840 (and later in Bohn's Scientific Library) designed to explore the adaptation of nature to various conditions of man and the evidence of design in the natural world. One specific volume in this series, titled *The Habits and Instincts of Animals with reference to Natural Theology*, was authored by William Kirby.

## William Kirby and Entomological Research
William Kirby (1759–1850) was an English entomologist born in Witnesham, Suffolk. Educated at Ipswich grammar school and Caius College, Cambridge, where he graduated in 1781, Kirby took holy orders in 1782 and spent his life in a country parsonage at Barham. While his primary passion was natural history, he eventually focused his leisure on entomology. 

Kirby gained recognition among international entomologists with his 1802 work, *Monographia Apum Angliae*. He later co-authored the *Introduction to Entomology* (published in four volumes between 1815 and 1826) with William Spence of Hull, which became one of the most popular scientific books ever published. In 1830, Kirby was selected to contribute to the *Bridgewater Treatises*, producing *The History, Habits, and Instincts of Animals* in 1835, though this work is noted to have fallen short of his earlier scientific achievements.

## Concepts of Instinct and Behavior
Instinct is described as a sure and simple operation compared to the complexities of reason. In the study of behavior, it is observed that the appetites of a quadruped are easier to ascertain than the speculations of a philosopher. Among humans, those in "savage tribes" who exist closer to the condition of animals maintain a stronger resemblance to one another due to the uniform stability of their manners, which results from the imperfection of their faculties.

In the broader context of animated life, the natural instincts of both animals and humans are identified as self-interest and self-preservation. Furthermore, animals are noted to possess a "natural love" and instinctive affection. In humans, the instinct to imitate is present from infancy, a trait shared with other animals who express feelings and ideas through sound and gesture.

## Mammalian Habits and Social Association
Mammals are characterized as family animals, a trait that introduces a continuity of experience. Unlike the "closed-in life" of a lizard, mammals possess a disposition for protecting their young and are viviparous. This allows offspring to "pick up" experience from the mother and "hand on" to their own offspring. 

This capacity for pre-adult dependence and imitation is found across various species, including the dog, man, rhinoceros, and hyæna. This biological framework allows for the addition of "tradition" to hard instinct, facilitating social association.

## Philosophical Perspectives on Animal Nature
Different philosophical schools have interpreted animal instincts differently:
*   **The Automaton Theory:** René Descartes asserted that animals are automata. In this view, animals are not merely governed by sensation and instinct, but are mechanical in the same sense as a watch, lacking mind or self-originated energy.
*   **The Evolutionary Perspective:** Zoologists such as Edward Drinker Cope (1840–1897) applied the doctrine of evolution to the classification of vertebrates. Cope was noted for having a particular "instinct" for perceiving the relationships between different animals, which aided his work on fishes, reptiles, and mammals.
