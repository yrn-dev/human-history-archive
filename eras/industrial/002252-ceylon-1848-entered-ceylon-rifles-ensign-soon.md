# Ceylon

Ceylon is an island known by several names throughout history, including Taprobana and Serendib. Its geography and history have been recorded by various civilizations, from the Romans and Chinese to the British.

## Ancient History and Early Contact
The Roman knowledge of the lands east of Cape Comorin was imperfect, and geographers, including Ptolemy, magnified the size of Ceylon by more than fifteen times, extending it as far as the equator and the vicinity of China. During the reign of Claudius, a freedman who farmed Red Sea customs was driven by winds to the coast of Ceylon. After spending six months with the natives, the king of Ceylon was persuaded to send an embassy to the Roman emperor. However, some authorities, such as M. Letronne, suggest this freedman and the subsequent embassy may have been an impostor.

In the East, Chinese annals record that during the reign of Yung Lo (who took the throne in 1403), missions led by eunuchs were dispatched to various regions, including Ceylon. A tablet unearthed at Galle in 1911 supports these records; the tablet contains inscriptions in Tamil, Arabic, and Chinese. The Chinese text indicates the tablet was erected in 1409 to commemorate a visit by the eunuch Cheng Ho, who traveled the route multiple times.

## Religion and Chronology
Buddhism, founded by Gautama in the sixth century B.C., spread to Ceylon along with other regions such as China, Japan, Tibet, and Burmah. The "Ceylon Chronicles" provide specific chronological data used by scholars to date the Buddha's life. These chronicles state that 218 years passed between the death of the Buddha and the accession of Asoka to the throne. While European scholars often calculate the Buddha's birth as 568 B.C. and death as 488 B.C. based on Asoka's accession around 270 B.C., the dates accepted in Ceylon, Siam, and Burma are approximately half a century earlier, placing the birth at 623 B.C. and death at 543 B.C.

## Natural History and Flora
The flora of Ceylon shows a distinct connection to the peninsula of India and eastern tropical Africa. This is evidenced by the identity of various families and genera and a similarity in mountain flora. Notably, both Ceylon and the Indian peninsula lack conifers, cycads, and oaks, even in mountain elevations where such plants are abundant to the north and east. The genus *Nepenthes* is found in Ceylon and the Malayan area, though it is absent from the Himalaya and the Indian peninsula.

The island's fauna includes the bandicoot-rat (*Nesocia bandicota*), a large rat measuring 12 to 15 inches to the root of the tail, with a tail measuring 11 to 13 inches. The name is derived from the Telegu *pandi-koku*.

## British Administration and Military Presence
Sir Edward Barnes served as the governor of Ceylon from 1824 to 1831. During his tenure, he conducted the first census of the population and introduced coffee cultivation using the West Indian system in 1824. He also oversaw the construction of several lines of communication, most notably the great military road connecting Kandy and Colombo. A memorial statue of Barnes was erected in Colombo, and his portrait was painted for the island by John Wood.

The island also served as a location for British military personnel and artists. Valentine Baker, a British soldier, was educated in Ceylon and entered the Ceylon Rifles as an ensign in 1848. Additionally, the artist Samuel Daniell traveled to Ceylon in 1806, spending the remainder of his life there and publishing a work titled *The Scenery, Animals and Natives of Ceylon*.

## Demographics
The population of Ceylon is primarily composed of two major races: the Sinhalese, numbering 2,000,000, and the Tamil, numbering 954,000. Other groups inhabiting the island include Malays, Arabs, Chinese, and Eurasians. In a broader colonial context, the archive records a "high type, brown and mixed" population for Ceylon totaling 3,568,824.
