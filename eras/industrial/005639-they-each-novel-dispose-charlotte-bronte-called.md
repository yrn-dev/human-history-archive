# The Brontë Sisters

The Brontë sisters—Charlotte (1816–1855), Emily (1818–1848), and Anne (1820–1849)—were English novelists and poets who attained significant literary distinction. They were three of the six children of Patrick Brontë, a Church of England clergyman who served as the perpetual incumbent of the parish of Haworth in the West Riding of Yorkshire for the final forty-one years of his life.

## Early Life and Education
Patrick Brontë, born in Ireland in 1777, married Maria Branwell, a Cornishwoman, in 1812. Their first two daughters, Maria and Elizabeth, died in 1825. Charlotte, Emily, and Anne, along with their brother Patrick Branwell, were raised at Haworth. In 1824, Charlotte and Emily were sent to the Clergy Daughters' school at Cowan Bridge. While the school was characterized by drastic discipline and bad food, Charlotte and Emily returned home in 1825. During their youth, the four surviving children engaged in precocious literary aspirations, creating tiny booklets of essays, verses, and stories.

## Early Literary Efforts
The sisters' first professional venture was a volume of verse published in 1846 by Aylott & Jones, titled *Poems, by Currer, Ellis and Acton Bell*. These pseudonyms were used to disguise the identities of Charlotte, Emily, and Anne. The collection contained nineteen poems by Charlotte and twenty-one each by Emily and Anne. Despite the effort, only two copies were sold. While the venture was largely ignored at the time, later consensus suggests that Emily's verse revealed a true poetic genius, a fact recognized then only by Charlotte.

## The Novels of Charlotte Brontë
Following the failure of their poetry, the sisters sought to publish novels. Charlotte's first manuscript was titled *The Master*, which she later retitled *The Professor*. This work was sent to the firm Smith, Elder & Co., where the reader R. Smith Williams and his employers were impressed. However, in August 1847, Charlotte was informed that the novel was too short for the then-imperative three-volume form and lacked "varied interest." 

In response, Charlotte submitted a longer novel, *Jane Eyre*, which was published in October 1847 to wild acclaim. In this novel, Charlotte used the thin disguise of "Lowood" to critique the Cowan Bridge school, with the principal, Rev. William Carus Wilson, serving as the counterpart to the character Mr. Naomi Brocklehurst. Charlotte later wrote *Shirley*, published in October 1849, and *Villette*, published in January 1853. *Jane Eyre* and *Villette* are noted for their independence, intensity, and rough individuality.

## The Works of Emily and Anne Brontë
Emily and Anne's novels were accepted by T. C. Newby and published together in three volumes in December 1847. Emily's *Wuthering Heights* is described as a monument of intensity and a "thing apart," characterized by grey melancholy and grimness. Emily is regarded as great both as a novelist and a poet, with her poems "Old Stoic" and "Last Lines" cited as some of the finest achievements of poetry by any woman in English literature.

Anne Brontë wrote *Agnes Gray* and *The Tenant of Wildfell Hall*, the latter of which was published by Newby in June 1848. While her novels are constantly reprinted, they are noted to survive principally through the vitality of the Brontë tradition. Additionally, Anne is remembered as a hymn writer.

## Family Tragedy and Later Years
The sisters' brother, Patrick Branwell, struggled with artistic failure and became a confirmed drunkard. Despite early promise at the Royal Academy in 1835, he spent his later years in "painful sottishness" and died on September 24, 1848. Charlotte noted that Branwell never knew his sisters had published any of their work.

The family suffered a series of rapid losses in 1848 and 1849. Emily died at Haworth on December 19, 1848. Anne became ill and died in Scarborough on May 28, 1849. In exactly eight months, Charlotte lost all three of her youth companions.

In 1854, Charlotte married Arthur Bell Nicholls, her father's curate, on June 29. The couple made their home with Mr. Brontë at Haworth. Charlotte died in 1855.

## Biographical Legacy
The life of the Brontës has been documented by several authors. Mrs. Gaskell published *The Life of Charlotte Brontë* in 1857, though the third edition was revised due to controversies regarding the identity of Lowood and the behavior of Branwell Brontë. Other significant biographical contributions include works by T. Wemyss Reid, Augustine Birrell, William Wright, and Clement Shorter, the latter of whom published *The Brontës: Life and Letters* in 1907, containing Charlotte's letters in chronological order.
