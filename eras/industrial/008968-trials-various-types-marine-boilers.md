# TRIALS OF VARIOUS TYPES OF MARINE BOILERS

A boiler is a vessel designed to heat water or another liquid to its boiling point. Specifically, it is the apparatus used to produce steam from water, serving as a step in the process of converting the potential energy of fuel, such as coal, into mechanical work via a steam engine. To function safely and effectively, all boilers must include a furnace for burning fuel, a receptacle strong enough to withstand the highest intended steam pressure, and sufficient heating surfaces to transmit heat from combustion to the water.

## Classes of Boilers
Boilers are generally categorized into two distinct classes, though some designs combine properties of both.

**Tank Boilers**
Tank boilers contain relatively large quantities of water and typically feature a wholly or partially cylindrical outer shell, a shape that provides necessary strength without requiring a large number of stays. These are generally internally fired, with furnace plates surrounded by water to create the most efficient heating surfaces. After leaving the furnace, combustion products pass into a chamber and then through flues or numerous small tubes to transmit heat to the water.

**Water-Tube Boilers**
In water-tube boilers, the water is mainly contained within numerous comparatively small tubes, which form the major portion of the heating surface. The fire is usually placed beneath these tubes, and both are enclosed in a casing made of brickwork or other fire-resisting materials. In certain designs, the fire is entirely surrounded by water-tubes, ensuring the casing is not exposed to direct fire. Because of this design, special provisions must be made to maintain the circulation of water through the tubes.

## Materials and Construction
The materials used in boiler construction vary by component and application:

*   **Plates and Stays:** In marine and ordinary land boilers, these are almost invariably mild steel. Shell plates and long stays typically have a tensile strength of 28 to 32 tons per sq. in. For flanged plates, furnaces, flues, and short-screwed stays, a softer steel with a strength of 26 to 30 tons per sq. in. is used.
*   **Tubes:** Ordinary marine and land boilers generally use lap-welded wrought iron tubes. However, seamless steel tubes are invariably used in water-tube boilers intended for naval purposes.
*   **Locomotive Boilers:** These typically utilize mild steel shells and copper fire-box plates (though steel is used in America). Fire-box side stays are made of copper or special bronze, while other stays are steel. Tubes are usually brass (composed of either two parts copper to one part zinc, or 70% copper and 30% zinc), though steel or copper tubes are occasionally employed. Seamless steel is used for water tubes.

## Marine Boiler Designs and Variations
The cylindrical multitubular return tube boiler is used almost universally in merchant steamers. These boilers can reach 17 ft. in diameter and usually operate at pressures between 160 and 200 lb per sq. in., though some reach 265 lb per sq. in.

These boilers are available in two types:
1.  **Single-ended:** The most common type, where furnaces (ranging from one to four) are fitted at one end. Three furnaces are the standard practice.
2.  **Double-ended:** Furnaces are fitted at both ends.

Furnaces may be plain cylindrical (with plate thickness from 3/8 in. to 3/4 in.), utilize "Adamson" joints, or be corrugated or ribbed (such as the Morison, Fox, and Deighton types) to better resist collapse.

Regarding the connection between furnaces and combustion chambers, two methods exist. One involves flanging the furnace at the crown for riveting to the tube plate and riveting the lower part to the combustion chamber side plate. The other, the "Gourlay back end," contracts the furnace end into an oval conical form and flanges it outwards. The Gourlay design allows a damaged furnace to be replaced without disturbing the end plates, although it is less stiff and requires more longitudinal stays.

Other specialized designs include the Thornycroft-Schulz boiler, used extensively in the German and British navies. This type features four chambers and a fire-grate in two separate portions, utilizing water-walls and large-diameter outer circulating pipes to manage water and steam.

## Performance and Thermal Storage
The efficiency of boilers is often published by makers, though these results are frequently obtained under favorable circumstances rather than ordinary working conditions. To obtain more accurate data, the British admiralty conducted trials using skilled observers to measure feed-water and weigh all coal. These trials utilized excellent quality South Welsh coal to ensure comparability across different boiler types.

In applications where work is intermittent, "thermal storage" may be used. This involves placing a large cylindrical storage vessel above the boiler. Feed-water is delivered into the upper part of the vessel and runs over extended surfaces exposed to steam, raising its temperature to the evaporation point. During periods of low steam demand, excess hot water accumulates in the chamber; when maximum output is required, the feed-pump is stopped and the boiler is fed with this stored hot water. This system also allows impurities in the feed-water to deposit in the chamber, where they can be easily removed.
