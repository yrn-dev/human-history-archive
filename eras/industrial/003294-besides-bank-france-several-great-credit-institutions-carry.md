# Credit Institutions and Banking in France

The banking system in France is characterized by the central dominance of the Bank of France, supported by several large joint-stock credit institutions and a wide network of provincial banks.

## The Bank of France
The Bank of France is the most powerful financial institution in the country. It was founded in 1800 by Napoleon I, who was assisted by his minister, Mollien. The institution was established with the goals of supplying loanable capital at a moderate charge and supporting French industry and trade. Since its inception, it has performed these functions with great judgment and vigour, utilizing a system of branches and attached towns across the nation.

Historically, the Bank of France was not the first attempt at a bank of issue. In 1716, John Law, the author of the *Systeme* and the *Mississippi Scheme*, established the *Banque Generale*. This was converted into the *Banque Royale* in 1718 with notes guaranteed by King Louis XV, but it ended in 1721. A subsequent attempt at reconstruction in 1767 was suppressed in 1793. While other banks operated with limited success afterward, they lacked real power. 

The Bank of France eventually secured an exclusive privilege of issue. In 1848, it absorbed nine departmental banks that had been formed under an 1803 law. Later, in 1863, it took over the Bank of Savoy following the province's union with France. Today, the bank maintains a very large reserve, which allows it to keep a steady rate of discount even when precious metals move due to natural causes. This stability is aided by the fact that French trade activity and enterprise are less intense than those found in Germany or the United Kingdom, though the reserve is held in part against a gigantic note issue.

## Provincial Banking
France also possesses a large number of banks, primarily located in the provinces, which conduct a considerable amount of business with discretion and prudence. In 1898, 182 of these firms were members of the French Country Bankers' Association. These banks operate in 66 of the 86 departments of France. 

The organizational structure of these provincial banks often follows the English method of utilizing small branches in nearby small towns. For example, one such bank possesses 18 offices, including its head office. The French Country Bankers' Association holds its meetings in Paris to discuss matters of interest to the banking profession.

## Classification and Regional Presence
In the broader context of European banking since the 16th century, institutions have been divided into "exchange banks" (which dealt with foreign exchange and trade) and "banks of deposits" (which receive deposits and make loans to associate with general industry). The Bank of France is categorized as a bank of deposit.

The reach of the central banking system is evident in major cities; for instance, Bordeaux serves as a site for a branch of the Bank of France, alongside its other roles as a seat of an archbishop and a center for various judicial and educational institutions.
