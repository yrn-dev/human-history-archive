# The State Banks and the Bank of the United States

The banking landscape of the early United States was characterized by a tension between national institutions and banks chartered by individual states. While the Bank of the United States served as a central national bank of issue, it faced powerful rivals in the form of state-chartered banks, which eventually became its successors.

## The First Bank of the United States
Incorporated in 1791 as part of Alexander Hamilton's system for organizing Federal government finances, the first Bank of the United States began with a capital of $10,000,000, divided into 25,000 shares of $400 each. The institution discounted commercial paper, issued circulating notes, and supported the government's financial operations. The government initially subscribed to one-fifth of the capital, though this was handled via a process that effectively functioned as a loan from the bank to the treasury. By the end of 1795, other loans to the government brought the total obligation to $6,200,000. The government eventually sold its stock, completing the disposal by 1802 for a profit of $671,860.

A statement submitted to congress by Gallatin on January 24, 1811, indicated the bank had resources totaling $24,183,046. This included $5,009,567 in specie, $2,750,000 in United States stock, and $14,578,294 in discounts and loans.

## The Rise of State Banks and the Banking Mania
The first bank's charter expired in 1811 following a party contest. Opponents argued that congress lacked the constitutional power to create such an institution and raised concerns regarding foreign ownership, despite the fact that directors were required to be U.S. citizens and foreign shareholders could not vote by proxy.

The expiration of the charter triggered a "banking mania." Between 1811 and 1815, the number of banks grew from 88 to 208. In Pennsylvania, 37 of 41 newly authorized banks were operational by 1814. However, the available specie was insufficient to support the resulting credit, and much of the country's metallic reserves drifted toward New England. This led to the collapse of several banks in 1814 and a prolonged period of business prostration.

During the War of 1812, the government was forced to rely on state banks. In 1814, these banks suspended specie payments, which hindered the treasury's ability to transfer funds across the Union because notes from one section did not circulate easily in another. Gallatin opined that this suspension might have been avoided if the first Bank of the United States had still existed.

## The Second Bank of the United States
Due to the government's deteriorating financial condition during the war, the second Bank of the United States was authorized in April 1816, based on a project by Treasury Secretary Alexander J. Dallas. This new bank had a capital of $35,000,000, with the government owning one-fifth of the stock via a stock note. The president of the United States appointed five of the twenty-five directors.

The legality of the bank's charter was challenged in the 1819 Supreme Court case *McCulloch v. Maryland*. Chief Justice Marshall ruled that creating the bank fell within the implied powers of the Federal constitution and that states could not levy taxes on the bank's property or circulating notes, except in common with other property.

## Decline and Liquidation
By 1835, the bank had deposits of $5,061,456, loans of $59,232,445, and a circulation of $23,075,422. However, the institution was destroyed by the enmity of President Jackson, who in 1833 suspended the deposit of public money in the bank's custody. This "removal of the deposits" caused a bitter political controversy led by Webster and Clay in opposition, though Jackson maintained public support.

The Federal charter expired in 1836. President Nicholas Biddle obtained a charter from the state of Pennsylvania to continue business, but the effort was unsuccessful, and the bank went into liquidation in 1841.

## Characteristics of State Banking Systems
State-chartered banks acted as rivals to the national bank and as its successors. In the early, undeveloped days of the country, banking was often speculative and unsound. The most successful systems were located in New England and New York, where the country's surplus capital was primarily concentrated. Conversely, the least successful systems were found in poorer and newer sections of the country. In these regions, inexperience and poverty made it difficult to set aside capital for investment in tools of exchange, causing the systems to grow progressively worse.

Later, alongside the national banking system, state banks, trust companies, and private banks continued to develop. While they lacked the privilege of note-issue—a privilege exclusive to national banks—they obtained other privileges that were sometimes greater than those of the national banks.

## Other Early Institutions
The Bank of North America, authorized by congress on May 26, 1781, was another outgrowth of continental government needs. Robert Morris was empowered to create the bank with an initial capital of $400,000. Because of doubts regarding the continental congress's power to charter a bank, a charter was also obtained from the state of Pennsylvania. The bank began business on January 7, 1782, and helped restore order to Federation finances, including loaning $1,249,975 to Morris as the government superintendent of finance. The Bank of North America operated until it was absorbed into the national banking system in 1863.
