# The British East India Company and the Dutch East India Company

The struggle for supremacy in Southern Asia and the East Indies was characterized by the competition between European maritime powers, primarily the English and the Dutch. Both nations entered the region as commercial companies, combining trade with territorial acquisition to secure European interests through the presence of settlers and merchants.

## Early Competition and Settlements
The British East India Company was organized by London merchants who were stimulated by a spirit of rivalry with the Dutch. This competition extended to the Far East; when the English learned that the Dutch intended to establish a trading station in Japan, the Company dispatched the ship *Clove*, commanded by Saris, which reached Hirado on June 11, 1613.

In India, the first English settlement in the Bombay presidency was established in 1618 with a factory at Surat, protected by a charter from Emperor Jahangir. The rivalry with the Dutch was evident in early attempts to secure territory; in 1626, the English and Dutch made an unsuccessful joint attempt to gain possession of the island of Bombay.

## The Acquisition and Defense of Bombay
Bombay was ceded to the English crown in 1661 as part of the dower of the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II. Due to the unsuccessful administration of crown officers and a low estimation of the acquisition in England, the crown transferred Bombay to the East India Company in 1668 for an annual payment of £10. 

Along with the transfer, the Company was granted powers for the administration of justice and defense. The Company enrolled a European regiment and erected fortifications, which proved successful in deterring an intended attack by the Dutch in 1673. Bombay later became the head of all Company possessions in India in 1687, though it became subordinate to Calcutta in 1753.

## Expansion and Supremacy
While the Dutch East India Company remained a significant power, the British East India Company demonstrated a unique spirit of expansion. By the mid-18th century, the British trading company had become completely dominant over the Portuguese, Dutch, and French in the Indian peninsula. This ascent was facilitated by the decay of the great Mongol Empire established by Baber and Akbar.

The ultimate victory of England is attributed to a superior command of the seas and the influence of European politics rather than a specific aptitude for oriental problems. By 1815, the East India Company ruled approximately one-third of the Indian peninsula. Its power continued to spread into regions such as Oudh, Sind, and Assam.

## Interactions with Other European Powers
The English and Dutch occasionally acted in concert to eliminate other European rivals. In 1733, both the English and Dutch stirred up a Mahommedan general at Hugli to attack Bankipur, the principal settlement of the German Ostend Company. This action contributed to the disappearance of German interests in India.

By the end of the Napoleonic wars, the distribution of power in the East had shifted. While the British dominated much of India, Holland retained Java, Sumatra, and other islands.

## Transition to Crown Rule
The East India Company operated as a quasi-independent state, levying revenues, conducting wars, and sending ambassadors to Asiatic powers. However, following a serious mutiny of native troops in 1859, the Company's empire was annexed to the British Crown. Under the *Act for the Better Government of India*, the Governor-General was replaced by a Viceroy representing the Sovereign, and a Secretary of State for India, responsible to the British Parliament, took the place of the Company.
