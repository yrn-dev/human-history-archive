# Bolton

Bolton, also known as Bolton-le-Moors, is a municipal, county, and parliamentary borough located in Lancashire, England. It is situated 11 miles northwest of Manchester and 196 miles north-northwest by north from London. The borough covers an area of 15,279 acres, with a recorded population of 146,487 in 1891 and 168,215 in 1901.

## Geography and Layout
The town is divided into Great Bolton and Little Bolton by the Croal, which is a small tributary of the Irwell. As indicated by its full name, the area is surrounded by high moorland. To the northwest, the moors include Rivington Pike, which reaches an elevation of 1,192 feet.

## History and Governance
The earliest recorded forms of the town's name include Botheltun and Bodleton, with later variations such as Boltune, Bowelton, Bodeltown, Bolton-super-Moras, Bolton-in-ye-Moors, and Botheltun-le-Moors. 

The manor was originally granted by William I to Roger de Poictou. It subsequently passed through the Pilkington and Ferrers families before being held by the Harringtons of Hornby Castle. Due to their adherence to Richard III, the Harringtons lost the manor, and in 1485, Henry VII granted it to the first earl of Derby. While the manor is currently held by various lords, the earls of Derby still retain a fourth part. The manor of Little Bolton was distinct from that of Great Bolton at least since the reign of Henry III and was held by the Boltons or Botheltons until the 17th century.

The parliamentary borough was created in 1832 and returns two members.

## Industry and Economy
Bolton was famous for woollen manufactures from its early days. During the reign of Richard I, an aulneger was appointed to stamp and measure woollen goods. The industry grew further in 1337 following the settlement of Flemish clothiers. By 1566, an act of parliament appointed deputies to assist aulnegers in Bolton. While Leland recorded in 1558 that the town made "cottons," these were actually woollen goods. 

True cotton manufacture began in Lancashire in 1641, at which time Bolton was the chief seat for the production of dimities, vermilions, and fustians. The industry was later stimulated by the arrival of French refugees following the revocation of the edict of Nantes. Notable milestones in textile production include the first manufacture of velvets around 1756 by Jeremiah Clarke, and the production of cotton quiltings and muslins in 1763.

The cotton trade expanded rapidly due to the inventions of Samuel Crompton (1780) and Sir Richard Arkwright (1770), both of whom were born in the parish. The first spinning factory in Bolton was established in 1780; by 1851, the town had 66 cotton mills operating 860,000 throstle spindles. The damp climate particularly assists the town's specialty of fine spinning. Other significant industries include the bleaching, dyeing, and printing of calico and cotton, as well as iron and steel works, collieries, and the making of weaving and spinning machinery. Bleaching has been practiced since the early 18th century. In 1791, a canal was built connecting Bolton to Manchester, and Bolton Moor was enclosed by an act of parliament in 1792.

## Infrastructure and Public Institutions
The town's appearance is described as wholly modern, despite its early origins. Transportation is served by the Lancashire & Yorkshire and London & North-Western railways, with the Midland railway holding running powers.

Public and cultural institutions include:
* **Education:** Municipal technical schools and Lever's grammar school (founded 1641), which formerly employed John Lempriere and Robert Ainsworth as masters.
* **Civic Buildings:** A market hall, an exchange, assembly rooms, a town hall featuring a tower and classical facade, and several free libraries.
* **Culture:** Museums of art and industry, as well as a museum of natural history.
* **Parks:** A large public park opened in 1866, created as relief work for unemployed operatives during the cotton famine. Another public park is located on the northwestern moors.
* **Religion:** A variety of places of worship exist, including the church of St Peter. While a parish church existed on that site in the 12th century or earlier, the current building dates from 1870.

The only remaining structures of antiquity are two 16th-century houses in Little Bolton, one of which is noted as a high-quality example of Tudor architecture.
