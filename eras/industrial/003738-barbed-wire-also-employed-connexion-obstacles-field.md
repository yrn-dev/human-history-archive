# Barbed Wire

Barbed wire is a fencing material composed of steel wires with spikes or jagged projections. Originally developed to meet the needs of the cattle-raising industry in the western United States, where timber was scarce, it revolutionized the manufacture of wire and became a global industry.

## History and Development
Prior to the invention of barbed wire, the frontier of the United States and South America utilized No. 9 round or oval iron wire. However, iron lacked homogeneity and tensile strength. Single strands of iron wire frequently broke due to contraction in cold weather and expansion in heat, and cattle often broke the wire and loosened posts by rubbing against the smooth surface.

The development of the industry was made possible by the introduction of Bessemer and open-hearth steels. Early patents were filed in 1867 by Lucien B. Smith of Kent, Ohio, and William B. Hunt of Scott, New York, followed by Michael Kelly of New York a year later. The industry's practical foundation was established in 1874 through patents issued to Joseph F. Glidden of De Kalb, Illinois, for barbed fence wire, and a joint patent with Phineas W. Vaughan for a machine to manufacture it.

## Manufacture and Technical Properties
Barbed wire is produced from soft Siemens-Martin or Bessemer steel. The manufacturing process involves drawing the wire in a mill and galvanizing it through a continuous process. Coils of wire are passed through an annealing medium (heated fire-brick tubes or red-hot lead) to soften them, then through a muriatic acid bath to remove scale, and finally through a bath of spelter heated slightly above its melting point. This galvanization protects the wire from atmospheric corrosion and provides a bright finish. Alternatively, some wire is painted by submerging finished spools in paint and drying them.

The wire is formed by automatic machinery that twists two longitudinal strand wires together. For 2-point or 4-point Glidden wire, cross wires are fed into the machine at right angles to the strand wires, cut diagonally to form sharp points, and wrapped firmly around the strands. A machine can produce between 2,000 and 3,000 lb of wire in a ten-hour day.

The use of two twisted strands provides an automatic adjustment to temperature changes; heat loosens the twist and cold tightens it without causing sag or altering the relative lengths of the wires.

## Installation and Use
Barbed wire is typically shipped on wooden spools holding approximately 100 lb or 80 to 100 rods. It is stretched into place using a barbed wire stretcher and attached to posts using galvanized steel wire staples (ordinarily made from No. 9 wire). While wooden posts were common, steel and reinforced concrete posts are now popular due to their increased durability.

The material is valued for several advantages:
* **Effectiveness:** It acts as a barrier against dogs, wild beasts, and all kinds of stock. Cattle avoid pressing against it once they discover its nature.
* **Economy:** It is cheap to construct and covers large amounts of land.
* **Maintenance:** It does not harbor weeds, does not impede the view across a farm, and does not contribute to the formation of snow drifts.
* **Stability:** Because it does not oppose the wind with a large surface, it does not rack the posts or cause water to settle and rot them.

## Field Fortifications and Military Use
Beyond agriculture, barbed wire is employed in field fortifications as "obstacles," specifically in "high wire entanglements." In these setups, pointed stakes or "pickets" 4 feet high are planted in rows and secured to the ground with pegs or holdfasts using ordinary wire. Each picket is then connected to the surrounding pickets at the top and bottom by lengths of barbed wire.

In military contexts, such as those used by German commanders in Flanders, barbed-wire defenses were designed with alleys. These alleys were intended to trap unwary advancing forces, exposing them to enfilading fire from "pill-boxes"—small concrete forts bristling with machine guns.

## Legal Regulations and Alternatives
In the United States, some state laws regulate the placement of barbed wire on highways or prohibit its use as a property line marker unless both owners agree. Some states require a top rail of lumber. In England, the Barbed Wire Act 1893 allows local authorities to require the removal of barbed wire fences adjoining highways if they are deemed a nuisance or injurious to persons and animals.

Since 1899, there has been an increased demand for meshed wire field fencing, particularly along railway lines and in thickly settled areas of the United States. This shift is attributed to the high cost and scarcity of labor and the decline of rail fence construction. Meshed wire is often considered a superior enclosure for small animals, such as hogs and sheep.

Production statistics for the United States show the growth of the industry: barbed wire production rose from 5 tons in 1874 to 250,000 tons by 1907. During the same period, meshed field fencing grew from no recorded production in 1874 to 425,000 tons by 1907.
