# Sulphuric Acid and Sulphur Bacteria

## Biological Role of Sulphur Bacteria
Sulphur bacteria, including genera such as *Thiothrix*, *Chromatium*, *Spirillum*, and *Monas*, play significant roles in the circulation of sulphur within nature, particularly in estuaries, sulphur springs, and marshes. In marsh environments, cellulose bacteria release marsh gas, and the resulting nascent gas reduces sulphates (such as gypsum), liberating hydrogen sulphide ($\text{SH}_2$). Sulphur bacteria thrive under these conditions by oxidizing the $\text{SH}_2$ and storing the sulphur within their own protoplasm.

These bacteria utilize $\text{SH}_2$ as a respiratory substance, similar to how higher plants use carbohydrates. Rather than liberating energy as heat through the respiratory combustion of sugars, they generate energy by oxidizing hydrogen sulphide. In environments with quiet waters, some partially anaerobic sulphur bacteria exist in specific strata below the surface. In these zones, $\text{SH}_2$ is released by the bacterial decomposition of vegetable mud and rises to meet atmospheric oxygen descending from above. The zone of physiological activity for these bacteria fluctuates based on the partial pressure of gases and the rate of $\text{SH}_2$ evolution. In the deeper sections of this zone, the bacteria absorb $\text{SH}_2$; as they rise, they oxidize it and store sulphur. Upon ascending into more highly oxygenated planes, they further oxidize the stored sulphur to $\text{SO}_3$. If the supply of $\text{SH}_2$ runs short, these bacteria oxidize the sulphur again to sulphuric acid, which then forms sulphate again by combining with any present calcium carbonate.

Conversely, certain anaerobic forms, such as *Spirillum desulphuricans*, attack and reduce sulphates, effectively reversing the work performed by the sulphur bacteria.

## Chemical Applications and Reactions
Sulphuric acid is employed in various chemical processes and laboratory titrations. In the reduction of iron by metallic zinc, sulphuric acid is added after the iron is reduced and the zinc is dissolved to prepare the solution for titration. It is also used in the reduction of ore by stannous chloride; in this process, a solution containing sulphuric acid, phosphoric acid, and manganous sulphate is used before titration with a standard permanganate solution. Additionally, pure oxalic acid can be oxidized by a standard solution in the presence of sulphuric acid.

In the production of potassium bichromate ($\text{K}_2\text{Cr}_2\text{O}_7$), sulphuric acid is used to convert a potassium chromate solution into bichromate, from which the salt is then crystallized. Potassium bichromate itself is used as an oxidizing agent in organic chemistry when placed in a dilute sulphuric acid solution. Furthermore, adding concentrated sulphuric acid to a cold saturated solution of potassium bichromate results in the separation of red chromium trioxide ($\text{CrO}_3$) crystals.

## Industrial and Analytical Uses
Sulphuric acid is utilized in the manufacture of bromine through a periodic process involving the interaction of a bromide and manganese dioxide (pyrolusite). This operation is conducted in sandstone stills heated to $60^\circ\text{C}$. In the purification of commercial bromine, distillation over sulphuric acid is used to remove chlorine, and the final product may be dried over sulphuric acid.

In the determination of lead in ores, sulphuric acid is used to convert lead into lead sulphate. Because nearly all lead ores contain sulphur, which is oxidized to sulphuric acid during solution in nitric acid, adding sulphuric acid ensures all lead is converted to the very insoluble lead sulphate. This allows for the separation of other salts (such as those of copper and iron) via water dissolution, while the lead sulphate remains as an insoluble matter. The lead sulphate is eventually re-precipitated using an excess of alcohol and sulphuric acid, then ignited and weighed. Lead sulphate is noted to contain 68.30% metallic lead.

## Other Chemical Interactions
Concentrated sulphuric acid is capable of decomposing metallic bromides, resulting in the liberation of bromine and sulphur dioxide and the formation of a metallic sulphate. It is also used in the preparation of bromic acid ($\text{HBrO}_3$) through the addition of diluted sulphuric acid to a barium salt.

In the context of combustible elements, sulphur is used for the liquidation of sulphur-bearing rocks. This involves igniting large heaps of ore at the bottom, sacrificing one-fourth to one-third of the sulphur content to generate enough heat to melt the remaining sulphur. Additionally, in "pyritic smelting," ores of copper containing iron pyrites ($\text{FeS}_2$) are smelted; the oxidation of the iron and sulphur provides sufficient heat to liquefy the metal and slag without the need for preliminary roasting.
