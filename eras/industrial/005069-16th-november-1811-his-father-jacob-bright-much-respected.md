## John Bright
John Bright was born at Rochdale on 16th of November 1811. He was the second child of eleven born to Jacob Bright and Martha Wood. Although he had an elder brother, that brother died in childhood, making John the eldest son. His mother, Martha Wood, was the daughter of a tradesman from Bolton-le-Moors and was described as having refined taste and great strength of character.

Bright's father, Jacob Bright, was a respected Quaker who established a cotton mill at Rochdale in 1809. The family's history in Lancashire was the result of two migrations; Abraham Bright, a Wiltshire yeoman, had moved to Coventry in the early 18th century, where Jacob Bright was born in 1775. Jacob was educated at the Ackworth school of the Society of Friends and apprenticed to a fustian manufacturer at New Mills before settling in Rochdale in 1802.

As a child, John Bright was delicate and attended a boarding school near his home run by Mr. William Littlewood. His further education included one year at Ackworth school, two years at a school in York, and a year and a half at Newton, near Clitheroe. While he stated he learned little Greek and Latin, he developed a love for outdoor pursuits and English literature, the latter of which was encouraged by his mother.

## Early Career and Political Activism
At the age of sixteen, Bright entered his father's mill and eventually became a partner in the business. During this time, he participated in two major agitations in Rochdale: one led by his father against a local church-rate, and another seeking parliamentary reform, which successfully resulted in Rochdale claiming a member under the Reform Bill.

Bright was an ardent Nonconformist. He took pride in his ancestry, specifically John Gratton, a friend of George Fox and a preacher for the Society of Friends who had been imprisoned and persecuted.

## Later Political Life
Bright later became a leader of a movement alongside Cobden. Together, they led an agitation that included the League's resolution in one autumn to raise £100,000, supported by meetings in farming counties and Covent Garden theatre. While Cobden's argumentative style was often viewed more sympathetically in the House of Commons, Bright was noted for his "stately genius" and rhetorical appeals, which made him a master of his audiences.

In 1845, Bright moved for an inquiry into the operation of the Game Laws, a speech that established his position in the House of Commons. Despite their close partnership, Bright and Cobden differed on a few issues over twenty-five years of parliamentary life, most notably when Bright voted against the Maynooth Grant while Cobden voted for it.
