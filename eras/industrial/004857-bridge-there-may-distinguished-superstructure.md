# Bridge Structure and Design

In the engineering of bridges, a fundamental distinction is made between the superstructure and the substructure. The design and composition of these two elements are governed by the intended purpose of the bridge, the local conditions of the site, and the necessity for economic efficiency.

## The Superstructure
The superstructure comprises the main supporting members of the bridge. These members may take several forms, including arched ribs or arch rings, suspension ropes or chains, or a pair of trusses, beams, or girders. The bridge flooring rests upon these supporting members, with its specific type varying according to the bridge's purpose. In larger bridges, wind-bracing is incorporated to provide stiffness against horizontal forces.

The choice of material and design significantly impacts the bridge's performance. Since 1830, the expansion of railways has led to a high volume of girder bridges. These structures are characterized by a distribution where approximately half of the superstructure is in compression and half is in tension. While girders require more material than chains or arches—acting as a combination of both—they are easier to erect and impose only a vertical load on the substructure, avoiding the horizontal thrust associated with arches or suspension chains. The use of mild steel and wrought iron has rendered the construction of girder bridges economical and convenient.

## The Substructure
The substructure is divided into two primary components: the piers and end piers (abutments), and the foundations. 

Piers are designed to sustain vertical loads. Abutments, however, must resist more complex forces depending on the superstructure: they may handle the pull of a suspension chain, the thrust of an embankment, or the oblique thrust of an arch. In arch bridges, abutments must distribute the resultant thrust to the foundation in a safe direction to prevent undue compression. Intermediate piers in arch bridges must possess enough stability to counterbalance thrust when one arch is loaded while an adjacent one is not. For suspension bridges, the abutment serving as the anchorage must remain stable under the maximum pull exerted by the chains.

The foundations are located below ground level. These are often the most costly and difficult parts of the construction because the bridge's position is frequently dictated by requirements that preclude the selection of a site naturally suited for heavy structures.

## Materials and Engineering Theory
Modern metal bridges rely on the development of structural theory to ensure that every member has a calculated straining action and a definite function. This has reduced the need for empirical safety allowances. Engineers must classify the steel used based on tenacity and elongation:
*   **Soft steel:** Tenacity of 22½ to 26 tons per sq. in.; used always for rivets and sometimes for entire superstructures.
*   **Medium steel:** Tenacity of 26 to 34 tons per sq. in.; generally used for angle bars and plates, reducing bridge weight by about 7% for a given safety factor.
*   **Moderately hard steel:** Tenacity of 34 to 37 tons per sq. in.; used for larger members in long-span bridges.
*   **Hard steel:** Tenacity of 37 to 40 tons per sq. in.; used only for compression members to minimize the risk of flaws.

For medium or moderately hard steel, rivet holes are typically drilled or reamed out (after being punched 1/8 in. smaller) to remove material strained by the punch.

## Economic and Limiting Spans
The design of a bridge is often a balance between the cost of the superstructure and the substructure. As the span increases, the cost of the superstructure rises significantly. However, a higher cost for the substructure can make a larger span more economical. Broadly, the most cost-effective arrangement occurs when the cost of one pier and its foundation equals the cost of the superstructure for one span.

There is also a "limiting span." As the span of a bridge increases, the dead weight of the structure becomes a larger fraction of the total load. Eventually, the dead weight of the superstructure becomes so immense that it imposes a limit on any further increase in span.

## Erection Methods and History
The method of erection is often a controlling factor in design and is influenced by local conditions. Four primary methods are utilized:
1.  **Staging or falsework:** Using timber piles (typically spaced 20 to 30 ft.), earth banks, or iron staging. In deep rivers or great headways, clusters of piles spaced 50 to 100 ft. may carry temporary steel or timber trusses. For example, the Kuilenburg bridge in Holland used 81,000 cub. ft. of timber for its staging to support a 2150-ton superstructure.
2.  **Floating and raising:** Floating the structure to the site.
3.  **Rolling out:** Moving the structure from one abutment.
4.  **Member-by-member building:** Using the completed portion of the bridge as a stage for further additions.

Historically, bridges with timber superstructures and stone piers were used from Roman times. In the 18th century, the brothers Grubenmann constructed timber bridges at Schaffhausen (spans of 172 and 193 ft.) and Wittingen (a span of 390 ft.). In Great Britain, early cyclopean bridges on Dartmoor consisted of stone slabs on stone piers; one such bridge over the East Dart featured slabs measuring 15 ft. by 6 ft. and is reputed to have lasted 2000 years.
