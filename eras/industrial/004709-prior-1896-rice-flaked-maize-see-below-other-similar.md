# Rice and Flaked Maize in Brewing and Agriculture

## Classification and Regulatory History
In the context of brewing and wort-producing powers, the classification of rice, flaked maize, and similar preparations underwent a significant change in the late 19th century. Prior to 1896, these materials were classed as corn or malt. However, after 1896, they were deemed sugar in regard to their wort-producing capacities. 

Under the act of 1880, the equivalent of one bushel of malt was deemed to be either 28 lb weight of sugar or 42 lb weight of corn. Following the 1896 reclassification, these materials were classified at 28 lb, though by 1897, the standard shifted to a rate of 32 lb to the bushel.

## Use as Malt Substitutes
In the United Kingdom, sugar was the only allowed substitute for malt before the repeal of the Malt Acts. Over time, other substitutes gained favor, including preparations of maize and rice as well as unmalted corn. The use of these substitutes grew significantly; for example, the quantity of sugar used in brewing increased from 295,865 cwt. in 1870 to 2,746,615 cwt. by 1905. By 1905, the total amount of rice, flaked maize, and similar materials used in United Kingdom brewing reached 1,348,558 cwt., while unmalted corn accounted for 125,671 bushels. 

Statistical data shows a steady increase in the percentage of substitutes used relative to total brewing materials, rising from 6.05% in 1878 to 23.22% in 1905. While raw grains are not used extensively in Great Britain, brewers in America employ maize, rice, or similar materials at rates of 50% or more.

## Processing and Application in Brewing
The rice and maize preparations used in England are processed to be practically pure starch. This involves removing water, oil, and other subsidiary constituents. In the case of maize, the germ—which contains an oil with an unpleasant flavor—must be eliminated. The processing sequence for maize includes degerming, unhusking, wetting, heating to rupture starch cells, drying, and finally rolling the material into a flaky condition. Rice is treated in a similar manner.

These materials are used in various beer types:
* **Pale ales:** Made from pale malt, sometimes with the addition of rice, flaked maize, glucose, or invert sugar.
* **Stouts:** Made from a mixture of roasted (black), amber, and pale malts, occasionally with the addition of flaked maize or sugar.
* **Running beers (mild ale):** Produced from a mixture of amber and pale malts, flaked goods, and sugar.

When raw grain is used, the mashing process is modified. The rice or maize is typically gelatinized using steam at a high temperature in a separate vessel known as a cooker or converter, sometimes with the addition of malt meal. This gelatinized mass is then mixed with the main mash shortly before taps are set. Alternatively, the limited-decoction system allows for a fair percentage of raw grain to be used directly in the mash-tun, removing the need for a converter.

## Botanical and Agricultural Characteristics
Rice and maize are monocotyledonous plants, meaning the embryo contains a single cotyledon. In these plants, a shield-shaped body called the scutellum envelopes the plumule. In maize, the scutellum completely invests the plumule, whereas in rice, it only sends small prolongations over the anterior face at the apex. Rice also possesses a large epiblast—a projection of cellular tissue on the side of the embryo opposite the scutellum.

Agriculturally, both rice and maize are cultivated in the Indian region. In areas north of the tropic, rice is freely raised during the summer heat and rain, typically being sown in early July and reaped in September or October. In southern India and similar Asian climates, the cultivation of these cereals may occur in all stages and seasons if artificial irrigation is practiced, though operations are generally limited to the rainy months.

## Trade and Duties
In Spain, the import duty on rice varied by its state; it was 2s. 1-3/4d. per cwt. when in the husk and 4s. 3-3/4d. when not in the husk. Regarding maize, the Spanish duty was 1s. 9-1/2d. per cwt. In Portugal, maize was subject to a duty of 4s. 1-1/2d. per cwt.
