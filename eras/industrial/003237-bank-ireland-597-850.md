# Bank of Ireland

The Bank of Ireland is a financial institution established in 1783. Its creation followed a period of early banking history in Ireland characterized by legislation that was less favorable to the formation of a dependable system than that found in England. As early as 1695, principal merchants in Dublin had met to propose a public bank modeled after the Bank of England, though this proposal received no favor for many years.

## Establishment and Early Monopoly
When the Bank of Ireland commenced business in 1783, David La Touche, junior, served as the first governor, and two other members of his family were included on the first board of directors. While the bank achieved great success, it operated within a legislative environment of extreme jealousy toward rival establishments. 

The act that formed the Bank of Ireland stipulated that no company or society consisting of more than six members—with the exception of the Bank of Ireland—could borrow or take up money on notes or bills payable on demand. This restriction hindered the establishment of other banks for a significant period. In 1821, the act was modified to allow banking companies exceeding six members to be established if they were located at least 50 miles from Dublin. This provision required further clarification via another act in 1824, and the 50-mile limit was not fully withdrawn until 1845.

## Regulatory Framework and Note Issue
Ireland's banking system was influenced by the policies of Sir Robert Peel. Under the acts of 1844–1845, Irish and Scottish banks were permitted to exceed their authorized issues provided they held specie (gold) to the amount of the excess. This gold was held at the offices of the issuing banks to ensure that the circulating medium—consisting of both notes and specie—would fluctuate as if it were a metallic circulation only, thereby ensuring the uniform equivalency of bank notes to coin.

Unlike England, Ireland maintained the same limit of authorized circulation established by Peel's Act of 1845. Six banks that held the privilege of issue at that time have retained it. Additionally, notes for amounts less than £5 continued to be allowed in Ireland and Scotland. This power of issuing notes assisted the six of the nine Irish banks that possessed it in maintaining a large proportion of local branches.

## Financial Position and Market Standing
By 1906, the Bank of Ireland held a dominant position among Irish joint-stock banks. Its paid-up capital was £2,769,230, and it paid an annual dividend rate of 11%. For comparison, other joint-stock banks in 1906 included the National Bank (capital of £1,500,000), the Provincial Bank (£540,000), and the Northern Banking Co. (£500,000).

In terms of circulation, the Bank of Ireland's authorized issue was 3,597,850. In May 1907, the total authorized issue for all Irish banks was reduced, with 12 private banks having an authorized issue of £482,744 and 17 joint-stock banks having £1,084,836. The actual circulation of these banks often remained far below the authorized issue because their circulation was limited to the immediate neighborhood of the places where the notes were issued. While these notes were payable in London, they were often difficult to cash.

## Historical Context and Evolution
The early era of Irish banking was marked by instability. Sir Robert Peel noted in 1845 that a report from 1804 indicated there were fifty registered banks at that time, all of which failed, leading to "fearful distress." Public perception was also volatile; in one instance, a Dublin banker named Beresford became so unpopular that a group of country people collected and publicly burnt his notes.

Following the legislation of 1845, however, the Irish banking business achieved a level of steadiness and success equal to any other part of the United Kingdom. The system developed similarly to England's, where circulation preceded and fed deposits, and the acceptance of notes attracted customers.

## General Banking Environment in Ireland
The scale of banking in Ireland grew significantly over the 19th and early 20th centuries. In 1844, there were 180 banking offices in Ireland, serving approximately 45,417 inhabitants per office. By 1906, the number of offices had increased to 777, reducing the ratio to 5,738 inhabitants per office. Total bank deposits in Ireland reached £73,707,000.

Banks in Ireland also observed specific bank holidays. Under the Bank Holidays Act 1871, holidays included Easter Monday, the Monday in Whitsun week, the first Monday of August, and December 26th (if a week-day). The Bank Holiday (Ireland) Act 1903 added March 17th as a special bank holiday for Ireland.
