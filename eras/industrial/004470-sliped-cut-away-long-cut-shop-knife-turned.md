# Sliped

In the context of traditional basket-making, the term "sliped" refers to a specific technical method of preparing the butts of stakes. When a basket's bottom is constructed upon a hoop, the butts of the stakes are sliped, which consists of cutting them away using a long cut made with a shop-knife. Once sliped, these stakes are turned tightly around the hoop; this specific process is described as being "scallomed" on.

## Tools of the Basket-Maker
The process of sliping is performed using a shop-knife, which is one of the few and simple tools required by a basket-maker. The shop-knife is primarily used for cutting out material. Other tools in a basket-maker's full kit include:
*   A picking knife, used to cut off protruding tops and butts of rods after the work is finished.
*   Bodkins of various sizes.
*   A narrowly triangular flat piece of iron used to drive work closely together.
*   A stout pair of shears.
*   A "commander" or "dog" used for straightening sticks.
*   A lapboard for fixing the upsetted bottom during the siding process.
*   A vice or screw block for gripping the cover and bottom sticks of square work.

While a full kit is available, a bodkin and a shop-knife are the only tools necessary to create a common oval or round basket.

## Basket-Making Framework and Techniques
The sliping of stakes is part of the broader technical process of establishing a basket's rigid framework. In this craft, "bye-stakes" are inserted into the "upsett," while stakes are pricked up and driven in at each side of the "bottom-sticks." If a single continuous rod forms both the stake and the bottom-stick, the result is termed a "league."

Beyond the initial framework, several chief strokes are employed in construction:
*   **Slew:** Two or more rods woven together.
*   **Rand:** Rods woven singly.
*   **Fitch:** Two rods worked tightly and alternately one under the other, typically used for skeleton work such as waste-paper baskets or cages.
*   **Pair:** Two rods worked alternately one over the other, used for covers and bottoms of oval and round baskets.
*   **Wale:** Three or more rods worked alternately to form a binding course or string.

The final stages of construction may involve tracking, roping, or various forms of plaiting to finish or border the piece.
