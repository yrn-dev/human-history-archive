# Ludwig van Beethoven

Ludwig van Beethoven was a composer whose development was characterized by a determination to completely master his work before its release. This artistic conscientiousness is evident in his early orchestral career; while his sketch-books reveal ambitious ideas for a first symphony, he deliberately scaled these ambitions down to a "masterly little comedy." His subsequent second symphony demonstrated an advance in power and breadth, allowing him to later channel his energies into the string-quartet and the orchestra.

## Influence and Study of Earlier Masters
Beethoven profoundly studied all accessible works by Bach, frequently quoting them in his sketch-books in ways that directly bore upon his own compositions. His "Thirty-three Variations on a Waltz by Diabelli" were noted by his publisher for their kinship with Bach's "Goldberg Variations," a connection specifically visible when comparing Beethoven's 31st variation with Bach's 25th. 

In the final period of his life, Beethoven acquired a collection of Handel's works. This acquisition served as a stimulus for him to project "any amount of choral music," which he believed would make all his previous compositions seem like a mere prelude.

## Orchestral and Chamber Works
Beethoven's orchestral output includes several symphonies and overtures. His 5th symphony (op. 67), composed in 1806, was the first in which he utilized trombones. His 9th symphony (op. 125) featured a choral finale; the project of setting Schiller's *Freude* for this work had been in his mind since before he left Bonn. He also wrote the *Prometheus* overture, in which he employed the basset horn.

In his symphonies, Beethoven made extensive use of the bassoon, writing independent parts for the instrument that are characterized by originality and great beauty.

His chamber music is equally extensive. His last pianoforte work was a group of six bagatelles (op. 126). His final pianoforte sonata was the Sonata op. 111 (C mi.), written in 1823. In the realm of string quartets, his later works include op. 127 (1824), op. 130 (1825), op. 132 (1825), op. 131 (1826), and op. 135 (1826). The new finale to op. 130 stands as his last composition.

## Other Major Compositions
Beethoven composed the mass in D (*Missa Solemnis*), op. 123, which was begun in 1818 for the installation of Archduke Rudolph as archbishop of Olmutz, though it was not finished until 1826. Other notable works include the oratorio *Christus am Oelberge* (op. 85), the opera *Fidelio* (the final version of *Leonore*), and the "Thirty-three Variations on a Waltz by Diabelli." For the latter, Beethoven contributed the greatest set of variations among fifty composers, a group that included Schubert and the eleven-year-old Franz Liszt.

## Health and Final Years
Beethoven suffered from deafness, which affected his conversation long before it impacted his music. He was still able to conduct *Fidelio* in 1806 and performed his B flat trio (op. 97) as late as 1814. However, by November 1822, an attempt to conduct proved disastrous. He used ear-trumpets to play for himself, though his friends noted that he sometimes failed to make notes sound during soft passages or played on a pianoforte that was badly out of tune.

In his final years, Beethoven faced straitened circumstances. He received £100 in advance of a benefit concert from the London Philharmonic Society, an organization for which he had written his 9th symphony and projected a 10th.

Beethoven's death was preceded by a period of bad health. While staying with his brother Johann, he was not allowed a fire in his bedroom and was sent back to Vienna in an open chaise during vile weather. The resulting chill led to a fatal illness. Even within a week of his death, he remained full of projects, including music for Goethe's *Faust* and a 10th symphony. Three days before he died, he added a codicil to his will and saw Schubert. He died on March 26, 1827, during a fierce thunderstorm.
