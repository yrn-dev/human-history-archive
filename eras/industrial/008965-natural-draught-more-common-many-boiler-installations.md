# Boiler Draught Systems

In boiler installations, the draught necessary for combustion is categorized primarily as either "natural" or "forced." While natural draught is the more common method, forced draught arrangements are frequently employed in specific industrial and marine applications to increase the rate of fuel combustion.

## Natural and Forced Draught
Natural draught is produced by a chimney. In boilers utilizing this system, the combustion rate is typically between 15 to 20 lb. of coal per square foot of grate area per hour. In these installations, the tubes are generally 3 or 3-1/4 inches in outside diameter and rarely exceed 7 feet in length.

Forced draught allows for significantly higher rates of combustion. In larger vessels with moderate air pressure, rates range from 20 to 35 lb. per square foot. In express types of boilers used in craft such as torpedo boats, these rates can reach 70 or 80 lb. per square foot. Boilers employing forced draught typically use tubes that are 2-1/2 inches in diameter and 8 to 8-1/2 feet long.

## Marine Draught Systems
In marine environments, two distinct forced draught systems are utilized:

*   **Closed Stokehold:** This system is almost universally adopted in war vessels and some fast passenger ships. The stokehold compartment is closed so that air can only exit through the fires. Fans drive air into the compartment to maintain a pressure above that of the outside atmosphere. In large vessels, this pressure usually corresponds to a height of 1 to 1-1/2 inches of water, though torpedo boats may use up to 4 inches. This pressure operates in addition to the natural chimney-draught provided by the funnel.
*   **Closed Ashpit (Howden System):** In this arrangement, the stokehold remains open. Fans drive air through tubes located in the uptake, where the air is heated by the products of combustion. This heated air is then delivered partly into the ashpit below the fire and partly into a casing around the furnace front to enter the furnace above the fire.

Additionally, locomotive boilers produce draught via exhaust steam or blast.

## Express Boilers
Express boilers are a class of water-tube boilers used in cruisers and torpedo-boat destroyers where maximum power is required relative to the total weight of the installation. These boilers are designed for a high rate of fuel combustion achieved through a high degree of forced draught. They utilize tubes of a smaller diameter than standard boilers; for example, the Yarrow express boiler features tubes that are smaller in diameter and more closely arranged than those in the large tube Yarrow boiler.

## Destructor Station Draughts
In destructor stations, where refuse is burned in cells, forced draught or hot blast is supplied from fans through a conduit that commands all the cells. 

The Beaman and Deas destructor utilizes a closed ash-pit with forced draught. Air is supplied by Schiele's patent fans at a pressure of 1-1/2 to 2 inches of water. This forced draught serves to lessen wear and tear and keep the bars cool. 

The Meldrum "Simplex" destructor also employs forced draught, though it localizes the draught by dividing the ash-pit into parts corresponding to different grate areas. Each ash-pit is closed air-tight. This system uses Meldrum steam-jet blowers to provide blast pressure up to 6 inches of water column, although 1-1/2 inches is typically employed. In this system, the heat from gases passing through the boilers is further used to heat the air supplied to the furnaces via an air heater or continuous regenerator, delivering air through the blowers at approximately 300° F.

## Steam-Jet Blowers
The jet blower, such as those made by Messrs Meldrum Bros. of Manchester, uses a jet of steam to induce a current of air. Steam flows through a small opening (approximately one-eighth of an inch) in a trumpet-shaped pipe, drawing fresh air through an annular opening. These blowers are widely used to deliver air under the fire bars of furnaces. In some instances, steam jets are directed into the boiler furnace above the fire to assist the chimney draught and reduce smoke.

## Fuel and Space Considerations
In fast small vessels, the confined space allotted to boilers is a significant drawback for liquid fuel. However, experiments by the British admiralty have shown that the quantity of oil consumed by forced draught in these confined boilers now more nearly equals the quantity of coal consumed under similar conditions.
