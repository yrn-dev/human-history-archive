# The Galloway Boiler

The "Galloway" boiler is a modification of the "Lancashire" boiler, categorized as a type of large stationary "tank" boiler. Tank boilers are characterized by an outer shell that is wholly or partially cylindrical, a design that allows for necessary strength without requiring a large number of stays. These boilers are generally internally fired, with furnace plates surrounded by water to create the most efficient heating surfaces.

## Design and Construction
Galloway boilers typically measure between 26 to 30 feet in length, with diameters ranging from 6-1/2 to 8 feet. While the standard Lancashire boiler features two cylindrical furnace flues that extend for the entire length of the boiler, the Galloway modification alters this arrangement. In a Galloway boiler, the two furnaces extend only for approximately 9 or 10 feet into the boiler. These furnaces lead into a large flue or chamber that extends from the furnace end to the end of the boiler.

The furnace flues are constructed in sections, usually 3 to 3-1/2 feet long. Each section is made from a single plate bent into a cylindrical form with a welded longitudinal joint and flanges at both ends. These sections are connected using an "Adamson" joint. This specific joint serves two primary purposes: it acts as a stiffening ring to prevent the flue from collapsing and ensures that neither double thickness of plate nor rivets are exposed to the action of the fire.

## Galloway Tubes
To increase the heating surface, a number of "Galloway" tubes are fitted into the large flue. These are conical tubes featuring a flange at each end for connection to the furnace plate. They are proportioned so that the diameter of the large end is slightly greater than the flange of the small end, a design that allows the tubes to be easily replaced or removed if necessary.

The integration of these tubes provides several operational advantages:
* **Heating Surface:** They increase the total area available for heat transfer.
* **Structural Integrity:** They provide additional stiffening to the flue.
* **Water Circulation:** They promote the circulation of water within the boiler.
* **Efficiency:** They improve evaporative efficiency by mixing the flue gases.

## Working Pressures
The working pressure of these boilers varies based on the type of engine they supply:
* **Older boilers:** Approximately 60 lb. per sq. in.
* **Compound engines:** Between 100 lb. and 120 lb. per sq. in.
* **Triple expansion engines:** Between 150 lb. and 170 lb. per sq. in.
* **Special constructions:** Some have been built to handle pressures of 200 lb. per sq. in.

## Related Boiler Types
The archive distinguishes the Galloway and Lancashire boilers from other tank and water-tube designs. For instance, when a boiler with a diameter of less than 6-1/2 feet is required, a "Cornish" boiler—which is similar to the Lancashire type but contains only one furnace—is used. 

In contrast to tank boilers, "water-tube" boilers contain water primarily within numerous small tubes, with the fire usually placed beneath them. Examples include the "Stirling" boiler, which utilizes horizontal drums and vertical tubes, and vertical boilers such as the "Tyne" (featuring a hemispherical furnace and inclined water-tubes) and the "Victoria" (featuring a hemispherical furnace and horizontal smoke-tubes).
