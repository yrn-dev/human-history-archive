# BONE-LACE

## Definition and Production
Bone-lace is a specific variety of lace crafted from linen thread. The production process involves using a cushion upon which the desired pattern is marked out with pins. The lace-maker twists different threads around these pins, with each thread wound on its own individual bobbin. The name "bone-lace" is derived from the historical practice of manufacturing these bobbins from bone.

## Regional Industry in Buckinghamshire
In the county of Buckinghamshire, lace-making developed into a highly profitable industry. The craft was introduced to the region by Flemish refugees fleeing the Alva persecution, and is also attributed to French immigrants and Catharine of Aragon during her residence at Ampthill around 1532.

The industry was particularly prominent in Olney and Newport Pagnell. In the parish of Hanslape, the manufacture of lace is reported to have generated an annual profit ranging between £8,000 and £9,000. Historically, lace-makers in the region observed a general holiday on November 25th, referred to as "Cattarn's Day," a tradition that persisted until the later part of the 19th century.

## Economic Decline
Despite its initial profitability, the bone lace-making trade suffered significant decay. This decline was attributed in part to the monopolies established by James I. By 1623, the distress of the population resulting from the decay of the industry was formally represented in a petition addressed to the high sheriff of Buckinghamshire.

## Related Household Industries
The weaving of lace, often paired with the weaving of linen, has appeared as a household industry in other regions, such as the southern parts of provinces where flax is grown in large quantities. In Buckinghamshire, lace-making existed alongside other local trades, including straw-plaiting and the production of turnery articles—such as bowls and chairs—from hard woods like beech.
