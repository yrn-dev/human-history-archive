# Barrack Construction in Great Britain

The history of barrack construction in Great Britain reflects a transition from temporary billeting and fortress-based housing to a systematic, sanitary approach to military accommodation. Originally, barracks were not required for troops levied specifically for a particular war, with the exception of those for the royal bodyguard. To avoid building additional barracks even as the standing army grew, the military utilized billeting, which involved quartering soldiers among the town populations. This practice was found to be subversive of discipline, burdensome to the public, and prejudicial to military efficiency, while also exposing soldiers to temptations of disloyalty.

## Early Development and Administration
Barracks were gradually introduced in areas where populations were too small for billeting or where the concentration of troops was necessary due to disaffection among inhabitants. The earliest recorded barracks in England were those for the foot guards, erected in 1660. In Ireland, the Royal Barracks at Dublin date from 1700, with further construction throughout the 18th century. Most early English barracks were built between the end of the 18th and the beginning of the 19th century.

Initially, because barracks were primarily connected to fortresses, construction was the responsibility of the King's Engineers (later the Corps of Engineers) under the master-general of the ordnance. In 1796, a special civil department was formed under commissioners for the affairs of barracks to handle non-fortification buildings. By 1816, a civilian comptroller of the barrack department was appointed to oversee the erection and upkeep of barracks and barrack hospitals outside fortified places.

## Sanitary Reform and Standards
A significant shift in construction occurred following a 1857 commission appointed to improve the sanitary conditions of barracks and hospitals in the United Kingdom. This commission, which reported in 1861 and was later followed by similar inquiries in India and the Mediterranean stations, established general sanitary principles for military buildings. Prominent contributors to this work included John Sutherland, M.D., Sidney Herbert, and Captain Douglas Gallon, R.E. In 1862, the commission became a standing body known as the Army Sanitary Committee, which advises the secretary of state for war on type plans, sanitary fitments, and new sites.

The commission established a minimum scale of 600 cubic feet of space per man. This was a significant increase over previous conditions; while a 1845 general order had directed 450 to 500 cubic feet for new home stations, inspections revealed that some men in existing buildings had less than 250 cubic feet. Only 2,003 men out of a nominal 76,813 had the 600 cubic feet minimum. Implementing this standard required reducing barrack accommodation by nearly one-third.

These improvements had a measurable impact on health. In 1857, the annual mortality rate for the army at home was 17.5 per 1,000 men (compared to 9.2 for the civil male population); by 1897, this rate had dropped to 3.42 per 1,000.

## Location and Planning
Early barracks were often built in large towns for political reasons, allowing troops to quickly suppress disturbances. These were frequently cramped, multi-storey buildings located near slums, such as the cavalry barracks at Hulme, Manchester, and the Ship Street barracks in Dublin. In some instances, existing buildings like the Linen Hall in Dublin were converted into barracks with little regard for the circulation of pure air.

Following the Crimean War, policy shifted toward facilitating peacetime training for war. This required quartering troops in large bodies in open country where there was space for maneuvering, rifle practice, and gun practice. Between 1856 and 1860, camps were established at Curragh, Shorncliffe, Colchester, and Aldershot, followed by the acquisition of Strensall Common near York. Modern communications via telegraph, road, and rail allow troops to support civil power without being permanently stationed in crowded towns.

## The British System and Specialized Buildings
Modern British barrack accommodation is governed by the *Barrack Synopsis*, which provides particulars on authorized buildings, fittings, and the required areas and cubic contents of rooms. This is supplemented by "Standard Plans" that utilize the best points of previous designs to avoid creating entirely new designs for every building. These designs prioritize sound sanitary construction and convenience over ornamentation.

Military stations include various specialized structures:
*   **Army Schools:** Located between barracks and married quarters, these provide education for soldiers and their children.
*   **Garrison Churches:** Built when local places of worship are unavailable.
*   **Detention Barracks:** Used for ordinary sentences, while military prisons at Dover and Curragh remain for those discharged with ignominy.
*   **Support Facilities:** These include expense stores for bedding and utensils, supply depots with bakeries and abattoirs, and engineer offices with workshops. Large stations like Aldershot may require specialized sewage disposal, water supply schemes, and electricity or gas.

## Colonial and International Construction
In colonial stations, the *Barrack Synopsis* is modified for climate. In tropical or sub-tropical regions, floor areas and room heights are increased to ensure the freest possible circulation of air. Buildings are often raised 4 feet off the ground to allow air to circulate beneath the floors, and walls are protected from the sun by verandahs. Kitchens are typically kept separate from dwelling-houses, and buildings are oriented to catch prevailing winds.

Additionally, the Engineer Corps has been involved in international construction, such as the creation of numerous barracks and hospitals in various French ports.

## Administration of Works
Barrack construction remained under the Corps of Royal Engineers until 1904. At that time, a civil department was re-established under a "director of barrack construction" to manage the construction of home station barracks and the maintenance of military hospitals. The Royal Engineers continue to maintain the buildings, as well as the system of pipes, mains, and roads within the barracks.
