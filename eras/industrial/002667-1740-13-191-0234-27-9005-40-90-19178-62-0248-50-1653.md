## The Invasion of Silesia and the War of the Austrian Succession
In 1740, Frederick II of Prussia initiated an invasion of Silesia. At this time, Prussia was a small, compact, and thoroughly organized power possessing an army of 100,000 soldiers. Although the army was regarded as one of the minor forces in Europe—having only seen desultory service in the Rhine campaign of 1733-35—it was drilled to a level of perfection not previously attained. The Prussian infantry was so well-equipped and trained that a soldier could fire five shots for every three fired by an Austrian soldier, despite the Prussian artillery and cavalry being less efficient.

Frederick utilized the advantage of a standing army that had developed undisturbed by wars, allowing his regiments to take the field immediately while the Austrians waited for drafts to complete their forces. On December 16, 1740, without a declaration of war, the Prussian army crossed the frontier into Silesia after concentrating quietly upon the Oder. The Austrian generals were unable to do more than garrison a few fortresses and fell back to the mountain frontier of Moravia and Bohemia. This allowed the Prussian army to hold all of Silesia, invest the strong places of Neisse, Brieg, and Glogau, and eventually enter winter quarters.

This invasion served as the beginning of the War of the Austrian Succession (1740-1748), which concluded with the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle (Aachen) in 1748. While nearly all European powers became involved after 1741, the primary enduring interest of the conflict was the struggle between Austria and Prussia for Silesia. During the war, France and Prussia were constantly allied with Bavaria and Spain, while England and Holland—traditional enemies of France—supported Austria. Sardinia and Saxony also served as important allies to Austria at various intervals.

## British Political and Personal Events
In the British Isles, 1740 saw the parliamentary entry of William Wildman Shute, 2nd Viscount Barrington. Born on January 15, 1717, and having succeeded to his title in 1734, Barrington spent time traveling before being returned to parliament in March 1740 as the member for Berwick-upon-Tweed. Additionally, in 1740, he married Mary, the daughter of Mr. Henry Lovell; the couple had no children.
