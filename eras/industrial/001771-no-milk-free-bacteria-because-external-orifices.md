# Bacteria in Dairy Products

## Presence and Nature of Bacteria in Milk
No milk is entirely free from bacteria. This is because bacteria are always contained within the external orifices of the milk-ducts. In the ordinary course of supply, milk serves as a medium that is not only admirably suited for bacterial growth but is always contaminated with these organisms.

The forms of bacteria typically present in normal fluid are primarily those that cause the "turning" or souring frequently seen in standing milk—phenomena examined by Lord Lister between 1873 and 1877. Additionally, bacteria are responsible for the various fermentations and changes that occur in cheese and butter.

## Impact on Butter and Cheese Production
The quality of butter and cheese is heavily dependent on the specific types of bacteria present. Certain "foreign germs" can dominate the fluid and totally destroy the flavors of these products. Conversely, the search for bacteria that provide approved properties has led to the use of pure cultures. For example, American dairies employ a definite bacillus to achieve a peculiarly fine flavor in certain butters, and in Denmark, pure cultures and specific treatments to suppress rancidity-causing forms allow some butters to keep fresh for longer periods.

In cheese production, myriads of bacteria are present. Some are involved in the processes of ripening and other changes; practical applications of these discoveries from the decade 1890-1900 have been applied to Edam cheese. Additionally, the Japanese produce cheeses through the bacterial fermentation of boiled Soja beans.

The distinction between "good" and "bad" cheese often depends on the presence of lactic acid bacilli. It has been found that first-rate quality butter and cheese cannot be produced without the aid of these bacilli. To accelerate the development of acidity in milk, a "starter"—consisting of buttermilk, whey, or sour milk in an advanced stage of fermentation—is added. This starter is essentially a bacterial culture. Professor J. R. Campbell's experiments with Cheddar cheese-making indicate that first-class cheese can be made using pure cultures of a lactic organism, and that a whey starter produces results equal to those of a milk-starter.

Conversely, researchers have made strides in identifying bacteria that cause "diseases" or undesirable changes, such as rendering butter "oily" or "rancid," and making milk "ropy."

## Pathogenic Bacteria and Sterilization
A significant concern in dairy bacteriology is the presence of tubercle bacilli. F. Lafar reported that 20% of cows in Germany suffer from tuberculosis. Between 1891 and 1893, 17.7% of cattle slaughtered in Copenhagen were affected. Samples of milk examined in Paris showed tubercle bacilli in one out of every thirteen samples, and in Washington, one in every nineteen samples contained them.

Because of this, the sterilization of milk for domestic use is considered imperative. Efficient pasteurization is more readily achieved in closed receptacles than in open vats or bottles. Research indicates that exposing tuberculous milk in a tightly closed commercial pasteurizer for ten minutes destroys the tubercle bacillus. However, if conditions allow a membrane or pellicle to form on the surface of the milk, the tubercle organism can resist heat at 140° F. (60° C.) for longer periods.

To thoroughly destroy tubercle bacilli without injuring the consistency or creaming properties of the milk, it is recommended to heat the milk in closed pasteurizers at 140° F. for at least twenty minutes. This standard has been used at the Wisconsin University Creamery for over a year, confirming results previously reported by Russell and Farrington.
