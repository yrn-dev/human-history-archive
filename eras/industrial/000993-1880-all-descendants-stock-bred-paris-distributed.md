# Axolotls in Europe (1870–1880)

## Distribution and Origin
Between 1870 and 1880, axolotls were particularly abundant in Europe. It is generally accepted that the specimens kept alive during this period were all descendants of a single stock bred in Paris. This stock was distributed primarily by dealers, and it is believed that the late P. Carbonnier was originally responsible for this distribution.

The foundation of this population began with six original specimens received in 1864 at the Jardin des Plantes. These six animals were carefully kept separate from their progeny and remained in a branchiate condition. Between 1865 and 1868, they bred eleven times, and they bred again in 1870 following a two-year period of rest. According to a report by Aug. Dumeril, these original specimens and their offspring produced between 9,000 and 10,000 larvae during that timeframe.

## Dissemination to Institutions and Private Owners
Due to the high number of axolotls produced, the Paris Museum was able to distribute more than one thousand examples to various entities. These included private individuals, dealers, and other institutions. Through these channels, the animals spread to all parts of Europe. In England, numberless specimens were kept from 1866 onward. While specimens were exhibited in the London Zoological Gardens as early as August 1864—likely part of the original stock received from Mexico by the Societe d'Acclimatation—those specific animals do not appear to have bred.

## Biological Characteristics and Transformation
The axolotl is associated with the genus *Amblystoma*, which was established by J. J. Tschudi in 1838 for various North American salamanders. These animals were previously described as *Salamandra* or *Lacerta*. In terms of general appearance, they differ little from European salamanders, possessing a smooth, shiny body with vertical grooves on the sides, a moderately large eye with movable lids, and a nearly straight upper lip. Their tails are only feebly compressed.

A distinguishing feature of the genus is the dentition of the palate; the small teeth form a long series (either interrupted or continuous) behind the inner nares or choanae, appearing in a single row similar to the jaws. The axolotl is known to transform into *Amblystoma tigrinum*, one of the largest and most widely distributed species of the genus. *Amblystoma tigrinum* is found in cooler parts of Mexico and both the east and west of the United States. It rarely exceeds nine inches in length and is typically blackish or brown with yellow spots, which are sometimes arranged in transverse bands. The final stage of the metamorphosis into this form is marked by the loss of the gills after the animal leaves the water.

## Decline in Population
In later periods, the number of axolotls decreased, making specimens more difficult to procure and causing their prices to rise, particularly in France and England. This decline is attributed to close in-breeding, as no new blood was infused into the stock.
