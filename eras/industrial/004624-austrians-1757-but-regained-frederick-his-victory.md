# Breslau and the Military Campaigns of 1757

## History of Breslau
Breslau (Latin: *Vratislavia*) is first mentioned in A.D. 1000 by Thietmar, the bishop of Merseburg, and was likely founded several years prior. By the early 11th century, the city served as the seat of a bishop. After a period as part of Poland, it became the capital of an independent duchy in 1163. Although the Mongols destroyed the city in 1241, it recovered its prosperity and saw a large influx of German colonists. In 1290, the bishop was granted the title of a prince of the Empire.

Following the death of the last duke, Henry VI, in 1335, the city was purchased by John, king of Bohemia. The Bohemian kings provided various privileges to the city, which expanded its commerce and led the citizens to adopt a more independent stance. In 1463, due to a dislike of the Hussites, Breslau sought the protection of Pope Pius II. The city later came under the rule of the Hungarian king, Matthias Corvinus, until his death in 1490, at which point it returned to Bohemian subjecthood. In 1526, when Ferdinand (later emperor) was chosen king of Bohemia, Breslau passed to the Habsburgs along with the rest of Silesia.

## Prussian and Austrian Conflict
Breslau remained largely undisturbed through the Thirty Years' War and the Reformation until 1741, when it was forced to acknowledge the authority of Frederick the Great. The city was recovered by the Austrians in 1757, but Frederick regained it later that same year following his victory at Leuthen. Since then, the city has belonged to Prussia, despite being held briefly by the French in 1807 (after the battle of Jena) and again in 1813 (after the battle of Bautzen).

Following the 1807 French dismantling of the city's fortifications, King Frederick William III gave the sites to civic authorities to be turned into promenades. In March 1813, the monarch issued the appeals *An mein Volk* and *An mein Kriegesheer* from Breslau. The city then served as the center for Prussian preparations for the campaign that concluded at Leipzig. Later, in 1866, William I made a complimentary and triumphant entry into the city after the Prussian victory at Sadowa.

## The Campaigns of 1757
The year 1757 was marked by significant military engagements between the forces of Frederick the Great and the Austrian army. During the peace preceding the Seven Years' War, the Austrian army underwent reorganization, including the 1751 establishment of a military academy at Wiener-Neustadt.

In 1757, Daun was placed in command of the army raised to relieve Prague. On June 18, 1757, Daun defeated Frederick for the first time in his career at the battle of Kolin. This victory resulted in the evacuation of Bohemia by the Prussians and the raising of the siege of Prague. In recognition of this exploit, the queen instituted a military order in her name, naming Daun the first grand cross.

Following this, the relieving army joined the forces of Prince Charles at Prague. Daun, now second in command, participated in the victory of Breslau and the pursuit of the Prussians. However, Frederick subsequently won a brilliant victory at Leuthen. While Daun was present at Leuthen, he was not held accountable for the disaster. When Prince Charles resigned his command, Daun was appointed to replace him.

## Subsequent Military Actions
Following the events of 1757, a war of manoeuvre began in 1758. Daun maintained a cool and steady resistance to Frederick's strategy, though he is noted for missing opportunities to crush the Prussians due to over-caution. In 1758, acting under Daun's instructions, Major-General Loudon forced Frederick to raise the siege of Olmutz. Later that year, on October 14, Daun surprised Frederick at Hochkirch, inflicting a severe defeat.

In the following year, on November 20 and 21, Daun surrounded General Finck's entire corps at Maxen, forcing a Prussian surrender. These successes were later offset by the defeat of Loudon at Liegnitz—attributed to Daun's dilatoriness—and Daun's own defeat at the battle of Torgau, where he was severely wounded and forced to return to Vienna.
