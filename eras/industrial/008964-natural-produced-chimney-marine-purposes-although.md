# Chimneys and Draught Systems

In industrial and domestic applications, the movement of air and combustion gases—referred to as draught—is essential for the operation of boilers, kilns, and fireplaces. This process is primarily managed through the use of chimneys and flues, which can be configured to produce either natural or forced draught.

## Draught in Boiler Systems
In most boilers, the draught required for combustion is "natural," meaning it is produced by a chimney. In marine boiler installations, while natural draught is more common, many are equipped with "forced" draught arrangements. These forced systems are categorized into two distinct types:

*   **Closed Stokehold:** This system, used almost universally in war vessels and some fast passenger ships, seals the stokehold compartment so that air can only exit through the fires. Fans drive air into the compartment to maintain a pressure above that of the outside atmosphere. In large vessels, this pressure typically corresponds to a height of 1 to 1-1/2 inches of water, though torpedo-boats and similar craft may use up to 4 inches. This pressure acts in addition to the chimney-draught created by the funnel's height.
*   **Closed Ashpit (Howden System):** In this arrangement, the stokehold remains open. Fans drive air through tubes located in the uptake, where the air is heated by the products of combustion. This heated air is then delivered into the ashpit below the fire and into a casing around the furnace front.

The type of draught used affects boiler construction and efficiency. Boilers using natural or chimney draught generally utilize tubes with an outside diameter of 3 or 3-1/4 inches and lengths rarely exceeding 7 feet. In contrast, forced draught boilers typically use tubes with a 2-1/2 inch diameter and lengths of 8 to 8-1/2 feet.

Fuel consumption rates also vary by system. Natural draught allows for the combustion of approximately 15 to 20 lb. of coal per square foot of grate area per hour. Forced draught allows for much higher rates: 20 to 35 lb. in larger vessels with moderate pressure, and up to 70 or 80 lb. per square foot in express boilers used in torpedo boats. In locomotive boilers, draught is produced by the exhaust steam or the blast.

## Brick-Kiln Draught Configurations
Brick-kilns are divided into intermittent and continuous types, utilizing different draught methods:

*   **Up-draught Kilns:** In these older intermittent kilns, combustion products move from the fire-mouth through flues into the bottom of the firing-chamber, exiting directly through the top.
*   **Down-draught Kilns:** In modern plans, combustion products are introduced near the crown of the kiln and drawn downwards through holes in the bottom into flues connected to an independent chimney. These kilns use "bags" (short chimneys built around the inside wall) to conduct flames to the upper chamber. Down-draught kilns are more economical in fuel and provide a more regular fire.
*   **Continuous Kilns:** Invented by Friedrich Hoffman, these consist of multiple firing chambers in series connected to a main chimney flue. This allows combustion products from one kiln to pass through several others before exiting.

## Domestic and Architectural Construction
The construction of chimneys and flues requires precision to ensure efficiency and safety. To prevent down-draughts, flues must include at least one bend of no less than 60°. Bends should be gradual curves to minimize resistance to the up-draught. 

Architecturally, it is common to collect multiple flues into a single "stack." This is aesthetically preferable to having numerous single chimneys on a roof and increases efficiency, as heat from one flue assists the up-draught in adjacent flues. To prevent smoke, each flue must be separated by smoke-proof divisions called "withes," usually half a brick thick. 

The chimney shaft should ideally be higher than the roof and adjacent buildings to avoid down-draughts; if this is impossible, revolving cowls or patent chimney-pots are used. Standard flue sizes are 14x9 inches for ordinary grates and 14x14 inches for kitchen stoves. The outer wall of a stack is typically 9 inches thick. In some cases, fireclay tubes are used instead of pargetting for better durability and cleanliness.

## Specialized Applications and Controls
Chimneys are also utilized in specialized heating and waste systems:

*   **Warm-water Tanks:** Some tanks use a vertical chimney heated by an oil lamp or gas flame. A thermostatic capsule controls a lever that raises or lowers a lid on the chimney orifice. When the lid is closed, heated air is forced through flues to heat the water; when raised, the air escapes directly upward.
*   **Destructors:** In refuse-burning furnaces, a main flue serves as a dust chamber. In some early designs, a "cremator" was placed in the main flue leading to the chimney-shaft to resolve organic matters in the vapour and reduce offensive odours.
*   **Dampers:** A chimney or flue may be equipped with a "damper," which restricts the draught to lessen the rate of combustion.

Finally, when using wood as fuel in boilers, the rapid draught can cause ignited particles to be projected from the chimney. To prevent this, special spark-catchers made of wire gauze or similar contrivances are employed.
