# John Bright and Richard Cobden

## Early Life and Partnership
John Bright began his public speaking in his twenty-first year, though he did not initially intend to pursue a public career. At the time, he was a prosperous businessman and an active participant in the social, political, and educational life of his native town. He was a founder of the Rochdale Literary and Philosophical Society, where he engaged in debates and delivered a lecture on his travels in the East.

Bright first met Richard Cobden in 1836 or 1837. Cobden, then an alderman of the Manchester corporation, was approached by Bright to speak at an education meeting in Rochdale. Cobden was impressed by a short speech Bright delivered at that meeting and urged him to speak against the Corn Laws. Bright delivered his first speech on the Corn Laws in Rochdale in 1838. That same year, he joined the Manchester provisional committee, which founded the Anti-Corn Law League in 1839.

The two men first appeared together on a Free Trade platform in November 1839 at a dinner in Bolton for Abraham Paulton. While Bright was initially a local figure—opposing the Rochdale church-rate and John Feilden's proposed factory legislation—his energy and grasp of subjects soon positioned him for a leading role in the agitation against the Corn Laws.

## Leadership of the Anti-Corn Law League
Bright and Cobden became the leaders of the movement to repeal the Corn Laws. Under their leadership, the League resolved to raise £100,000 in the autumn of one year, appealing to agricultural interests through meetings in farming counties. The importance of the League was eventually recognized by *The Times*, which described it as a "great fact."

In London, the movement held large meetings at the Covent Garden theatre, featuring William Johnson Fox as the chief orator. Despite their leadership, Bright publicly deprecated the tendency of the public to view himself and Cobden as the primary movers of the agitation. Cobden noted that he typically spoke first, followed by Bright, whose "more stately genius" made him a master of his audiences' feelings.

## Parliamentary Career
Bright's transition to the House of Commons was slower than his success as a public orator. His rhetorical appeals were initially met with impatience, particularly during a debate on Villiers's annual motion against the Corn Laws. However, his position was established during the 1845 session when he moved for an inquiry into the operation of the Game Laws. Despite Peel advising county members not to be led by a "violent speech" from the member for Durham, Bright was not violent; Cobden described his work as admirable.

During the 1845 session, Bright and Cobden experienced a rare political disagreement when Cobden voted for the Maynooth Grant and Bright voted against it. Over twenty-five years of parliamentary life, this and a vote for South Kensington were the only two occasions they voted in opposite lobbies. In the autumn of 1845, while Bright was in Scotland, he received a letter from Cobden announcing a determination that had been forced upon him.

Bright was later elected for Manchester in July 1848. In parliament, he opposed legislation restricting labour hours and, as a Nonconformist, spoke against clerical control of national education. He advocated for the repeal of the Game Laws and opposed the Ecclesiastical Titles Bill, which he called a "paltry, miserable measure." He also spoke against flogging in the army, capital punishment, church-rates, and the Irish Established Church.

## Later Political Activity and Oratory
Bright became a prominent voice for peace, delivering impassioned but fruitless speeches against the Crimean War. His most memorable speech occurred on February 23, 1855, in which he spoke of the "angel of death" abroad throughout the land.

Following the death of Cobden in March 1865—a loss that overwhelmed Bright with grief—Bright continued his political ascent. He was returned for Birmingham and eventually gave his support to the government under Lord John Russell. He later became a popular hero during the reform agitation of 1866-1867. After the Reform Bill was carried, Bright was again returned for Birmingham in 1868 and accepted the post of president of the Board of Trade under Mr. Gladstone. This appointment made him a member of the privy council with the title of Right Honourable and a recognized leader of the Liberal party.

## Final Years and Legacy
In his later years, Bright received significant literary and social recognition. He was elected lord rector of the university of Glasgow in 1882, though he admitted to Dr. Dale that he had grown weary of public speaking. He received an honorary degree from the university of Oxford in 1886, and a statue was erected in his honor at Birmingham in 1888.

The 3rd marquess of Salisbury characterized Bright as the greatest master of English oratory of several generations, praising his robust and vigorous style that gave expression to "burning and noble thoughts."
