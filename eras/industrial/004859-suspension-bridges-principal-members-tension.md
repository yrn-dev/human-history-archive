# Suspension Bridges and Bridge Classification

In the study of bridge engineering, structures are primarily classified by the nature of the forces acting upon their principal members. Bridges are categorized into three main types: arched bridges, where the principal members are in compression; suspension bridges, where the principal members are in tension; and girder bridges, in which half of the principal members are in compression and half are in tension. There are also bridges of a mixed type.

## Characteristics of Suspension Bridges

The suspension bridge is defined by the fact that its principal members are in tension. The development of this bridge type was significantly advanced at the end of the 18th century with the introduction of iron link chains, and later by the use of wire ropes possessing even greater tenacity. These materials allowed for the construction of road bridges with spans that were previously impossible to achieve using any other construction system.

A primary advantage of the suspension bridge is that it dispenses with the compression member required for girders and requires less stiffening than that needed for metal arches. However, these structures necessitate the construction of massive anchorages and lofty towers. 

The main defect of the suspension bridge is its flexibility. To address this, the bridge can be stiffened using bracing and girders, resulting in a mixed-type bridge. While this stiffening reduces the economic advantages of the system, the stiffened suspension bridge is expected to be the preferred type for very great spans in the future. As an example of this ambition, a bridge with a span of 3,200 ft. has been projected for New York.

## General Bridge Components and Design

Bridges are divided into two main parts: the superstructure and the substructure. The superstructure contains the main supporting members, which may include suspension ropes or chains, an arch ring, arched ribs, or a pair of trusses, beams, or girders. The bridge flooring rests upon these supporting members. Large bridges also incorporate wind-bracing to provide stiffness against horizontal forces.

The substructure consists of the foundations below ground level and the piers and abutments. While piers sustain a vertical load, the end piers or abutments must resist additional forces, such as the pull of a suspension chain, the thrust of an embankment, or the oblique thrust of an arch. Foundations can be costly and difficult to construct because the location of a bridge is often determined by factors that make the site naturally unsuitable for heavy structures.

## Selection of Bridge Types and Materials

The choice of which bridge type to employ depends on several complex considerations:

*   **Cost and Materials:** For moderate spans, concrete, masonry, or brick can be used affordably. Steel becomes more economical for longer spans and is imperative for very long spans.
*   **Permanence and Maintenance:** Durability varies by material; masonry and concrete are more durable than metal, and metal is more durable than timber.
*   **Aesthetics:** In urban settings, aesthetic considerations are important. Masonry bridges are generally preferred in appearance, and metal arch bridges are considered less objectionable than most girder forms.

## Comparison with Other Bridge Systems

Unlike suspension chains or arches, which create horizontal thrust, a girder imposes only a vertical load on its abutments and piers. Girders are also easier to erect. However, from a superstructure perspective, girders require more material than a chain or arch because a girder acts as a combination of both.

In early braced bridges in America and England, wrought iron was used for tension members, while cast iron (often in circular or octagonal tubes) was used for compression members. Similarly, combination bridges—common on the Pacific coast due to the availability of timber and the distance of steel works—use timber for compression members and steel or iron for tension members. An example is the cantilever combination bridge at Roseburgh, Oregon, which features a total distance of 584 ft. between anchor piers.
