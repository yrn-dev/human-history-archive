# Baden

Baden, a grand-duchy in Germany, is characterized by a diverse physical landscape and a complex political history marked by constitutional struggles, religious conflict, and shifting international alliances.

## Geography and Natural Resources
Baden's physical configuration results in extreme temperature variations. The Rhine valley is the warmest district in Germany, with a mean temperature of approximately 50°F, while the higher elevations of the Black Forest experience the greatest cold in the south, with a mean temperature of 43°F on the high table-land. July is the hottest month and January the coldest.

The region's mineral wealth is modest, though it produces high-quality coal, zinc, lead, and iron, alongside smaller quantities of gold, silver, cobalt, copper, sulphur, and vitriol. Salt, gypsum, china-clay, potters' earth, and abundant peat are also found. Baden is particularly celebrated for its numerous mineral springs, with the most frequented located at Petersthal, Freiersbach, Griesbach, Antogast, Badenweiler, and Baden-Baden.

## Early History and Succession
The people inhabiting the Margraviate of Baden, as well as the Brisgaw and Ortenau near the Rhine, are of Alemannic origin and do not consider themselves Suabians. 

In 1815, Baden became a member of the Germanic confederation. Following the Congress of Vienna, a dispute arose regarding the succession to the grand-duchy. By a treaty on April 16, 1816, the succession to the Baden Palatinate was guaranteed to Maximilian I, king of Bavaria, should the line of Zaehringen become extinct. In response, Grand-duke Charles issued a pragmatic sanction (*Hausgesetz*) in 1817, declaring the counts of Hochberg—the issue of a morganatic marriage between Grand-duke Charles Frederick and Luise Geyer von Geyersberg—capable of succeeding to the crown. This controversy was settled in favor of the Hochberg claims via a treaty signed on July 10, 1819, by Baden and the four great powers at Frankfort.

## Constitutional and Revolutionary Struggles
To secure popular support for the Hochberg heir, Grand-duke Charles granted a liberal constitution in 1818, establishing two chambers whose assent was required for taxation and legislation. However, the administration under the unpopular Grand-duke Louis was managed by inefficient bureaucrats. The population, influenced by French ideas, often modeled the activity of the new chambers on the French Revolution's Convention.

Tensions escalated in early 1849 when the issue of a new constitution, based on Frankfort parliament resolutions, failed to satisfy Radicals. On May 11, 1849, a military mutiny occurred at Rastatt, followed by a revolution proclaimed at Offenburg on May 13. A simultaneous mutiny at Karlsruhe forced the grand-duke to flee. A committee under Lorenz Brentano attempted to direct affairs until a provisional government was established on June 1, followed by a "constituent diet" of advanced politicians on June 10.

The revolution ended when the grand-duke requested Prussian intervention. Prussian forces under Prince William invaded in mid-June. On June 20, the insurgent forces, commanded by Ludwig von Mieroslawski, were defeated at Waghaeusel. Prince William entered Karlsruhe on June 25, and the provisional government dispersed from Freiburg by the end of the month. Following the return of the grand-duke on August 19, the diet was dissolved and reactionary laws were passed to strengthen the government.

## Religious and Internal Politics
Internal politics in Baden centered largely on religion. A 1859 concordat with the Holy See placed education under clergy oversight and facilitated the establishment of religious orders. This sparked a constitutional struggle that ended in 1863 with a victory for Liberal principles, making communes responsible for education.

Under the premiership of Julius von Jolly in 1867, further Liberal changes were introduced, including compulsory education, freedom of the press, and ministerial responsibility. A law passed on September 6, 1867, required priesthood candidates to pass government examinations. This was resisted by the archbishop of Freiburg; following the archbishop's death in April 1868, the see remained vacant. The introduction of civil marriage in 1869 and the 1870 proclamation of papal infallibility further intensified the strife. This period, known as the "Kulturkampf," saw the government encourage the formation of Old Catholic communities. Reconciliation with Rome occurred in 1880 after Jolly's ministry fell, and the archbishopric of Freiburg was refilled in 1882.

By the late 19th century, political tendencies in Baden mirrored those of Germany. In 1891, National Liberals held a majority of only one in the diet; by 1893, they required Conservative aid to maintain power. In 1897, an opposition coalition of Socialists, Social-democrats, Radicals (*Freisinnige*), and Ultramontanes won the majority.

## Foreign Relations and Unification
Between 1850 and 1866, Baden supported Austria. During the war of 1866, Baden's contingents fought the Prussian army of the Main. However, the second chamber petitioned the grand-duke to end the war and ally with Prussia. Following the resignation of the ministry, Baden withdrew from the German confederation and signed a peace and alliance treaty with Prussia on August 17. While Bismarck prevented Baden from joining the North German confederation to avoid giving Napoleon III an excuse for intervention, Baden's opposition to a South German confederation made ultimate union inevitable. Baden's troops participated in the war of 1870, and the grand-duke of Baden was the first to hail the king of Prussia as German emperor at the assembly in Versailles.

## Notable Locations
Baden-Baden is a fashionable watering-place located in the Black Forest valley, 23 miles south-by-west of Karlsruhe. It features 29 hot springs flowing from the castle rock. Known for treating gout, chronic rheumatism, and various internal complaints, it attracts over 70,000 annual visitors. Key landmarks include the *Trinkhalle* (pump-room), a *Conversationshaus*, and a 15th-century parish church.

Badenweiler is another health resort at the western edge of the Black Forest, visited by 5,000 people annually for its whey cure and warm mineral springs. It contains a grand-ducal castle and Roman baths discovered in 1784.
