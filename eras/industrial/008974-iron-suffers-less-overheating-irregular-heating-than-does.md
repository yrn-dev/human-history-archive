# Iron and Steel: Thermal Properties and Industrial Applications

## Thermal Resistance and Material Comparison
In industrial practice, the choice between malleable iron and mild steel is often dictated by their reactions to heat. Iron is noted for suffering less from irregular heating and overheating than steel. While steel offers higher ductility, it is more susceptible to developing cracks when it is subjected to improper treatment. These characteristics have been established through early testing of steel and continue to influence contemporary industrial practices.

## Boiler Construction and Flanging
The construction of boilers involves specific techniques to manage temperature changes and structural integrity. A critical process is flanging, which is the bending or turning over of a plate's edges to create a union with other plates. This method has replaced the older system of using angle iron rings, which were a frequent cause of explosions and grooving in steam boilers due to a lack of elasticity caused by their sharp angular form. In contrast, flanges turned with a large radius provide the necessary elasticity to counteract the effects of temperature changes.

Flanging is utilized in various components, including:
* The crowns of vertical boilers and the fire-boxes of locomotive boilers.
* The front and back plates of marine boilers.
* The back end-plates of Cornish and Lancashire boilers.
* The ends of conical cross-tubes and the Adamson seams of furnace flues.

Historically, this work was performed by skilled angle ironsmiths who heated lengths of edges (approximately one foot or 10 inches) and bent them by hammering them around iron blocks. Modern boiler shops now utilize hydraulic presses with massive dies to bend entire flanges—ranging from 4 to 8 feet in diameter—in a single squeeze. For marine boiler ends that are too large for this method, special presses bend edges in successive heats, while Adamson seam flanges are rolled over using a special machine with revolving rollers. Machine-flanging is preferred not only for time savings but because the material suffers far less injury than it does during hand-flanging.

## Boiler Maintenance and Overheating
The efficiency and safety of boilers are heavily impacted by the quality of feed-water. Impurities such as magnesia or lime salts can precipitate as mud or scale on heating surfaces. Scale is particularly dangerous because it obstructs heat transmission; if excessive, it can cause plates to become weakened by overheating. To mitigate this, certain boiler types—such as the Cornish, Galloway, and Lancashire—are used when feed-water is very impure because they allow for easier cleaning than water-tube or multitubular boilers. Alternatively, "softeners" are used to purify water by mixing it with chemical reagents and raising the temperature to precipitate impurities before the water is filtered and pumped into the boiler.

## Metallurgical Classifications and Chemical Processes
Different historical and chemical perspectives provide further insight into iron and steel. According to Roger Bacon's account of Avicenna's fifth book, *De Animâ*, there are three species of iron:
1. **Iron (simply):** Used for anvils and hammers; it is suitable for bearing heavy strokes and being forged by fire and hammer, though not for cutting tools.
2. **Steel:** Purer and possessing more heat, making it better for cutting tools, though it is less malleable.
3. **Andena:** A material intermediate between iron and steel that is ductile and malleable even under a very low degree of heat.

In the Bessemer process of steel-making, silicon is used to produce heat. Because the resulting product of combustion (silica) is non-volatile at furnace temperatures, the heat developed is available to keep the iron melted, rather than being consumed by volatilization. Additionally, in "pyritic smelting," ores of copper containing iron pyrites ($\text{FeS}_2$) are smelted in a hot blast; the oxidation of the iron and sulphur in the pyrites provides sufficient heat to liquefy the metal and slag.

## Other Industrial Applications
Iron and steel are employed in various other heavy industries. In the manufacture of chain cables, bars are heated, bent by machinery, and welded. While cast iron studs were previously used, steel studs were introduced in 1898 because cast iron studs tended to work loose. Furthermore, in soldering, a soldering iron is used to heat borax on the metal surface to remove the superficial film of oxide, as solder only adheres to untarnished metal.
