# Petition and Receiving Order

In the administration of bankruptcy, the petition and receiving order serve as the preliminary proceedings for initiating the legal process against a debtor. The specific requirements for these proceedings vary by jurisdiction and the nature of the debtor.

## Proceedings in England and Wales
In England and Wales, a court exercising bankruptcy jurisdiction in the district where a debtor resides or conducts business may issue a receiving order. This order can be made against any debtor, regardless of whether they are a trader. The process may be initiated through two primary channels:
*   **Debtor's Petition:** The debtor may file their own petition.
*   **Creditor's Petition:** One or more creditors may petition the court, provided their aggregate claims are not less than £50.

When a creditor petitions, they must provide proof of the debt and evidence that an "act of bankruptcy" was committed within the three months preceding the petition date. Acts of bankruptcy include:
*   Failure to satisfy a creditor's claim upon a bankruptcy notice.
*   Making an assignment for the general benefit of creditors.
*   Absconding or keeping house.
*   Giving notice of the suspension of payments.
*   The seizure or sale of goods under execution.
*   Filing a declaration of inability to pay debts in court.
*   Granting a conveyance or fraudulent preference.

## Scottish Bankruptcy Law
Bankruptcy in Scotland is primarily regulated by the Bankruptcy (Scotland) Act of 1856. "Notour" (legally declared) bankruptcy is constituted either by sequestration or by insolvency. Insolvency must concur with either a duly executed charge for payment, the sale of the debtor's effects under a sequestration for rent or poinding, or an application for the benefit of *cessio bonorum*.

Sequestration may be awarded in the following instances:
1.  **Living Debtors:** Subject to Scottish court jurisdiction, sequestration can occur via the debtor's own petition (with the concurrence of qualified creditors) or via a petition from qualified creditors, provided the debtor is a notour bankrupt and had a place of business or dwelling-house in Scotland within the previous year.
2.  **Deceased Debtors:** Subject to court jurisdiction at death, sequestration can be petitioned for by the deceased's mandatory or by qualified creditors.

Additionally, the Debtors Act 1880 and the Bankruptcy and Cessio Act 1881 modified the process of *cessio bonorum*. While previously used primarily to avoid imprisonment for debt, the 1881 Act extended the right to petition the court for a decree of *cessio* to any creditor of a notour bankrupt, regardless of the debt amount. In such cases, the court has the discretionary power to award sequestration if the debtor's liabilities exceed £200.

## United States and Other Jurisdictions
In the United States, the administration of bankruptcy is entrusted to district courts and managed through court-appointed officers known as referees. Involuntary proceedings are restricted to debtors owing $1,000 or more, with certain exceptions; a petitioning creditor's debt must amount to at least $500. A notable peculiarity in this system is that while a corporation may be petitioned against, it cannot file a petition itself.

In Ireland, bankruptcy is regulated by statutes from 1857 and 1872, alongside the Irish Debtors Act 1872. While these laws correspond to older English enactments and some modifications from the English act of 1869, Ireland did not import the system of composition and liquidation by arrangement without court control.

## Legal Effects and Obligations
Once bankruptcy proceedings commence, the trustee's title to property relates back to the date of the first act of bankruptcy committed within the three months preceding the petition. This allows the trustee to invalidate assignments or payments made to creditors during that period if the creditor had knowledge of the act of bankruptcy. Furthermore, any payment made to prefer one creditor over others within three months of the petition is void. Settlements of property are void if made within two years (unless for marriage or valuable consideration) or within ten years (unless the settlor was solvent at the time and interest passed to trustees).

The bankrupt is legally bound to assist the trustee. Failure to deliver property can result in contempt of court. Under the Debtors Act, criminal prosecution may occur if the debtor:
*   Conceals or removes property valued at £10 or more with intent to defraud.
*   Fails to deliver books, documents, or property to the trustee.
*   Falsifies accounts or permits false debts to be proved.
*   Obtains credit through false representation within four months before the petition.
*   Obtains credit exceeding £20 without disclosing their status as an undischarged bankrupt (under the act of 1883).

## Appeals and Discharge
The discharge is considered the key to the efficiency of the bankruptcy system, allowing the court to exercise moral censorship over the debtor's conduct. In the United States, a judge may deny discharge if the bankrupt committed an imprisonable offense, concealed financial records, obtained credit via materially false written statements, or transferred property to defraud creditors within four months prior to filing.

Regarding legal appeals, the Bankruptcy Appeals (County Courts) Act 1884 stipulates that in bankruptcy cases, the decision of the court of appeal on an appeal from a divisional court is final and conclusive, meaning there is no further appeal to the House of Lords.
