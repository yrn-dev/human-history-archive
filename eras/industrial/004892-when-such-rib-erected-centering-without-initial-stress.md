# Centering and Rib Construction in Arch Engineering

Centering refers to the temporary timber or framing structures erected to support concrete floors or arches made of stone or brick. These structures carry the weight of the construction until the work has set, at which point the centering is removed gradually.

## Types and Components of Centering
Centering for concrete floors typically consists of scaffold boards resting on wood bearers. In one common method, a wood bearer is placed on top of steel joists, with long bolts suspended through it to a second bearer, which then supports the scaffold boards. An alternative, though less common and more costly method, involves propping boards of the floor's size on legs, which consumes more space.

For the construction of arches, specific types of centering are employed:
*   **Turning Pieces:** Used for turning an arch over, these are generally cut from 3 or 4 inch stuff and are 4-1/2 inches wide on the bed. They are secured with struts from sills or the ground and nailed to reveals. For semicircular or elliptical arches with a 4-1/2 inch soffit, turning pieces may consist of ribs cut from 4 inch stuff with braces and ties, or thin ribs (1 inch stuff) used in double thicknesses to lap joints. Thin ribs are used for spans up to 15 feet, while 4 inch stuff and upwards is used for spans exceeding 15 feet.
*   **Laggings:** These are small strips of wood nailed to the ribs to create the building surface. For ordinary arches, they are spaced 1 inch apart; for gauged arches, they are nailed close together and planed off.
*   **Ties and Braces:** The lower member of the centers is the tie, which prevents the center from spreading. In cases of great spans, ties may be given a rise to allow for traffic or access underneath. Braces are used to prevent ribs from buckling and must be arranged to withstand all stresses.

## Centering in Vaulting and Groining
In architectural vaulting, the intersection of two crossing vaults forms an angle or "arris" known as a groin. When vaults have different radii or heights, their intersections form winding curves that are difficult to provide centering for. This problem was addressed by the introduction of the rib, which governs the vaulting surface of the cell.

Terminology regarding vaulting centering includes:
*   **Groin Centering:** This term is used when the entire surface is supported by centering during the erection of a vault without ribs.
*   **Ribbed Work:** In this method, only the stone ribs are supported by timber ribs during progress, while light materials are used to fill in the spandrils.
*   **Welsh Groin (Underpitch):** This refers to a vaulting surface where the main longitudinal vault is higher than the transverse vaults.

## Ribs and Initial Stress in Steel Arches
In the construction of steel arch bridges, such as the Niagara Falls and Clifton steel arch, the center span is designed as a two-hinged parabolic braced rib arch. A critical consideration in this type of construction is the application of initial stress to the top chord.

When a rib is erected on centering without initial stress, the subsequent compression of the arch under its own weight results in an excess of compression in the upper member at the crown and inflicts a bending stress. To prevent this bending action, engineers may adopt specific expedients to put initial stress on the chord.

## Other Arch and Bridge Construction Methods
Various materials and methods have been used for arch ribs throughout history:
*   **Cast Iron:** Early examples include the Coalbrookdale bridge (1773-1779), which used five cast iron arched ribs. Other examples include the Wearmouth bridge (1793-1796), which used cast voussoirs due to the difficulty of casting heavy ribs, and the Southwark bridge (1814-1819).
*   **Wrought Iron:** Used in bridges such as those over the Rhine at Coblenz.
*   **Building Out:** Some bridges are built out from abutments or piers, using the completed sections as erecting stages. For example, at the St Louis bridge, half arches were built from each side of the pier to balance the load. The Douro bridge utilized girders of side spans that overhung the great span by 105 feet to serve as a platform for suspending parts of the arch.
