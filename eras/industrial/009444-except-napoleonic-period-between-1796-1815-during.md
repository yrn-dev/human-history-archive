# The Napoleonic Period (1796–1815)

The Napoleonic period was a transformative era of European history characterized by extensive military campaigns, shifting territorial sovereignties, and the rise and fall of Napoleon I. This period saw the reorganization of various regions across Europe and the Mediterranean, as well as significant advancements in engineering and naval technology.

## Territorial Changes and Regional Impacts

The influence of Napoleon extended across diverse territories, often resulting in rapid changes of administration. In Italy, the city of Bologna remained under the sway of the papacy for much of its history, but it was an exception during the Napoleonic period between 1796 and 1815. In Dalmatia, the fall of the Venetian republic in 1797 initially gave the region to Austria via the treaty of Campo Formio. However, by the peace of Pressburg in 1805, Dalmatia was handed over to France. Napoleon further expanded French control by depriving the republics of Poglizza (1807) and Ragusa (1808) of their independence. Although French troops were withdrawn in 1809, Dalmatia was restored to France and united to the Illyrian kingdom by the treaty of Vienna. This French presence lasted until 1814, when allied British and Austrian forces drove out the last garrison, leading to Dalmatia's final incorporation into the Austro-Hungarian empire in 1815.

In Corsica, the period was marked by conflict between French and British interests. Following a revolt against France led by Paoli, the Corsican assembly offered sovereignty to King George III, and the British occupied the island from 1794 to 1796. In October 1796, after his victorious Italian campaign, Napoleon sent an expedition that returned the island to French hands. Great Britain occupied Corsica again briefly in 1814, but the settlement of 1815 restored it to the French crown.

## Military Campaigns and Administration

Napoleon's rise was supported by a circle of devoted aides and family members. His brother, Louis (born 1778), accompanied Napoleon during the Italian campaign of 1796–97 and served as his aide-de-camp in Egypt from 1798 to 1799. Louis later became the governor of Paris and entered the council of state to study administrative affairs.

Among Napoleon's most devoted supporters was Maret, the duc de Bassano, who accompanied the emperor through most of his campaigns, including that of 1809. As minister of Foreign Affairs starting in 1811, Maret concluded the treaties with Prussia and Austria that preceded the 1812 invasion of Russia. Despite the disastrous conclusion of the 1812 and 1813 campaigns, Maret remained as Napoleon's private secretary through the campaigns of 1814 and 1815.

Other family members had more strained relationships with the emperor. Lucien Bonaparte, for instance, showed marked hostility toward Napoleon while living in Italy. In 1807, Napoleon attempted to arrange for Lucien to become a French prince on the condition that he annul his marriage, a request Lucien refused. After a period of surveillance in England, Lucien returned to offer Napoleon his help during the Hundred Days in 1815.

## Technological and Intellectual Developments

The Napoleonic era coincided with significant engineering breakthroughs, some of which were tested under Napoleon's direct commission. Robert Fulton, an American engineer, resided in Paris where he constructed a submarine boat called the "Nautilus." In 1801, this vessel was tried in Brest harbour before a commission appointed by Napoleon I, during which Fulton successfully used a torpedo to blow up a small vessel. In 1803, while still in Paris, Fulton also succeeded in propelling a boat using steam-power.

The period also impacted the intellectual and legal spheres. In Poland, the preparation of a constitution was interrupted by the events of 1812. In Germany, the university at Halle was suppressed by Napoleon in 1813, an event that affected students such as the dramatist Karl Leberecht Immermann.

## The Fall of the Empire

The conclusion of the Napoleonic era was marked by a series of defeats and political transitions. Following the battle of Waterloo in 1815, the French government was managed by an executive commission. Bignon, serving as minister of foreign affairs, signed the convention of July 3, 1815, which handed Paris over to the allies. In the same year, the settlement of 1815 restored Corsica to the French crown and incorporated Dalmatia into the Austro-Hungarian empire. Napoleon's subsequent captivity at St Helena has been the subject of various historical accounts, including those by W. Forsyth and Gourgaud.
