# Lloyd's Bank v. Cooke [1907]

*Lloyd's Bank v. Cooke* [1907], 1 K.B. 794, is a legal decision concerning the application of the common law of estoppel to negotiable instruments. The case served to vindicate a significant legal principle regarding the responsibilities of individuals who provide negotiable instruments to agents.

## Legal Principles and Rulings
In this case, the court affirmed that the common law of estoppel remains in effect concerning cheques, notes, and bills, provided that these principles have not been specifically abrogated or annulled by the Bills of Exchange Act. 

The ruling established that if a person places inchoate negotiable instruments into the hands of an agent for the purpose of raising money, that person is held responsible to any party who takes those instruments for value and in good faith (*bona fide*). This responsibility persists even in instances where the agent has abused or fraudulently exceeded their authority, provided the specific case does not fall under the provisions of the Bills of Exchange Act.

## Context of Bank Securities
The decision in *Lloyd's Bank v. Cooke* is part of a broader body of leading decisions regarding securities used by banks to cover overdrafts or advances. While banks taking a guarantee or a mortgage (whether equitable or legal) occupy a position similar to any other guaranteed party or mortgagee, specific precedents have clarified the handling of negotiable securities.

For example, in *London Joint Stock Bank v. Simmons* ([1892], A.C. 201), the House of Lords explained a previous decision from *Sheffield v. London Joint Stock Bank* (13 A.C. 333). That ruling determined that commercial or other negotiable securities may be safely taken in pledge for advances even if the person providing them is likely—based on their known position—to be acting merely as an agent for others. This is permissible as long as the securities are taken honestly and there is no tangible evidence outside of the person's position to create suspicion.

## Institutional History of Lloyds Bank
Lloyds Bank has a history of absorbing other financial institutions. In 1884, the bank absorbed Barnetts, a firm that had been among the goldsmiths listed in the *London Directory* of 1677 as keeping "running cashes."
