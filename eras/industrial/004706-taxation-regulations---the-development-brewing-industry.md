# Taxation and Regulations in the English Brewing Industry

The development of the brewing industry in England is closely linked to the history of its taxation and the regulations established to protect government revenue. This relationship has shaped the industry's transition from small-scale production to a centralized manufacturing trade.

## Evolution of Taxation
The first duty on beer in the United Kingdom was introduced during the reign of Charles II (1660), set at 2s. 6d. per barrel for strong beer and 6d. per barrel for weak beer. These rates increased over time, reaching 4s. 9d. for strong and 1s. 3d. for weak beer by the final decade of the 17th century. By the year 1800, the duty had risen to between 8s. and 10s., a rate that remained until the beer duty was repealed in 1830.

In addition to beer duties, a malt tax was introduced in 1697 during the reign of William III. For over a century, until 1830, both beer and malt were taxed. The malt tax began at under 7d. per bushel, rising to 2s. 7d. before the 1830 repeal of the beer duty, and later increasing to 4s. 6d. Between 1856 and the eventual abolition of the malt tax, the rate stayed constant at slightly under 2s. 8½d. In 1829, the combined beer and malt taxes totaled 13s. 8d. per barrel (or 4½d. per gallon).

A hop duty, which ranged from 1d. to 2½d. per pound, existed from 1711 until 1862. This tax was abolished primarily because crop uncertainty caused wide fluctuations in revenue; for example, revenue dropped from £728,183 in 1855 to £149,700 in 1861.

## Industrial Development and Centralization
Until the 12th or 13th century, large-scale beer preparation in England occurred mainly in monasteries. The industry shifted toward a specialized trade as London brewers formed an association during the reign of Henry IV and received a charter in 1445. Following the Reformation, the trade grew as monks from expropriated monasteries joined the ranks of trade brewers.

Until the 18th century, professional "brewers for sale" primarily served the masses, while wealthier classes brewed their own beer. However, due to improved brewing methods and economic factors, the wealthy began utilizing regular breweries. The typical charges for this service were 30s. per barrel for bitter ale and approximately 8s. for small beer.

This trend toward centralization continued into the 20th century. Between 1895 and 1905, the number of private brewers fell from 17,041 to 9,930. Most remaining private brewers were exempt from beer duty, such as farmers in houses with an annual value not exceeding £10 who brewed for laborers, or others in houses not exceeding £15. Private houses subject to both license and beer duty produced fewer than 20,000 barrels annually. Consequently, it is estimated that over 99% of United Kingdom beer is produced by public brewers. The number of licenses for brewers for sale decreased from 8,863 in 1894-1895 to 5,164 by 1904-1905.

## Production and Scale
By the early 20th century, the industry reached vast proportions. Production peaked in 1900 at 37,090,986 barrels. While production declined after 1900, the 1905-1906 figure of 34,109,263 barrels still exceeded any year prior to 1897. In the year ending September 30, 1905, the exchequer received £13,156,053 in beer duty.

The industry was dominated by a few large firms. Of the 5,180 brewers for sale, Messrs Guinness owned the world's largest brewery, producing over two million barrels and paying roughly one million sterling to the revenue. Three other firms produced nearly a million barrels or more. In 1905-1906, the materials used included 51,818,697 bushels of malt, 125,671 bushels of unmalted corn, 1,348,558 cwt. of rice, flaked maize, and similar materials, 2,746,615 cwt. of sugar, 62,360,817 lb of hops, and 49,202 lb of hop substitutes.

Consumption was highest in England, which consumed 28,590,563 barrels in 1905-1906, compared to 3,265,084 in Ireland and 1,648,463 in Scotland. Capital investment in the liquor trade, calculated at £117,000,000 in 1871, was estimated to have doubled by 1908.

## Regulatory Requirements
Brewing operations are subject to stringent regulations. Under the 1880 act, licenses were required at an annual duty of £1 for brewers for sale, and either 4s. or 9s. for other brewers. 

Regarding materials, after 1896, flaked maize and rice were deemed sugar rather than malt or corn. Regulations specify that 42 lb of corn or 28 lb of sugar are equivalent to one bushel of malt. Brewers are generally expected to produce at least one barrel (36 gallons) of worts at standard gravity for every two bushels of malt (or equivalents), minus a 4% allowance for wastage. If a brewer fails to meet this standard due to inferior machinery or lack of skill, the charge is applied directly to the material.

Mashing operations are strictly monitored. Brewers must use a brewing book provided by the Inland Revenue to record the day and hour of mashing at least 24 hours in advance. Two hours before mashing, they must record the quantity of sugar, corn, and malt to be used, as well as the time worts will be drawn off. Worts must be collected within 12 hours of the start of collection and remain undisturbed in the vessel for 12 hours unless an officer has taken account of them. Regulations also prohibit altering the shape or size of gauged vessels without notice and forbid mixing worts from different brewings unless each is accounted for separately.
