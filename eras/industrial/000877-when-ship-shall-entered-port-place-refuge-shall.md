# Ports and Places of Refuge

In maritime operations and international law, the entry of a vessel into a port or place of refuge is governed by specific regulations regarding costs, safety, and diplomatic rights. These regulations vary depending on whether the entry is due to emergency circumstances, diplomatic agreements, or navigational requirements.

## General Average and Expenses at Port of Refuge
Under specific maritime rules, when a ship enters a port or place of refuge—or returns to its original port of loading—due to sacrifice, accident, or other extraordinary circumstances necessary for common safety, the expenses associated with entering that port are admitted as General Average (G.A.). If the ship subsequently sails from that location with all or part of its original cargo, the corresponding expenses for leaving the port are also admitted as G.A.

The cost of discharging cargo is admissible as G.A. if the action was necessary for common safety or to allow for repairs to damage caused by accident or sacrifice during the voyage, provided those repairs are essential for the safe prosecution of the voyage. When the discharge of cargo is admitted as G.A., the subsequent costs of reloading, storing the cargo on board, and all associated storage charges are likewise admitted. However, if the voyage is abandoned or the ship is condemned, storage expenses incurred after the date of abandonment or condemnation are not admitted.

Furthermore, the wages and maintenance costs for the master, officers, and crew during the extra period of detention in a port of refuge for repairs are admitted as G.A. Similar to storage costs, these wages and maintenance expenses are not admitted if they are incurred after the ship has been condemned or the voyage abandoned.

## Cargo Handling and Vessel Recovery
In instances where a ship is ashore, the costs associated with lightening the vessel to float her—including the discharge of ship's stores, bunker coals, and cargo—are admitted as G.A. This includes the loss or damage sustained during the process, as well as the costs of lighter hire and reshipping.

If a ship under average is in a location where repairs are practicable to enable the carriage of the full cargo, but the vessel is instead towed to another repair site or destination to save expenses, the extra costs of such forwarding, transhipment, or towage are payable by the parties to the adventure. This payment is proportional to the extraordinary expense saved. Additionally, any damage or loss of cargo necessarily caused during the acts of storing, reloading, stowing, or discharging is made good as G.A., provided the costs of those specific measures were themselves admitted as G.A.

## International and Diplomatic Regulations
Different nations and treaties have established specific rights for foreign vessels entering their ports. In historical Japanese regulations, foreign vessels obliged to touch at a principality or enter a Japanese harbor due to stress of weather were protected from having their belongings or cargo taken from them. Violence in the purchase or sale of commodities brought by such ships was rigorously prohibited. English ships, specifically, were granted the freedom to visit any Japanese port and put into any harbor if disabled by storms. They were permitted to erect houses, reside, and trade in Yedo, with the right to return to their own country and dispose of their houses as they wished.

In the context of post-war agreements involving Germany, the state was required to grant freedom of transit by water or mail to ships, goods, and persons from allied or associated powers without discrimination based on nationality or place of entry. Ships of allied and associated powers were granted the same rights in German ports as German vessels for a period of five years, including most favored nation treatment in towage, coasting trade, and fishing.

## Navigational Markers for Harbor Entry
For mariners entering a harbor, river, or estuary from seaward, specific buoy systems are used to denote direction. The "starboard-hand" refers to the right side of the mariner, while the "port-hand" refers to the left. 

- **Starboard-hand buoys** are identified by a conical shape (a pointed top of a cone above water) and are painted in one color only. They may be surmounted by a staff and globe.
- **Port-hand buoys** are identified by a "can" shape (a flat top above water) and are painted in a different characteristic color, which may be single or parti-colour. They may be surmounted by a staff and cage.
- **Spherical buoys**, marked by horizontal white stripes, denote the ends of middle grounds.
- **Pillar buoys**, featuring a tall central structure on a broad face, and other special buoys (such as automatic sounding, gas, or bell buoys) mark special positions in the approaches to harbors or on the coast.
- **Spar-buoys** are identified by showing only a mast above water.
