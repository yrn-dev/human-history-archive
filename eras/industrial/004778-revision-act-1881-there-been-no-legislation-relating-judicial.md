# Judicial Legislation and Corruption

## Judicial Bribery in England and Europe
The process of removing judicial bribery from the bench has occurred gradually across most European nations. In France, this movement was encouraged during the 16th century by the chancellor Michel de L'Hôpital. In England, judicial corruption has been remarkably rare. Aside from a statute enacted in 1384, which was later repealed by the Statute Law Revision Act 1881, there has been no legislation specifically relating to judicial bribery.

Despite the lack of extensive legislation, there are recorded instances of judicial corruption in England. The earliest known case occurred in 1351, when Sir William Thorpe was removed from office and fined for accepting bribes. Other notable cases include the chancellor of England, Michael de la Pole, in 1387; Lord Chancellor Bacon in 1621; Lionel Cranfield, the earl of Middlesex, in 1624; and Sir Thomas Parker, the 1st earl of Macclesfield, in 1725.

## Official and Administrative Corruption
Corruption was more prevalent in the highest official quarters between the Revolution and the end of the reign of Queen Anne. During this era, the great Marlborough was unable to clear his character of pecuniary dishonesty, and a speaker of the House of Commons was expelled due to bribery. Under the administration of Walpole, members of parliament were paid in cash for their votes, reflecting the characteristic government method that every man has his price.

While official bribery has become extremely rare for lower-level officers connected to revenue, it persisted longer among those whose power was constitutional rather than official, as they were viewed as less responsible to the public. One of the most difficult forms of administrative corruption to eradicate is the appointment to office.

## Judicial and Legal Systems in Other Regions
### Scotland and the British Empire
In Scotland, the bench faced suspicions of interested partiality for some years following the Revolution, but by the beginning of the 19th century, a perfect reliance on the purity of the bench existed throughout all parts of the empire. 

Regarding corporal punishment, the Whipping Act 1862 and the Prisons (Scotland) Act 1860 (S 74) regulate the whipping of male offenders under 14 in Scotland. The Whipping Act 1862 further stipulates that offenders over 16 may not be whipped for property or person offences. In British colonies, judicial orders for corporal punishment generally follow modern English legislation, though some areas, such as Queensland (Criminal Code 1899, arts. 212, 213, 216), have a larger list of punishable offences.

### Japan
In Japan, the Yedo Bakufu conducted significant legislative work. In 1767, the shogun Ieharu collected and collated all laws and regulations promulgated since the days of Ieyasu, forming a prefatory volume to the "Rules for Judicial Procedure." These were combined into a single enactment called Kajo-ruiten. In 1790, Matsudaira Sadanobu revised this code, reducing it to one hundred articles and renaming it the Tokugawa Hyakkajid (or "One Hundred Laws and Regulations of the Tokugawa"). These laws were not disclosed to the general public, serving only as official reference in accordance with the Confucian maxim to make the people obey without making them know.

### British Honduras
From 1638 to 1786, colonists in British Honduras were independent and elected magistrates who handled all executive and judicial functions. These precedents were codified as "Burnaby's Laws" in 1756. English common law remains valid in British Honduras, though it is subject to the Consolidated Laws of British Honduras (revised and published between 1884 and 1888) and local enactments.

## Specialized Judicial Acts and Regulations
### Bankruptcy and Factors
The Judicial Factors Act of 1889 abolished the office of accountant in bankruptcy (established in 1856), merging its duties into the office of accountant of the court of session. This act also includes provisions to check excessive administration costs by requiring that the rate of remuneration for a trustee be intimated to the accountant of court and creditors before being acted upon.

### Criminal Law and Homicide
In England, the definition of "wilful murder" was influenced by legislation from Henry VIII in 1531, which removed the benefit of clergy for "wilful murder with malice prepensed." Parliament has never provided a legislative definition of "malice aforethought"; instead, the substantive law rests on judicial rulings and extrajudicial commentaries by Foster, Hale, and Coke. By 1879, criminal code commissioners identified three states of mind covered by the term "malice aforethought," including the intent to kill or do grievous bodily harm, or the intent to commit any felony.
