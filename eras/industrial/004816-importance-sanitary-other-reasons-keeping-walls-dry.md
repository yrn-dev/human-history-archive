# The Importance of Keeping Walls Dry

The maintenance of dry walls is recognized by all who have observed the deleterious effects of dampness on a building. Ensuring that walls remain dry is considered important for sanitary reasons as well as other structural concerns.

## Causes of Dampness in Walls
Walls are susceptible to becoming damp through three primary channels:
1. **Ground Moisture:** Wetness rising up through the wall from the earth.
2. **Top-Down Soaking:** Water soaking down from the top of the wall.
3. **Weather Penetration:** Rain being driven onto the face of the wall by the wind.

## Methods of Prevention and Protection
Various construction techniques and materials are employed to prevent moisture from infiltrating a building.

### Ground and Exterior Protection
To prevent moisture from rising from the earth, builders may introduce damp-proof courses or construct dry areas. Dry areas, or air drains, involve creating a hollow space at least 9 inches wide around portions of the walls situated below ground. This prevents the main brickwork from contacting the earth and imparting moisture to the building. These areas must be drained, ventilated, and kept clear of vermin. However, because dry areas are seldom considered sanitary, they are frequently superseded by the application of cement or asphalt to the face of the wall.

For walls above the ground line, the exterior can be protected by coating the surface with rough cast or cement. Alternatively, slates or tiles may be fixed on battens, similar to a roof.

### Top-Wall Waterproofing
To stop water from soaking down from the top of a wall, a coping of non-porous material—such as cement, stone, or cast-iron—is used. These copings are often "weathered" or sloped to throw rain off the structure. Other methods include using specially made non-porous coping bricks or ordinary bricks set on edge in cement with a double course of tiles, known as a "creasing," placed immediately below.

### Hollow and Cavity Walls
In exposed positions, buildings are frequently constructed with cavity walls. This design consists of a main inner wall and an outer skin (usually half a brick thick) separated by a cavity of 2 or 3 inches. This structure provides several benefits:
* **Water Management:** Water that penetrates the outer skin drops to the base of the cavity and trickles out through gratings located a few inches above ground level.
* **Internal Environment:** Hollow walls help maintain an equable temperature and keep the building dry internally.
* **Structural Ties:** The two walls are connected by iron or stoneware ties (approximately 8 inches long and ¾ inch wide) placed at 12 to 18-inch vertical intervals and 3-foot horizontal intervals. These ties feature a bend or twist in the center to prevent water from being transmitted to the inner wall.

To ensure the effectiveness of cavity walls, it is essential that the cavity and ties remain free of mortar or rubbish during construction; builders may use a narrow board or a wisp of straw to catch falling material. Additionally, proper lead or other flashing must be built into door and window frames to direct penetrating water away from the main wall and frames.

## Maintenance and Risks
While hollow walls protect the interior, they have the disadvantage of potentially harboring vermin. To mitigate this, the top of the wall is usually sealed with brickwork. Air gratings are also introduced at the top to allow air to circulate through the cavity, which facilitates drying after rain.

Furthermore, the integrity of the wall's bond is critical. "Snapped headers"—the practice of cutting heading bricks in half to save material while maintaining an outward appearance—is considered a pernicious practice. This results in a brickwork skin only 4½ inches thick with a straight mortar joint behind it, destroying the proper bonding between the face and the back of the wall.
