# Specific Gravity and Gravimetric Density of Cordite

In the study of ballistics and the use of smokeless propellants, a critical distinction is made between the specific gravity (S.G.) of a solid propellant grain and the gravimetric density (G.D.) of the charge as it fills a given volume. This distinction is particularly relevant to cordite, the smokeless propellant utilized by the British army and navy.

## Definitions of S.G. and G.D.
The term gravimetric density is used by artillerists to differentiate the specific gravity of the powder when it fills a volume in a state of gas from the specific gravity of the separate solid cord or grain of powder. 

To illustrate this relationship, the archive provides the example of lead. A lump of solid lead has a specific gravity (S.G.); however, if that lead is formed into a charge of closely packed equal spherules (lead shot), the gravimetric density (G.D.) of that charge is calculated as $0.7403$ of the S.G. of the solid lead lump.

In the case of cordite, which is produced as a bundle of cylindrical sticks, the relationship is defined by the formula:
$\text{G.D. of charge of cordite} = \frac{1}{6} \pi \sqrt{3} \times \text{S.G. of stick of cordite}$, which equals $0.9067$ of the S.G. of the stick.

## Physical Properties and Density of Cordite
Cordite is a waterproof substance produced by pressing material in a soft, pasty state through dies or perforations in a steel plate. This process results in cylindrical rods or strings. The thickness of these rods varies based on the intended charge, ranging from approximately 1 mm in diameter for revolver cartridges to 5 mm or more for heavy guns. The size is designated by the number of hundredths of an inch of the die diameter; for example, size 30 cordite is squeezed through a hole 0.30 in. diameter.

At an ordinary temperature of 15°C, cordite has a density of approximately 1.56. Because its coefficient of expansion is small, this density does not change significantly under variations in climatic temperature.

## Composition and Varieties
The material's composition has evolved over time. The original version, known as Cordite Mark 1, was devised in 1891 by the Ordnance Committee under Sir Frederick Abel and consisted of:
* 58% nitro-glycerin
* 37% gun-cotton
* 5% mineral jelly

A later modification, known as Cordite M.D., contains:
* 65% gun-cotton
* 30% nitro-glycerin
* 5% mineral jelly

Cordite M.D. offers several advantages over Mark 1, including a more regular pressure in the gun, higher velocities, a slightly reduced rate of burning, and lower temperature. The mineral jelly (vaseline), a mixture of hydrocarbons, paraffins, and olefines obtained during petroleum distillation, serves as a preservative.

## Manufacturing and Stability
The production of cordite involves mixing gun-cotton (which is pressed into small cylinders and dried before the addition of nitro-glycerin) with nitro-glycerin and a gelatinizing material called acetone. After the material is pressed into rods, it is dried in heated rooms for a period ranging from a few days to several weeks.

The stability of the propellant is measured via "heat tests," such as the Abel heat test. In this process, 2 grams of finely divided cordite are subjected to a constant temperature of 70°C in a test tube. A piece of filter paper impregnated with starch paste and potassium iodide is suspended in the tube; the appearance of a brown color on the paper indicates the evolution of oxides of nitrogen, signaling decomposition.

## Ballistic Application
In interior ballistics, the gravimetric density of the charge is adjusted relative to the weight of the shot. This ensures that the shot advances during the combustion of the cordite, preventing the maximum pressure from exceeding safe limits. For example, in a 6-in. range table, a charge with a G.D. of 0.504 and a weight of 13 lb 4 oz. is used. If the shot were unable to move, the closed chamber pressure for a charge at a G.D. of 0.5 would be nearly 49 tons per sq. in., which is considered unsafe.
