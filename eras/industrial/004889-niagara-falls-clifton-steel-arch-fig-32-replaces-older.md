# The Niagara Falls and Clifton Steel Arch Bridge

The Niagara Falls and Clifton steel arch bridge is a structure that replaced the older Roebling suspension bridge. This transition occurred between 1896 and 1897, as the previous suspension bridge was no longer strong enough to accommodate the increasing weight of railway traffic.

## Design and Structure
The bridge features a center span designed as a two-hinged parabolic braced rib arch. In addition to the main span, the structure includes side spans measuring 210 ft. and 190 ft. The main span of the bridge weighed 1629 tons, while the side spans weighed 166 tons and 154 tons, respectively.

The bridge is designed for multi-modal transport, carrying two footways, two roadways, and two tracks for electric cars.

## Engineering Advancements
Prof. Claxton Fidler noted that the bridge represented the furthest advance made in its type of construction, specifically regarding the arrangement used to apply initial stress to the top chord. 

In standard construction, when a rib is erected on centering without initial stress, the weight of the arch causes subsequent compression. This typically results in an excess of compression in the upper member at the crown and inflicts a bending stress. However, the engineer of the Niagara Falls and Clifton bridge employed bold expedients that successfully annulled this bending action.

## The Preceding Roebling Bridge
The steel arch replaced a remarkable suspension bridge built by J. A. Roebling between 1852 and 1855. At the time of its construction, the Roebling bridge had a span of 821 ft., making it the largest railway bridge of that era, with a height of 245 ft. above the river.

The suspension system of the older bridge consisted of four cables, each 10 in. in diameter. Each cable was composed of seven strands, with 520 parallel wires per strand, totaling 3640 wires per cable. These cables were supported on separate saddles with rollers on each pier. The original stiffening girder was a box-shaped braced girder made primarily of timber, measuring 25 ft. wide and 18 ft. deep, which housed a roadway within and a railway on top. This timber girder was replaced by an iron one in 1880 before the entire structure was eventually dismantled to make way for the steel arch.
