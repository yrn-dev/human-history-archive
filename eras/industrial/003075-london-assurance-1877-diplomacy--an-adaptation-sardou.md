# London Assurance (1877) and Diplomacy

## Production and Premiere
*London Assurance*, written by Boucicault, and *Diplomacy*—which was an adaptation of the play *Dora* by Sardou—were both premiered at the Prince of Wales's theatre. The Prince of Wales's theatre was a small playhouse located off Tottenham Court Road. These productions were part of the premieres that contributed to the fame of the venue.

## The Bancroft Management
The productions of *London Assurance* (1877) and *Diplomacy* occurred under the management of Sir Squire Bancroft and his wife, Effie Marie Wilton. Born on May 14, 1841, near London, Sir Squire Bancroft began his stage career in 1861 in Birmingham and spent several years performing in the provinces. He made his first London appearance in 1865 in *A Winning Hazard* by Wooler.

Effie Marie Wilton, born in 1840, managed the Prince of Wales's theatre and married Bancroft in 1868. Together, the couple associated themselves with the production of all the Robertson comedies, including *Society* (1865), *Ours* (1866), *Caste* (1867), *Play* (1868), *School* (1869), and *M.P.* (1870). Following the death of Robertson, they produced revivals of old comedies with an admirable company.

## Impact on English Drama
The management provided by the Bancrofts at the Prince of Wales's theatre is credited with initiating a new era in the development of the English stage. Their work had the effect of reviving interest in modern drama within London. 

During the 1870s, the English consciousness began to slowly recognize the enormous superiority of French drama, as exemplified by the works of Sardou, Dumas *fils*, and Augier. This influence is further reflected in the broader history of dramatic technique; the playwright Kotzebue, who was unprecedentedly popular on European and German stages, had an unmistakable influence on the technique of modern drama, extending from Scribe to Sardou.

## Later Career of the Bancrofts
In 1879, the Bancrofts moved their operations to the Haymarket. Their productions there included *Fedora* and *Odette* by Sardou—the latter for which they engaged Madame Modjeska—as well as *Lords and Commons* by Pinero and *Sweethearts* by W. S. Gilbert, along with revivals of their previous successes.

The Bancrofts retired in 1885 after having made a considerable fortune. Sir Squire Bancroft, who received a knighthood in 1897, returned to the stage in 1889 to perform as the abbe Latour in a revival of *Dead Heart* by Watts Phillips, appearing alongside Sir Henry Irving.
