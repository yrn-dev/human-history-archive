# Brewing and the Bass Family

## The Bass Brewing Firm
The Bass family established a prominent English brewing business founded by William Bass (born 1720). Originally a carrier, William Bass worked with Benjamin Printon, a brewer in Burton-on-Trent. By 1777, Bass began brewing his own beer to meet the increasing demand for Burton beer. Initially, the primary market for this product was St Petersburg, with beer transported via Hull and the Trent.

The business faced a significant shift in 1822 when the Russian government imposed a prohibitory duty on Burton ales, forcing brewers to focus on the domestic market. William Bass established a profitable trade in London. During this period, a misunderstanding between London brewers (proprietors of Hodgson's India Pale Ale) and the East India Company led to Bass being requested to create a beer suitable for the Indian market and climate. Following experiments, he developed Bass's pale ale, a lighter beer that became popular throughout India and helped the firm become the largest in Burton.

The company expanded further due to a chance event in 1827, when a vessel carrying Bass's beer wrecked in the Irish Channel. Salved cargo sold in Liverpool was well-received, allowing the firm to establish regular trade in Ireland and the north-west of England. The beer gained world-wide reputation after being supplied on draught at the 1851 Exhibition in London.

Following William Bass, the business was managed by his son, M. T. Bass, and subsequently by his grandson, Michael Thomas Bass (1799-1884). Michael Thomas Bass was known for his philanthropy and public spirit in Derby and Burton. In 1880, the business was converted into a limited liability company.

## Brewing Industry Standards and Literature
The brewing industry has been documented in various technical manuals and journals. Notable texts include *Principles and Practice of Brewing* by W.J. Sykes (1897), *A Handy Book for Brewers* by H.E. Wright (1897), and *Brewing and Malting* by Frank Thatcher (1898). Other significant works include *The Brewing Industry* by Julian L. Baker (1905), *Grundriss der Bierbrauerei* by E.J. Lintner (1904), and *Die Theorie und Praxis der Malzbereitung und Bierfabrikation* by J.E. Thausing (1898).

Technical knowledge was also disseminated through journals such as *The Brewers' Journal* (published in both London and New York), *The Brewing Trade Review* (London), *The Journal of the Institute of Brewing* (London), *Wochenschrift für Brauerei* (Berlin), and *Zeitschrift für das gesammte Brauwesen* (Munich).

Regarding industry standards, classifications in 1896 were set at 28 lb, but from 1897 onward, the standard rate was 32 lb to the bushel. In some contexts, these figures include rice and maize, while in others they are exclusive of them. Additionally, duty is typically assessed in fermenting vessels, as there is generally no separate "collecting vessel."
