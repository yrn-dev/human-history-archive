# Political and Intellectual Developments of 1847

The year 1847 was marked by significant political maneuvering in Bohemia, shifts in the parliamentary career of Thomas Babington Macaulay, and the publication of a major logical treatise by Augustus De Morgan.

## Bohemian National Aspirations
Following the re-establishment of European peace in 1815, national aspirations in Bohemia began to revive, initially manifesting through the revival of Bohemian literature. Political action was rendered impossible by the absolutist and arbitrary government of Prince Metternich within the lands ruled by the house of Habsburg. Despite this pressure, the estates of Bohemia adopted an attitude of opposition to the government of Vienna starting in 1845, asserting their right to vote on the country's taxes based on the constitution of 1627.

In 1847, in an effort to secure the support of the wider population, the estates determined to propose several measures at their session scheduled for the following year. These proposals included:
* Granting the towns a more extensive representation at the diet.
* Implementing more stringent control by the estates over the country's finances.
* Introducing the Bohemian language into all higher schools within the country.

These plans were interrupted by the revolutionary outbreak of 1848. Following the February revolution in Paris, great excitement occurred in Prague. On March 11, a large public meeting voted for a petition to the government of Vienna. This petition demanded liberal reforms, the convocation of a general diet for all Bohemian lands elected via extensive suffrage, and equal rights for the Bohemian language alongside German in all government offices. While an initial deputation received an equivocal response, Emperor Ferdinand declared on April 8 that equality of rights for both nationalities would be secured. He further stated that a new meeting of the estates of Bohemia, including representatives from principal towns, would be convoked, and that a general meeting of representatives from all parts of Austria would decide the reunion of Silesia and Moravia to Bohemia.

## Parliamentary and Literary Transitions of T.B. Macaulay
In England, 1847 marked a turning point in the life of Macaulay. Having entered parliament as the member for Edinburgh in 1838, he served as secretary at war in Lord Melbourne's ministry starting in 1839. After the fall of the Melbourne ministry, he published *Essays* in 1843 and *Lays of Ancient Rome* in 1842. 

Macaulay returned to office in 1846 as paymaster-general under Lord John Russell's administration. During the sessions of 1846-1847, his parliamentary activity was minimal, as he spoke only five times. This period allowed him to focus on his *History*, as his official duties were light. However, in the general election of July 1847, Macaulay lost his seat for Edinburgh. This event coincided with a shift in his faculties toward literature; he began to recoil from publicity and the social efforts required to represent a large constituency, viewing business as a source of worry.

## Logical Treatises of De Morgan
Late in 1847, De Morgan published his primary logical treatise, titled *Formal Logic, or the Calculus of Inference, Necessary and Probable*. The work included an elaborate development of his doctrine of the numerical definite syllogism and the syllogism, as well as a reprint of the *First Notions*. The treatise featured chapters on induction, probability, fallacies, and old logical terms. De Morgan utilized his perfect memory and wide reading to include quaint anecdotes and touches of humour to relieve the severity of the text.
