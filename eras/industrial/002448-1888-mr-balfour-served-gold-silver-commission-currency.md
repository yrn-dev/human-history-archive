# Mr. Balfour and the Gold and Silver Commission

## Service on the Commission
In 1888, Mr. Balfour served as a member of the Gold and Silver Commission. His involvement with the commission was linked to his interest in currency problems, specifically from the standpoint of bimetallism, which was among the more academic subjects that had engaged his attention.

## Context of Currency Standards
During the period surrounding Mr. Balfour's service, global monetary systems were characterized by a tension between different standards. In the early 19th century, the United States (1792) and France (1803) had legally established the double standard. In contrast, England had accepted the gold standard by law in 1816. While silver served as the currency for other European nations, the discovery of gold in Australia (1851) and California (1848) led to gold displacing silver in France and remaining the principal currency metal in the United States.

By 1865, the Latin Union was established, adopting the French currency system. At an international conference in Paris in 1867, gold was unanimously accepted as the standard for a proposed international system. However, subsequent political and economic shifts altered this trajectory. The Franco-German War (1870-71) resulted in Germany establishing a gold currency and demonetizing its previous silver currencies. Similarly, the United States formally established the gold dollar as the standard coin in 1873 and returned to specie payments in 1878.

## Bimetallism and the Depreciation of Silver
The late 19th century saw a rapid fall in the gold price of silver, driven by a decrease in gold production (which had peaked between 1850 and 1870) and the discovery of highly productive silver mines in the American Pacific states. This depreciation made the finances of the Indian government and Eastern trade insecure, and negatively impacted the German government and American silver producers.

In response to these pressures, some advocated for the double standard. This sentiment was reflected in the 1876 American silver commission and the English parliamentary committee on the depreciation of silver. Although an international monetary conference met in Paris in 1878 and recognized the need for silver to have a place in world money, European states rejected the proposal to adopt the double standard for general use. The United States, however, provided for the coinage of a specific amount of silver per month via the Bland-Allison Act of February 1878.

## Theoretical Perspectives
Monetary theory during this era included various proposals. Bimetallism was one such approach, while others proposed a "Joint-standard" or "Symmetallism," which consisted of a standard composed of both gold and silver. These theories were debated by various scholars and organizations; for example, the Bimetallic League and authors such as Barbour and F.A. Walker supported bimetallism, while others, including Giffen and Lord Farrer (of the Gold Standard Defence Assoc.), opposed it.

## Subsequent Career of Mr. Balfour
Following his work on the commission, Mr. Balfour's political career progressed. In 1891, he became the leader of the House of Commons and first lord of the treasury. He later led the opposition for three years before the Unionists returned to power in 1895. During his subsequent leadership of the House, he was involved in debates regarding foreign and domestic questions, including the championship of voluntary schools and the proposal of a Roman Catholic university for Ireland. He also managed the foreign office during Lord Salisbury's illness in 1898 and absence abroad, conducting negotiations with Russia regarding railways in North China.
