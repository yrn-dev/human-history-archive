# Chuffs

In the context of brickmaking and construction within Great Britain, "chuffs" refers to a specific quality of brick characterized by being badly burnt, cracked, and defective in shape.

## Classification Among British Bricks
The terminology used to describe brick qualities in Great Britain is noted as being most confusing. Chuffs are categorized alongside several other types of bricks based on their sound, shape, and the degree to which they were burnt:

*   **Stocks:** These are sound, hard, and well-burnt bricks used for ordinary purposes.
*   **Hard Stocks:** Bricks that are sound but over-burnt; these are typically used in positions where a good appearance is not required, such as wall footings.
*   **Shippers:** These are hard-burnt and sound bricks that possess an imperfect shape. They are frequently used as ballast for ships.
*   **Grizzles:** Bricks that are sound and of fair shape but are under-burnt. They are used for inferior work and in situations where they will not be heavily loaded.
*   **Place-bricks:** These are defective and under-burnt, used primarily for temporary work.
*   **Burrs:** These are lumps that have run together or vitrified during the burning process, used for garden work and rough walling.

Other specialized bricks include **Rubbers** (or **Cutters**), which are sandy and suitable for rubbing to shape or cutting with a wire saw; **Pressed bricks**, which are moulded under hydraulic pressure and often feature a deep hollow or "frog" to reduce weight and provide a key for mortar; **Blue bricks**, which are vitrified, heavy, and durable for engineering work; **Fire-bricks**, designed to withstand great heat; and **Glazed bricks**, which are either enamelled or salt-glazed for sanitary or decorative purposes.

## Material Properties and Manufacturing
The quality of bricks, including those made from fireclays, depends heavily on their ability to resist fusion at high temperatures. To prevent warping or cracking before firing, clays must be plastic enough to be moulded but stiff enough not to contract too strongly during drying. To guard against this tendency, a proportion of burnt material—such as ground potsherds or old bricks—is often added to the fresh clay paste. In the case of crucible making, graphite or coke dust is used for this purpose.
