# Commercial Payments and Financial Instruments

Commercial transactions throughout history have utilized a diverse array of payment methods, ranging from the exchange of physical goods and produce to the development of complex banking systems and credit instruments.

## Payment in Kind and Early Commerce
Payment in kind—the use of goods or produce as a substitute for money—has been a recurring feature of commercial and fiscal systems. In early bookkeeping, before the widespread use of money, records were often limited to the receipt and issue of various kinds of goods. 

In certain ancient legal frameworks, such as the Code mentioned in the archive, payment in kind remained common in commercial matters. While contracts typically stipulated cash payments based on standards from cities such as Larsa, Babylon, Carchemish, and Assyria, the Code mandated that debtors be allowed to pay in produce according to a statutory scale. Furthermore, if a debtor possessed neither crops nor money, creditors were prohibited from refusing other goods.

Similar systems of payment in kind appeared in royal villages where reeves collected dues. Landowners provided payments known as *fostor* or *feorm*, which consisted of fixed quantities of articles. According to Ine's Laws, these payments for a unit of ten hides could include cheese, butter, honey, ale, loaves, fodder, oxen, sheep, geese, hens, and eels or salmon. Other payments, such as those from the *gafolgelda* (*tributarius*), included barley (six pounds per worker) and a white cloak (*hwitel*) valued at six pence per household.

## The Evolution of Cash and Currency
The term "cash" originates from the Old French *casse* (a box or chest for money) and currently refers to ready money or coin. In banking and commercial contexts, it may be specifically confined to specie or used to describe bank-notes in opposition to securities, drafts, or bills. "Cashing" refers to the process of converting negotiable instruments, such as cheques, into coin.

To address the difficulties merchants faced with the varying values of foreign currencies, exchange banks were established. The Bank of Hamburg served as a primary example of such an institution. By creating "bank money"—a money of account that could be transferred between holders—the bank provided a common standard of value. This eliminated the delays and expenses associated with verifying the purity, weight, and intrinsic value of various international coins. In this system, the "Mark Banco" acted as a representative of an equal value of silver, ensuring the currency remained purely metallic.

## Credit and Bills of Exchange
As credit became a more central factor in commerce, bookkeeping evolved beyond the "cash system" (which records only receipts and payments of money) to include movements of credit. While governments and charitable institutions still frequently use the cash system, businesses must record unpaid services and credit to ascertain their actual financial position.

The bill of exchange emerged as a device to avoid the physical transmission of cash across different cities or countries to settle trade debts. Originally, a bill was a direction for one person in a specific location to pay a sum to another for value received. While bills now serve as a substitute for money and act as a commercial currency, they are not identical to money; a creditor is not obligated to accept a bill in payment of a debt unless previously agreed. In England, the obligation to pay a bill rests on actual agreement, and if a bill is dishonoured, the original debt revives. To be valid, a bill of exchange must be an unconditional order to pay; instruments dependent on a contingency or a specific fund are invalid as bills.

## Commercial Law and Bankruptcy
In France, the Commercial Code of 1807 (amended in 1838) regulates bankruptcy, which is defined as the state of a trader unable to meet commercial engagements. This state is known as *faillite*, while insolvency involving fraud or misconduct is termed *banqueroute frauduleuse* or *banqueroute simple*. Only traders or commercial companies can be declared bankrupt, and the debt involved must be a commercial debt.

A trader may be adjudicated bankrupt by the court *proprio motu*, via a creditor's petition, or through their own petition. A petitioning debtor must file a declaration of suspension within fifteen days, including a true account of assets, debts, profits, losses, and personal expenses. The Tribunal of Commerce then appoints a *juge commissaire* to supervise a *syndic provisoire*, who manages the estate. Bankruptcy concludes through a sale of assets (*union*), a composition by relinquishment of assets, or an ordinary composition (*concordat*). Under the influence of a National Convention resolution from the French Revolution, any unpaid balance remaining after bankruptcy is considered a debt of honour, and the trader is not rehabilitated until every penny is paid.
