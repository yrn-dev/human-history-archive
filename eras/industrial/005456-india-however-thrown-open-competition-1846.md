# Trade and Commercial Competition in India

The history of commerce in India has been defined by a succession of European powers struggling for political and commercial footholds, transitioning from early monopolies to a system of free trade.

## Early European Competition
Following the voyage of Vasco da Gama to Calicut, various European powers competed for control of the east. Initially, the sea trade of India was managed by the Red Sea Arabs, but the Portuguese seized control through a series of sea fights, utilizing larger ships with heavier armaments. This allowed Lisbon to surpass Venice as the primary mart for oriental spices.

By the seventeenth century, the Dutch challenged this monopoly, establishing a vast network of fortified stations. Their holdings included twelve establishments in India, six in Ceylon, two in Persia, and a settlement at the Cape of Good Hope, as well as Mauritius. However, the Dutch resolution to exclude other European traders provoked hostile competition from the English, French, Danes, and Swedes. The English republican admiral Blake dealt early blows to the Dutch monopoly in European waters. By the start of the eighteenth century, the English and French were in vigorous competition with the Dutch for privileges and trade throughout India, establishing headquarters at Calcutta, Bombay, and Madras for the English, and Chandernagore and Pondicherry for the French.

## The East India Company and the Shift to Free Trade
Within the British imperial system, the East India Company held one of the most important monopolies. For nearly two hundred years, British trade was governed by a mercantile system intended to curtail commercial intercourse with the rest of the world. This was exemplified by the Navigation Act of 1660, which restricted the import and export of goods from British colonies to British ships.

A significant shift occurred in 1813, when the trade of India was thrown open to competition. This movement toward commercial emancipation continued as Great Britain introduced free trade at home following the repeal of the Corn Laws in 1846. Consequently, in 1846, British colonies that had not yet been granted responsible government were specifically empowered to abolish differential duties on foreign trade. The Navigation Acts themselves were eventually repealed in 1849.

## Regional Commercial Development
The development of specific Indian ports was often tied to political and infrastructural changes. Bombay experienced a period of unrestricted development following the annexation of the Deccan and the British victory over the Mahrattas. Its growth was further fostered by Mountstuart Elphinstone in 1819. The city's population grew from 236,000 in 1838 to 821,000 by 1891, a leap attributed to the foundation of cotton factories, the opening of the Suez Canal, and the completion of the first railway line in 1853. Although a financial crisis occurred in 1866-1867 due to falling cotton prices after the American war, the city recovered, though it later suffered from a plague that caused a 7% decline in exports.

In the North Kanara district of Bombay, the sea-coast town of Kumta maintained a considerable trade and specialized in sandal-wood carving. However, its commercial importance declined following the opening of the Southern Mahratta railway system.

## Social Perspectives on Competition
The role of the caste system in India has been a subject of debate regarding its impact on commercial competition. Some writers, such as James Mill, argued that caste was fatal to the principle of free competition and opposed to individual happiness. 

Conversely, other authorities challenged this view. In 1798, Colebrooke observed that professions were generally open to all descriptions of persons and that those unable to earn a subsistence in their own profession could follow the trade of a higher or lower caste, noting that even Brahmans sometimes exercised the menial professions of a Sudra. He asserted that in Bengal, there were sufficient people willing to apply to any occupation to allow for the unlimited extension of any manufacture. This was supported by Elphinstone, who stated he had never encountered a case of degradation from caste during his experience in India, citing the Indian army—where men of all castes unite—as an illustration.
