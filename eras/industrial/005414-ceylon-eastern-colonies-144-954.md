# Ceylon and Eastern Colonies

The Ceylon and Eastern Colonies represent a significant grouping of British dependencies in Asia. This region, which includes Ceylon, the Straits Settlements, Hong-Kong, North Borneo, and Sarawak, has a combined population of 5,144,954. These territories serve as critical intermediate ports for coaling stations on the eastern trade routes, completing a chain of connection that includes other strategic locations such as Aden.

## Ceylon: History and Governance
The island of Ceylon, also known in antiquity as Taprobana and Serendib, has a history distinct from that of India, despite being geographically an annex to it. Tradition states that an Aryan-speaking colony from the Ganges valley invaded the island in the 6th century B.C. During the time of Asoka, Ceylon received Buddhism from north India, subsequently becoming a center of religious culture that influenced Siam and Burma.

The island's medieval period was characterized by conflicts between Tamil invaders and native sovereigns. While a powerful native dynasty ruled in the 12th century, the island was attacked by the Chinese in 1408. Starting in 1505, Ceylon was plagued by European squabbles and attacks, leading to partial subjugation first by the Portuguese and later by the Dutch. The English expelled the Dutch in 1796.

Administratively, Ceylon is classified as a crown colony. In such colonies, the British crown maintains entire control over legislation, and public officers are managed by the home government. The rule of the home government through the Colonial Office in Ceylon has been described as verging on autocracy. Laws are made by the governor with the concurrence of a crown council; the council in Ceylon is an example of one whose authority rests wholly on the crown, typically seen in colonies acquired by conquest or cession.

## Flora and Natural Environment
The flora of Ceylon shows a distinct connection to the peninsula of India and eastern tropical Africa. This is evident in the similarity of tropical forms and the identity of various families and genera. Notably, the mountain flora of Ceylon and the Indian peninsula resembles that of Abyssinia. A defining characteristic of the flora in both Ceylon and the Indian peninsula is the absence of cycads, conifers, and oaks, even in mountain elevations where these plants are abundant to the north and east.

Additionally, the genus *Nepenthes* is specially developed in the Malayan area and is found in Ceylon and as far north as the Khasi hills, though it is absent from the peninsula of India and the Himalaya.
