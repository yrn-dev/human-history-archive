# The Close of the 19th Century: Geodetic and Political Developments

The close of the 19th century was a period characterized by the completion of significant technical surveys, the navigation of political crises in Europe, and the evolution of imperial and naval infrastructures.

## Geodetic Triangulation in Asia
During the close of the 19th century, the final links of the great geodetic triangulation of India were forged, specifically regarding the peninsula. These efforts were part of a broader system of exact surveys in Asia based upon triangulation. In Russia, these surveys extended as far east as Chinese Turkestan, reaching a longitude of approximately 75 degrees East of Greenwich. In India, geodetic triangulation provided the foundation for exact surveys extending as far east as the eastern boundaries of Burma, at approximately 100 degrees East.

Despite these advancements, the geodetic connection between these systems and European systems remained to be accomplished. To determine longitudes differentially with Greenwich, the telegraphic method was applied with increased rigor after 1890. This resulted in a slight correction of about 2" of arc to previous determinations made through Suez. This specific determination was carried out through four distinct arcs:
1. Greenwich to Potsdam
2. Potsdam to Teheran
3. Teheran to Bushire
4. Bushire to Karachi

## Political Crisis in the Dual Monarchy
At the close of the 19th century, both states of the Dual Monarchy experienced severe political crises. In Austria, parliamentary life became paralyzed due to a feud between Czechs and Germans. This conflict was a direct result of the Badeni language ordinances of 1897 and an indirect result of the growth of Slav influence—specifically that of Poles and Czechs—during the Taaffe era from 1879 to 1893.

During the closing years of the century and the beginning of the next, political life in Austria reached a low ebb. The constitution was followed in letter but not in spirit. Government operations were managed by cabinets of officials utilizing the emergency clause (paragraph 14) of the constitution. While ministers were nominally responsible to parliament, they were in practice responsible only to the emperor.

## Naval Evolution and Oil Reserves
The transition of the British Fleet during this era was marked by a sequence of dependent developments. An original desire to enlarge the gun led to the creation of the Fast Division, which in turn necessitated a reliance on oil fuel for vital units of the Fleet. This shift led to the general adoption of oil fuel and the requirement to establish a great oil reserve.

Although this transition faced tremendous opposition on the Naval Estimates and involved enormous expense, it led to the Anglo-Persian Oil agreement and contract. This agreement began with an initial public investment of two millions, which later increased to five millions. This contract secured a substantial proportion of the Navy's oil supply and allowed the Government to acquire a controlling share in oil interests and properties, which were subsequently valued at scores of millions sterling.

## Imperial and Commercial Links
Throughout the development of the empire, trade served as the most potent bond of union and the most active cause of expansion. The foundations of the empire were laid upon settlement in temperate regions and trade with the tropics. Various trading companies played central roles: one won India, others colonized the north-western districts of Canada and founded most of the West Indian and American colonies. 

In the 19th century, chartered companies extended the British flag into the interior of the African continent from the west, east, and south. Additionally, trading companies developed Fiji and Borneo. The material links connecting the crown with its outlying possessions were further strengthened by the imperial navy's protection of colonial commerce, while prosperous trade kept the Australasian colonies within the empire.

## European River Communications
During the latter half of the 19th century, significant efforts were made to improve the Danube as a means of communication. In 1816, Bavaria and Austria arranged for the common utilization of the river's upper portion. Key developments included the 1844 construction of the Ludwigs Canal by King Louis of Bavaria, which connected the Rhine at Mainz with the Danube at Kelheim via the Main, Regnitz, and Altmuhl rivers.

Further improvements included the removal of obstructing rocks below Grein in 1853 and the draining of the Donau-moos between Ingolstadt and Neuburg, which was completed around 1835. In Vienna, an artificial channel 10 miles in length and 330 yards in width was constructed starting in 1866 at a cost of over three millions sterling. This project removed 12,000,000 cubic metres of sand and gravel to protect the city from inundations and improve navigability. One remaining uncompleted link in the navigable channel between Hamburg and Sulina is a 125-mile canal from the Danube to the Moldau at Budweis.
