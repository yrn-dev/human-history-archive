# Adamson Seams and Flanging in Boiler Construction

In the field of boilermaking, flanging is the process of bending or turning over the edges of a plate to create a means of union with other plates. This practice is utilized in various components, including the fire-boxes of locomotive boilers, the crowns of vertical boilers, the front and back plates of marine boilers, the back end-plates of Cornish and Lancashire boilers, and the ends of conical cross-tubes. Specifically, flanging is used for the Adamson seams of furnace flues.

## The Adamson Joint and Furnace Flues
In large stationary boilers, such as the Lancashire boiler—which typically measures 26 to 30 feet in length with diameters between 6-1/2 and 8 feet—two cylindrical furnace flues extend the length of the boiler. These furnace flues are constructed in sections, usually 3 to 3-1/2 feet long. Each section consists of a plate bent into a cylindrical form with a welded longitudinal joint.

The ends of these sections are flanged and joined together using an "Adamson" joint. These joints provide two primary structural advantages: they act as stiffening rings to prevent the flue from collapsing, and they ensure that neither double thickness of plate nor rivets are exposed to the action of the fire.

## Evolution of Flanging Methods
The adoption of flanging superseded an older system of union that utilized angle iron rings to form two sides of a rectangular section. Because these rings had a sharp angular form and lacked elasticity, they were a frequent source of explosions in steam boilers and grooving. In contrast, a flange turned with a large radius provides the necessary elasticity to counteract the effects of temperature changes.

Historically, flanging was performed by a skilled workman known as an angle ironsmith. This manual process involved heating a length of edge (approximately a foot or 10 inches) and hammering it around the edge of an iron block of a suitable shape. This process was repeated in successive "heats" until the work was finished.

## Modern Machine Flanging
In modern boiler shops, hand work has been largely replaced by machinery to save time and reduce injury to the material. Different types of machinery are used depending on the size and type of the component:

*   **Hydraulic Presses:** Plates with diameters of 4, 6, or 8 feet, as well as locomotive boiler fire-box plates, have their flanges bent in a single squeeze between massive dies.
*   **Specialized Presses:** For the ends of marine boilers that are too large for standard hydraulic presses, a special form of press is used to bend edges over in successive heats.
*   **Adamson Seam Machines:** The flanges of Adamson seams are rolled over using a special machine. In this process, a length of flue is rotated on a table while the flange is turned over between revolving rollers, a process completed within a minute.

## Materials and Engineering Requirements
Boiler, bridge, and girder shops employ similar materials—chiefly steel and iron—and methods involving rolled sections and plates. However, boilermaking requires specific provisions due to high pressures and temperatures. To prevent fractures or grooving, boilers must be constructed with materials that combine strength when heated with high ductility. 

Engineering solutions to ensure elasticity and freedom of movement include the judicious use of staying, the limitation of tube lengths, and the flanging of certain parts. Additionally, materials are subjected to bending tests, both cold and hot, and steel or iron with a high percentage of elongation is selected. While welding is common in bridge and girder work, it is even more prevalent in boilermaking; furnace flues, for example, are always welded.
