# Karl Gottlieb Bretschneider

Karl Gottlieb Bretschneider (1776–1848) was a German theologian and scholar. Born in Gersdorf, Saxony, he began his higher education at the university of Leipzig in 1794, where he spent four years studying theology. After a period of hesitation regarding his career, he passed the examination for *candidatus theologiae* with great distinction in 1802. This achievement brought him to the attention of F.V. Reinhard, the court-preacher at Dresden and author of *System der christlichen Moral* (1788–1815), who served as Bretschneider's patron and friend for the rest of his life.

## Academic and Ecclesiastical Career

Between 1804 and 1806, Bretschneider served as a *Privat-docent* at the university of Wittenberg, delivering lectures on theology and philosophy. During this tenure, he produced his work on the development of dogma, titled *Systematische Entwickelung aller in der Dogmatik vorkommenden Begriffe nach den symbolischen Schriften der evangelisch-lutherischen und reformirten Kirche* (1805; 4th edition 1841). He also produced an edition of Ecclesiasticus featuring a Latin commentary.

The advance of Napoleon's French army into Prussia prompted Bretschneider to abandon his university career and leave Wittenberg. With the assistance of Reinhard, he became the pastor of Schneeberg in Saxony in 1807. By 1808, he was promoted to superintendent of the church of Annaberg. In this role, he handled various matters of ecclesiastical law according to the canon law of Saxony. However, due to official duties that interfered with his theological studies and a climate that did not suit him, he sought a change. He earned a doctor of theology degree from Wittenberg in August 1812. In 1816, he was appointed general superintendent at Gotha, a position he held until his death in 1848. This period in Gotha marked his most significant era of literary activity.

## Research on China and the Mongols

Bretschneider conducted extensive research into ancient Chinese works and the history of the Mongol Empire. He translated the *Ch’ue keng lu*, a text containing detailed descriptions of the palace grounds and the palace of the Mongols. Through his work, he analyzed the layout of the ancient palaces in Peking (Khan-baligh), noting that ancient works mentioned three concentric enclosures:
*   The innermost enclosure, which enclosed the *Ta-nei*.
*   The middle enclosure, known as *Huang-ch’eng* or *Kung-ch’eng*, which corresponded to the wall of the prohibited city and had a circuit of approximately 6 *li*.
*   An outer wall or rampart with a circuit of 20 *li*, corresponding to the wall of the imperial city.

Bretschneider also provided insights into the architecture of Cambaluc, suggesting that Marco Polo may have mistaken the *lou* (tower-like buildings) located over the gates and in the angles of the galleries for palaces. He hypothesized that these towers may have been used to store the war harness of the Khan. Additionally, he identified the *Ling yu* (Divine Park) to the east of the *Wan-sui shan* as a place where rare beasts and birds were kept and where officers were entertained before the Emperor departed for Shangtu.

## Other Scholarly Contributions

In his work *Mediæval Researches*, Bretschneider detailed the movements of Hulagu, noting that Hulagu left the residence of his brother in Karakorum on May 2, 1253, to organize his army at his ordo, subsequently starting for the west on October 19 of that year and arriving at Samarkand in September 1255.

Bretschneider also contributed to the understanding of historical artifacts. In his work *Peking*, he described a large varnished wooden jar lined with silver, suggesting this might explain the *verniques* or *vaselle vernicate d’oro* mentioned by Marco Polo, which were large enough to hold drinks for eight to ten people.

## Posthumous Works and Legacy

Following his death in 1848, Bretschneider's life and work continued to be documented. He wrote an autobiography titled *Aus meinem Leben: Selbstbiographie von K.G. Bretschneider*, published in Gotha in 1851. This work was later translated with notes by Professor George E. Day and appeared in the *American Biblical Repository and Bibliotheca Sacra* in 1852 and 1853. Other mentions of his life and work appear in the writings of Wüstemann (*Bretschneideri Memoria*, 1848), Neudecker in *Die allgemeine Kirchenzeitung* (1848), A.G. Farrar's *Critical History of Free Thought* (1862), and the *Realencyklopädie* edited by Herzog-Hauck (1897).
