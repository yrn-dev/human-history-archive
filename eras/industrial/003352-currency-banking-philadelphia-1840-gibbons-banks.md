# Currency and Banking

The history of banking and currency in the United States is characterized by early experimentation and the gradual development of formal financial institutions. In the colonial era, most enterprises focused on the issuance of paper money rather than the establishment of commercial banks. Speculative banking during this period was significantly limited by the Bubble Act (6 Geo. I. c. 18), an English law passed following the bursting of the South Sea Bubble. This act forbade the creation of banking companies without a special charter and was extended to the American colonies in 1740.

## Early Banking in Pennsylvania
The serious history of banking in the United States is considered to have begun with the foundation of the Bank of Pennsylvania. This institution was created through a project by Philadelphia citizens intended to provide rations for the continental army. The bank issued its first bills in 1780, which consisted of interest-bearing notes payable at a future date. To secure the advances made in continental money, shareholders utilized bills of exchange totaling L150,000 drawn on American envoys in Europe, though these were not intended for negotiation.

## The Bank of the United States and Nicholas Biddle
A central figure in American finance was Nicholas Biddle (1786–1844), a Philadelphia native who graduated from Princeton in 1801. After serving as secretary to ministers in France and Great Britain and practicing law in Philadelphia, Biddle was appointed by President Monroe in 1819 as one of five government directors of the Bank of the United States. He became the bank's president in 1823, succeeding Langdon Cheves.

Biddle was noted for his marked ability and conservative management policy. However, he faced violent assaults on his character and policies from President Andrew Jackson and his followers. When the bank's national charter lapsed in 1836, it was immediately chartered by the state of Pennsylvania as the "Bank of the United States, of Pennsylvania." Biddle continued as president until 1839; the bank subsequently failed two years later.

## National Banks and Regulatory Framework
National banks in the United States possess most of the powers associated with commercial banks, though they are prohibited from holding real estate unless it is taken for debt or serves as their banking house. These institutions are subject to oversight by the comptroller of the currency, to whom they must submit five reports annually at unannounced dates. They are also subject to visitations by bank examiners.

Under existing law, the only required reserves against notes are 5%, which are kept in Washington for redemption. This redemption system has been described as defective because redemptions are not authorized in other locations, resulting in notes reaching the treasury only about once every two years on average. Regarding the retirement of notes, banks were for many years limited to retiring $3,000,000 monthly, a limit that was increased to $9,000,000 per month by an act on March 4, 1907.

## Financial Instability and the Panic of 1907
The vulnerability of the banking system was evidenced during the panic of 1907. The crisis was precipitated by over-speculation by a group of national banks and the suspension of the Knickerbocker Trust Company on October 22, which held deposits of $48,000,000. This led to runs on other companies and a deficit in New York bank reserves of $38,838,825 during the week of November 2.

To combat the crisis, arrangements were made to import nearly $100,000,000 in foreign gold. During the panic, the amount of currency that either disappeared or was added to circulation exceeded $275,000,000, representing nearly one-tenth of the country's usual circulation volume. Payment of cheques in currency was suspended in New York from October 28, 1907, until approximately the beginning of 1908. By December 28, 1907, the total bank-note circulation had risen to an abnormal level of $687,340,835, which was reduced during the spring of 1908.
