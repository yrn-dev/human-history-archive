# James Watt and the Development of the Steam Engine

James Watt was a mathematical instrument maker from Glasgow who is credited with improving the steam-pumping engine and making it available for the driving of machinery. His work contributed to a wave of universal change in human power and the material conditions of life, serving as a primary "firstfruit" of scientific efforts that transformed Western civilization.

## Early Development and the Bo'ness Model
In the 18th century, the first steam engines were primarily pumping engines used to keep water out of newly opened coal mines, which provided coke for iron smelting. Watt's improvements to this technology allowed the engine to be used for broader industrial applications. 

A significant moment in this development occurred at Kinneil House, located one mile south of Bo'ness, Scotland. Kinneil House was occupied for several years starting in 1764 by Dr. John Roebuck, the founder of the Carron Iron Works. At the invitation of Dr. Roebuck, James Watt constructed a model of his steam engine at this location. The model was tested in a colliery that is now disused. While Roebuck eventually lost all his money in the salt works and coal mines he established at Bo'ness, the site remains notable for this early testing of Watt's engine.

## Industrial Application and the Soho Factory
The perfection of the steam engine is associated with the partnership between James Watt and Matthew Boulton in Birmingham. Their collaboration centered on the Soho factory, which is regarded as one of the town's most precious heritages. Watt maintained a private workshop in the garret of his house at Heathfield, which has been preserved exactly as he left it.

Watt was a member of the Lunar Society, a group that met monthly around the time of the full moon. This brilliant company included other distinguished figures such as Boulton, Joseph Priestley, Josiah Wedgwood, Erasmus Darwin, Samuel Parr, Dr. William Withering, Richard Lovell Edgeworth, Sir Joseph Banks, Sir William Herschel, Dr. Solander, John Roebuck, and James Keir.

The practical application of Watt's improved engine expanded throughout the late 18th century. In 1785, the first engine used for driving machinery was installed in a Nottingham cotton mill. Later, in 1804, Trevithick adapted the Watt engine for transport, resulting in the creation of the first locomotive.

## Influence on Navigation and Engineering
Watt's influence extended to other engineers and the field of maritime transport. Robert Fulton, an American engineer, became acquainted with James Watt, as well as the Earl Stanhope and the duke of Bridgewater, during a period in England. This acquaintance, along with their influence, led Fulton to focus on engineering and canal construction.

Fulton later applied steam power to navigation. In 1803, while in Paris, he succeeded in propelling a boat by steam power. In 1807, Fulton and Robert R. Livingston operated the "Clermont" on the Hudson between Albany and New York; this vessel was engined by Boulton & Watt of Birmingham.

## Commemorations and Legacy
The legacy of James Watt is preserved through various monuments and institutions:
* **Birmingham:** A "singularly beautiful" statue of Watt by Alexander Munro is located in Ratcliff Place. Additionally, a famous statue by Sir Francis Chantrey is housed in a special chapel at Handsworth church.
* **Greenock:** The town features the James Watt dock, completed in 1886. This dock covers an area of 2000 ft. by 400 ft. with a low-water depth of 32 ft., constructed at a cost of £650,000.
* **The Watt Institution:** Founded in 1837 by Watt's son, this institution contains a public library (established 1783), a scientific library presented by Watt himself in 1816, and a marble statue of Watt created by Sir Francis Chantrey.
