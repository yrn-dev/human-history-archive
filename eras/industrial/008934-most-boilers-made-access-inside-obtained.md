# Access and Maintenance of Boiler Interiors

In the construction of boilers, openings must always be provided to allow for examination and cleaning. The method of obtaining access to the interior varies depending on the size, type, and specific design of the boiler.

## Man-holes and General Access
When the openings provided for cleaning and examination are large enough to permit a person to enter the boiler, they are termed man-holes. In most boilers, access to the inside is achieved through man-holes located in the water-chamber ends and the steam-chest. 

These man-holes are typically oval in shape. This specific geometry allows the closing doors to be placed on the inside of the boiler, ensuring that the internal steam pressure tends to keep the doors shut. These doors are secured by one or two bolts attached to "dogs" or cross-bars on the exterior of the boiler. It is critical that these doors fit the holes with high accuracy to prevent jointing material from being forced out of position. In the rare instances where doors are fitted on the outside—meaning steam pressure would tend to push them open—they are secured by several bolts to ensure that the failure of a single bolt does not allow the door to be forced off.

## Access in Specific Boiler Types

### Water-Tube Boilers
Different water-tube designs employ various methods for internal access and tube maintenance:

*   **Yarrow Boilers:** In large Yarrow boilers, man-holes in the steam-chest and water-chamber ends provide access. However, in smaller versions used in torpedo boats, the water-chambers are too small for man-holes; instead, they are constructed in two parts connected by a bolted joint to make all tube ends accessible. Additionally, the tubes in Yarrow boilers are spaced to allow for external cleaning from the front and cross-ways in two directions.
*   **Reed Boilers:** Access to the top ends of the tubes is obtained via the steam-chest, while the lower tube ends are accessed through numerous doors located in the water-chambers.
*   **Babcock & Wilcox Boilers:** These boilers feature headers with holes on two opposite sides. One side connects the headers to the tubes, while the other side provides access for cleaning and fixing the tubes. These outer holes are oval and closed by special fittings with outer cover plates. The oval shape allows the inside fitting to be placed from the outside, which prevents a major outrush of water or steam if a bolt breaks.
*   **Woodeson Boilers:** The design of the Woodeson boiler allows for the insides of all tubes to be readily inspected and for any individual tube to be renewed without displacing other parts of the boiler.
*   **Durr Boilers:** In these boilers, holes in the outer plate are closed by small round doors that are fitted from the inside.

### Vertical and Cylindrical Boilers
For smaller installations or areas with limited floor space, vertical boilers are often used:

*   **Simple Vertical Boilers:** These typically feature a hand-hole closed by a suitable door in the outer shell, positioned opposite each cross tube for cleaning purposes.
*   **Cochran Boilers:** In this design, the combustion chamber is made with a "dry" back. Rather than having a water space at the back of the chamber, doors lined with firebrick are fitted to provide easy access to the tube ends.

## Auxiliary Components
Beyond access for personnel and cleaning, boilers are equipped with several fittings for operational maintenance. Blow-out cocks are installed to allow the boiler to be emptied. To regulate and monitor the system, every boiler is supplied with a steam-gauge to indicate pressure and a stop-valve to regulate the admission of steam into the steam-pipes. Safety-valves are also required; in marine and locomotive boilers, these are held closed by steel springs, while stationary boilers use levers with weights. At least one safety-valve includes easing gear, allowing it to be lifted manually to blow off steam.
