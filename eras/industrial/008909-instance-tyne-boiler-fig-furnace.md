# Boiler Furnaces and Designs

The design and configuration of boiler furnaces vary significantly depending on the boiler type, the intended application—such as stationary use or marine installation—and the fuel source employed. These designs focus on optimizing heating surfaces, managing the flow of combustion gases, and ensuring structural integrity under pressure.

## Vertical Boiler Furnaces
Vertical boilers are typically utilized for small installations or where floor space is limited. The most common variety is the simple vertical boiler, which features a plain vertical fire-box that tapers slightly in diameter. In this design, water fills the space between the fire-box and the shell, and an internal smoke stack traverses the steam space.

For applications requiring more heating surface than a simple cross-tube boiler can provide, specialized vertical designs are used:
*   **The "Tyne" Boiler:** This boiler features a hemispherical furnace. The products of combustion are directed into an upper combustion chamber. This chamber is traversed by several vertical water-tubes of smaller diameter and four or more inclined water-tubes approximately 9 inches in diameter.
*   **The "Victoria" Boiler:** Produced by Messrs Clarke, Chapman & Co., this boiler also employs a hemispherical furnace. However, it leads furnace gases to an internal combustion chamber and then through numerous horizontal smoke-tubes to a smoke-box located on the side of the boiler.

## Cylindrical and Tank Boilers
Stationary "tank" boilers, such as the Lancashire and its modification, the Galloway, are typically 26 to 30 feet long with diameters between 6-1/2 and 8 feet. The Lancashire boiler contains two cylindrical furnace flues that extend the full length of the boiler. These flues are constructed in sections of 3 to 3-1/2 feet, joined by "Adamson" joints. These joints serve as stiffening rings to prevent collapse and ensure that neither double thickness of plate nor rivets are exposed to the fire.

The Galloway boiler modifies this design so that the two furnaces extend only about 9 or 10 feet into the boiler. They lead into a large flue or chamber containing "Galloway" tubes—conical tubes with flanges at each end—which extend to the end of the boiler. These tubes increase the heating surface, promote water circulation, and improve evaporative efficiency by mixing flue gases. A similar single-furnace version for boilers under 6-1/2 feet in diameter is known as a "Cornish" boiler.

## Marine and Multitubular Boilers
Cylindrical multitubular return tube boilers are widely used in merchant steamers, with diameters reaching up to 17 feet and working pressures generally between 160 and 200 lb per sq. in. These are categorized as single-ended or double-ended:
*   **Single-ended boilers:** Furnaces are fitted at one end only, ranging from one to four in number (three being most common). Each furnace generally has its own combustion chamber, though in four-furnace models, one chamber may be shared by the two middle furnaces or by pairs of side furnaces.
*   **Double-ended boilers:** Furnaces are fitted at both ends. While some have separate combustion chambers, it is more common for one chamber to serve two furnaces (one at each end).

Furnace construction in these boilers varies by size and pressure. Smaller sizes often use plain cylindrical types with plate thickness from 3/8 to 3/4 inch. For higher pressures and larger sizes, corrugated or ribbed furnaces (such as the Morison, Fox, and Deighton types) are adopted because they are stronger against collapse than plain furnaces of the same thickness.

## Combustion Chamber Connections
There are two primary methods of connecting furnaces to combustion chambers. In one design, the furnace is riveted to the "wrapper" or side plate of the combustion chamber and flanged at the crown for riveting to the tube plate. The second design, known as the "Gourlay back end," involves contracting the furnace end into an oval conical form and flanging it outwards around its circumference. The furnace is then riveted to the tube plate, which extends to the bottom of the combustion chamber. The Gourlay design allows a damaged furnace to be replaced without disturbing the end plates, although it is less stiff and requires more longitudinal stays.

## Fuel Systems and Furnace Gear
Furnaces are adapted based on the fuel used:
*   **Liquid Fuel:** In boilers like the ss. "Murex," furnace gear includes an oil-pipe, an injector, and a swivel to move the injector clear of the furnace. Practice shows that removing the fire-bars used for coal produces better results with liquid fuel. In the ss. "Trocas," fire-brick is used to protect riveted seams and the back of the combustion-box from the direct impact of petroleum flames. The Meyer system utilizes an annular projection at the furnace mouth and spiral passages to heat air before it enters the furnace.
*   **Gaseous and Other Fuels:** For new constructions intended for fuels other than coal alone, it is desirable to use longer, narrower boiler tubes and larger combustion chambers.
*   **Mechanical Stoking:** To avoid the labor and cold-air intake of hand-firing, "chain-grate" stokers may be used. This system uses an endless chain of cast-iron bars that move coal slowly from front to back, allowing volatile portions to distill at the front and combustion to complete at the back.
