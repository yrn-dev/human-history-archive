# ASBURY, FRANCIS (1745-1816)

Francis Asbury was an American clergyman and a prominent figure in the development of Methodism in the United States. Born on August 20, 1745, at Hamstead Bridge in the parish of Handsworth, near Birmingham, in Staffordshire, England, he was born to poor parents. His early education took place at the village school of Barre, and at the age of fourteen, he began an apprenticeship with a maker of tongues, also known as "buckle chapes."

## Early Ministry and Arrival in America
Asbury converted to Methodism during his thirteenth year, and it is probable that his parents were among the early converts of John Wesley. By the age of sixteen, Asbury had become a local preacher. He was characterized as a fluent and simple speaker, a skill that led John Wesley to enroll him as a regular itinerant minister in 1767.

In 1771, Asbury volunteered for missionary work in the American colonies, arriving in Philadelphia in October of that year. At the time of his arrival, Methodism had been introduced to the colonies for only three years, and the number of converts was fewer than 300. Asbury's efforts infused new life into the movement, resulting in the membership of several congregations more than doubling within a single year. In 1772, Wesley appointed him "general assistant" to oversee the work in America. Although Thomas Rankin, an older preacher, superseded him in 1773, Asbury remained practically in control of the operations.

## The War of Independence and Exile
During the War of Independence, the Methodist community, which had grown to include several thousand people, faced unjust suspicions of Loyalism. This was primarily due to their refusal to take the prescribed oath. While many ministers, including Thomas Rankin, returned to England, Asbury chose to remain at his post, feeling that his duties and sympathies lay with the colonies. Despite being threatened and arrested once, he continued his itinerant preaching.

Eventually, hostility from the authorities in Maryland forced Asbury into exile in Delaware. He remained there for two years, during which time he continued to be active. In 1782, he was reappointed to supervise the affairs of the Methodist congregations in America. In 1784, John Wesley took the radical step of disregarding the authority of the Established Church by appointing Francis Asbury and the Rev. Thomas Coke as "bishops" or superintendents of the church in the United States.

## Later Influence and Legacy
As Bishop, Asbury continued to shape the church, including the ordination of other ministers. For example, in 1806, he ordained Peter Cartwright, a preacher known as the "Kentucky Boy," as a deacon.

Asbury's life and work are documented in his *Journals* (published in three volumes in New York, 1852). Beyond their value as a history of his own ministry, these journals serve as a valuable commentary on the industrial and social history of the United States during its first forty years of existence. His legacy is further preserved in various biographies, including works by F.W. Briggs, W.P. Strickland, J.B. Wakeley, and W.C. Larrabee. Additionally, the city of Asbury Park in Monmouth County, New Jersey—founded in 1869—was named in his honor.
