# Case-making Machine

The case-making machine is a large and complicated piece of equipment used in commercial bookbinding to produce "case" covers for books. Described as beautifully effective, the machine utilizes a complex system of over fifty springs, ranging in size from powerful components to small fittings similar to those found in watches.

## Operational Process
The machine is fed with three primary materials: a long roll of cloth for the cover, pieces of cardboard cut exactly to the size of the required boards, and additional cardboard pieces cut to the size of the book's back. 

The process begins as the roll of cloth is gradually unwound and glued via contact with a roller. As the cloth is drawn along, the two boards are dropped onto it one by one in an ingenious fashion. Following this, a long arm swings back and forth to pick up the cardboard for the back, placing it gently and precisely upon the glued bed located between the two previously fixed boards.

As the cloth continues to move, two rectangular gouges cut out the corners. Hollow raisers then gradually turn up the remaining side pieces, which are subsequently flattened by small rollers. A delicate mechanism finishes the corners, and a final arrangement of rollers and raisers, acting at right angles to the previous set, turns over and presses out the remaining pieces of cloth. Before the turning process begins, each piece of cloth is cut across at the proper point.

## Capacity and Production
The case-making machine is capable of producing 1,200 cases per hour for any size that the machine is able to take. 

## Related Binding Machinery
The production of a cased book involves other specialized machinery, often of American or German origin. These machines are typically power-driven and must be accurately set to a specific book size, making them most advantageous for large editions of a single title rather than books of varying sizes.

Following the creation of the case, a Smyth casing-in machine is used to attach the cover. This machine pastes the sides of the book and uses cleverly arranged rollers and regulated pressure to fix the cover in position. It features a "jointing-in" device that forces the joints of the cover into the joints of the book. The Smyth machine can handle books between 1/4 to 3 inches in thickness and 4 to 22 inches in length, covering between 10 and 15 books per minute.

For magazines and pamphlets ranging from 5 to 12 inches in length, a Sheridan wrappering machine may be used, which operates at a rate of 40 per minute.

## Decoration and Finishing
Once the case is made, designs may be blocked onto the covers using blocking presses, which are primarily of German, Scottish, or English make. These machines use a strong metal bed to hold a block and a movable table with gauges to position the cover.

*   **Gold Work:** The block is heated by gas jets. The cover is overlaid with gold leaf—fastened to the leather or cloth using the heat-solidification of egg albumen—and pressed against the block. Precise control of heat is required; if the block is too hot, gold adheres where it is not wanted, and if too cool, it will not adhere.
*   **Colour Work:** These blocks are not heated but are inked by a roller. Because a separate printing is required for each colour, the operator must carefully monitor each pull for any necessary readjustments.

## Comparison to Hand Binding
While machine-binding offers significant savings in time and labour, it differs from hand-binding in strength. In books sewn by hand on bands, the loose ends of the bands are drawn through the boards and fastened through their substance. In contrast, casing machines generally only paste the loose ends of the tapes to the inner surfaces of the boards. Consequently, a "cased" book is more likely to slip out of its cover once the paste perishes. Additionally, older hand-labour methods for sewing and rounding backs are considered safer and stronger and are less likely to injure or bruise the paper.
