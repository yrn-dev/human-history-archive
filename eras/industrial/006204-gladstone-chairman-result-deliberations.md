# The Building Societies Act of 1894

## Origins and the Liberator Disaster
The impetus for legislative change regarding building societies arose from the Liberator disaster. This event convinced observers that action was necessary to prevent similar calamities in the future. In response, the government of the period introduced a bill, while several private members developed their own measures, many of which were more stringent than the government's proposal.

## The Select Committee and Legislation
All proposed bills were referred to a select committee for review. Mr. Herbert Gladstone served as the chairman of this committee. The deliberations of the committee led to the passing of the Building Societies Act of 1894.

During the committee's work, a member named the Rt. Hon. W.L. Jackson (who later became Lord Allerton) moved for an address to the crown to obtain a return of the property held in possession by building societies. This request was unprecedented and met with significant resentment from the managers of the societies. Because there were no existing means to enforce the return, many large societies failed to provide the information despite frequent requests from the registrar.

## Provisions of the Act
The Building Societies Act of 1894 established new requirements for incorporated societies, mandating that they furnish returns in a prescribed form. These returns were required to include specific schedules detailing:
* Mortgages for amounts exceeding £5,000.
* Properties that the societies had possessed for more than twelve months due to the default of mortgagors.
* Mortgages that had been in arrear of repayment subscription for more than twelve months.

## Implementation and Impact
The act did not come into operation until January 1, 1895, and the first complete return under the new regulations was not due until 1896. This first return revealed that the properties in possession at the time of Mr. Jackson's earlier request must have been valued at at least seven and a half millions in the assets of the societies.

In the years following the passing of the act, building societies reduced their properties in possession from 14% of all mortgages to 5%. In numerical terms, this was a reduction from 7½ millions to 2½ millions, effectively reducing the original amount to one-third. Although this process involved sacrifice for many societies, the overall balance of profit increased rather than diminished. Consequently, while the provisions of the act initially alarmed society managers, they proved to be a blessing in disguise.
