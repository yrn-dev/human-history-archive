# Fermentation of Cellulose

The fermentation of cellulose is a biological process involving the decomposition of cellulose—the primary material deposited annually on the earth's surface via chlorophyll action on atmospheric carbon dioxide—back into water and carbon dioxide. This process is essential to the circulation of carbon in nature, breaking down organic materials such as wood, leaves, straw, rags, and paper that accumulate in forests, marshes, and cesspools.

## Biological Agents of Decomposition
The decomposition of plant matter occurs in stages involving different types of bacteria. Certain anaerobic bacteria are responsible for decomposing the substance of the middle lamella, which consists chiefly of pectin compounds. This action results in the isolation of cellulose fibres, a process observed when flax is "retted" or steeped. However, these specific bacteria are unable to attack the cellulose itself.

The actual decomposition of cellulose is carried out by other anaerobic bacteria found in the mud of rivers, marshes, and cloacae. These organisms are believed to first hydrolyse the cellulose and subsequently split the resulting products into marsh gas and carbon dioxide.

## Symbiosis in Cellulose Fermentation
Research by van Senus has demonstrated that the fermentation of cellulose by his *B. amylobacter* requires the concurrence of two bacteria, indicating a symbiotic relationship. Symbiosis is a recurring theme in bacterial fermentation; for example, *Bacillus chauvaei* and *Micrococcus acidi-paralactici* produce different acids when alone, but produce large quantities of normal butyl alcohol when occurring together. Similarly, *B. coli* and *B. denitrificans* must act together to completely undo the structure of sodium nitrate.

Other examples of bacterial co-operation include the relationship between mud bacteria, which evolve sulphuretted hydrogen, and sulphur bacteria that utilize it. Additionally, the anaerobic *Clostridium pasteurianum* can only fix nitrogen if aerobic species protect it from oxygen. It is probable that many symbiotic fermentations in soil, including those involving *Tetanus*, rely on this co-operation between oxygen-protecting aerobic species and anaerobic ones.

## Chemical By-products and Environmental Impact
The fermentation of cellulose in anaerobic environments often produces specific gases. When calcium sulphate is present during the process, the nascent methane induces the formation of water, sulphuretted hydrogen, and calcium carbonate. This chemical sequence explains the presence of sulphuretted hydrogen and marsh gas in bogs.

Similar putrefactive changes are believed to occur in the intestines of herbivorous animals, where undigested cellulose remains are broken down, leading to the existence of these same gases.
