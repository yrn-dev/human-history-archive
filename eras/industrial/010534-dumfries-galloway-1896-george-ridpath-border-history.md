# Dumfries and Galloway

Dumfries and Galloway is a region of Scotland characterized by its border history, diverse industries, and the administrative center of Dumfries. The area's historical and geographical identity is documented in various works, including Sir Herbert Maxwell's *History of Dumfries and Galloway* (1896) and George Ridpath's *Border History of England and Scotland* (1776).

## Dumfries: History and Governance
Dumfries, meaning "the fort in the copse" in Gaelic, serves as the capital of the county of Dumfriesshire and is a royal and parliamentary burgh. Situated on the left bank of the Nith, approximately 8 miles from the Solway Firth, the town was once the site of a camp for the Selgovian Britons. While little is known of its history immediately following the Roman withdrawal, William the Lion (who died in 1214) established it as a royal burgh, though the oldest existing charter dates to 1395, granted by Robert II.

The town was historically embroiled in the struggles for Scottish independence. It initially supported the claims of the Red Comyn and John Baliol—the latter's prosperity was aided by his mother, Devorgilla, daughter of Alan, lord of Galloway, who built the stone bridge over the Nith. This stood in contrast to Robert Bruce, who found support in Annandale. In 1300, Edward I lodged in the Franciscan monastery during the siege of Carlaverock Castle; this same monastery was the site of Comyn's murder on February 10, 1306.

From that period until nearly the end of the 16th century, the burgh faced frequent raids from English freebooters and partisans of turbulent chiefs, specifically the Maxwells, Johnstones, and Douglases. Despite this, Scottish sovereigns including Mary, James IV, and James V visited the town. On August 3, 1617, James VI was entertained in the town and presented the seven incorporated trades with a silver gun to encourage musketry practice. This led to an annual competition, with a major festival every seven years, which continued until 1831 and was later memorialized in a poem by native John Mayne.

Politically, the town showed strong opposition to the Union with England; on November 20, 1706, Cameronians burned the articles of the Union at the Market Cross with the approval of the inhabitants, and the provost voted against the measure.

## Regional Geography and Population
The region's population has seen a slight decline at the turn of the century, from 39,985 in 1891 to 39,383 in 1901. In 1901, 98 residents spoke both English and Gaelic. Key towns in the shire include Maxwelltown (pop. 5,796), Dalbeattie (3,469), Castle Douglas (3,018), Kirkcudbright (2,386), Gatehouse of Fleet (1,013), and Creetown (991).

Administratively, the shire returns one member to parliament. Kirkcudbright serves as the county town and is part of the Dumfries district group of parliamentary burghs, while Maxwelltown is combined with Dumfries. The area falls under the sheriffdom of Dumfries and Galloway, with a resident sheriff-substitute located at Kirkcudbright. Education is managed under school-board jurisdiction, with an academy at Kirkcudbright, high schools in Newton Stewart and Dumfries, and technical classes available in Dumfries, Castle Douglas, Dalbeattie, and Kirkcudbright.

## Economy and Industry
The region's economy is based on agriculture, quarrying, and localized manufacturing. The shire is the second-largest granite-yielding county after Aberdeen, providing significant employment. Other industries include:
*   **Manufacturing:** Production of cotton, woollen, and linen goods in various towns, as well as paper-making, tanning, brewing, and distilling. Dalbeattie is noted for its tile and brick works, and Kirkcudbright engages in limited ship-building.
*   **Agriculture:** Farmers began raising stock for southern markets in the mid-18th century. By the mid-19th century, 20,000 head of heavy cattle were sent to English markets annually. While Galloways were originally the primary breed, they have been largely replaced by Ayrshire dairy cattle and shorthorns. Sheep breeding is extensive, with Cheviots in the hilly country and half-bred lambs (crosses of Leicesters or other long-woolled rams and Cheviots) in the low-lying pastures. Horse-breeding is also pursued on a large scale, though grain crops, primarily oats, have declined.
*   **Fisheries:** While the Solway fishery is considered of small account, salmon fishing is valuable, particularly at the mouth of the Nith and Annan rivers.

## Infrastructure and Landmarks
Transportation is primarily facilitated by rail and coach. The Glasgow & South-Western railway connects Glasgow to Carlisle via Nithsdale and lower Annandale, with branches extending to Castle Douglas and Kirkcudbright. The Caledonian railway runs through Annandale with branches at Lockerbie (to Dumfries), Beattock (to Moffat), and Kirtlebridge (crossing the Solway to Bowness). The North British railway provides a line to Langholm from Riddings Junction. Coach services supplement these routes, connecting points such as New Galloway to Carsphairn and Dumfries to Dalbeattie and New Abbey.

Notable landmarks in Dumfries include St Michael's (1746), where Robert Burns attended and was buried in a mausoleum alongside his wife, Jean Armour. The Gothic Greyfriars church (1866-1867) stands on the site of the old town castle and a Franciscan monastery. St Mary's (1837-1839) was built where Christiana, sister of Robert Bruce, had raised a chapel for her husband, Sir Christopher Seton. Additionally, St Andrew's (1811-1813) serves as the Roman Catholic pro-cathedral for the diocese of Galloway.
