# Hudson's Bay: or, Life in the Wilds of North America

*Hudson's Bay: or, Life in the Wilds of North America* is the first book published by the Scottish fiction writer Robert Michael Ballantyne. Published in 1848, the work drew upon Ballantyne's personal experiences in Canada, where he had spent six years in the service of the Hudson's Bay Company after arriving there at the age of sixteen.

## Author Background
Robert Michael Ballantyne was born in Edinburgh on April 24, 1825, into a family of famous publishers and printers. Following his time in Canada, he returned to Scotland in 1847. While he was briefly employed by the publishers Messrs Constable, he transitioned fully to the profession of literature in 1856. Ballantyne became widely known for a series of adventure stories for young readers, including *The Young Fur-Traders* (1856), *The Coral Island* (1857), and *Ungava: a Tale of Eskimo Land* (1857). Throughout his career, which produced over a hundred books, Ballantyne adhered to a rule of writing from personal knowledge of the scenes he described. In addition to his writing, he was an artist who exhibited water-colours at the Royal Scottish Academy. He died in Rome on February 8, 1894.

## The Hudson's Bay Company and Trade
The Hudson's Bay Company is one of ten large American and Canadian companies that maintain extensive systems for gathering annual hauls of skins from scattered trappers. The company's sales of furs take place before those of other companies; because no reserves are placed on any lot, these sales are viewed as exact indicators of current values.

The company's operations have historically influenced the region's geography and language. For example, the term "dalle"—a French word for a flat tile or flag-stone referring to rapid falling over smooth rock surfaces in a river bed—entered common usage in America through the French employees of the Hudson's Bay Company.

In terms of territorial administration, the company formed a district called Assiniboia in 1835. This district was governed by a local council nominated by the Hudson's Bay Company and centered around Fort Garry at the junction of the Assiniboine and Red rivers in Rupert's Land, North America. The district covered a circular area with a 50-mile radius from Fort Garry and existed until Rupert's Land was transferred to Canada in 1870.

## Natural Resources and Fur Quality
The Hudson Bay district is noted for producing high-quality furs. Specifically, the best wolves are obtained from this district, characterized as being full-furred and a very pale bluish-grey with fine flowing black top hair. Additionally, the darkest and best cross foxes—which feature a pale yellowish or orange tone with silvery points and a dark cross marking on the shoulders—come from Hudson Bay and Labrador. Lynx skins from the Hudson Bay district are also considered among the best, possessing fine, silky, flowing top hair.

The region's flora includes the canoe or paper birch (*B. papyracea*), which ranges to the southern shores of Hudson's Bay. This tree is of great value to inhabitants; its bark is used for baskets, dishes, drinking-cups, and the construction of canoes, while the wood is used for dog sledges, axe-handles, and furniture.

## Historical Context
By 1750, the British had established power along the east coast of North America. Through treaties with the French, the British acquired considerable northern areas, including the Hudson Bay Company territories. During this period, while France maintained settlements in Montreal and Quebec and claimed territories across the continent through treaties with American Indians of the great plains, the British colonies were more solidly settled, with a population exceeding one million compared to less than a tenth of that number for the French.
