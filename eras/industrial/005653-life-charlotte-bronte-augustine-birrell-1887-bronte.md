# Charlotte Brontë

Charlotte Brontë (1816–1855) was an English novelist and one of three sisters who attained literary distinction. She was born in Thornton, some three miles from Bradford, to Patrick Brontë and Maria Branwell. Her father, a clergyman of the Church of England, had previously been a weaver and schoolmaster in Ireland before attending St John's College, Cambridge in 1802. Charlotte was one of six children; her eldest sisters, Maria and Elizabeth, died in 1825.

## Early Life and Education
In April 1820, Patrick Brontë accepted the living of Haworth, a village near Keighley in Yorkshire. Following the death of Maria Brontë in September 1821, the family was joined by Elizabeth Branwell, the father's sister-in-law, who moved from Penzance to care for the children. 

In 1824, Charlotte and her sister Emily were sent to the Clergy Daughters' school at Cowan Bridge. The school was characterized by drastic discipline and bad food. Charlotte later depicted this institution under the name "Lowood" in her novel *Jane Eyre*, with the principal, Rev. William Carus Wilson, serving as the counterpart to the character Mr Naomi Brocklehurst. Charlotte and Emily returned to Haworth in 1825, where they spent a prolonged period of home life. During these years, the four surviving children engaged in study and developed precocious literary aspirations, producing various essays, verses, and stories in tiny booklets.

## Literary Career and Pseudonyms
Charlotte and her sisters, Emily and Anne, entered the literary world in 1846 with a volume of verse published by Aylott & Jones. To disguise their identities, they used the pseudonyms Currer, Ellis, and Acton Bell. The volume contained nineteen poems by Charlotte, twenty-one by Emily, and twenty-one by Anne. Despite the effort, the venture cost the sisters approximately £50 and only two copies were sold.

Following the failure of the poetry volume, the sisters sought to publish novels. Charlotte's first manuscript, originally titled *The Master*, was retitled *The Professor*. It was eventually sent to the firm Smith, Elder & Co. In August 1847, the firm's reader, R. Smith Williams, informed Charlotte that while the work was considered, it was too short for the required three-volume form. In response, Charlotte submitted a longer novel, *Jane Eyre*, which was published in October 1847. The novel was wildly acclaimed, though it later received a critical review in the *Quarterly* in December 1848 by Miss Rigby (Lady Eastlake).

## Later Works and Family Tragedy
Charlotte is recognized for four novels, with *Jane Eyre* and *Villette* noted for their independence, intensity, and rough individuality. While she is considered undistinguished as a maker of verse, her novels—particularly *Villette*—contain passages of magnificent poetry.

The late 1840s were marked by significant personal loss. In the summer of 1848, Charlotte and Anne visited London to interview their publishers in Cornhill to establish their separate identities, as the publisher T.C. Newby had attempted to persuade the public that the novels of Emily and Anne were written by the author of *Jane Eyre*. Shortly after returning home, Charlotte's brother, Patrick Branwell, died on September 24, 1848. This was followed by the death of Emily on December 19, 1848. In May 1849, Charlotte accompanied Anne to Scarborough in hopes that the sea air would help her illness; Anne died there on May 28. Within eight months, Charlotte lost all three of her youth companions and returned to Haworth to support her father, who was becoming blind.

## Biographies and Legacy
The first major biography, *Life of Charlotte Brontë*, was written by Mrs Gaskell and published in 1857. The third edition of this work was significantly altered due to controversies regarding the identity of Lowood school, the relationship between Branwell Brontë and his employer's wife, and the peculiarities of Mr Brontë. A satisfactory version of this biography is found in the 1902 Haworth edition, which includes letters from Charlotte to her publisher, George Smith.

Other significant contributions to the study of Charlotte Brontë include:
* *Charlotte Brontë: a Monograph* (1877) by T. Wemyss Reid.
* *A Note on Charlotte Brontë* (1877), an essay by A.C. Swinburne.
* *Life of Charlotte Brontë* (1887) by Augustine Birrell.
* *Charlotte Brontë and her Circle* (1896) and *Charlotte Brontë and her Sisters* (1906) by Clement Shorter.
* *The Brontës: Life and Letters* (1907) by Clement Shorter, which compiles Charlotte's letters in chronological order.
