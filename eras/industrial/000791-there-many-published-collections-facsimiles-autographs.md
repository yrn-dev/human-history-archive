# Facsimiles of Autographs and Historical Documents

The production and collection of facsimiles—exact reproductions of original handwritten documents (autographs) and printed materials—serve as critical tools for historical preservation, literary study, and typographical analysis. These collections range from private albums of signatures to official national archives and specialized scholarly volumes.

## The Practice of Autograph Collecting
The habit of collecting autographs as a specific fashion did not exist in England during the time of William Shakespeare, which contributed to the loss of autographs from him and other great writers of that era. In contrast, the practice is believed to have originated in the Low Countries and Germany, primarily among university students. These individuals maintained *alba amicorum* (albums of friends), which were small, oblong pocket volumes used to collect signatures and inscriptions. A significant collection of these survives in the British Museum, with the earliest album in the series, Egerton MS. 1178, beginning in 1554.

Once established, the taste for collecting autographs expanded from living persons to those of the past. While many famous collections were eventually dispersed when owners lost interest or heirs failed to inherit the taste, some remained intact. A notable recent English example is the collection of Mr. Alfred Morrison, which is documented in a sumptuous catalogue containing many facsimiles.

Historically, documents were often preserved for their literary or historic value rather than as handwriting specimens. For instance, the library of Sir Robert Cotton (now in the British Museum) and the collection formed by Philippe de Bethune, Comte de Selles et Charost, and his son during the reign of Louis XIV (now in the Bibliotheque Nationale), were gathered for historical purposes.

## Published Collections of Autographs
Numerous published collections of autograph facsimiles exist across different nations. In England, several notable works were produced during the 19th century, including:
* *British Autography* by J. Thane (1788-1793, with a 1854 supplement by Daniell).
* *Autographs of Royal, Noble, Learned and Remarkable Personages in English History* by J. G. Nichols (1829).
* *Autographs of the Kings and Queens and Eminent Men of Great Britain* by J. Netherclift (1835).
* *One Hundred Characteristic Autograph Letters* by J. Netherclift and Son (1849).
* *The Handbook of Autographs* (1862), *The Autograph Miscellany* (1855), and *The Autograph Souvenir* (1865) by members of the Netherclift family.
* *The Autographic Mirror* (1864-1866) and *The Autograph Album* by L. B. Phillips (1866).
* A five-series publication of *Facsimiles of Autographs* from the British Museum (1896-1900).

Official government publications have also produced facsimiles of national manuscripts, including those from the Master of the Rolls covering the period from William the Conqueror to Queen Anne (1865-1868), the Lord Clerk Register for Scotland (1867-1871), and the Public Record Office for Ireland (1874-1884).

## Survival of Early Autographs
In a restricted sense, "autographs" refers to signatures or documents written by persons of eminence or notoriety. Because private documents were rarely preserved, the only early autographs likely to survive are those of royal figures and high officials attached to important public deeds.

Autographs from the early middle ages are rare and difficult to identify. While a charter at Canterbury is claimed to be written by Dunstan, a duplicate in the British Museum makes it probable that both are copies. However, some authentic autograph manuscripts do exist, such as the chronicles of Sigebert of Gembloux, Robert de Monte, and Ordericus Vitalis. The Cottonian MSS contain autograph writings of the English chronicler Matthew of Paris, and the British Museum holds documents in the hand of William of Wykeham. Additionally, the historian Joinville left autograph writings in French archives, and the 15th-century Paston Letters contain numerous autographs of historical figures from the Norfolk family.

## Facsimiles in Other Disciplines
Facsimiles are also employed in the study of early printing (incunabula) and cartography. Because many early printed books lack information regarding their origin, "typographical detectives" like Robert Proctor (d. 1903) used facsimiles to track printers. Key works in this field include K. Burger's *Monumenta Germaniae et Italiae Typographica* (1892), J. W. Holtrop's *Monuments typographiques des Pays-Bas* (1868), and Gordon Duff's *Early English Printing* (1896).

In the realm of art and maps, perfect facsimiles have been published for the Joshua Roll of the Vatican and the *Natural History* of Dioscorides of Vienna (c. 500). Cartographic facsimiles include J. G. Kohl's American section of maps (1860) and works by J. Fischer and F. M. von Wieser (1903) regarding maps from 1507 and 1517.
