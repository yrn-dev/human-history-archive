# Bagehot's Conversation and Intellectual Life

## Characteristics of Bagehot's Conversation
It is impossible to give a full idea of the brightness and life of Bagehot's conversation. While those who did not know him personally may find a way to understand this quality through the conversational style of his writing, he was considered greater than his books. He was consistently full of ideas and possessed a manner of speaking described as "winged words," which he used to stamp a true idea so firmly that it could not be forgotten or to transfix a fallacy. This fullness of ideas also contributed to his effectiveness as an ideal editor.

## Intellectual Interests and Proposed Works
Bagehot envisioned several literary projects that he did not write himself but suggested as potential contributions to knowledge. One such idea was a history of recent politics that would provide new lights by utilizing a writer familiar with the family history and connections of English politicians. This notion arose from the passage of a specific bill through parliament; in that instance, the chancellor of the exchequer—a relative of the permanent head of the department concerned—took over the management of the measure after the head of the department in the House of Commons failed, allowing the chancellor to carry his own legislative ideas despite the failure of his political chief.

Additionally, Bagehot expressed a desire for a written account regarding the differences in the administrative systems of Scotland and England. He was greatly impressed by these differences, noting that they were not found in the details, but rather in the form and fundamental idea. He observed that no judicial or other officers in one system were represented by corresponding functionaries in the other.

## Approach to Statistics and Logic
As a journalist, Bagehot provided assistance to the study of statistics, though he maintained a strong aversion to the actual manipulation of figures and boasted of his inability to add up. Despite this, he was a most excellent mathematician. He exercised extreme care regarding the logic of the figures gathered for his articles, which he scrutinized meticulously. Bagehot frequently cautioned that his figures were merely illustrative and did not, in and of themselves, establish an argument. He was consistently anxious to impress others with the belief that accounts and figures alone were insufficient for the study of a subject.
