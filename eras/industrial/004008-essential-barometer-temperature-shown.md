# The Barometer: Construction, Observation, and Temperature Correction

The barometer is an instrument used to measure atmospheric pressure. In its simplest form, such as the cistern barometer, it consists of a glass tube plunged into a vessel containing mercury. The true height of the barometer is defined as the perpendicular distance between the top of the mercury column and the surface of the mercury in the cistern.

## Construction and Materials
The highest quality barometers are constructed using three primary materials: glass for the tube that holds the mercury, brass for the case (which also serves as the surface for the engraved scale), and the mercury itself. 

The physical properties of these materials affect the accuracy of the instrument. Mercury has a considerably greater coefficient of expansion than brass. Consequently, changes in temperature can alter the reading even if the atmospheric pressure remains constant. For example, if a barometer stands at 30 inches when the temperature of the brass and mercury is 32°F, it will rise to 30.1 inches if the temperature is increased to 69°F. This increase of one-tenth of an inch is caused entirely by the greater expansion of the mercury relative to the brass case.

## Observation Techniques
To ensure an accurate observation, the barometer must be hung in a perpendicular position. Because the surface of the mercury column is convex, observers do not use the chord of the curve to note the height, but rather its tangent. This is achieved using a vernier—a sliding appendage that allows the height to be read with a precision of 0.001 or 0.002 inches.

Furthermore, the instrument is subject to specific errors:
*   **Capillarity:** In a glass tube of small bore, the mercury level sits below the level of the mercury in the vessel. In standard barometers with a tube diameter of about one inch, this error is less than 0.001 inches. Because capillarity depresses the column, an addition must be made to the observed height to determine true pressure.
*   **Error of Capacity:** This arises from changes in the level of the cistern surface as mercury moves in and out of the tube.

## The Role of Temperature
It is essential that the barometer is at the temperature indicated by its attached thermometer. An observation is not considered good if the thermometer indicates a temperature that differs from the rest of the instrument by more than one degree. For every degree of difference, the observation becomes faulty by approximately 0.003 inches, a serious margin when discussing diurnal range.

To compare observations made at different temperatures with exactness, readings must be reduced to a uniform temperature. The standard temperature for such reductions is 0°C or 32°F. This process neutralizes inequalities caused by the heat-induced expansion of different scales. For instance, the English measure of length is standard at 62°F, the metric scale at 32°F, and the old French measure at 61.2°F.

## Measurement Systems and Variations
Barometric height is expressed in different units depending on the region and purpose:
*   **England and America:** English inches are used.
*   **Scientific Work:** The metric system is used for all scientific work except meteorology.
*   **France and Europe:** Millimetres are typically used (one millimetre is the thousandth part of a metre, equaling 39.37079 English inches).
*   **Russia:** Until 1869, Russia used half-lines (one-twentieth of an English inch), though it has since adopted the metric scale.
*   **Other European regions:** The Paris line (0.088814 inches) is occasionally employed.

Other variations of the instrument include the sympiezometer, invented in 1818 by Adie of Edinburgh. A revived form of Hooke's marine barometer, it uses a glass tube with a small chamber of air at the top and glycerin in the lower part and cistern. While it includes a thermometer and sliding scale to correct for temperature-induced pressure errors in the contained air, it is suited for traveling or rough use at sea rather than exact observation. It has largely been superseded by the more handy Aneroid barometer.

## Related Phenomena and Applications
The "barometric light" is a luminous glow emitted when mercury in a barometer tube is shaken. First observed by Jean Picard and later studied by Francis Hawksbee, this glow is caused by electricity generated by the friction between the air and the mercury. Hawksbee demonstrated that a Torricellian vacuum is not required for this to occur, as the glow appears even when the air is only partially rarefied.

In chemistry, barometer tubes have been adapted for determining vapour density. In 1868, Hofmann developed a method using an ordinary barometer tube to effect volatilization in a Torricellian vacuum. Additionally, certain regulators (such as those by Bunsen, Kemp, and Muenke) act as air-thermometers but are sensitive to barometric pressure; a change of 0.5 inches in the barometer column can cause a temperature variation of up to 2°.
