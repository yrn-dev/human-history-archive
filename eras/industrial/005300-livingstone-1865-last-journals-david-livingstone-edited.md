# Last Journals of David Livingstone

The *Last Journals of David Livingstone* is a published record of the explorations and final years of David Livingstone. Edited by the Rev. Horace Waller and published in 1874, the journals serve as a primary source for the study of Central Africa and the geographical discoveries made by Livingstone during his travels.

## Explorations and Discoveries
David Livingstone is credited with being the first person to reach Bangweulu in 1868, although the location had been heard of previously by a Portuguese traveller named Francisco de Lacerda in 1798. Livingstone's activities in the region continued until his death six years later, which occurred among the swamps located to the south of Bangweulu. His *Last Journals* are cited as a key resource for those researching the geography of this area, alongside other works such as Victor Giraud's *Les Lacs de l'Afrique equatoriale*.

## Related Expeditions and Search Efforts
The efforts to locate Livingstone in Central Africa were notable enough to prompt James Gordon Bennett, who managed a newspaper, to send Henry M. Stanley on a mission to find him. Livingstone's broader body of work and travels are further documented in other publications, including *Missionary Travels and Researches in South Africa* (1857) and the *Narrative of an Expedition to the Zambezi* (1865), the latter of which was co-authored with Charles Livingstone.

## Geographical Context
The regions documented in Livingstone's journals and related narratives involve complex river systems and terrains. For instance, the nomenclature of the rivers in these areas is described as perplexing; the Luanga (or Lunga) is a tributary of the Luengwe-Kafukwe (often called the Kafue), while the Luangwa (or Loangwa) is an independent affluent of the Zambezi. Additionally, the "Livingstone mountains" are noted as a distinct geographical feature in the region, where the Kinga speech is spoken.
