# The First Baptist Degree-Conferring Institution

## Origins and Planning
In approximately 1762, the Philadelphia Association began developing plans to establish a Baptist institution of learning intended to serve the entire denomination. Rhode Island was selected as the location for this institution for several reasons: it was considered an abode of religious liberty, it possessed a Baptist constituency that was relatively wealthy, influential, and intelligent, and there was a perceived likelihood of obtaining a charter from the state legislature. Additionally, planners believed that other denominations might cooperate in an institution that remained under Baptist control.

## Establishment and Early Development
James Manning (1738–1791), a high-honors graduate of Princeton, was identified as a suitable leader for the project. In 1763, Manning was sent to Rhode Island to consult with influential men from the Baptist faith and other backgrounds. These efforts resulted in the legislature granting a charter in 1764.

Following a few years of preliminary work conducted at Warren, the institution eventually moved to Providence in 1770. At this location, Manning served as president, while his Princeton classmate, Hezekiah Smith (1737–1805), acted as an influential supporter and financial agent. The institution, which has been known as Brown University since 1804, served for many years as the only degree-conferring institution controlled by Baptists.

## The First Degrees
The first degrees ever bestowed by a Baptist institution were conferred in 1769. These degrees were granted during the period of preliminary work when the institution was located at Warren, prior to its move to Providence.

## Related Denominational Efforts
Parallel to the establishment of the college, the Warren Association was organized in 1767. This organization was created under the influence of Hezekiah Smith and James Manning, using the Philadelphia Association as a model. The Warren Association became a primary agency for the promotion of denominational education, the consolidation of denominational life, and the securing of religious liberty.

The growth of Baptist educational infrastructure expanded significantly in later years. By 1812, American Baptists possessed only one degree-conferring college characterized by a small student body, a small faculty, and almost no endowment. However, by 1906, the denomination had more than 100 colleges and universities with an annual income of approximately $3,000,000 and equipment and endowments valued at about $30,000,000. Additionally, while there were no theological seminaries for American Baptists in 1812, there were 11 by 1906, supporting 1,300 students with 100 instructors.
