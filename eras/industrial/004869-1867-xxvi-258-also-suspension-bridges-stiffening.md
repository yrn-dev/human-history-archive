# Suspension Bridges

Suspension bridges are structures characterized by the use of principal members in tension. This system allows for the construction of road bridges with spans that were previously impossible using other construction methods. The basic structure consists of two or more chains—made of pins connecting links, twisted wire strands, or parallel wires—which pass over lofty piers. These chains typically rest on saddles carried by rollers and are led down to anchorages in rock chambers. A level platform is then hung from these chains via suspension rods.

## Structural Components and Design
A bridge is divided into the superstructure and the substructure. In a suspension bridge, the superstructure's main supporting members are the suspension ropes or chains. The substructure consists of foundations below ground level and the piers and abutments. 

The abutments serving as anchorages must be designed to remain thoroughly stable under the maximum pull exerted by the chains. The piers must be carried above the platform, and their design is modified based on the specific type of suspension bridge used. When the resultant pressure on the piers is not vertical, they must be constructed to accommodate the inclined pressure. In stiffened suspension bridges, the pier's action is analogous to that of a pier situated between two arches.

## Stiffness and Stability
The primary defect of the simple suspension bridge is its flexibility. This flexibility becomes less serious as the dead weight of the structure increases in proportion to the temporary or live load. Some bridges have failed due to oscillations caused by men marching in step; for example, a bridge at Angers collapsed in 1850 when 487 soldiers marched over it, resulting in 226 deaths.

To obtain greater stiffness, various plans have been adopted:
*   **Combined Systems:** Bridges can be stiffened by using bracing and girders, creating a mixed type. While this reduces the economy of the design, it is likely the type adopted for very great spans. A project in New York has proposed a span of 3200 ft.
*   **Stiffening Girders:** Around 1850, it was recognized that combining supporting chains with suspended stiffening girders could create a bridge strong enough for railway trains. W. J. M. Rankine proved that the required strength of such a stiffening girder would be only one-seventh of that of an independent girder of the same span carrying the same moving load.
*   **The Ordish System:** This method uses oblique chains to support girders at intermediate points in the span, with a curved chain supporting the oblique chains to keep them straight. An example was built at Prague in 1868.
*   **Inverted Arch Principle:** A bridge over the Danube canal at Vienna (1860) featured two parallel chains on each side, 4 ft. apart and connected by bracing to form a stiff inverted arch. Despite a sound principle, errors in calculation made it weak, and it was removed in 1884.

## Notable Examples
### The Niagara Bridge
Built by J. A. Roebling between 1852 and 1855, this was the largest railway bridge of its time with a span of 821 ft. and a height of 245 ft. It utilized four suspension cables, each 10 in. in diameter and composed of 3640 parallel wires. The stiffening girder was originally a box-shaped braced timber structure 18 ft. deep and 25 ft. wide, carrying a railway on top and a roadway within. The timber girder was replaced by an iron one in 1880, but the bridge was eventually taken down in 1896-1897 and replaced by a steel arch because it could not support increasing railway traffic weights.

### The Clifton Bridge
I. K. Brunei constructed the abutments and towers for this 702 ft. span bridge over the Avon in 1836, though it was not completed until 1860 using link chains from the Hungerford suspension bridge. It features three chains on each side supporting wrought iron stiffening girders. The chains are arranged with a suspending rod every 8 ft. and are carried at 45° from the saddles to the anchorages, where each chain has a 5 ft. by 6 ft. anchor plate.

### The Runcorn Bridge
This cable suspension bridge with stiffening girders spans 1000 ft. across the Mersey and the Manchester Ship Canal. It features an electrically propelled car, 55 ft. long and 24½ ft. wide, which runs on a trolley on the underside of the stiffening girders. The car is powered by two 36 B.H.P. steel-clad series-wound motors, with electricity provided by two 75 B.H.P. gas engines. The total cost of the structure was £133,000.

### Other Early Bridges
T. Telford began the Menai bridge in 1819 with a span of 570 ft. and a dip of 43 ft. Other early examples include the Fribourg bridge (870 ft. span), the Pest bridge (666 ft. span), and the Hammersmith bridge (422 ft. span).
