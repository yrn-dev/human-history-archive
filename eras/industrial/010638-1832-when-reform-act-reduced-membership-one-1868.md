# Parliamentary Representation and the Reform Acts of 1832 and 1868

The parliamentary representation of various English boroughs and counties underwent significant transformations during the 19th century, primarily driven by the Reform Act of 1832 and subsequent legislation in 1868 and 1885. These acts altered the number of members returned to parliament, disfranchised several boroughs, and redistributed seats across different divisions.

## Impact of the Reform Act of 1832

The Reform Act of 1832 resulted in the reduction or removal of representation for numerous constituencies. In the borough of Arundel, which had returned two members to parliament from 1302, the membership was reduced to one. Similarly, the borough of Ashburton, which had returned two members after 1640, was deprived of one member by the 1832 act.

In Cornwall, the 1832 act targeted "rotten boroughs." Fourteen Cornish boroughs were disfranchised, including Bossiney, Camelford, Lostwithiel, East Looe, West Looe, and Fowey. The scale of this imbalance was evident near Liskeard, where an area that would later be represented by only one member had, until 1832, nine parliamentary boroughs returning eighteen members for a population of only 14,224.

Other regions also saw changes under the 1832 act:
* **Kent:** The county returned four members in two divisions, and while Greenwich gained two members and Chatham gained one, Queenborough was disfranchised.
* **Dorsetshire:** The county returned three members, and Corfe Castle was disfranchised.
* **Derbyshire:** The county and town of Derby, which had each returned two members since 1295, were changed such that the county returned four members in two divisions.
* **Buckinghamshire:** Total representation for the county and its boroughs was reduced to eleven.
* **Durham:** The county returned two members for two divisions, while the boroughs of Sunderland, South Shields, and Gateshead acquired representation.

## Changes under the Act of 1868

Further adjustments were made in 1868, often continuing the trend of disfranchisement or expanding county representation. The borough of Arundel, which had been reduced to one member in 1832, was disfranchised altogether in 1868. In Dorsetshire, the borough of Lyme Regis was disfranchised.

Conversely, some areas saw an increase in representation or new acquisitions:
* **Kent:** The county's representation increased to six members in three divisions, and Gravesend returned one member.
* **Derbyshire:** The county's representation increased to six members for three divisions.
* **Durham:** The boroughs of Hartlepool, Stockton, and Darlington each returned one member starting in 1868.

## Later Redistributions and the Act of 1885

The Redistribution Act of 1885 further streamlined parliamentary seats. In Dorsetshire, the remaining boroughs were disfranchised. In Buckinghamshire, the boroughs were deprived of representation entirely, leaving the county to return three members for three divisions.

In Kent, the county representation rose to eight members in eight divisions, though the representation for Rochester, Maidstone, and Canterbury was reduced to one member each. In Cornwall, the act of 1885 ended the separate representation of St Ives, Liskeard, Launceston, Helston, and Bodmin. Additionally, Truro and Penryn with Falmouth, which had previously returned two members each, saw their representation changed.

## Regional Historical Context

The shifts in representation occurred against a backdrop of varying regional economic histories. Derbyshire had been a mining and manufacturing hub since Roman times, with lead, iron, and coal production, and a silk and cotton industry that grew in the 18th century. Dorsetshire was known for wheat, wool, and a clothing trade that declined after a plague in 1626, as well as lace production in Sherborne, Blandford, and Lyme Regis. 

Buckinghamshire remained primarily agricultural, though it had a profitable bone lace-making industry—particularly in Olney and Newport Pagnell—and a straw-plait industry introduced during the reign of George I. Arundel, once a thriving seaport connected to London by canal in 1813, had been a borough and port since the time of the Conquest, having been granted to Roger de Montgomery by the Conqueror.
