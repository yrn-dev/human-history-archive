# Sir J.D. Hooker and the Flora of South-Eastern Asia

## Botanical Estimates of South-Eastern Asia
Sir J.D. Hooker estimated that the total number of indigenous plant species in the region of south-eastern Asia—an area encompassing the Malayan peninsula, various islands, India, and the region from approximately the 105th to the 65th meridian—ranges between 12,000 and 15,000. 

The flora of this region is characterized as a heterogeneous assemblage of alpine, temperate, and tropical plants. While there is significant variation between the vegetation of arid and humid regions, and the flora of the plains differs entirely from that of the higher mountain ranges, tropical plants are dominant and define the character of the flora as a whole. The Indian flora, in particular, provides a more general and complete illustration of almost all the chief natural families from all parts of the world than any other country.

## Principal Plant Orders
According to the numerical importance established by Sir J.D. Hooker, the principal orders of plants in this region are arranged as follows:
* Leguminosae
* Rubiaceae
* Orchidaceae
* Compositae
* Gramineae
* Euphorbiaceae
* Acanthaceae
* Cyperaceae
* Labiatae

The distribution of these orders varies by location. Orchidaceae are universally present and found in specially large proportions, as are Euphorbiaceae and Scrophulariaceae. Conversely, Compositae are comparatively rare, and certain areas show a deficiency in Leguminosae, ferns, Cyperaceae, and Gramineae.

## Biography of Sir Joseph Dalton Hooker
Sir Joseph Dalton Hooker (1817– ) was an English traveller and botanist born on June 30, 1817, in Halesworth, Suffolk. He was the second son of the famous botanist Sir W. J. Hooker. Educated at Glasgow University, he earned his M.D. degree in 1839.

Hooker's early career was marked by extensive exploration. He served as an assistant-surgeon on the "Erebus" during Sir James Ross's Antarctic expedition, resulting in the publication of *Flora Tasmanica*, *Flora Novae Zelandiae*, and *Flora Antarctica*. Between 1847 and 1851, he traveled to the northern frontiers of India. During this expedition, Hooker and Dr. Campbell were imprisoned for a period by the raja of Sikkim. Despite this, they conducted a survey of unexplored regions (published by the Calcutta Trigonometrical Survey Office) and gathered botanical observations that served as the foundation for elaborate works on the flora of India and the rhododendrons of the Sikkim Himalaya.

Other scientific journeys included trips to Morocco (1871), the United States (1877), and Palestine (1860).

## Professional Career and Honors
Sir J.D. Hooker established a high scientific reputation in England. He was elected a fellow of the Royal Society at age thirty and later served as its president starting in 1873. His contributions were recognized with three medals from the Royal Society: the Royal medal (1854), the Copley medal (1887), and the Darwin medal (1892).

In 1855, he was appointed assistant-director of Kew Gardens, and in 1865, he succeeded his father as the full director, a position he held for twenty years. In 1868, while acting as president of the British Association at its Norwich meeting, he delivered an address that championed Darwinian theories. He was an early supporter and friend of Charles Darwin, and along with Lyell, he was one of the individuals who first induced Darwin to make his views public.
