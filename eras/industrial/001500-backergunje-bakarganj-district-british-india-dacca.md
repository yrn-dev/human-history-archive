# BACKERGUNJE

Backergunje, also known as Bakarganj, is a district of British India located within the Dacca division of Eastern Bengal and Assam. The district covers an area of 4,542 square miles and is situated as part of the joint delta of the Brahmaputra and Ganges rivers.

## Geography and Climate
The district is characterized by a flat, even landscape without a single hill or hillock. It is defined by a dense network of sluggish, dark-colored streams and water-courses, making travel by boat necessary in any season. The terrain is low-lying, and the boundaries between land and water are often blurred by the profusion of reeds, long grasses, and other aquatic plants. In the north-west, the region is highly marshy, consisting of rice lands and unreclaimed swamps where huts are built on earth mounds. To the south, along the coast of the Bay of Bengal, are the Sundarbans forest tracts, which serve as the habitat for leopards, tigers, and other wild beasts.

The vegetation is notably fresh and green, particularly during the rains, and the district never appears burnt-up or dried. The landscape is dotted with clusters of betel-nut trees and bamboos, which also form walls around the villages. The climate is considered one of the healthiest in Eastern Bengal due to a strong south-west monsoon from the Bay of Bengal that cools the atmosphere. The average annual temperature ranges between 78°F and 85°F, with a general range from 62°F to 98°F. However, humidity and the use of bad water, combined with heavy rainfall, are sources of disease.

## Hydrography
The district's primary rivers include the Meghna, the Arial Khan, and the Haringhata (also known as the Baleswar). The Meghna, which carries the combined waters of the Ganges and Brahmaputra, flows south along the eastern boundary for approximately 100 miles before entering the Bay of Bengal. In its lower course, it expands into a large estuary featuring several islands, most notably Dakshin Shahbazpur; these sea-front islands are susceptible to cyclonic storm-waves.

The Arial Khan, a branch of the Ganges, enters from the north and flows south-easterly into the Meghna estuary. It is navigable year-round by the largest native cargo boats, with a width ranging from 1,700 yards in the dry season to 3,000 yards during the rains. The Haringhata (Baleswar), which also goes by the names Garai and Madhumati, enters near the north-west corner and forms the western boundary. It is navigable by large sea-going ships as far as Morrellganj in the neighboring Jessore district. A significant tributary of the Baleswar is the Kacha, a stream navigable by large boats throughout the year. Other rivers of lesser importance include the Khairabad, Nihalganj, Bishkhali, Barisal, Kumar, and Ghagar. All rivers in the district are subject to tidal action from the Bay of Bengal and the Meghna.

## Population and Society
As of 1901, the population of Backergunje was 2,291,752, representing a 6% increase over the previous decade. The population includes a Buddhist community consisting of Maghs (people of Arakan) who settled in the district around 1800 and assisted in clearing the Sundarbans. There is also a gipsy-like tribe known as the Bebajias; they profess Mahommedanism, live primarily in boats, and sustain themselves through fishing, trading in trinkets, fortune-telling, and wood-cutting in the Sundarbans. The Faraizis, a Puritan sect of Mahommedans, are also exceedingly numerous. The archive notes that the Mussulmans of the district are characterized as being steeped in prejudice and ignorance, litigious, grossly immoral, and easily excited to murder and violence, necessitating the enforcement of disarmament due to an epidemic of murders.

## Economy and Infrastructure
The material condition of the inhabitants is described as good. Most residents are small landholders who cultivate enough rice and necessities for their families, which results in a scarcity of hired labour. Rice is the primary crop, with three annual harvests: *boro* (spring), *aus* (autumn), and *aman* (winter). Small trading villages are spread throughout the district, each hosting periodical fairs for traffic.

The headquarters of the district is the town of Barisal, located on the west bank of the Barisal river. In 1901, Barisal had a population of 18,978. It serves as a major center for river trade and is a stop on the steamer route from Calcutta to the Brahmaputra through the Sundarbans. The town's infrastructure includes several schools, a first-grade college, a students' union dedicated to the physical and intellectual improvement of boys and aid for the poor and sick, and a public library established by subscription in 1858. Pirojpur is the second largest town, with a population of 14,119.

Barisal is also associated with a physical phenomenon called the "Barisal guns"—noises resembling cannon reports heard in the Brahmaputra delta channels during the rising tide, the cause of which remains unexplained.
