# John Edwards of Halifax and the Patenting of Transparent Vellum

## Innovations in Vellum Treatment
In 1785, John Edwards of Halifax introduced a technical advancement in the field of bookbinding by patenting a method to make vellum transparent. This specific application of vellum was utilized as a covering for delicate paintings. 

## Bookbinding and Artistic Style
Beyond his work with transparent vellum, Edwards was known for his artistic contributions to the physical construction of books. He practiced the technique of painting pictures on the fore-edges of many of his volumes. This method, which had been followed in the 17th century by Samuel Mearne, ensured that the artwork remained hidden until the book was opened.

Edwards shared a preference for classical ornaments and borders with another binder, John Whitaker. Because of this shared aesthetic, the bindings produced by both Edwards and Whitaker are frequently referred to as "Etruscan."

## Historical Context of Vellum and Binding
The use of vellum in book production has a long history. By approximately the 5th century, the practice of folding vellum into pages was established. In this process, sheets were folded once to create gatherings of four or more sheets, which were then held together by stitches through the fold at the back. These gatherings, or quires, were subsequently fastened together using threads tied around a strong strip of vellum or leather held at right angles to the backs. This method of sewing is known as "flexible" work and continues to be used for valuable books.

In the earliest vellum manuscripts, independent decoration appeared in primitive forms, such as scrolls, dotted patterns, cables, and cross-hatchings—sometimes featuring simple domestic objects or birds—located at the end or head of the text. Other decorative practices included the gilding of vellum, the use of purple staining, and the writing of text in coloured or gold inks.

## Contemporary Bookbinding in England
Edwards operated during a period toward the end of the 18th century when decorative binding in England was considered to be at a low level. Other notable figures of the era included Roger Payne, a native of Windsor who worked in London. Payne is credited with introducing ornamental inside linings (known as "doublures") and richly gold-tooled corner-pieces. He also invented "straight grain" morocco by graining the leather in a single direction.

While Edwards and Whitaker focused on the "Etruscan" style, John Whitaker utilized calf for his bindings, which he ornamented using prints from engraved metal plates and strong acids.
