# Brewing and the Cooling of Wort

The production of beer involves a sequence of seven main processes: grinding, mashing, boiling, cooling, fermenting, cleansing, and racking and storing. Central to this process is the creation and treatment of wort, the liquid portion of the finished mash.

## The Production of Wort
The process begins with malt, which is cleaned, graded, and crushed in a malt mill to create "grist." This grist is moved to a grist hopper and then into a mashing machine, where it is mixed with hot water from a hot liquor vessel. This mixture enters the mash-tun, a large wooden or metal vessel featuring a perforated false bottom to separate the wort from spent grains. In the mash-tun, the starch of the malt is rendered soluble. A rotating automatic sprinkler called a sparger is used to wash remaining wort from the grains.

The clear wort is then transferred to the copper for boiling with hops. Following this, it is turned into a hop back—a vessel with a perforated false bottom—to settle and separate insoluble hop constituents.

## The Cooling Process
Once the wort has boiled for the necessary time and rested in the hop back, it must be cooled before fermentation. This is achieved through two primary stages: the cooler and the refrigerator.

The cooler is a shallow vessel with a large area. By exposing the hot wort to a large volume of air, certain substances and hop constituents are precipitated and rendered insoluble. While some breweries omit this step to avoid the danger of wild yeast and bacterial infection, others use coolers placed in air-tight chambers with sterilized air. In America, cooling tanks—enclosed vessels capable of artificial aeration—are frequently used as replacements.

Because the cooler cannot bring the wort to the proper temperature for fermentation, a refrigerator is employed. Refrigerators may be horizontal or vertical. The operational principle involves allowing a stream or thin film of hot wort to trickle over a series of pipes through which cold water circulates. In the lager brewery of Messrs Allsopp, for example, the hot wort trickles over these pipes and is then conducted from shallow collecting trays to the fermenting backs.

## Fermentation and Chemistry
The wort is typically "pitched" with liquid yeast (or barm) at a temperature of approximately 60° F, though some systems use higher temperatures. The yeast splits the sugar in the wort into carbonic acid and alcohol. During this process, a froth appears, developing into a "curly head" (or cauliflower), then a "rocky head," and finally a compact "yeasty head" that emits gas with a hissing sound. To prevent the temperature from exceeding a maximum of 70-75° F, fermenting vessels use "attemperators," which are systems of pipes for running cold water.

From a chemical perspective, the maltster aims to break down protein substances in the barley so the wort has maximum nutritive value for the yeast, which assimilates nitrogenous compounds like amides. The degradation of proteins by proteolytic enzymes (specifically pancreatic and peptic enzymes) is necessary for producing stable beer, while albumoses may assist in creating foaming properties.

## Measurement of Wort
In the brewing industry, the specific gravity of wort is referred to as "gravity." The brewer's scale is 1000 times that of the physicist; for instance, a wort with a specific gravity of 1.045 is described as having a gravity of 1045, or "forty-five degrees of gravity." The "original gravity" refers to the wort before fermentation.

Gravity is also expressed in pounds per barrel, representing the weight excess of a barrel of wort over a barrel of water (which weighs 360 lb for 36 gallons). For example, a wort with a specific gravity of 1.05513 weighs 379.8 lb per barrel, making it a "19.8 lb. beer."
