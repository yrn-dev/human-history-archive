# Fixing Bricks and Joinery Provisions

In the construction of brick walls, specific provisions are made to allow for the attachment of joinery. One historical method involved the use of "fixing bricks," which were made of wood and crafted to the same size as ordinary bricks. These wooden bricks were built directly into the wall wherever fixing for joinery was required.

## Evolution of Fixing Materials
The use of wooden fixing bricks has been practically abandoned due to their tendency to decay and shrink. In modern practice, they have been replaced by bricks made of coke-breeze concrete. These concrete alternatives are preferred because they do not rot or shrink, and they provide a secure hold for screws or nails driven into them.

Other methods for providing joinery fixings include:
* **Wood Slips:** Building in slips of wood that are 4½ inches wide and the thickness of a joint.
* **Wood Plugs:** When no prior provision for fixing has been made, wood plugs may be driven into the brick joints. However, this requires great care, particularly at the corners of walls or in the joints of reveals, to avoid causing damage.

## General Principles of Wood in Brickwork
It is generally held that the integration of wood into brickwork should be avoided whenever possible. Despite this, wood wall plates are necessary in instances where wood joists are utilized. If these plates cannot be supported by iron or corbels of projecting brickwork, they must be let flush into the wall, replacing a course of bricks to create a uniform bed for the joists and facilitate easy fixing.

## Brickmaking and Standards
The materials used for fixing are integrated into walls composed of bricks that may be produced by hand or machine. Hand-made bricks are created by pressing tempered clay into wooden or metal moulds, using a "strike" (a strip of wood) to level the top. Some moulds utilize a "stock-board" with an elevated tongue of wood to create a "frog" (a hollow) in the bottom of the brick.

Machine-made bricks are categorized as plastic or semi-plastic. Plastic bricks are often produced using grinding-mills and pug-mills. The pug-mill delivers a continuous mass of clay through a nose approximately the size of the required brick, which is then sliced by a wire cutting frame; these are known as "wire-cuts."

In England, brick sizes varied until 1625, when a statute regulated the standard size to 9 x 4½ x 3 inches.

## Other Brick Applications and Structures
Beyond standard walling, bricks are used in various specialized capacities:
* **Paving:** Ordinary bricks laid flat or on edge were once common for paving but have largely been replaced by cement paving or special tiles for sanitary reasons. Specialized hard-texture bricks with grooves for drainage are used for stables. Dutch clinkers, which are light yellow and measure 6 x 3 x 1½ inches, are also used for paving.
* **Arches and Lintels:** Arches may be built in English or Flemish bonds. Square-headed openings may use lintels made of stone, iron, steel, or concrete. Unless made of steel or reinforced concrete, these lintels should have relieving arches turned immediately over them.
* **Chimneys:** Flues are built with precision, often separated by smoke-proof divisions called "withes," which are typically half a brick thick.
* **Specialized Buildings:** Brick is recommended for the winter portion of aviaries because it is practically vermin-proof and maintains a more stable temperature than wooden structures.
