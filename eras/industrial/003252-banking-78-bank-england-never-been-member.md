# Banking in the United Kingdom and Ireland

## Early Banking Structures in England and Wales
From 1694 until 1833 (with a specific restriction period from 1694 to 1826), legislation prohibited the formation of banks with more than six partners. This resulted in a landscape dominated by small, isolated firms throughout England and Wales for over a century. These banks were often managed by members of the Society of Friends. Because of the partner limit, the death of a partner frequently led to the withdrawal of capital, necessitating the search for new partners with sufficient means. This caused constant change and hindered the growth of confidence unless a relation or son could fill the vacancy.

Structurally, banks in London were limited to a single office, whereas banks in country districts frequently maintained branches in nearby small market-towns. By 1844, there were 976 banking offices in England and Wales; by 1906, this number had grown to more than 5,880. Despite the increase in offices, the number of individual banks decreased. By 1906, English banking was conducted primarily by approximately sixty joint-stock banks and ten private banks.

## The Bank of England and Note Issuance
The Bank of England serves as a central organ of the London money market. Its notes are printed in black from surface-plates without special protection or color, utilizing only a watermark in the paper. These notes are not reissued once returned to the bank and have a short average life of approximately six weeks. Forgery is relatively uncommon due to this policy and the fact that the lowest denomination is £5.

The Bank Act of 1844 sought to create a monopoly for the Bank of England as the sole issuer of notes in England and Wales. Prior to this act, nearly all provincial banks possessed the privilege of issue. On October 12, 1844, the act came into operation; by 1906, nearly all provincial banks in England had lost their right of issue. In October 1844, the authorized issue for 207 private banks was £5,153,417 and for 72 joint-stock banks was £3,478,230. The actual circulation in October 1844 included £20,228,800 from the Bank of England, £4,674,162 from private banks, and £3,331,516 from joint-stock banks.

## The Clearing House System
City banks established a "Clearing House" by 1776, though records from the Grasshopper bank suggest a less organized settlement system existed as early as 1773. In 1810, a dedicated house was taken for this purpose, with forty-six banking houses settling accounts there. In 1844, twenty-six of the forty-nine London banks were connected to the Clearing House, at a time when only private banks were permitted membership. Joint-stock banks joined in 1854. By 1906, the membership consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock and one private.

The Bank of England has never been a member of the Clearing House, although it "clears on one side." This means its claims on clearing bankers are processed through the Clearing House, while the claims of clearing bankers on the Bank of England are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times daily. Banks located at Charing Cross or Fleet Street also did not belong to the Clearing House. In 1858, Sir John Lubbock (Lord Avebury) arranged for the clearing of country cheques, allowing cheques from distant English villages to be handled as conveniently as those from London.

## Financial Crises and the Money Market
The London money market at the start of the 20th century operated through several organs. The most active included the Bank of England, private and joint-stock banks (including foreign banks), bill-brokers, and discount houses. Intermittently active participants included certain finance houses, great merchants, and members of the Stock Exchange. Many great merchant houses were registered as bankers, though they focused on finance business, such as issuing capital for companies and making loans to foreign governments.

The Bank of England has managed severe financial pressures through the "bank rate"—the rate of discount charged by the leading bank. During the crisis of May 1866, £4,000,000 left the bank in a single day, reducing reserves to £415,000 by June 1st. The bank rate was raised to 10%, and while the government permitted the suspension of the act, it was not done. The rate did not return to 8% until August 6th. In contrast, the crisis of October 1890 was less severe due to the actions of the directors and governor Mr. Lidderdale, who imported £3,000,000 in gold from Paris, keeping reserves above £10,000,000.

## Banking in Ireland
Banking in Ireland faced legislation that was less favorable than in England. In 1695, Dublin merchants attempted to form a public bank based on the Bank of England model, but the Bank of Ireland was not established until 1783, with David La Touche, junior, as the first governor. 

The Bank of Ireland enjoyed a protected position; its founding act prohibited any company or society exceeding six members from borrowing or taking up money on notes or bills payable on demand. This restriction was modified in 1821 to allow companies larger than six members to operate if they were more than 50 miles from Dublin. This distance limit was not fully withdrawn until 1845.
