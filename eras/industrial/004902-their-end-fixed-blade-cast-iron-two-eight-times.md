# Cast Iron and Steel Applications

## Civil Engineering and Foundations
Cast iron has been utilized extensively in the construction of piers and bridges. In pier founding, "screw piles" are employed in loose or compressible soil. These are cast iron piles that are screwed into the ground rather than driven. Each screw pile features a cast iron blade at its end, which ranges from two to eight times the diameter of the pile's shaft. The pitch of this screw is between one-fourth and one-half of the blade's external diameter.

In bridge construction, cast iron was used for arches from the late 18th century into the early 19th century. Due to the difficulty of casting heavy ribs, some bridges used cast voussoirs, similar to masonry. Examples include the Wearmouth bridge (1793-1796) with a 235 ft. span, and the Southwark bridge (1814-1819) with a center span of 240 ft. and a rise of 24 ft. In Paris, the Carrousel (1834-1836) and Austerlitz (1800-1806) bridges also featured cast iron arches. Additionally, an aqueduct bridge in Washington, erected in 1858 by M.C. Meigs, utilized two arched ribs made of cast iron pipes with a 200 ft. span.

In early braced bridges in America and England, cast iron was typically used for compression members—often as circular or octagonal tubes—while wrought iron was used for tension members. The Newark Dyke bridge (1851-1853), a pin-jointed Warren girder bridge, utilized cast iron struts and hollow cast iron top flanges. Similarly, the early American Fink truss used cast iron for compression members. While cast iron was once used for early railway girders, it is now primarily used for arched bridges of moderate span.

## Industrial and Mechanical Equipment
Cast iron is used in the fabrication of furnaces and boilers. Deville's portable blast furnace consists of a single cast iron cylinder lined with fireclay and closed at the bottom by a perforated cast iron plate. 

In naval and land-based electric-light installations, the "Niclausse" large-tube boiler employs headers usually made of malleable cast iron with cast-in diaphragms, though steel is sometimes used. These headers are bolted to socket-pieces riveted to the steam-chest.

In the construction of bicycles, malleable cast iron is commonly used for lugs or junctions, which are bored to fit the outside of weldless cold-drawn steel tubes and joined via brazing.

## Metallurgy and Weaponry
Historically, Indian iron and steel were highly esteemed across the East and imported into Persia and Abyssinian ports. Edrisi noted that the Hindus excelled in fusing iron to create a soft iron known as "Indian Steel" (HINDIAH or *al-hadíd al-Hindí*), used to forge some of the world's most famous sabres. Ancient sources, including Ctesias, mention wonderful swords made of such material, and the Malli and Oxydracae once sent 100 talents of *ferrum candidum* as a gift to Alexander.

In the context of fencing equipment, the epee consists of a blade and a haft. The haft is inserted into a grip (poignet) made of tough wood covered in materials such as leather, wound cord, or india-rubber. The pommel, which serves as a counterbalance and is usually made of steel, is either eight-sided or roughly spherical. In "epees demontables," the pommel can be unlocked or unscrewed to allow the blade to be changed.

## Mythological References
In Japanese mythological legends, the "eight hundred myriads" of Kami assembled in the "tranquil river" (the Milky Way) to plan the enticement of a goddess from her retirement. As part of this plan, the Kami of "thought combination" obtained iron from the "mines of heaven," which was forged into a mirror measuring eight feet.
