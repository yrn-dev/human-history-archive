# Joint-Stock Banks

Joint-stock banks are banking institutions organized as joint-stock companies, distinguishing them from private banks. Their development in the United Kingdom was shaped by a series of legislative acts that gradually removed restrictions on their establishment, their location, and the liability of their shareholders.

## Legal Evolution and Establishment
The early legal framework of English banking was defined by the acts of 1694 and 1697, the latter of which declared that no joint-stock bank was to be established within England during the continuance of the Bank of England. This was further reinforced by the act of 1708, which prohibited any company or partnership exceeding six persons from borrowing or taking up money on bills or notes payable on demand or in less than six months. This restriction was confirmed by the act of 1800.

Significant changes began with the Banking Act of 1826, which permitted the establishment of co-partnerships of more than six persons—effectively joint-stock companies—provided they were located more than 65 miles from London. The act of 1833 subsequently removed this distance restriction, allowing joint-stock banks to be established within the 65-mile radius. However, banks within this radius were still prohibited from issuing notes.

## Liability and Corporate Structure
Until 1862, joint-stock banks operated without limited liability. Every shareholder was liable for the bank's obligations to the extent of their entire means. This lack of protection deterred many wealthy individuals from becoming shareholders. The risks of this system were evidenced by the failures of the Western Bank of Scotland in 1858 and the City of Glasgow Bank in 1878, both of which caused great hardship to overconfident investors.

The act of 1862 introduced "limited liability," though the Companies Act of 1879 later enabled unlimited companies to adopt this status. Under limited liability, a shareholder who has paid the nominal amount of their holding is not liable for further amounts, unless the company issues bank notes; in the case of note issuance, shareholders remain liable as if the company were an unlimited company. By 1879, almost every joint-stock bank in the United Kingdom had utilized these facilities, except for those already limited by special act or charter.

## Note Issuance and Circulation
Originally, most provincial banks in England and Wales held the privilege of issue. The Bank Act of 1844 significantly altered this. Prior to the act's operation on October 12, 1844, one joint-stock bank (the Western District Joint-Stock Banking Co.) and eight private banks ceased issuing notes; the former was dissolved.

Under the 1844 act, the maximum circulation for English issuing banks was fixed based on the average circulation of the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. At that time, 72 joint-stock banks were allowed an authorized issue of £3,478,230, while 207 private banks were allowed £5,153,417. By October 1844, the actual circulation of joint-stock bank notes in England was £3,331,516.

By May 1907, the number of joint-stock banks with authorized issues had fallen to 17, with an actual issue of £437,693. The average circulation for joint-stock banks in England in 1906 was £429,000.

## Growth and Market Influence
The number of joint-stock banks grew steadily in the early 19th century. Registered records show there were 6 joint-stock banks in 1827, 35 in 1833, and 118 by 1842. By 1844, the *Banking Almanac* recorded 640 joint-stock offices in England and Wales. By 1906, while the number of individual banks had diminished, the number of offices had increased significantly; the banking business of England was carried out by approximately sixty joint-stock banks and ten private banks.

The growth of large joint-stock banks occurred primarily through the absorption of smaller banks by three or four large institutions. These large banks gained significant influence over foreign exchanges due to their large holdings of bills, sometimes wielding more influence than the Bank of England. Furthermore, these institutions began to coordinate to support the Bank of England in maintaining its reserve to stabilize the money market. This included private negotiations where the Bank of England obtained temporary control of large sums from big banks to take cash off the market.

## Integration into the Clearing House
The London "Clearing House," where banks settled accounts with one another, was established by 1776. Initially, only private banks were permitted to be members; of the 49 London banks in 1844, 26 were connected to the Clearing House. Joint-stock banks were eventually admitted to the body in 1854. By 1906, the Clearing House consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank.
