# The Bank Act of 1844

The Bank Act of 1844 was a piece of legislation regulating the note-issue of the country in England and Wales. Prior to this act, nearly all provincial banks in England and Wales, in addition to the Bank of England, held the privilege of issue.

## Implementation and Immediate Effects
The act came into operation on October 12, 1844. During the period when the act was being discussed in parliament, provincial banks continued their operations as they had previously. However, once the arrangements created by the act were made public, nine banks ceased to issue their notes before the official start date of October 12. Of these nine banks, eight were private and one was a joint-stock bank. The Western District Joint-Stock Banking Co. was dissolved, and one private bank was closed. The remaining seven banks began issuing Bank of England notes and were granted certain privileges for doing so.

## Regulation of Note Circulation
The act restricted the power of provincial banks to issue notes. The maximum circulation for English issuing banks was fixed based on the average circulation during the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. At that time, the authorized issues were distributed as follows:
*   **Private Banks:** 207 banks with an authorized issue of £5,153,417.
*   **Joint-Stock Banks:** 72 banks with an authorized issue of £3,478,230.
*   **Total Authorized Issue:** £8,631,647.

By the monthly return ending October 12, 1844, the actual circulation in England consisted of £20,228,800 from the Bank of England, £4,674,162 from private banks, and £3,331,516 from joint-stock banks. In Scotland, the circulation from chartered, private, and joint-stock banks was £2,987,665.

## Structural Changes to the Bank of England
Under the act, the Bank of England became the sole source for obtaining legal tender notes, a position of significant importance during times of financial pressure. The act divided the bank's accounts into two departments: the "issue department" and the "banking department." The notes held in the banking department are technically referred to as the "Reserve."

This division created a system where, if the Bank of England is called upon for a sum in specie or notes larger than the Reserve, the government must grant permission to "suspend the Bank Act" to meet the demand, regardless of the amount of specie held in the issue department. Between 1844 and 1906, the government granted such authority on three occasions during crises in 1847, 1857, and 1866. The limits of the act were actually exceeded only in 1857; in the other two instances, the granting of permission served to stay alarm.

## Economic Impact and Criticism
The act's influence on the English money market has been a subject of criticism and discussion. While there has been no anxiety regarding the payment of note circulation in specie since the act took force, the division of specie into two parts is considered to have disadvantages. Specifically, the smaller of the two parts must absorb the full strain of any demands for specie or notes.

Consequently, the liability to constant fluctuations in the Bank's rate of discount—a primary characteristic of the English money market—increased. Between the operation of the act in 1844 and the end of 1906, there were more than 400 changes in the rate. Furthermore, the hopes expressed by Sir Robert Peel in 1844 that commercial crises would cease after the act came into force were not realized.

## Long-term Trends and Amendments
The act remained unaltered despite discussions regarding the possibility of granting the Bank of England an automatic power to augment note issue on securities, similar to the system used by the Bank of Germany (*Reichsbank*). A proposal by Mr. Lowe in 1873 to charge 12% for such advances failed because such a high rate was associated with business risks and panic.

The original issue on securities allowed by the act to the Bank of England was £14,000,000, which was increased to £18,450,000 by March 29, 1901. As a result, the bank holds £4,450,000 less gold against issued notes than it would have under the original 1844 arrangements.

Additionally, data from a 1875 banking inquiry indicated that the proportion of notes in circulation relative to banking deposits in various districts had diminished greatly since 1844. In some cases, deposits increased three-fold, five-fold, seven-fold, or nearly twelve and a half times, while the circulation as a percentage of deposits dropped significantly (for example, from one-third to 9%, or from 22% to 1.5%).
