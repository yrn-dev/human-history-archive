# Bessemer and Siemens-Martin Steel in Wire Production

The production of modern barbed wire fencing relies upon the use of soft Bessemer or Siemens-Martin steel. These materials are preferred over iron wire, which was utilized for fencing prior to the introduction of steel. Iron was found to be unsuitable for this purpose because it lacks the homogeneity and sufficient tensile strength that are possessed in a high degree by open-hearth and Bessemer steels.

## The Manufacturing Process
The general process for manufacturing barbed wire begins with the steel, which is drawn in a wire mill in the usual manner. The resulting wire then undergoes a continuous galvanizing process to provide a bright finish and protect the material from the corrosive action of the atmosphere. 

The galvanizing sequence is as follows:
1. **Annealing**: A coil of wire is placed on a reel, and the first end is led longitudinally through an annealing medium—either heated fire-brick tubes or red-hot lead—of sufficient length to soften the wire.
2. **Cleaning**: The wire is fed through a bath of muriatic acid to remove scale, followed by a thorough washing in water.
3. **Coating**: The wire passes through a bath of spelter, which is heated slightly above its melting point.
4. **Finishing**: After being cooled by water, the wire is wound onto take-up blocks into finished coils.

To ensure a large output, this continuous apparatus allows 30 to 60 wires to pass through in parallel lines simultaneously. For customers requesting painted fencing, the galvanizing step is omitted; instead, spools of finished barbed wire are submerged in paint and dried after they leave the barbing machine.

## Barbing and Assembly
The twisting and barbing of the two longitudinal strand wires are performed by automatic machinery. In the production of 2 and 4 point Glidden wire, two coils of wire serve as the main strand wires. One main wire passes longitudinally through the machine while one or two additional coils (depending on whether 2 or 4 point wire is being made) are fed into the machine at right angles to the strand wire. 

The feeding mechanism pushes the cross wire forward, and a diagonal cut creates a sharp point on the first end. The wire is then fed forward again, wrapped firmly around one strand wire, and cut to leave a second sharp point.

## Distribution and Installation
Finished barbed wire is typically shipped on wooden spools, with each spool holding approximately 80 to 100 rods or 100 lb. These spools feature a center hole for a bar, allowing the reel to revolve for unwinding. 

Installation requires the use of a suitable barbed wire stretcher to ensure the wire is stretched with great care. The wire is attached to posts using galvanized steel wire staples, usually made from No. 9 wire, which are cut with a long, sharp, diagonal point for easy driving into the posts. While wooden posts were traditionally used, they are subject to rapid destruction and decay; consequently, reinforced concrete posts and steel posts have become popular alternatives to increase the durability of the fence.

## Historical Development and Metallurgy
The Bessemer process was introduced in 1856, followed by the open-hearth process in 1864. These advancements allowed steel and various types of iron to be purified, melted, and cast on an unprecedented scale. In the specific case of Bessemer steel converters, ganister—a slightly plastic siliceous sand found near Sheffield—is used for the lining.

In the United States, the barbed wire industry saw early patent activity in 1867 and 1868 by Lucien B. Smith, William B. Hunt, and Michael Kelly. However, the industry's practical beginning is attributed to Joseph F. Glidden, who received patents for barbed fence wire in 1874, and a subsequent patent for a manufacturing machine issued to Glidden and Phineas W. Vaughan that same year.
