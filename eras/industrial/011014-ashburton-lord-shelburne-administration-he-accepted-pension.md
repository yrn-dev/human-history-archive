# Ashburton

The name Ashburton refers to a market town in Devonshire, England, a river in Western Australia, and a title of nobility held by two distinct individuals.

## The Town of Ashburton
Ashburton is a market town located in the Ashburton parliamentary division of Devonshire, situated 24 miles north-west by west of Plymouth on a branch of the Great Western railway. The town lies in a valley surrounded by hills near the river Dart, with scenery extending toward Dartmoor and the vicinity of Holne Chase and Buckland. As of 1901, the urban district had a population of 2,628.

Historically, Ashburton is an ancient stannary town and a borough by prescription. According to Domesday, it was held in chief by Osbern, the bishop of Exeter, and rendered geld for six hides. The town was governed by a bailiff and portreeve, who were elected annually at the court leet held by the lord of the manor. In 1552, the bishop sold the manors of Ashburton Foreign and Ashburton Borough, which subsequently became crown property before being acquired in moieties by the Clinton family. The current joint lords of the manor are Sir Robert Jardine and the Lord Clinton.

The town's commercial history includes several markets and fairs. Bishop Stapledon obtained a Saturday market and two annual fairs during the reign of Edward II. In 1672, John Ford was granted a Tuesday market for the sale of woollen goods and wool made from English yarn. Later, in 1705, Andrew Quicke obtained two annual fairs in March and June for the sale of merchandise, corn, and cattle. Local industry includes the manufacture of serge, as well as saw-mills, paint factories, and breweries. The area also possesses a large deposit of umber, and formerly had valuable tin and copper mines and slate quarries.

The town's church, St. Andrew, was built in the early 15th century and features a cruciform design with a lofty tower and a fine old oak roof over the north aisle. It contains a tablet for John Dunning, the 1st Baron Ashburton, with an inscription written by Dr. Johnson. The town also houses a grammar school founded as a chantry in 1314.

## John Dunning, 1st Baron Ashburton
John Dunning (1731–1783) was an English lawyer born in Ashburton to John Dunning, an attorney. Educated at the local free grammar school, he entered the Middle Temple at age nineteen and was called to the bar in 1756. After a slow start to his practice on the western circuit, Dunning gained reputation and emolument in 1762 by drafting a defense for the British East India Company against the Dutch East India Company.

Dunning further distinguished himself in 1763 as counsel for Wilkes, specifically through his powerful arguments against general warrants in the case of *Leach v. Money*. By 1776, his practice had grown to the point where he reportedly received nearly £10,000 per annum. He served as the recorder of Bristol starting in 1766 and was appointed solicitor-general in December 1767, a post he held until May 1770, when he retired alongside his friend Lord Shelburne.

In parliament, Dunning represented the borough of Calne from 1768 until his promotion to the peerage. He became a regular member of the opposition and was known for able speeches. In 1780, he carried a motion by a majority of eighteen stating that the influence of the crown was increasing and ought to be diminished. Although he strongly opposed pensions and the system of sinecure officers, he later accepted a rich sinecure himself when he was appointed chancellor of the duchy of Lancaster in 1782 under the prime minister, the marquis of Rockingham.

Beyond his legal and political work, Dunning is suspected of having written a pamphlet on the law of libel and is the author of *A Letter to the Proprietors of East India Stock, on the subject of Lord Clive's Jaghire* (1764). He was also once suspected of being the author of the *Letters of Junius*.

## Alexander Baring, 1st Baron Ashburton
Alexander Baring (1774–1848) was an English financier and politician, the second son of Harriet and Sir Francis Baring. He was trained in his father's business and sent to the United States, where he married Anne, the daughter of William Bingham of Philadelphia. Baring became the head of the firm Baring Brothers & Co. upon his father's death in 1810.

Baring served in parliament from 1806 to 1835, representing North Essex, Thetford, Callington, and Taunton. He viewed politics through the lens of a businessman, opposing the restrictions on trade with the United States in 1812, the orders in council, and the 1826 act for the suppression of small bank-notes. He was a strong opponent of Reform.

In 1832, he accepted the post of chancellor of the exchequer in the projected ministry of the duke of Wellington, though he later advised the recall of Lord Grey. In 1834, he served as master of the mint and president of the board of trade under Sir Robert Peel. On April 10, 1835, he was created Baron Ashburton, a title previously held by his aunt's husband, John Dunning.

In 1842, Baring was sent to America to negotiate the Webster-Ashburton (or Ashburton) treaty. This agreement settled the north-east boundary of Maine, arranged for the extradition of certain criminals, and established that each state would maintain a squadron of at least eighty guns on the African coast to suppress the slave trade. The two governments also agreed to work together to persuade other powers to close slave markets.

Despite his history with Peel, Baring opposed the Bank Charter Act of 1844 and Peel's free-trade projects. He was a privy councillor, a D.C.L. of Oxford, and a trustee of the National Gallery and the British Museum.

## Other References
The name Ashburton also refers to a river in Western Australia. Rising in the mountains west of the Great Sandy Desert, it flows north-westward for 400 miles into the Exmouth Gulf. The river is non-navigable but traverses a vast tract of pastoral country and a gold-bearing district. Its mouth is the site of the port of Onslow and several pearl-fishing stations.
