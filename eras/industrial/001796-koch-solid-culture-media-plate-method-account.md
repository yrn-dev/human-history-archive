# Koch's Solid Culture Media and the Plate-Method

The introduction of solid culture media and the "plate-method" by Robert Koch in 1881 represented an all-important factor in the progress of bacteriological research. These innovations greatly simplified the modes of cultivation and, most significantly, the separation of bacteria. While various modifications have been implemented since their introduction, the routine methods of bacteriological procedure still employed are largely based on those provided by Koch.

## Robert Koch and Early Discoveries
Robert Koch (1843–1910) was a German bacteriologist born in Hanover. He began his famous researches while practicing as a physician at Wollstein. By 1876, Koch had obtained a pure culture of the anthrax bacillus, and seven years later, he announced a method of preventive inoculation against the disease. 

Following his appointment as a professor at the School of Medicine in 1880 and later as a director of the Institute of Health and a chair at Berlin University, Koch utilized improved bacteriological investigation methods to make several landmark discoveries. In 1882, he discovered the bacillus of tuberculosis. In 1883, during an official mission to India and Egypt to study the etiology of Asiatic cholera, he identified the comma bacillus as the specific organism responsible for the malady.

## Principles of Culture Media
To cultivate bacteria outside the body, media must be prepared to serve as food material and sterilized using heat. The general principle is to provide nutriment that closely resembles the organism's natural habitat; for pathogenic bacteria, this means mimicking the natural fluids of the body.

Media may be prepared in fluid or solid conditions. Solid media are achieved through a process of coagulation or by adding a gelatinizing agent. Examples include blood serum solidified at a suitable temperature or media based on meat extract with the addition of agar or gelatine as solidifying agents. To compensate for proteids lost during coagulation, non-coagulable proteids (commercial "peptone") are added.

The reaction of the media is critical and is usually kept neutral or slightly alkaline. For delicate work, titration methods may be used to standardize the reaction. These media are stored in glass flasks or tubes protected by cotton-wool plugs and sterilized by heat.

## The Plate-Method and Cultivation
Solid media are generally preferred over fluid media because bacterial growth appears as a discrete mass, allowing accidental contamination to be easily recognized. To create a culture, a small amount of material containing bacteria is transferred to the medium using a sterile platinum wire. These inoculated tubes are kept in incubators with regulating apparatus to maintain constant temperatures, typically either 20°C (a warm summer temperature) or 37°C (body temperature).

Once growth appears, "subcultures" can be made by inoculating fresh tubes, allowing growth to be maintained indefinitely. When only one variety of bacterium is present, a "pure culture" is obtained.

For bacteria that grow only in the absence of oxygen (strictly anaerobic bacteria), different methods are required:
* **Deep Cultures:** Inoculation is made deep within a solid medium, particularly one containing a reducing agent like glucose.
* **Plate and Fluid Methods:** To grow anaerobic organisms on the surface of a medium using the plate method, or in fluids, air is displaced by an indifferent gas, typically hydrogen.

## Application in Bacteriological Identification
A full description of a bacterium requires an account of its microscopical characters, its biological properties, its effects on animals via inoculation, and its growth characters in various media. Specific media are devised to test these characters:
* **Fermentative action:** Tested by adding sugars such as glucose, lactose, or saccharose.
* **Reaction changes:** Indicated by the addition of litmus.
* **Indol formation:** Tested using peptone solution.
* **Curdling:** Determined by using sterilized milk as a culture medium.

## Further Research and Toxin Study
Beyond cultivation, Koch contributed to the study of bacterial toxins. To set free intracellular toxins, Koch developed a method of grinding tubercle bacilli in an agate mortar and treating them with distilled water until no deposit remained. 

In 1890, Koch announced the preparation of tuberculin, an agent intended to inhibit the growth of the tubercle bacillus. While it did not fulfill expectations as a remedy for consumption, it became a common means of diagnosing tuberculosis in animals intended for food. Later, in 1901, Koch maintained at a London congress that tuberculosis in cattle and humans are not the same disease, suggesting that the risk of infection to humans from meat and milk is lower than from other humans.
