# Heidelberg

Heidelberg is a city in Germany that has served as a significant center for education, science, and historical events. It is notably the site of a university that attained a high level of fame through the efforts of figures such as Johann von Dalberg.

## Academic and Intellectual History
The university of Heidelberg was developed into a prestigious institution with the assistance of Johann von Dalberg, a privy councillor to the elector palatine Philip. Dalberg was an ardent humanist who established the university library and a college for students of civil law. He also founded the first chair of Greek, which was held by his friend Rudolph Agricola.

In later centuries, the city continued to be a hub for academic achievement. In 1822, Heinrich Georg Bronn was appointed professor of natural history after receiving his doctor's degree from the university's faculty of medicine in 1821. Bronn became a foundational figure in German stratigraphical geology, producing the *Lethaea Geognostica* (1834-1838) and the *Index Palaeontologicus* (1848-1849). He also wrote the *Handbuch einer Geschichte der Natur*, which presented species as direct acts of creation.

The city also attracted chemists such as Kopp, who studied at Heidelberg before pursuing further studies at Giessen. Kopp eventually returned to Heidelberg in 1864 as a professor of chemistry, where he remained until his death in 1892. His work focused on the physical properties of substances, specifically the connection between the boiling-point of organic compounds and their composition, as well as the concept of specific volumes.

## Political and Religious Events
Heidelberg served as a place of refuge and a site for high-level political and religious interactions. Following the French Revolution, Louis Gabriel Ambroise, Vicomte de Bonald, emigrated in 1791 and joined the army of the prince of Conde. After settling in Heidelberg, Bonald wrote his first major work, *Theorie du pouvoir politique et religieux* (1796), a highly conservative text that was condemned by the Directory.

In 1815, the city was visited by Emperor Alexander of Russia. The emperor had previously met with the baroness von Krüdener at Heilbronn, where she preached a "strange gospel" to him. At the tsar's request, the baroness followed him to Heidelberg and subsequently to Paris.

## Paleontology and Prehistory
The region around Heidelberg is noted for a significant prehistoric discovery. In a sandpit at Heidelberg, a single quasi-human jaw-bone was found. This specimen is described as being chinless, narrower, and heavier than a true human jaw-bone, leading scientific men to conclude that the creature's tongue likely could not move for articulate speech. Based on this find, scientists have theorized that the creature—referred to as the "Heidelberg Man"—was a heavy, almost human monster, potentially possessing huge hands and limbs and a thick felt of hair.
