# The Niagara Bridge

The Niagara bridge is recognized as the most remarkable bridge constructed using the suspension system. Its history is characterized by an initial period of suspension design followed by a transition to a steel arch structure to accommodate the evolving demands of railway traffic.

## The Roebling Suspension Bridge
Constructed between 1852 and 1855 by J. A. Roebling, the original Niagara bridge was a suspension structure featuring a span of 821 ft., which was the largest of any railway bridge at that time. The bridge reached a height of 245 ft. above the river.

The structural integrity of the bridge relied on four suspension cables, each measuring 10 inches in diameter. These cables were composed of seven strands, with each strand containing 520 parallel wires, totaling 3,640 wires per cable. To support these, each cable was placed on a separate saddle equipped with rollers on each pier. 

The bridge featured a stiffening girder that was primarily constructed of timber. This girder was box-shaped and braced, measuring 18 ft. in depth and 25 ft. in width. The design allowed the railway to be carried on top of the girder, while a roadway was located within it.

## Modifications and Replacement
Over time, the bridge underwent various strengthenings and repairs. In 1880, the original timber girder was replaced with an iron one. However, by 1896-1897, the bridge was taken down because it lacked the strength required to handle the increasing weight of railway traffic.

In its place, a steel arch bridge was constructed. This Niagara Falls and Clifton steel arch features a center span designed as a two-hinged parabolic braced rib arch, complemented by side spans of 190 and 210 ft. The current structure supports two footways, two roadways, and two electric-car tracks. The main span of this steel arch weighed 1,629 tons, while the side spans weighed 166 and 154 tons.

According to Prof. Claxton Fidler, the bridge represented the furthest advance in its type of construction due to the arrangement used to apply initial stress to the top chord. This engineering approach was designed to annul the bending action and excess compression in the upper member at the crown that typically occurs when a rib is erected on centering without initial stress.

## General Principles of Suspension Systems
The Niagara bridge utilized a system where the principal members are in tension. Suspension bridges generally dispense with the compression members required for girders and much of the stiffening needed for metal arches, though they require massive anchorages and lofty towers. 

A typical suspension bridge consists of wires laid parallel, twisted wire strands, or chains made of links connected by pins. These pass over piers on saddles (usually with rollers) and lead down to anchorages in rock chambers. While the merit of a simple suspension bridge is its cheapness, its primary defect is flexibility. This flexibility can be mitigated by using girders and bracing, creating a mixed type of bridge, or by increasing the dead weight of the structure relative to the live load.

## American Bridge Construction Context
The construction of bridges like the Niagara bridge occurred within a broader American system where few railway companies designed their own structures. Instead, general specifications regarding loading and span were provided to expert bridge-building companies. These companies submitted designs, detail drawings, and strain sheets to railway engineers. This process resulted in American bridges generally following well-settled types with uniform designs, allowing for more systematic manufacture in workshops.
