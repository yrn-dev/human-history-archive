# Banking Practice and Legal Authorities

The study of banking practice is supported by a wide array of legal treatises, historical accounts, and professional guidelines. Key authorities include the Institute of Bankers (London) and its publication *Questions on Banking Practice* (6th ed., 1909), as well as works by J. Douglas Walker (*A Treatise on Banking Law*), Chalmers (*Bills of Exchange*), and Sir J. R. Paget and H. Hart, both of whom authored texts titled *The Law of Banking*.

## Legal Framework for Cheques and Drafts

The liability of bankers regarding drafts and cheques is governed by several legislative acts. Under section 19 of the Stamp Act 1853, a banker paying a draft or order payable to order on demand was relieved of liability if the endorsement was unauthorized or forged. This protection was maintained through the Bills of Exchange Act 1882. In the case of *London City & Midland Bank v. Gordon* (1903), it was determined that this protection extended to drafts drawn by a bank branch on its head office.

Section 60 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 provides similar protection for cheques, defined as "bills drawn on a banker payable on demand." However, this definition excludes documents issued by corporations that require payments to be made upon a signed, specific attached receipt. While the Revenue Act 1883 (section 17) applies crossed cheque sections of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 to such documents, it denies them the status of negotiable instruments.

Regarding crossed cheques, section 79 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882 stipulates that if a banker pays a crossed cheque contrary to the crossing, he is liable to the true owner for any resulting loss and likely cannot charge the customer. Conversely, protection is granted under section 80 and the proviso to section 79 if the banker pays according to the ostensible tenor.

## Banker's Obligations and Immunities

A banker is granted qualified immunity under section 82 when collecting crossed cheques for a customer, provided specific conditions are met:
* The cheque and money must be received from a customer with whom the bank has existing, definite business relations.
* The cheque must already be crossed generally or specially to the banker; a banker's own crossing under section 77 is ineffective.
* The banker must act in good faith and without negligence.

Negligence is defined as the failure to exercise due care for the true owner. To avoid this, the banker must ensure endorsements are ostensibly correct and verify authority for endorsements made per procuration. Bankers must not take cheques for private accounts if the holder is an agent, an official, or acting for a partnership, nor should they take "account payee" cheques for any account other than the one indicated. Furthermore, the *Gordon* case established that a banker must act strictly as an agent or "conduit pipe"; crediting cheques as cash before receiving the money is considered taking them as an owner, which removes the protection of section 82.

## Operational Practices and Risks

The practice of post-dating cheques allows drawers to achieve the results of a bill at a future date without a regular bill-stamp. Under section 13, subs. 1 of the Bills of Exchange Act 1882, a bill is not invalid because it is post-dated. Consequently, a banker cannot refuse a cheque presented after its apparent date of issue. However, the banker is bound to refuse payment if the cheque is presented before that date.

The authority to pay cheques is terminated by the customer's bankruptcy, insanity, or death, or by notice of an available act of bankruptcy. Revocation of authority must be unequivocal and come to the banker's conscious knowledge; unconfirmed telegrams are not binding.

Other banking facilities, such as the payment of bills accepted payable at the bank and the safe custody of valuables, are often extended due to competition. In the case of valuables, the Central Association of Bankers issued a memorandum stating that if valuables are delivered to the wrong person by mistake, the bank is liable for wrongful conversion regardless of negligence. If valuables are stolen, lost, or destroyed, the banker is a gratuitous bailee and is only liable if he failed to use the care an ordinary prudent man would take of his own valuables, considering the nature of the goods and available protections like strong-rooms.

## Trust Accounts and Fiduciary Responsibility

While theoretically expected to refuse trust accounts, bankers often open accounts with headings indicating fiduciary capacity. The primary obligation remains to honor the customer's cheque. A banker is not entitled to question the object of a cheque unless there is clear evidence of impending fraud. A banker may only be impeached if his conduct makes him party to fraud, such as pressing for the reduction of a private overdraft via a cheque from a trust account. For safety, bankers dealing with known trust moneys may insist on the authority of the entire body of trustees. Under the Public Trustee Act 1906, Joint Stock Banks may serve as custodian trustees, managing payments and securities while leaving active direction to managing trustees.

## Historical and Professional Context

The Institute of Bankers was first presided over in 1879 by John Lubbock, 1st Baron Avebury. Lubbock was a banker, politician, and naturalist who introduced the Bank Holidays Act 1871 and served as chairman of the London County Council from 1890 to 1892.

Historical records of banking include Thorold Rogers' account of the first nine years of the Bank of England and the 1619 act translation found in the *Quarterly Journal of Economics*. Other notable historical sources include Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873) and various works on the banking systems of Scotland and Ireland.
