# Automatic Trumpeters of Kaufmann and Maelzel

## Definition and Nature of Automata
An automaton is defined as a self-moving machine, or a device in which the principle of motion is contained within the mechanism itself. While this description technically encompasses watches and clocks, the term is generally used to describe contrivances designed to simulate the motions of animal life. When such a machine represents human figures and actions, it is sometimes specifically referred to as an *androides*.

## History of Mechanical Figures
The construction of automata has early roots, with historical notices mentioning the moving figures of Daedalus and the tripods of Vulcan. In 400 B.C., Archytas of Tarentum is said to have created a wooden pigeon capable of flight. During the middle ages, further instances were recorded, including an iron fly and an eagle created by Regiomontanus, a speaking brazen head forged by Roger Bacon, and an *androides* doorkeeper possessed by Albertus Magnus.

In the 18th century, the field advanced significantly through the work of Jacques de Vaucanson. He exhibited three notable figures: a tambourine-player, a flute-player, and a duck that could imitate the natural voice of the fowl as well as eat and drink. The visibility of the means used to produce these results encouraged the creation of similar mechanical figures.

## The Work of Kaufmann and Maelzel
Following the impulse provided by Vaucanson, other mechanicians developed ingenious figures. Knauss exhibited an automaton in Vienna that could write, while a father and son named Droz constructed mechanical figures capable of playing music and writing. Within this tradition of mechanical ingenuity, Frederick Kaufmann and Leonard Maelzel created automatic trumpeters. These specific automata were designed with the capability to play several marches.

## Related Mechanical Traditions
The Swiss have been particularly celebrated for their mechanical ingenuity. They are known for constructing various curious toys, including singing and flying birds, which are frequently displayed at industrial exhibitions.
