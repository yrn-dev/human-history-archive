# Bookbinding

Bookbinding is the process of creating a protective and often decorative covering for books. In technical terms, a "case" refers to the back and boards in which a book is bound. The materials used in this process vary, including buckram—a coarse fabric of cotton or linen stiffened with glue or other substances—as well as leather, vellum, and calf.

## Historical Developments in England
The appreciation for old bindings in England increased significantly following an 1891 exhibition at the Burlington Fine Arts Club. Collectors have shown a particular interest in English blind stamped bindings, embroidered bindings, and those attributable to Samuel Mearne, who worked during the time of Charles II.

Toward the end of the 18th century, Roger Payne of Windsor introduced several advancements to the craft in London. He is credited with inventing the "straight grain" method of graining morocco leather and introducing ornamental inside linings, known as "doublures," and richly gold-tooled corner-pieces. Payne is also noted for cutting his own exquisitely made iron binding tools.

Other notable 18th-century practitioners include John Edwards of Halifax, who patented a method in 1785 to make vellum transparent for use as a covering over delicate paintings. Edwards also painted pictures on the fore-edges of books, a technique previously used by Samuel Mearne in the 17th century. John Whitaker utilized calf for his bindings, which he ornamented using prints from engraved metal plates and strong acids. Because both Edwards and Whitaker favored classical ornaments and borders, their style is often referred to as "Etruscan."

## The Modern School and Art Binding
In large cities such as New York, Paris, and London, a distinction exists between large trade binders and a small class of art binders. While trade binders rely on machinery to produce large quantities of work, art binders follow principles of first-class work that have been in use since approximately the 5th century.

This revival was initially spurred by William Morris, who created designs for both blind and gold bindings. The modern school of design for gold-tooling on book-covers was later founded by T.J. Cobden-Sanderson. His pupils include Douglas Cockerell, known for a scholarly appreciation of ancient methods and original treatment, and Alfred de Sauty, who developed a style using delicate pointille work and inlaid leathers.

Women artists in America and England have also established themselves in the field. Sarah Prideaux has contributed both original work and literature on the subject. E.M. MacColl is credited with inventing a method for producing small curved gold lines using a tiny wheel, with designs provided by her brother, D.S. MacColl. Additionally, Joanna Birkenruth has utilized jewels cut *en cabochon* to recall decorative medieval bindings.

## Machine Binding and Commercial Production
Commercial binding is largely driven by American and German ingenuity. Modern machines are typically power-driven and must be set to a specific book size, making them most effective for large editions of a single title. 

There is a structural difference between hand-sewn and machine-bound books. In hand-sewing on bands, the loose ends of the bands are drawn through the boards and strongly fastened. In contrast, machines generally paste the loose ends of the sewing tapes to the inner surfaces of the boards. Consequently, a "cased" book is more prone to slipping out of its cover once the paste perishes.

## Related Crafts and Tools
The production of bindings requires specialized tools. William Caslon, who started his business in London in 1716, worked as a bookbinder's tool-cutter before becoming a famous typefounder. 

The value of a book to a collector is often influenced by its binding. In France, the high prices of French first editions and Elzevirs are frequently attributed more to their 17th and 18th century jackets than to the text itself.
