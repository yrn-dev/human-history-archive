# Reichsbank

The Reichsbank is the most powerful banking institution in Germany. It is managed by a bank directory appointed by the chancellor of the empire, through whom the government exercises complete powers of control. Shareholders participate in management via a committee, with membership requiring the holding of at least three shares. The influence of the bank extends across all separate kingdoms of the empire, a process facilitated by the uniformity of coinage established by the laws of 1871-1873.

## Note Circulation and Legal Framework
The business of the Reichsbank is heavily influenced by note circulation, which is governed by the law of 1875 and an amending law of 1899. These regulations are somewhat similar to the Bank of England's Peel's act of 1844. Under these laws, an uncovered limit for notes was established; originally set at £12,500,000, it was increased to £14,811,450 and later extended by the act of June 5, 1902, to £23,641,450.

Notes not represented by specie must be held against treasury notes (*Reichskassenscheine*), notes from issuing banks reckoned as specie, or discounted bills. These bills must mature no later than three months after being taken and generally require three good indorsements, though never fewer than two. Furthermore, at least one-third of notes in circulation must be covered by gold in bullion, foreign coin (reckoned at £69, 12s. per pound fine), money, current German notes, or notes of the imperial treasury.

The Reichsbank is legally bound to redeem its notes in current German money, which may consist of silver thalers, gold coin, or bar-gold at a rate of 1392 marks per pound fine of gold. While the importance of this obligation is admitted, the Reichsbank has not always provided facilities for the payment of these obligations in gold in practice.

## Financial Status and Statutory Powers
In the 1906 balance sheet, the Reichsbank held bills amounting to £67,000,000 and loans and advances totaling £14,200,000. The average notes issued for the year were £69,000,000, and as of December 30, 1906, the gold held was £24,069,000. 

A distinct feature of the German banking act is the automatic statutory power to exceed the legal limit of note circulation if business conditions require it, provided the bank pays 5% on the surplus. This differs from the English act, which allows no such automatic power. This arrangement has been utilized annually since 1895 to provide necessary banking accommodation and prevent panic. Between 1881 and the end of 1906, the duty on these extra issues totaled £839,052.

## The Clearing System and Business Facilities
The Reichsbank assists business primarily through note circulation and the clearing system (*Giro-Verkehr*). The bank absorbed and developed the clearing business of the Bank of Prussia in 1876 and established an office in Hamburg in a building previously owned by the Hamburg Clearing House.

Through this system, money can be remitted without charge to the sender or receiver between any of the 443 locations where the Reichsbank maintains an office. The recipient must hold an account at the bank, but the payer does not need one; they may pay their debt at any convenient office, and the amount is credited to the creditor's account the following day. This system effectively functions as a risk-free, free money-order system, unifying the German empire for monetary purposes from the Baltic shores and the frontiers of Russia to the frontiers of Switzerland. In 1906, these transfers exceeded £1,860,000,000.

## Relations with Note-Issuing Banks
The Reichsbank maintains an arrangement with four private note-issuing banks to ensure they work in harmony when the Reichsbank must raise its bank-rate to protect gold reserves. The official published discount rate of the Reichsbank is binding on these private banks once it reaches or exceeds 4%. At other times, private banks may not discount at more than 1/4% below the official rate, or 1/8% below the rate if the Reichsbank itself is discounting below the official rate.

The 1899 amending act also raised the capital of the Reichsbank to £9,000,000 and increased the reserve fund (drawn from surplus profits) to £3,240,000, exceeding the £3,000,000 required by the act. This act also increased the government's share of profits while diminishing the dividends for stockholders.

## Currency Issues
Under a 1906 law, the Imperial Bank began issuing notes (*Reichsbanknoten*) in denominations of 20 marks (£1) and 50 marks (£2, 10s.), adding to the existing 5, 10, 100, and 1000 mark notes. Consequently, treasury notes of 20 and 50 marks were withdrawn from circulation. While the bank receives deposits and allows cheques to be drawn against them, notes remain the primary means of payment for ordinary business in Germany.
