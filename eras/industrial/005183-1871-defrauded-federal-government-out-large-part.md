# 1871: Global Political and Legal Developments

The year 1871 was marked by significant geopolitical shifts in Europe and North America, as well as major legal and financial scandals within the United States federal government.

## European Conflict and the Unification of Germany
In 1870, a struggle for predominance in Europe began between Prussia and France. While Prussia had prepared for this conflict, France was characterized by financial corruption. The resulting defeat of France was swift; a major French army under the Emperor capitulated at Sedan in September, followed by another surrender at Metz in October. By January 1871, Paris fell to German hands after a period of bombardment and siege.

The subsequent peace treaty signed at Frankfort required France to surrender the provinces of Lorraine and Alsace to the Germans. This period saw Germany unified as an empire (excluding Austria), with the King of Prussia becoming the German Emperor.

## North American Political Transitions
In 1871, British Columbia entered the confederation and became part of the Dominion of Canada. As part of this transition, the province sent six members (later increased to seven) to the House of Commons of the federal parliament, as well as three senators. A primary condition for British Columbia entering the dominion was the promise of the speedy construction of the Canadian Pacific railway.

Within Canada, 1871 also saw a change in provincial leadership in Ontario. John Sandfield Macdonald, a Canadian statesman who had ruled the province with efficiency and economy, was defeated by the Liberals in December 1871, leading to his resignation as premier.

## The "Whisky Ring" Fraud
In the United States, a significant fraud against the federal government known as the "Whisky Ring" operated from approximately 1870 or 1871. Headquartered in St Louis, the ring consisted of revenue officers and distillers in cities including Cincinnati, Milwaukee, and St Louis. These individuals defrauded the federal government of a large portion of its rightful revenue derived from whisky distillation.

The illicit gains were divided between the revenue officers and the distillers. The officers levied assessments on the distillers, claiming the funds were for a Republican campaign fund to support the re-election of President Grant. In St Louis alone, these gains likely exceeded $2,500,000 between 1870 and 1876.

The prosecution of the ring was led with vigour by Benjamin Helm Bristow, who served as secretary of the treasury from 1874 to 1876. Bristow's efforts resulted in the indictment of more than 200 men, some of whom were convicted and imprisoned before eventually being pardoned. The prosecution was complicated by the personal friendship between President Grant and Orville E. Babcock, the President's private secretary and an alleged accomplice of the ring, which led Grant to interfere indiscreetly in the legal proceedings.
