# Book-keeping Systems and the Ledger

The ledger serves as the center of all book-keeping systems. While other books are maintained for practical convenience—and are thus frequently termed "subsidiary books"—the ledger remains the primary record. Transactions are typically first recorded in "books of entry" or "books of first entry" before being classified into the ledger. Some subsidiary books, which are used for statistical purposes rather than as a basis for ledger entries, are known as auxiliary or statistical books.

## Evolution of Ledger Methods
In the early stages of book-keeping, the ledger was not a complete record of all transactions but contained only those accounts deemed necessary to keep accessible. Many instances focused specifically on "personal accounts," which recorded transactions with other business houses. 

Initially, transactions that reduced indebtedness were recorded by date beneath the transactions that created the debt, with the reduction subtracted from the total. Because this was inconvenient, a second account—the "contra account"—was introduced to record the extinguishing or reduction of debt. These two accounts were placed on opposite sides of the ledger, leading to the evolution of the Dr. and Cr. account system currently in general use.

## Double-Entry Principles
The fundamental principle of double-entry book-keeping is the creation of a complete record of every business transaction. Because transactions are cross-dealings involving the simultaneous receipt of a benefit by one party and the imparting of a benefit by another, an entry of equal amount must be made on both the debit and credit sides of the ledger.

If clerical work is performed correctly, the aggregate amount on the debit side must always equal the aggregate amount on the credit side. A list of all ledger balances used to verify this agreement is called a "trial balance." However, a trial balance is a mechanical test and does not guarantee absolute accuracy; it cannot detect omitted transactions, records entered into the wrong accounts, incorrect money values, or fictitious entries. Consequently, it is not an adequate safeguard against fraud.

## Modern Adaptations and Specializations
To accommodate the growth of business and allow large staffs to work simultaneously, the ledger is often divided into sections:

*   **Cash Book:** This combines the cash and bank accounts in parallel columns. A third column for discounts can be added to remove the need for a separate discount journal. For petty payments, a "petty cash book" is used.
*   **Sales and Purchases Ledgers:** Personal accounts are often separated from nominal and real accounts. Customers' accounts are kept in "sales ledgers" (or "sold ledgers"), while trade creditors are kept in "purchases ledgers" (or "bought ledgers").
*   **General Ledger:** This contains nominal and real accounts. It may be further split into a "nominal ledger" and a "private ledger." The private ledger is typically managed by a confidential employee or the principal and often contains the trading and profit and loss accounts.

To make these various ledgers self-balancing and localize clerical errors, "adjustment accounts" are employed to complete the double-entry record in each ledger.

## Specialized Book-keeping Systems
Several modifications have been developed to increase speed and reduce clerical work:

**The Slip System:** This method utilizes movable records, reminiscent of Babylonian tablets. It can be applied to books of first-entry or ledgers. In books of first-entry, carbon sheets are used to create facsimile duplicates of original entries (such as invoices), which can be handled separately and sorted before being posted to the ledger. In some cases, these filed slips constitute the ledger record itself, though this is only practical when transactions per individual are few. More commonly, the slip system manifests as "loose-leaf ledgers" or "card ledgers," where accounts are on independent sheets of cardboard in cabinets, allowing "dead matter" to be removed from the active record.

**Tabular Book-keeping:** This system replaces usual forms with books ruled with numerous columns. In books of first-entry, this allows one book to handle multiple classes of transactions (e.g., separate columns for different business departments). In ledgers, it allows a large number of personal accounts to be recorded on a single opening if the number of entries per individual is small.

## Regional Practices and Legal Requirements
While fundamental principles are universal, practice varies by country due to legislative requirements:

*   **France:** The Code of Commerce requires a journal, an inventory, and a letter book. The compulsory nature of the journal is noted as making book-keeping methods comparatively backward due to the duplication required when using multiple books of first-entry.
*   **Germany:** Requirements are similar to France, though the journal is not compulsory. Accounts must be kept in bound books with consecutively numbered pages, which creates legal uncertainty regarding the use of loose-leaf or card ledgers. An annual balance sheet is required, though inventories may be taken every two years for large stocks.
*   **Belgium:** Merchants must keep a journal and balance book initialed by a prescribed officer. Letters and telegrams must be preserved for ten years.
*   **Spain and Portugal:** Spain requires an inventory, journal, ledger, letter book, and invoice book. Portugal prescribes a balance book, journal, ledger, and copy-letter book.
*   **Holland:** Businessmen must record commercial transactions and correspondence and prepare an annual balance sheet.

In Great Britain and North America, it is common to split the journal into several specialized books of first-entry, such as the sales journal (day book), invoice book (purchases book), returns inwards book, returns outwards book, bills receivable book, and bills payable book.
