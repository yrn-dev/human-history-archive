# PROGRESS OF NATIONAL BANKS, 1865-1908

## Origins of the National Banking System
The national banking system was primarily established to address the financial requirements of the Federal government during the Civil War. Because the government found it difficult to float bonds at profitable rates, Secretary of the Treasury Mr. Chase developed a scheme to create a compulsory market for these bonds. Under this plan, banks organized under Federal charters were offered special privileges; specifically, they could issue circulating notes only if they were secured by the deposit of government bonds. This system was authorized by the act of February 25, 1863, and was later supplemented by the act of June 3, 1864.

Initially, national banks did not hold predominance over state banking systems, which were firmly established in the confidence of the commercial community in older states. To shift this balance, a 10% tax was imposed on the face-value of state bank notes in circulation after July 1, 1866. This measure drove state banks out of the note-issuing business. While some state banks converted into national banks, others continued their commercial operations under state laws without the ability to issue notes.

## Growth and Expansion
The national banking system experienced remarkable growth in its early years. In 1864, there were 453 national banks with an aggregate capital of $79,366,950. By 1865, this number increased to 1,014 banks with an aggregate capital of $242,542,982.

Between 1865 and 1908, the system saw a steady increase in the number of institutions and the volume of their financial activity. In 1865, there were 638 banks with $166,448,718 in loans and discounts and $183,479,636 in individual deposits. By 1885, the number of banks grew to 2,664, with loans and discounts reaching $1,234,202,226 and deposits totaling $987,649,055. 

By the turn of the century, the growth continued. In 1900, there were 3,602 banks with $2,479,819,494 in loans and discounts and $2,380,610,361 in deposits. By 1908, the system had expanded to 6,625 banks, with loans and discounts totaling $4,585,337,094 and individual deposits reaching $4,176,873,717.

## Operational Regulations and Reserves
National banks operate under specific reserve requirements against deposits. In "reserve cities," the requirement is 25%, while "country banks" outside these cities are required to maintain 15%. These reserves do not have to be kept entirely in cash. New York, Chicago, and St. Louis serve as the three central reserve cities where cash is required, with only trifling deductions. In 1908, there were forty other reserve cities; banks in these cities are permitted to deposit half of their cash in national banks located in central reserve cities. Country banks are permitted to deposit three-fifths of their cash in any reserve city.

Regarding liability and legal status, shareholders of national banks are subject to double liability upon their shares in the event of liquidation. While national bank-notes are not legal tender, the government accepts them for all obligations except for customs dues.

## The Panic of 1907
The banking system faced a severe crisis in 1907, precipitated by over-speculation by a group of national banks. This was followed by the suspension of the Knickerbocker Trust Company on October 22, which held deposits of $48,000,000. This event triggered runs on other companies. In the week of November 2, New York banks experienced a deficit in required reserves of $38,838,825. To combat this, arrangements were made to import foreign gold, which soon approached $100,000,000.

During the autumn, national bank circulation increased by approximately $77,000,000, and the treasury transferred $72,000,000 to the banks. Despite these measures, the total amount of currency that either disappeared or was added to circulation during the panic exceeded $275,000,000, representing nearly one-tenth of the country's usual circulation volume. By December 28, 1907, the total bank-note circulation rose to an abnormal level of $687,340,835, which was reduced during the spring of 1908. In New York, the payment of cheques in currency was suspended from October 28, 1907, until approximately the beginning of 1908.

## Oversight and the Clearing-House
The New York Clearing-House Committee wields significant influence over the banking environment. It has the authority to refuse aid during emergencies to banks that are unwisely conducted. During the panic of 1907, this power was utilized to remove several speculative financial interests from the control of national banks. 

At that time, the Clearing-House membership consisted only of the sub-Treasury, state banks, and national banks. While their weekly reports of condition, released every Saturday, served as an index for the exchanges and the money-market, this index was often misleading or incomplete because trust companies did not provide regular weekly reports. To rectify this, the state superintendent of banking announced in early 1908 that he would use his legal power to require weekly reports from trust companies to provide a more complete mirror of New York banking conditions.
