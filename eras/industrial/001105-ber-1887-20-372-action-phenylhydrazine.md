# Organic Chemical Compounds and Derivatives

The study of organic chemistry involves a diverse array of compounds, including nitrogen-based substances, diketones, and specific hydrocarbons, each characterized by distinct preparation methods and chemical reactions.

## Nitrogen-Based Compounds

### Diazoimino Benzene and Azoxy Compounds
Diazoimino benzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N}_3$) is a yellow oil with a stupefying odour that boils at 59°C (at 12 mm.) and is prone to explosion when heated. It can be prepared through several methods: the action of ammonia on diazobenzene perbromide, the action of phenylhydrazine on a diazonium sulphate, or the action of hydroxylamine on a diazonium sulphate. Chemical conversions of diazoimino benzene include its transformation into chloraniline via hydrochloric acid (eliminating nitrogen) and into aminophenol via boiling sulphuric acid.

Azoxy compounds ($\text{R}[=\text{N.O.N}]\text{R}'$) typically appear as red or yellow crystalline solids. They are produced by the oxidation of azo compounds or by heating nitro or nitroso compounds with alcoholic potash, preferably using methyl alcohol. In acid solution, these compounds yield amines when reduced. Distillation with reduced iron produces azo compounds, while warming with ammonium sulphide results in hydrazo compounds. Azoxybenzene ($\text{C}_6\text{H}_5\text{N})_2\text{O}$ specifically crystallizes from alcohol as yellow needles with a melting point of 36°C. It can be found among the electro-reduction products of nitrobenzene in alcoholic-alkaline solution and yields azobenzene and aniline upon distillation. Concentrated sulphuric acid converts azoxybenzene into oxyazobenzene.

### Azoximes and Furazanes
Azoximes (furo [a.b.] diazoles) contain the ring system $\text{HC} = \text{N N} = \text{CH}[|]\/[|]\text{O}$. They can be produced from $\alpha$-benzil dioxime via the "Beckmann" change or by converting nitriles into amidoximes using hydroxylamine, followed by acylation with anhydrides or acid chlorides and the removal of water through boiling or heating. Most azoximes are easily soluble in benzene, alcohol, and water, and are highly volatile substances that sublime readily.

Furazanes (furo--a.a'--diazoles) are obtained by heating ammonia or alkalis with glyoximes (the dioximes of ortho-diketones). For example, dimethylfurazane is a liquid boiling at 156°C (744 mm.), prepared by heating dimethylglyoxime with excess ammonia at 165°C for six hours. Potassium permanganate can oxidize dimethylfurazane into methylfurazane-carboxylic acid and subsequently into furazanedicarboxylic acid. Furazanedicarboxylic acid melts at 107°C and forms nitrosocyanacetic acid with a deep yellow colour when dissolved in caustic soda. Furoxane is an oxide of furazane, and dibromfuroxane is known as Kekules dibromnitroacetonitrile.

## Diketones and Ketones

Diketones are categorized by the position of their two carbonyl groups: $\alpha$ (1.2 diketones, $-\text{CO}\cdot\text{CO}-$), $\beta$ (1.3 diketones, $-\text{CO}\cdot\text{CH}_2\cdot\text{CO}-$), and $\gamma$ (1.4 diketones, $-\text{CO}\cdot\text{CH}_2\cdot\text{CH}_2\cdot\text{CO}-$).

$\alpha$-Diketones, such as diacetyl ($\text{CH}_3\cdot\text{CO}\cdot\text{CO}\cdot\text{CH}_3$), are prepared by the action of isoamyl nitrite on isonitrosoketones or by boiling the product of alkaline bisulphites on isonitrosoketones with 15% sulphuric acid. Diacetyl is a pungent, yellowish-green liquid boiling at 87-88°C that combines with hydrocyanic acid and sodium bisulphite; dilute alkalis convert it into paraxyloquinone. $\alpha$-Diketones also form imidazoles (with aldehydes and ammonia) and quinoxalines (with orthodiamines).

$\beta$-Diketones, such as acetyl acetone ($\text{CH}_3\cdot\text{CO}\cdot\text{CH}_2\cdot\text{CO}\cdot\text{CH}_3$), form characteristic copper salts. Acetyl acetone is a liquid with a boiling point of 136°C, prepared by condensing ethyl acetate with acetone in the presence of sodium or by the action of aluminium chloride on acetyl chloride. It can produce $\alpha\gamma$-dimethyl quinoline through condensation with aniline. $\beta$-Diketones combine with phenylhydrazine to form pyrazoles, and with hydroxylamine to form isoxazoles.

$\gamma$-Diketones, such as acetonyl acetone ($\text{CH}_3\cdot\text{CO}\cdot\text{CH}_2\cdot\text{CH}_2\cdot\text{CO}\cdot\text{CH}_3$), are liquids; acetonyl acetone boils at 194°C. It is obtained via the hydrolysis of diaceto-succinic ester or by condensing mono-chloracetone with sodium aceto-acetate. $\gamma$-Diketones readily yield thiophene, pyrrol, and furfurane derivatives.

General ketones react with concentrated nitric acid to yield dinitroparaffins and with mercaptan to form mercaptols. They form isonitrosoketones when treated with nitrous acid. Treatment with the Grignard reagent in absolute ether solution produces addition products that decompose into tertiary alcohols when water is added.

## Ketenes and Hydrocarbons

### Ketenes
Ketene ($\text{CH}_2:\text{CO}$) is a gas with a penetrating smell, discovered among the gaseous products of electrically heated platinum wire under acetic anhydride. It can also be produced by the action of zinc on bromacetyl bromide. It can be condensed into a liquid and solidified (melting at -151°C). Its solution in water is acidic due to the formation of aceto-acetic acid. Other variants include dimethyl ketene, a yellowish liquid boiling at 34°C (750 mm.) that polymerizes rapidly; diethyl ketene, formed by heating diethylmalonic anhydride; and diphenyl ketene, an orange-red liquid boiling at 146°C (12 mm.) that does not polymerize.

### Indene and Furfurane
Indene ($\text{C}_9\text{H}_8$) is a hydrocarbon oil found in coal tar distillate (boiling between 176° and 182°C) with a specific gravity of 1.04 at 15°C. It boils at 179.5°-180.5°. It can be reduced to hydrindene ($\text{C}_9\text{H}_{10}$) by sodium in alcoholic solution or oxidized to phthalic acid by dilute nitric acid. Indene vapour passed through a red hot tube yields chrysene.

Furfurane is a substance with a characteristic smell that is insoluble in water. It reacts violently with hydrochloric acid to produce a brown amorphous substance. While it does not react with phenylhydrazine or sodium, it yields dye-stuffs when combined with phenanthrenequinone and isatin.
