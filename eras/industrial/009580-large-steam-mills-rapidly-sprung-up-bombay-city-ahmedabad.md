# Industrial Development in the Bombay Presidency

The modern industrial landscape of the Bombay Presidency is characterized by a significant expansion in the growth and manufacture of cotton, which serves as a primary feature of the region's industrial life. This development is most prominent in the form of large steam mills that have rapidly emerged in Ahmedabad, Khandesh, and Bombay City.

## The Cotton Industry
The cotton industry is heavily concentrated in Bombay City and Island, which houses nearly two-thirds of the mills. By 1905, there were 432 factories within the presidency, the vast majority of which were dedicated to the preparation and manufacture of cotton. In Bombay specifically, cotton-spinning is the staple manufacture, though the city also maintains workshops for local needs and flour mills.

The industry has experienced periods of volatility. Between 1881 and 1890, the number of factories in Bombay grew from 53 to 83, accompanied by an influx of industrial workers from surrounding districts. However, the decade from 1891 to 1901 was a period of depression caused by over-production and the ravages of famine and widespread plague. The plague, in particular, led to a population exodus from the island, disorganized labor at the mills and docks, and required significant municipal spending on sanitary improvements and plague operations. Following 1901, both trade and population began to revive. As of 1901, the cotton industry employed 131,796 people.

While steam mills have expanded, hand-loom weavers maintained a distinct position in the province as of 1901, with 178,000 weavers continuing to specialize in the manipulation of designs woven into cloth. In Broach, while handloom-weaving has become almost extinct, several cotton mills have been established.

## Other Manufactures and Crafts
Beyond cotton, the region produces a variety of specialized goods:

*   **Silk and Textiles:** Silk goods are manufactured in Bombay, Thana, Nasik, Yeola, Surat, and Ahmedabad. These materials are frequently decorated with woven or printed designs, though competition from European goods has caused most branches of this industry to decline.
*   **Furniture and Woodwork:** "Bombay blackwood furniture" is produced in Ahmedabad, Surat, and Bombay using Shisham (blackwood), a hard-grained, dark timber. While some pieces are considered clumsy due to original Dutch models, smaller ornamental items are highly prized. The most artistic furniture is created by a special class of workmen following inherited traditions, often passing a single design from father to son. Efforts have been made under European auspices to modernize this by integrating native work into Western designs. Additionally, "Bombay boxes" feature geometrical inlays of ivory, ebony, sapan wood, sandal wood, wire, and stags' horns. Ahmedabad and Surat are also noted for carved wood-work, with elaborate carving found on houses in Ahmedabad and examples existing in Yeola, Nasik, Surat, Baroda, and Broach.
*   **Metalwork:** Many goldsmiths find employment through the custom of investing savings in silver and gold ornaments; customers typically provide the metal, paying the goldsmith for labor.
*   **Salt and Other Production:** Government works at Udu and Kharaghoda in Ahmedabad produce large quantities of salt. The factory at Kharaghoda, known as Baragra salt, produces nearly 2,000,000 cwt. annually, most of which is exported via rail to Northern and Central India. Additionally, there is a brewery at Dapuri near Poona, and Broach maintains large flour-mills.

## Infrastructure and Economy
The industrial growth of the presidency is supported by an extensive railway network. In 1905, the total length of railway under the Bombay government open for traffic was 7,980 m (excluding Sind). Most lines concentrate at Bombay City, including the Indian Midland, Great Indian Peninsula, Southern Mahratta, Rajputana-Malwa, and Bombay, Baroda & Central India systems. A railway line also runs from Ahmedabad through the native state of Idar via Parantij.

Agricultural prosperity reached a peak under Sir Bartle Frere (1862-1867) due to the American Civil War, which created an enormous demand for Indian cotton in Europe. This period saw a significant influx of money into the country, leading to an epidemic of speculation.
