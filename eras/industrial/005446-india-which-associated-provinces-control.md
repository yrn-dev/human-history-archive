# India

India is described as an example of the practical federation of dependencies, characterized by associated provinces that are under the control of a central government.

## Administrative Structure
The Indian empire is divided for administrative purposes into four minor commissionerships and nine great provinces. The nine great provinces are presided over by the following officials:
*   **Governors:** Bombay and Madras.
*   **Lieutenant-Governors:** Bengal, Eastern Bengal and Assam, the Punjab, Burma, and the United Provinces (comprising Agra and Oudh).
*   **Chief Commissioner:** The Central Provinces.
*   **Agent to the Governor-General:** The North-West Frontier Province.

The four minor commissionerships are each presided over by a chief commissioner. The supreme executive authority within India is vested in the viceroy in council, which consists of the commander-in-chief and six ordinary members.

For legislative purposes, the governor-general's council includes fifteen members nominated by the crown. This council has the power, under certain restrictions, to create laws for British India, for British subjects residing in native states, and for native Indian subjects of the crown regardless of their location in the world.

## Home Government and Oversight
The administration of the Indian empire in England is managed by a secretary of state for India, who is assisted by a council of no fewer than ten members. The secretary in council maintains control over the expenditure of revenues.

Historically, the Board of Control for India was created in 1784. However, in 1858, following the complete transference of the East India Company's territories to the crown, the board of control was abolished. It was replaced by the India Council under the presidency of a secretary of state for India, with a specific provision that council members may not sit in parliament.

## Native States and Dependencies
Various native states have entered into a state of relative dependency upon Great Britain through treaties or as a result of conquest following the annexation of neighboring provinces. These states are organized under different government authorities:
*   **Central Indian Agency:** Includes Bhopal, Gwalior, Indore, and Rewa.
*   **Bombay Government:** Includes Bhaunagar, Cutch, Khairpur (Sind), and Kolhapur and its dependencies. The settlement of Aden, along with Sokotra Island and Perim, also forms part of the government of Bombay.
*   **Madras Government:** Includes Cochin and Travancore.
*   **Central Provinces Government:** Includes Bastar.
*   **Bengal Government:** Includes Kuch Behar and Sikkim.
*   **United Provinces Government:** Includes Garhwal and Rampur.
*   **Punjab Government:** Includes Bahawalpur, Chamba, Faridkot, Jind, Kapurthala, Mandi, Nabha, Patiala, and Sirmur (Nahan).
*   **Government of Burma:** Includes the Karen states and Shan states. Additionally, there are British tracts known as the Burma frontier and the Upper Burma frontier.

The state of Nepal has maintained close relations with Great Britain since the campaign of 1814-15. While independent in its internal administration, Nepal is required to receive a British resident, and the government of India controls its political relations with other states.

## British Baluchistan
British Baluchistan officially comprises the districts of Sibi, Peshin, and Thal-Chotiali. These districts were transferred to British authority via the treaty of Gandamak in 1879, though they had nominally been handed over to Kalat forty years prior. They currently function as an official province of British Baluchistan within the Baluchistan Agency, which extends from the Persian frontier and the Gomal to the Arabian Sea. While some districts within the agency remain highly independent, the political status of the province as a whole is similar to that of the native states of the Indian peninsula, with supreme control exercised by the agent to the governor-general of India and a staff of political assistants.

## Notable Locations
*   **Brindaban:** A town in the Muttra district of the United Provinces, located on the right bank of the Jumna. Founded in the 16th century, it is a popular pilgrimage site associated with the cult of Krishna as a shepherd. It features tanks, wells, bathing-stairs, and numerous temples, including the red sandstone cruciform vaulted temple of Govind Deva, dating from 1590. Its population in 1901 was 22,717.
*   **Badnur:** The headquarters of the Betul district in the Central Provinces. As of 1901, its population was 3,766. The town contains two bazaars, European houses, a *dak bungalow* (resting-place for Europeans), and a *serai* (inn for native travellers). Nearby is Kherla, the former residence of the Gond rajas, which contains an old fort in ruins.
