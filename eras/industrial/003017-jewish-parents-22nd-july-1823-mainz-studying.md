# Ludwig Bamberger

## Early Life and Education
Ludwig Bamberger (1823–1899) was a German politician and economist. He was born in Mainz on July 22, 1823, to parents of Jewish descent. His early professional preparation involved academic studies at the universities of Goettingen, Heidelberg, and Giessen. Following the completion of his studies, he entered the practice of law.

## Political Activity and Exile
Bamberger became an active participant in the revolution of 1848. In his native city, he emerged as one of the leaders of the republican party, serving as an editor for a local newspaper and acting as a popular orator. In 1849, he participated in the republican rising that took place in Baden and the Palatinate. Following the restoration of order, Bamberger was condemned to death, which necessitated his escape to Switzerland.

He spent several subsequent years in exile, residing first in London and later in Holland. In 1852, he moved to Paris. Through private connections, he secured a position at the bank of Bischoffheim & Goldschmidt. He eventually rose to the position of managing director of the bank, a role he maintained until 1866.

## Return to Germany and Financial Influence
The amnesty of 1866 allowed Bamberger to return to Germany. During his years in Paris, he had gained a thorough acquaintance with the practice and theory of finance and had saved a competence. He applied this expertise upon his return and was elected as a member of the Reichstag. In the Reichstag, he joined the National Liberal party, as he was among the exiles willing to accept the results of the work of Bismarck.

Bamberger became the leading authority in the German Reichstag on economics and finance, recognized as a persuasive and clear speaker. His influence was primary in the adoption of a gold currency and in the establishment of the German Imperial Bank in its present form. In his later years, he spoke and wrote strongly against bimetallism.

## Relations with Bismarck and Later Career
Bamberger maintained a complex relationship with Bismarck. In 1868, he published a short biography of Bismarck in the French language to foster a better understanding of German affairs. Later, in 1870, Bismarck summoned him to Versailles to assist in discussing peace terms, utilizing Bamberger's intimate knowledge of finance and France.

However, Bamberger eventually broke with Bismarck's policies. As a leader of the free traders, he refused to follow Bismarck after 1878 when the latter introduced policies regarding colonial development, state socialism, and protection. In a well-known speech, Bamberger described the day these policies were introduced as a *dies nefastus* for Germany. Consequently, he and several followers left the National Liberal party to remain true to their principles of free trade.
