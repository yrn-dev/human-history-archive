# Cesare Balbo

Cesare Balbo (1789–1853) was an Italian statesman and writer born in Turin on November 21, 1789. He was born into a noble Piedmontese family; his father, Prospero Balbo, was the mayor of the capital and held a high-ranking position within the Sardinian court. Following the death of his mother, a member of the Azeglio family, when he was three years old, Cesare was raised in the household of his great-grandmother, the countess of Bugino.

## Service Under the Napoleonic Empire
In 1798, Balbo joined his father in Paris. Between 1808 and 1814, he served the Napoleonic empire in various capacities. His service during this period took him to several locations, including Paris, Rome, Florence, and Illyria.

## Political Career and Exile
Following the fall of Napoleon, Balbo entered the service of his native country. During a period when his father served as the minister of the interior, Balbo joined the army and was tasked with political missions to London and Paris.

In 1821, a revolution broke out. Although Balbo disapproved of the revolution, he was suspected of sympathizing with it, which resulted in his forced exile. While he was permitted to return to Piedmont shortly thereafter, he was denied all further public service.

## Literary Work and Political Ideals
Because he was barred from public office, Balbo turned to literature as the sole remaining method to influence the destiny of his country. This transition was marked by reluctance and frequent, unsuccessful attempts to secure a government appointment. Consequently, much of his literary output is characterized as incomplete or fitful, often containing temporary or practical elements even in his most elaborate works.

The primary objective of Balbo's writing was to assist in securing Italian independence from foreign control. He did not desire or expect true Italian unity; instead, he was devoted to the house of Savoy, which he believed was destined to change Italy's fate. His genuine ideal was a confederation of separate states under the supremacy of the pope.

In his work *Speranze* (Hopes of Italy), Balbo addressed what he considered the first essential of independence—a point he felt was neglected by Gioberti in the *Primato*. In this text, Balbo suggested that Austria should seek compensation in the Balkans to offset the inevitable loss of its Italian provinces. He emphasized the necessity of patience, alertness, and both moral and military preparation.
