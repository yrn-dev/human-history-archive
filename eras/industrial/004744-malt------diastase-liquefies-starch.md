# Diastase in the Brewing Process

Diastase is a critical enzyme present in malt that plays a central role in the chemistry of brewing. It serves as the agent responsible for converting insoluble starch into soluble products, including sugar, dextrin, and intermediate substances. In the brewing sequence, this process occurs primarily within the mash-tun, where the starch of the malt is rendered soluble.

## Types and Roles of Enzymes in Malt
The brewing process involves a large number of enzymes. Within the malt, these are categorized by their specific roles:
*   **Cytase:** Dissolves the cell walls of starch granules.
*   **Diastase A:** Responsible for liquefying starch.
*   **Diastase B:** Responsible for the saccharification of starch.
*   **Proteolytic Enzymes:** These include peptic and pancreatic enzymes, which render a portion of nitrogenous matter soluble.

Other enzymes operate in the fermenting wort and yeast, such as catalase (which splits peroxides), invertase (which inverts cane sugar), glucase (which splits maltose into glucose), and zymase (which splits sugar into carbonic acid and alcohol).

## Chemical Action on Starch
The degradation of starch by diastase involves the transformation of starch-paste—a jelly created by treating starch with boiling water. When treated with an iodine solution, starch-paste and the first product of degradation, soluble starch, both exhibit a deep blue coloration. As diastase continues to act and the products become more diffusible and soluble, this blue reaction shifts to purple, then reddish, and eventually ceases entirely. This process is accompanied by a decrease in optical rotating power and an increase in cupric reducing power toward Fehling's solution.

Historically, it was believed that diastase first converted starch into a gummy substance called dextrin, which then became glucose. However, F.A. Musculus demonstrated in 1860 that sugar and dextrin are produced simultaneously. Between 1872 and 1876, Cornelius O'Sullivan proved that the specific sugar produced is maltose.

## Theoretical Models of Degradation
The theory proposed by Brown and Morris suggests that a starch molecule consists of five amylin groups. According to this model, diastase splits the complex molecule through hydrolysis into four groups of amyloins, while the fifth central group remains unchanged under brewing conditions, forming "stable dextrin." This reaction is represented by the equation:
5(C$_{12}$H$_{20}$O$_{10}$)$_{20}$ (starch) + 80 H$_2$O (water) = 80 C$_{12}$H$_{22}$O$_{11}$ (maltose) + (C$_{12}$H$_{20}$O$_{10}$)$_{20}$ (stable dextrin).

Amyloins are substances containing varying numbers of amylin groups in conjunction with maltose groups. As hydrolysis proceeds, amyloins become richer in maltose and poorer in amylin. Brown and J.H. Millar identify the final transformation products as maltose and glucose, with the latter derived from the hydrolysis of stable dextrin.

## Influence of Temperature and Quality Control
The mashing temperature is a primary factor in controlling the quality and type of beer produced. Higher temperatures tend to "cripple" the diastase enzyme. This results in the formation of more non-fermentable dextrinous matter relative to fermentable maltose.

*   **Pale or Stock Ales:** These require a "dry" character and high stability, necessitating a high proportion of dextrin and little maltose. Consequently, high mashing temperatures (typically 150° to 154° F) are employed.
*   **Mild Running Ales:** These are full, sweet beers intended for rapid consumption. They require low mashing temperatures to produce more fermentable sugars.
*   **Stouts and Black Beers:** These generally utilize a mashing temperature between 148° and 150° F.

In English brewing, mashing temperatures generally range from 140° to 160° F. The type of amyloins can be further regulated by combining kiln heats with mashing temperatures; for instance, a higher type of amyloin is obtained through low kiln and high mashing temperatures.

## Diastatic Energy and Substitutes
Malted barley contains diastatic energy in great excess. This surplus allows brewers to use the enzyme to transform starch from unmalted grains (substitutes) into sugar. The use of substitutes can simplify and accelerate brewing operations, increasing turnover and reducing costs. However, excessive use of substitutes—generally beyond 15-20%—can lead to a loss of flavor and poor beer quality.

## Comparative Processes
In the production of the Japanese beer *saké*, the functions of saccharification and fermentation are performed by a single micro-organism, the mould *Aspergillus oryzae* (used to prepare *Koji*). This mould replaces both the yeast of European breweries and the diastase found in malted grain.
