# Banking Institutions and Financial Management

The history of banking is characterized by a diverse array of institutional structures, ranging from small, isolated firms to powerful national banks. These institutions have varied significantly in their management, their relationship with government authority, and their impact on national and international commerce.

## Early English Banking
From 1694 to 1833 (with the exception of 1826), English and Welsh banking was constrained by laws that prohibited the formation of banks with more than six partners. Consequently, for over a century, the majority of banks were small, isolated firms. These institutions faced instability due to the death of partners; when a partner died, their capital often left the business, necessitating the search for a new partner with sufficient means. This led to constant change, which hindered the growth of confidence unless a vacancy was filled by a relative or son. 

While London banks never operated more than one office, banks in country districts frequently established branches in nearby small market-towns. Despite their size, some of these banks were powerful and generally well managed, with a considerable number founded by members of the Society of Friends.

## National Banks of Europe
Different European nations developed distinct central banking models to manage currency and stability.

### Germany
The Reichsbank stands as the most powerful banking institution in Germany. It is managed by a bank directory appointed by the chancellor of the empire, though the government maintains complete control through the chancellor. Shareholders participate in management via a committee, requiring a minimum of three shares for membership. The Imperial Bank's influence extends across all separate kingdoms of the empire, facilitated by the uniformity of coinage introduced between 1871 and 1873. It supports business through note circulation and a developed clearing system known as *Giro-Verkehr*.

### France
The Bank of France maintains a large reserve of specie, which provides stability to French trade and allows the bank to avoid the frequent fluctuations in discount rates seen in England. In 1906, the bank held an average of L42,000,000 in silver and L115,000,000 in gold. This gold reserve was generally larger than that of the Bank of England; for instance, during the Baring crisis in the autumn of 1890, the Bank of England borrowed L3,000,000 in gold from the Bank of France. While this large reserve imposes a heavy burden on shareholders, it results in a low rate of interest for borrowed capital, benefiting French industry and trade.

### Austria-Hungary
The Austro-Hungarian Bank originally belonged entirely to Austria in 1867, though it maintained branches in Hungary. Following a settlement in 1877, it was converted into an Austro-Hungarian bank with management divided between the two countries and head offices in both Vienna and Pest. This arrangement was renewed in 1887. The bank held the sole right to issue notes, although the government violated this charter during the war of 1866 by issuing *Staatsnoten* valued at 312 million gulden. An operation begun in 1892 sought to redeem these *Staatsnoten*, resume cash payments, and substitute a gold standard for the silver standard.

## Banking in the United States
In the United States, the Second Bank of the United States experienced poor management early in its career but achieved a firmer foundation in 1819 under the presidency of Langdon Cheves. The bank provided a currency described as more convenient and valuable than silver, which was eagerly sought in the interior, southern, and western parts of the Union. However, its policies drew complaints from private exchange dealers who had previously profited from currency fluctuations between states.

In New York, the Clearing-House Committee exercised significant influence by refusing emergency aid to banks that were unwisely conducted. This authority was utilized during the panic of 1907 to remove speculative financial interests from the control of national banks. At that time, membership was limited to state banks, national banks, and the sub-Treasury. Because trust companies did not provide regular weekly reports, the money-market index remained incomplete until 1908, when the state superintendent of banking used legal powers to require weekly reports from trust companies.

## International Finance and Taxation
Modern banking methods were exemplified by the Japanese war loans of 1904, 1905, and 1906. These loans were issued by three banks: one Japanese institution representing the government, a leading London bank, and the principal British bank operating in China. To minimize disturbance to the London money market, "application money" withdrawn from other banks was lent back into the short loan market as quickly as possible. The Japanese government managed its balances considerately, transferring a portion of the funds to the Bank of England.

In Spain, banking professionals are subject to specific taxation. Directors, managers, and representatives of banks pay a 10% income tax, while other bank employees pay 5%. Additionally, dividends on bank shares are taxed at a rate of 5%.
