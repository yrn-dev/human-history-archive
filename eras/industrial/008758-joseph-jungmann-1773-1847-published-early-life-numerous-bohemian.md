# Joseph Jungmann

## Overview and Role in the Bohemian Revival
Joseph Jungmann (1773–1847) was a pivotal figure in the revival of the language and literature of Bohemia. Along with Palacky, Safarik, and Kolar, Jungmann is credited as one of the four men primarily responsible for this cultural resurgence. This movement occurred during a period when the Bohemian language had been largely forgotten and was actively discouraged by the new Austrian government. Following the deaths of Comenius, Slavata, and Skala, a significant gap existed during which no important works were written in the Bohemian language. The revival began to take shape at the start of the 19th century, spurred by the romantic movement.

## Literary Contributions and Translations
In the early stages of the Bohemian revival, the movement was modest and focused almost exclusively on the publication of translations from foreign languages. Jungmann participated in this trend early in his life, publishing numerous Bohemian translations of writers from England and Germany.

## Major Works
Jungmann produced two monumental works that were central to the development of the Bohemian language and its literary history:

*   ***Dejepes literatury ceska***: A comprehensive history of Bohemian literature.
*   **German and Bohemian Dictionary**: A monumental lexicographical work that contributed largely to the development of the Bohemian language.

## Context of the Bohemian Revival
Jungmann's work followed the initial efforts of Joseph Dobrovsky (1753–1829), the first writer to draw attention back to the Bohemian language. While Dobrovsky wrote a history of Bohemian literature and a grammar, those works were mostly written in Latin or German. Jungmann and his contemporaries expanded upon these foundations to establish a more robust national literature. Other key figures in this era included the poet John Kolar, known for the personification of the Slavic race in *Slavy dcera*, and the historian Francis Palacky, who wrote a history of Bohemia covering the period from the earliest times to 1526.
