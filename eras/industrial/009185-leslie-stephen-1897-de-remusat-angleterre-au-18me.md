# Sir Leslie Stephen

Sir Leslie Stephen was a prominent English editor, critic, and author who played a central role in the development of national biography and the study of English literature and thought. He is noted as a representative of the "old schools" of scientific or motived criticism, which deduced its approach from the literatures of past times.

## The Dictionary of National Biography
In 1882, the publisher George Smith initiated a project to create a dictionary of national biography for England, following similar successful efforts in Sweden, the Netherlands, Austria, Belgium, and Germany. Smith consulted Leslie Stephen for the project, and Stephen served as the editor for the work's inception. 

The first volume of the *Dictionary of National Biography* was published on January 1, 1885. Under Stephen's editorship, volumes were published with complete punctuality every quarter. Stephen resigned the editorship in May 1891 and was succeeded by Mr. Sidney Lee. By the time the work reached its close—covering figures up to the death of Queen Victoria—it contained the lives of more than 30,000 persons. The archive notes that the value of this work in elucidating the private annals of the British people can hardly be exaggerated. Within this dictionary, Stephen authored the biography of Robert Burns (published in volume viii, 1886) and a biography of Bolingbroke (1897).

## Literary and Philosophical Works
Stephen produced a significant body of work focusing on the intellectual and literary history of England, particularly during the 18th century. His major contributions to this field include:

*   ***History of English Thought during the 18th Century***: Cited as a work of primary value for those seeking a general view of British letters during that era.
*   ***Lectures on English Literature and Society in the 18th Century*** (1904): A source used to glean information on the literary history of the period.
*   ***The Utilitarians***: A specific study on the subject.
*   ***Hours in a Library***: A work noted as an important reference for the study of 18th-century letters.

In addition to his own original works, Stephen served as an editor for other authors, including the *Letters of J. R. Green* (1901).

## Critical Influence and Associations
As a critic, Stephen was associated with figures such as Bagehot; together, they were known to criticize the sufficiency of titles produced by more "lyrical" and "enthusiastic" eulogists. His approach to criticism stood in contrast to the "copy-book formulae" of the early 1880s, such as Walter Pater's doctrine of art for art's sake or Arnold's definition of poetry as a "criticism of life."

Stephen was also involved in intellectual discourse through various meetings where papers were read and discussed. These discussions covered topics such as the immortality of the soul and the ultimate grounds of belief in the moral and objective sciences. He is listed among a membership that included other notable figures such as Huxley, Tennyson, Gladstone, and Lord Morley.
