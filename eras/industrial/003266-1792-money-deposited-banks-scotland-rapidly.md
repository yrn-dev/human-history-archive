# Banking in Scotland

## Growth of Deposits and Note Circulation
In the late 18th century and early 19th century, note circulation served as a very important part of banking business. However, around that period, deposits began to become far more important, a trend that continued thereafter. In Scotland, the money deposited with banks increased rapidly after 1792. Despite this growth in formal banking, the habit of hoarding savings in chests in amounts of £10 or £20 persisted until a much later period.

The Bank of Dundee provides a specific example of this transition. From 1764 until 1792, the bank's entire business consisted of issuing notes, with circulation varying between £23,000 and £56,000. The bank did not begin receiving money from the public until 1792. By the time the Bank of Dundee was amalgamated with the Royal Bank of Scotland in 1864, its deposits had reached approximately £700,000, while its note circulation had decreased to £41,000.

## Institutional Development and Structure
The Bank of Scotland is the only Scottish bank established by an act of parliament, following an act passed by the Scottish parliament in 1695. Its establishment terms were more favorable than those of the Bank of England, as it was granted the exclusive privilege of banking for twenty-one years without providing any consideration. Due to the reign of William III, the bank was prohibited under a heavy penalty from lending money to the king under any circumstances. The directors of the Bank of Scotland began granting cash credit accounts and receiving deposits with interest at a very early stage; a minute regarding the management of cash credit accounts is dated as far back as 1729.

Private banking did not have a considerable hold in Scotland. While eight private banks existed in 1819, all had disappeared by 1844. By 1906, the country's banking business was practically carried out by ten banks of issue, supplemented by two other small, more recently established banks. These ten banks operated 1,180 branches in 1906.

## Banking Infrastructure and Demographics
The number of banking offices in Scotland grew significantly between the mid-19th century and the early 20th century. In 1844, there were 368 offices, meaning there was one office for every 7,120 inhabitants. By 1906, the number of offices had increased to 1,180, reducing the ratio to one office for every 3,790 inhabitants.

## General Context of Deposits
In banking terms, a deposit is generally a sum of money lodged in a bank. More specifically, money lodged on a "deposit account" is acknowledged by a "deposit receipt." Such funds are typically not drawn upon by cheque, usually bear interest at varying rates, and require fixed notice for withdrawal.

Across the United Kingdom, bank deposits have shown progressive growth. Broadly speaking, deposits in the United Kingdom approximately doubled since 1872. By 1906, the total resources for the region—including capitals, reserves, and note circulation (which totaled roughly £177,500,000)—showed that Scotland held £135,042,000 in deposits, compared to £922,297,000 in England and Wales and £73,707,000 in Ireland.
