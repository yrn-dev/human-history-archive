# Auvergne

Auvergne is a former province of France that corresponds to the departments of Puy-de-Dome and Cantal, as well as the arrondissement of Brioude within Haute-Loire. The region is characterized by its geography, which includes numerous medicinal springs, vast pasture-lands, fertile valleys such as the Limagne, and several mountains of volcanic origin, including Mont Dore, Puy de Dome, and Plomb du Cantal. The population of the region retains strongly-marked Celtic characteristics.

## Early History and Roman Period
In the era of Caesar, the region was home to the Arverni, a powerful confederation. The most famous of the Gallic chieftains who fought against the Romans was the Arvernian Vercingetorix. During the Roman Empire, the district was known as Arvernia and formed part of Prima Aquitania. It continued to share the fortunes of Aquitaine through the Carolingian and Merovingian periods.

## Medieval Lordships and Political Evolution
Auvergne served as the seat of a separate countship prior to the end of the 8th century, with William the Pious (886) serving as the first hereditary count. The countship eventually passed under the suzerainty of the kings of England following the marriage of Henry Plantagenet to Eleanor of Aquitaine.

During this time, the domain was divided. William VIII, known as "the Old," was supported by Henry II of England in despoiling his nephew, William VII "the Young" (1145-1168), of a portion of his domain. William VII retained only the region bounded by the Coux and the Allier; this specific district became known from the end of the 13th century as the Dauphine d'Auvergne.

This familial conflict led to the intervention of King Philip Augustus of France. The king succeeded in taking possession of a large part of the country, which was annexed to the royal domains under the name Terre d'Auvergne. As part of this process, the lordship of the town of Clermont was granted to Robert I, the bishop of Clermont (1195-1227), which later evolved into a countship. These events established the four great historic lordships of Auvergne.

## The Dauphinate and Royal Annexation
The dauphinate of Auvergne is distinct from the county. It dates back to 1155, when William VII, count of Auvergne, was deposed by his uncle William VIII. William VII had married the daughter of Guigue IV. Dauphin, and their son was named Dauphin (Delphinus). While the name was used as a patronymic, it did not become a formal title until 1281, when Robert II, count of Clermont, used the style Dauphin of Auvergne (_Alvernie delphinus_) in his will regarding the portion of the county left to his house.

The dauphinate entered the royal house of France in 1428 through the marriage of Louis de Bourbon, count of Montpensier, to Jeanne, the heiress of dauphin Beraud III. The dauphinate was eventually annexed to the crown in 1693. Historically, the "prince dauphin" (the dauphin of Auvergne) was distinguished from the eldest son of the French king, who was referred to as "the king dauphin."

## Later Administrative and Cultural History
The Terre d'Auvergne served as an appanage for Count Alphonse of Poitiers between 1241 and 1271. In 1360, King John II erected it into a duchy in the peerage of France (duche-pairie) for his son John. By 1416, this title passed to the house of Bourbon via John's daughter.

The region has also been associated with notable French figures. Amable Guillaume Prosper Brugiere, Baron de Barante (1782-1866), a historian and statesman born in Riom, retired to his country seat in Auvergne after withdrawing from political affairs in February 1848. Additionally, the region appears as a subject in art; the painter Rosa Bonheur produced a work titled "Hay Harvest in Auvergne" in 1835.
