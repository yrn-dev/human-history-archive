# The Ingoldsby Legends

*The Ingoldsby Legends* is a series of grotesque metrical tales written by the English humourist Richard Harris Barham (1788–1845), who published the works under the *nom de plume* of Thomas Ingoldsby.

## Authorship and Origins
Richard Harris Barham was born in Canterbury on December 6, 1788. Following the death of his father when he was seven, Barham inherited a small estate that included the manor of Tappington, a location frequently mentioned within the *Legends*. His early education at St Paul's school was interrupted by an accident that shattered and partially crippled his arm for life. This limitation in bodily activity led him to become a diligent student and an extensive reader.

Barham entered Brasenose College, Oxford, in 1807. While he initially intended to study law, he later entered the church and was ordained in 1813. His ecclesiastical career included a country curacy and an appointment in 1821 as a minor canon of St Paul's cathedral in London. By 1824, he had become a city living appointee and one of the priests in ordinary of the King's Chapel Royal.

## Publication and Style
Barham first contributed to *Blackwood's Magazine* in 1826. However, it was upon the establishment of *Bentley's Miscellany* in 1837 that he began providing the series of verses known as *The Ingoldsby Legends*. The works gained significant popularity and were eventually published in a collected form, subsequently passing through numerous editions.

The verses are characterized by their whimsicality and variety of rhymes, with some comparisons suggesting they have hardly a rival since the era of *Hudibras*. While the tales possess an obvious popular quality, they are underpinned by solid antiquarian learning. This depth was the result of Barham's patient and enthusiastic research into obscure old books, a detail that often went undetected by readers who primarily laughed at the pages.

## Notable Content and Illustrations
The *Legends* included ballads on contemporary events. For example, in a ballad regarding the queen's coronation in 1837, Barham referred to the "faymale heiress, Miss Anjaley Coutts," referring to Angela Georgina Burdett-Coutts, who had recently inherited a vast fortune from her grandfather, Thomas Coutts.

In 1877, the *Ingoldsby Legends* were illustrated in collaboration by Du Maurier.

## Biographical Records
Following Barham's death, his son provided a short memoir that was prefixed to a new edition of *Ingoldsby* in 1847. A more comprehensive work titled *Life and Letters*, also written by his son, was published in two volumes in 1870.
