# England: Mathematical, Literary, and Political Developments

## Mathematical Instruction and the Calculating Engine
During the 19th century, efforts were made to elevate the standard of mathematical instruction in England. Charles Babbage (1792–1871), an English mechanician and mathematician educated at St Peter's College, Cambridge, worked alongside George Peacock and Sir John Herschel to achieve this goal. A primary focus of their efforts was the attempt to supersede the Newtonian notation in the infinitesimal calculus with the Leibnitzian notation.

Babbage, who was the Lucasian professor of mathematics at Cambridge from 1828 to 1839, became concerned with the frequency and significance of errors in astronomical and other calculations caused by inaccuracies in computation tables. This led him to investigate the relationship between mechanism and notation. In 1822, Babbage discussed the principles of a calculating engine in a letter to Sir H. Davy. He devoted many years of his life to the construction of this engine, utilizing both government grants and his own private fortune. 

While Babbage published his findings on machinery in *Economy of Machines and Manufactures* (1834) after traveling through Europe, the great calculating engine was never finished. As the first specimen neared completion, Babbage sought to change the principle from a difference engine to an analytical engine, a risk the government declined to accept.

## Literary and Publishing Evolution
The "Johnsonian period" served as a formative era for various classical literary forms in England. During this time, the novel, the economic treatise (exemplified by *The Wealth of Nations*), and the historico-philosophical tableau (exemplified by Gibbon and Robertson) were molded into definite forms. Other emerging genres included the diary of nature observers, such as Gilbert White, and the philosophy of Art, which Sir Joshua Reynolds sought to expound for England in his *Fifteen Discourses* (1769–1791). The period also saw the rise of light political parodies, such as *Anti-Jacobin* and *The Rolliad*, as well as monumental topographical or archaeological monographs, including John Nichols' *History of Leicestershire*.

The commercial landscape of literature also shifted during this era. Publishers, then known as booksellers, began to supersede traditional ties of patronage, establishing commercial relationships with authors. This transition was exemplified by William Strahan, a correspondent of Hume and friend of Johnson. The status of the author was further stabilized by the gradual extinction of piracy and the definition of copyright.

## Religious and Courtly Administration
The Church of England implemented a thorough reform in 1549, creating daily evening and morning services that served as a simplification and condensation of the Breviary offices. This reform used the model provided by Francis Quignonez, a cardinal of Santa Croce in Gerusalemme, whose 1536 revision of the Breviary was not accepted by Rome. Some prefaces in the English Prayer-Book are free translations of Quignonez's work.

Within the royal household, the Lord Steward serves as a peer, privy councillor, and member of the government, acting as the first dignitary of the court. The office, which held cabinet rank until 1782, is appointed by the sovereign and symbolized by a white staff. The Lord Steward presides at the Board of Green Cloth, which also includes the comptroller and treasurer of the household. In practice, however, the duties of these officials are performed by the master of the household. The master of the household is a permanent officer and white-staff officer who presides at the daily dinners of the suite; he has superseded the lord steward of the household, just as the lord steward of the household had previously superseded the lord high steward of England.

## International Relations and Military Architecture
England played a significant role in the diplomatic settlements regarding Belgium. Following the invasion of Belgium by William of Holland on August 2, 1831, and the subsequent intervention of a French army sent by Louis-Philippe, England and other powers became alarmed by the potential for French influence in the new kingdom. In response, they drafted the Twenty-four Articles to replace the previous eighteen articles. On November 15, 1831, England, France, and Belgium signed the Treaty of the Twenty-four Articles.

In the realm of military history, G.T. Clark's *Medieval Military Architecture in England* is considered the standard work on the subject, although recent research has superseded it on some points.

## Other Historical Notes
In the field of sports, the accession of the Stuarts brought prosperity to horse-racing. James I patronized the sport in England, running races at Enfield and Croydon. He also attempted to improve horse breeding by purchasing an Arab stallion known as Markham's Arabian, though the horse was unsuccessful in every race he ran. Additionally, in the context of military bands, England uses the bombardon, which differs in construction from the bass tuba used in Germany.
