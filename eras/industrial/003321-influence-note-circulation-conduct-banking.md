# On the Influence of a Note Circulation in the Conduct of Banking

The circulation of bank-notes has historically served as a primary mechanism for facilitating business and managing national liquidity, though its implementation varies significantly across different banking systems. The conduct of banking is influenced by the legal limits placed on note issuance, the assets required to back those notes, and the resulting impact on bank profitability and stability.

## Note Circulation in the United Kingdom

In the United Kingdom, legislation in the mid-19th century sought to regulate the relationship between paper notes and metallic currency. Sir Robert Peel, in speeches delivered in 1844 and 1845, intended for legislation to ensure that bank-notes remained uniformly equivalent to coin. To achieve this, the acts of 1844-1845 required banks in Scotland and Ireland to hold gold at their offices for every note issued in excess of a permitted limit. This amount of specie was determined by the average note-issue of the preceding four weeks. The goal was to ensure the circulating medium—composed of both notes and specie—would fluctuate as if it were a purely metallic circulation.

In England, the Bank Act of 1844 fixed the maximum circulation for issuing banks based on the average circulation of the twelve weeks prior to April 27, 1844. At that time, the authorized issue for 207 private banks was £5,153,417 and for 72 joint-stock banks was £3,478,230. Following the act, some banks ceased issuing notes; for example, nine banks stopped issuance before October 12, 1844, with one joint-stock bank dissolving and one private bank closing.

By 1906, the nature of the circulating medium had shifted toward cheques, rendering Sir Robert Peel's original vision of a specie-like fluctuation inapplicable. While the number of individual banks in England diminished by 1906 to approximately ten private and sixty joint-stock banks, the number of banking offices increased from 976 in 1844 to over 5,880. Much of the increased circulation of the Bank of England is believed to be held as till-money in these offices rather than serving as active circulation.

## The Scottish and Irish Systems

In Scotland, the limitation of note-issuing power to banks that possessed it in 1845 prevented the establishment of any important new banks in that country after that date. Because notes are so heavily used in ordinary Scottish business, banks without the power to issue them cannot practically supply customer needs or carry on business. Despite this limitation on the number of banks, Scotland maintains a larger number of banking offices per capita than Ireland, England, or Wales.

The conduct of Scottish banking involves a trade-off between the profits of note issuance and the expenses associated with it. These expenses include stamp duty, printing costs, and the charges for importing gold from London when circulation exceeds the 1845 limit. However, the extensive network of branches associated with this system has contributed to the stability and prosperity of the country, encouraging people of moderate means to lodge their money in banks.

In Ireland, the actual circulation of private and joint-stock banks has remained far below their authorized issue. By May 1907, the actual issue of 12 private banks was £122,536 and 17 joint-stock banks was £437,693. This is attributed to the fact that these notes only circulate in limited areas near where they are issued, and there is often difficulty in cashing them, even if they are payable in London.

## The United States National Banking System

The United States employed a system where circulating notes were issued upon United States bonds. To issue notes, a bank deposited bonds with the U.S. treasurer; the bank could then receive a circulation equal to 90% of the bonds' par-value. By law, banks were required to invest a portion of their capital in bonds—originally one-third, though this was reduced in 1874 to 25% (with a maximum mandatory requirement of $50,000).

This system allowed banks to earn "double interest," as they received interest on the bonds held by the treasury while simultaneously lending their notes to the public. However, profits were reduced by a 1% annual tax on notes, the inability to issue notes to the full par-value of bonds, and the necessity of maintaining gold or lawful money for reserves and redemption.

The volume of circulation was heavily influenced by bond prices. The original maximum circulation was $300,000,000, increased by $54,000,000 in 1870, and the limit was removed entirely in 1875. Circulation reached $362,651,169 on January 1, 1883, but declined as bonds became scarce and prices rose. For instance, when 4% bonds rose above 129 in 1889, net profits on circulation were greatly reduced. Circulation fell to $167,927,574 by July 1, 1891, before increasing again to meet the expenses of the war with Spain and treasury deficiencies between 1894 and 1896.

## Continental European Systems

In Germany, the Reichsbank provides facilities to business through its note circulation and a clearing system known as *Giro-Verkehr*. In Germany, notes are primarily used for payments in ordinary business. The bank is managed by a directory appointed by the chancellor of the empire, with shareholders participating through a committee.

The Austro-Hungarian Bank, founded in 1816 (and renamed from the Austrian National Bank in 1878), holds the exclusive legal right to issue bank-notes for the two states of the monarchy. Under a 1899 statute, it operates as a joint-stock company with a stock of £8,780,000. Its notes must be covered by legal specie (gold and current silver) to the extent of two-fifths, with the remainder covered according to bank usage. As of 1900, the bank's note circulation was £62,251,000, which grew to £84,501,000 by 1907.
