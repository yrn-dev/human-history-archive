# Events and Figures of 1847

The year 1847 was marked by significant developments in literature, finance, and academia, as well as the passing of notable intellectuals.

## Literary Developments
In 1847, the Brontë sisters sought publishers for their respective novels. Charlotte Brontë's work, originally titled *The Master*, was retitled *The Professor*. While the firm Smith, Elder & Co. found the work lacked "varied interest" and was too short for the then-imperative three-volume form, they encouraged the author to submit a longer novel. In August 1847, Charlotte Brontë replied with a longer work, and *Jane Eyre* was published in October 1847. The novel was wildly acclaimed on every hand, though this enthusiasm faced a counterblast in December 1848 via a review in the *Quarterly* by Miss Rigby (later Lady Eastlake). During this same period, Emily Brontë wrote *Wuthering Heights* and Anne Brontë wrote *Agnes Gray*, both of which traveled between various publishers.

Additionally, a new edition of *Ingoldsby* was released in 1847, which featured a short memoir written by the author's son.

## Financial Crisis in England
The year 1847 was one of five periods of pressure in English banking since the passing of the act of 1844. The crisis of 1847 was one of three periods—alongside 1857 and 1866—where difficulties reached the level of panic. This specific crisis was triggered by speculation in railway enterprise that had been ongoing since 1845.

The financial instability was reflected in the bank rate of discount, which began the year at 3% on January 1st. The rate rose steadily: to 3-1/2% on January 14th, 4% on January 21st, 5% on April 8th, 5-1/2% on August 5th, and 6% on September 30th, peaking at 8% on October 25th. Following an announcement on October 1st that no advances would be made on public securities, general alarm and anxiety ensued, and the bank's reserve was rapidly reduced to a very low ebb. The rate eventually lowered to 7% on November 22nd, 6% on December 2nd, and 5% on December 23rd.

## Academic and Intellectual Figures
In the field of history, the Swedish historian Anders Fryxell (1795–1881) reached a turning point in his career in 1847. Having been elected to the Swedish Academy in 1840 and serving as the incumbent of Sunne, Fryxell received permission from his bishop in 1847 to withdraw from all Church services. This allowed him to devote himself to historical investigation without interruption. In that same year, he published *Origins of the Inaccuracy with which the History of Sweden in Catholic Times has been Treated*.

The year also saw the death of James MacCullagh (1809–1847), an Irish physicist and mathematician. MacCullagh had been a fellow at Trinity College, Dublin, since 1832, holding the chair of mathematics until 1843, when he moved to the chair of natural philosophy. He died by his own hand in October 1847, following mental disease induced by overwork on subjects that exceeded the natural range of his powers.
