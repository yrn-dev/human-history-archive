# Wall Construction and Fortification

The construction of walls, ranging from architectural building projects to defensive fortifications and industrial chimneys, involves specific technical methods regarding height, stability, and accessibility.

## Building Wall Techniques and Scaffolding
In ordinary building practice, scaffolds are carried up alongside walls and are designed to rest upon them. A scaffolder erects these structures using putlogs, ledgers, and standards to support scaffold boards. Materials are transported to the scaffold via cranes, baskets, boxes, or hods; a single hod typically holds twenty bricks. Mortar is transported in pails or hods and placed on ledged boards, approximately 3 feet square, positioned at convenient intervals along the line of work. To ensure the bricklayers have a clear path against the wall, bricks are piled on the scaffold between these mortar boards.

The bricklayer typically begins at a quoin or the extreme left of a section and advances to the right. Throughout this process, the workman uses a straight-edge, spirit-level, and plumb-rule to test the work. Pointing may be finished as the work proceeds or left open until completion, at which point it is pointed down, potentially using a different mortar. When a wall reaches a height where the workman can no longer reach conveniently from the scaffold, the scaffolding is raised to a new level to allow work to continue.

To maintain structural integrity, all walls intended to sustain the same roof and floors should be raised simultaneously. No part of a wall should be built higher than what can be reached from the same scaffold until all walls have reached the same height. If a portion of a wall must be left at a lower level while adjoining work continues, the higher section should be "racked back" in steps rather than carried up vertically with only the toothing required for the bond.

## Industrial Chimney Construction
Tall furnace chimneys are built as separate constructions. If other work must be placed close to the chimney, a straight joint is employed. These shafts are built "overhand," meaning the workmen operate from the inside. Because cement is too rigid to allow a chimney to rock in the wind, lime mortar is used. To ensure the mortar sets before sustaining significant weight, construction is limited to 3 feet in height per day.

The standard bond for these structures is one course of headers to four of stretchers. While men work from the inside, external scaffolding is sometimes erected up to 30 or 25 feet to facilitate better pointing in prominent positions. According to the London Building Act, the brickwork at the top must be 9 inches thick, though 14 inches is preferred for shafts exceeding 100 feet. The thickness increases by half a brick for every 20 feet of downward measurement. The shaft must taper from base to top at a rate of at least 2.5 inches per 10 feet of height. The base width must be at least one-tenth of the height if square, or one-twelfth if round. 

Inside the lower portion of the shaft, firebricks (usually 4.5 inches thick) are provided as an independent lining, not bonded to the main wall, with a space of 1.5 to 3 inches between the lining and the wall. This lining extends from 25 feet for ordinary temperatures up to double that height for very great temperatures. Caps are typically made of terra-cotta or cast iron strengthened with straps and bolts, as stone is difficult to fix properly.

## Defensive Walls and Fortifications
Historically, walls have served as critical defensive barriers. In Athens, the "Long Walls" connected the city to the Peiraeus. These consisted of the North Wall, the Phaleric Wall, and the Middle (or South) Wall. The north and Phaleric walls were perhaps founded by Cimon and completed around 457 B.C., while the middle wall was built around 445 B.C. The middle wall ran parallel to the north wall at an interval of 550 feet.

In other contexts, the geography of the land affects wall accessibility. In Sikyon, a section of the city wall was nearly level with the ground on the inside because it was built against high, rocky ground, though it remained low enough on the outside to be reached by scaling-ladders.

In the siege of Jerusalem, the Romans utilized heavy engines, including one called "Nico," to beat upon the walls until they gave way. The defenders, the Bani Israel, were unable to reach the Romans because the Roman towers were too high, too heavy to overturn, and covered in iron plates to prevent fire. Once a breach was made by the Nico, the Romans mounted the wall and captured the first wall on the fifteenth day of the siege.

## Architectural Walls and Layouts
The walls of the temple described by Josephus featured complex heights and layouts. The outermost court had cloisters with white marble pillars 25 cubits high. A stone partition 3 cubits high separated the first court from the second court (the Sanctuary). The buildings of the Sanctuary wall were 40 cubits high on the outside, but only 25 cubits high on the inside, as the structure was built against a higher part of the hill, which hid the remaining height.

The city of Jerusalem was protected by three walls. The old wall, built by David, Solomon, and subsequent kings, was particularly strong and difficult to take due to its construction on a hill and the surrounding valleys. It extended from the tower Hippicus to the Xistus, joined the council-house, and ended at the temple's west cloister. The second wall encompassed the northern quarter, reaching from the Gennath gate to the tower Antonia. The third wall, built by Agrippa to encompass the growing population in the Bezetha area, ran from tower Hippicus to tower Psephinus, passed the monuments of Helena and the sepulchral caverns of the kings, and joined the old wall at the Valley of Cedron.
