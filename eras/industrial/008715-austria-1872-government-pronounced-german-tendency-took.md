# Austria

## Early Habsburg Establishment
The Habsburg family established its presence in Austria in 1282. This process began in 1273 when Rudolph, Count of Habsburg, became German king. Rudolph challenged the title of the Bohemian king, Ottakar, regarding three duchies. Following a legal declaration by the imperial court and a subsequent war, Ottakar submitted to Rudolph in November 1276, renouncing the duchies of Austria, Styria, and Carinthia. 

Rudolph sought to make these lands hereditary possessions for his family, moving the Habsburg headquarters from the Rhine to the Danube. On December 27, 1282, Rudolph invested his sons, Albert and Rudolph, with the duchies of Austria and Styria. He held Carinthia until 1286, when he bestowed it upon Meinhard IV, Count of Tirol. During this era, the principle of primogeniture was unknown in the house of Habsburg; consequently, the duchies were often ruled in common by multiple family members. Albert, who undertook the government of Austria and Styria, is described as an arbitrary ruler who suppressed a popular rising in Vienna in 1288.

## Mid-19th Century Political Turmoil
By 1848, revolution spread across Europe, impacting the Austrian system. In March 1848, Kossuth delivered a speech in the diet at Pressburg, declaring war on the "Vienna cabinet" and advocating for Hungarian Liberalism. He envisioned a "fraternization of the Austrian peoples" where a liberated Hungary would be the center of freedom for all races under the Austrian crown. However, this ideal conflicted with the traditional German ascendancy. While "Germanism" had previously served as a mediating element among various racial tendencies, the growth of the idea of German unity created a new ideal centered outside the Austrian monarchy, leading to the failure of the revolution.

By 1851, Austria appeared to have overcome these difficulties. Revolutionary movements were suppressed, and Vienna became the center of a despotic government. The state's objectives during this period were to suppress the free press, check constitutional agitation, and Germanize the Slavs and Magyars. This autocratic regime lasted for approximately ten years before being undermined by the establishment of Italian unity, the Crimean War, and Prussia's successful claim to leadership in Germany.

## Foreign Relations and the German Empire
Following the war of 1866, Austrian foreign policy under Chancellor Beust was influenced by the desire to recover lost positions in Germany. This period saw close friendship with France, including meetings between the emperor and Napoleon in 1868 and 1869. However, the war of 1870 and the rapid success of Germany ended these ambitions. Austria abandoned hopes of regaining its position in Germany, and Germany disclaimed intentions to acquire Austria's German provinces.

A reconciliation followed, marked by the appointment of Count Andrassy as chancellor and a visit by Emperor Francis Joseph to Berlin in 1872. For the next six years, a close friendship existed between the three empires (Austria, Germany, and Russia), removing the immediate danger of war. In 1879, this was formalized through a treaty arranged by Bismarck and Andrassy. Germany pledged to support Austria against Russian attacks, while Austria-Hungary pledged to assist Germany against a combined French and Russian attack. This alliance remained a foundation of Austria-Hungary's policy, surviving national discord and commercial differences.

## Internal Racial and Religious Tensions
The late 19th century was marked by increasing racial animosity. Around 1870, a party of young Germans emerged, focusing on the preservation and extension of their nationality over constitutionalism. This movement began at the university of Vienna with a German student reading club (_Leseverein_). Figures such as Herr von Schoenerer expressed a desire for union with the German empire, viewing Bismarck as a national hero. In 1881, various German opposition parties formed the "United Left," demanding that German be recognized as the official language.

These racial tensions extended to religion. The Roman Catholic Church was often viewed as favorable to Slav races because they provided more candidates for ordination. In response, some Germans exalted nationality above religion. This led to the "Los von Rom" movement in 1898, where many Germans in Styria and other territories left the Catholic Church for Protestantism or Old Catholicism as a political demonstration against the alliance of the Clerical party with Slav parties.

## Bohemian Relations
In 1871, Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reconcile Bohemia with the Habsburg empire by proposing a modernized version of Bohemia's ancient constitution. An imperial message on September 14, 1871, recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia. However, this attempt failed when a government with a pronounced German tendency took office in Vienna in 1872, leading Bohemians to temporarily boycott parliamentary assemblies.

Stability returned in 1879 under Prime Minister Count Eduard Taaffe. He persuaded Bohemian representatives to return to the parliament in Vienna, provided they maintained the view that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary. In return, Taaffe's government removed anomalies in the electoral system that had favored the German minority and consented to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.

## The First World War Period
During the First World War, the Austrian empire faced severe internal pressure due to hunger and privations. The situation in Austria was reported to be worse than in Germany. Evidence of Austria's desperate need for peace was found in a letter from the Austrian Emperor to Prince Sixtus of Bourbon-Parma. This desperation led to suspicions within the German government regarding the loyalty of its ally, with the belief that Austria might prefer a separate peace—regardless of the sacrifices required—over no peace at all.
