# Gunpowder and Interior Ballistics

The history and scientific application of gunpowder encompass its early discovery, its evolution in military artillery, and the modern experimental physics used to determine its effects within a firearm.

## Early History and Discovery
The precise origins of gunpowder are a subject of historical debate. Some modern writers have attempted to place the invention of gunpowder before the 14th century and Greek fire before the 7th century, though such evidence is often considered unclear or unsatisfactory. Early sieges utilized combustibles consisting of sulphur and oil, and Greek fire is noted for having affinities with gunpowder in both its nature and effects.

Friar Bacon is credited with revealing two of the ingredients of gunpowder: sulphur and saltpetre, while concealing the third. In the context of China, some sources maintain that the Mongols were acquainted with cannon as early as 1232. Records from the Ming Dynasty indicate that while "P’ao" were originally machines for hurling stones, fire was first employed in these machines during the siege of the city of Ts’ai chow in 1233, although the art of making them was not passed down and they were seldom used thereafter. Other interpretations suggest that arms mentioned in Mongol wars may have been rockets rather than cannon.

## Evolution of Artillery
In Europe, the use of gunpowder artillery evolved from primitive forms to more complex systems. Early infantry fire remained largely ineffective until the development of the Spanish arquebus around 1520. To disintegrate masses of pikes before a cavalry charge, early field artillery was used to provide a mechanical concentration of musketry. This included "carts with gonnes," which served as prototypes for the modern field gun and functioned as primitive machine-guns. These early machine-guns consisted of bindings or groupings of several small-calibre guns used for rapid succession of shots or volleys.

By the year 1344, the writer Petrarch described "terrestrial thunder" as having become common. Other accounts, such as those by John Villani, describe the English cannon at Crecy as having a material effect, causing the falling of horses and the killing of many people.

## Modern Ballistics and Experimental Results
Modern results employed with gunpowder are based on the experiments of Abel and Noble conducted between 1875 and 1894. These experiments utilize an explosion-chamber of a known volume (C) to fire a charge of powder of varying weight (P). The resulting pressure (p) is recorded using a crusher-gauge.

These results are plotted to show the relationship between pressure and two key metrics:
*   **Gravimetric Density (D):** The specific gravity of the powder when filling the volume C in a state of gas.
*   **Gravimetric Volume (G.V.):** The reciprocal of D, representing the ratio of the volume of the gas to the volume of an equal weight of water.

Earlier experiments, such as those by Chevalier d'Arcy in 1760, measured the velocity of bullets in musket barrels by successively shortening the barrel and using a ballistic pendulum. This was the reverse of Noble's procedure, which involved gradually lengthening the gun.

## Practical Application and Modern Explosives
The study of interior ballistics allows for the estimation of maximum pressure within a gun's bore and the design of chambers where the gravimetric density of the charge is suitable for the acceleration and weight of the shot. This requires the experimental determination of grain size and the time of burning under varying density and pressure.

In recent years, interior ballistics has undergone a significant change with the abandonment of black gunpowder in artillery, which had been used for six hundred years. It has been replaced by modern explosives that offer several advantages:
*   **Increased Power:** They can produce a volume of gas at a temperature and pressure more than threefold that of black powder.
*   **Efficiency:** Because of the increased power, the required charge may be reduced in proportion.
*   **Tactical Advantage:** Modern explosives are nearly smokeless.

Calculations regarding the work realized by expansion (for example, 2826 foot-tons) can determine the increase in muzzle velocity (such as from 1020 to 2250 f/s). By comparing these results to range tables, engineers can determine the "factor of effect" (f), which accounts for work lost to friction in the bore.
