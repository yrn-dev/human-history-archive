# Overthrow and Construction of Policy and Infrastructure

The historical record demonstrates a recurring tension between the act of overthrowing established systems—whether political, military, or physical—and the subsequent effort to construct new policies or structures. In many instances, the dismantling of a previous order is achieved with greater ease than the implementation of a sustainable replacement.

## Political Overthrow and Statecraft

The distinction between destructive and constructive political action is evident in the career of the Greek strategist and tactician Epaminondas. While he is credited with the final overthrow of Spartan predominance in the Peloponnese, he is noted for having displayed no special talent as a constructive statesman. Furthermore, the pan-Hellenic ambitions attributed to him never found practical expression.

In the context of early 17th-century England, political maneuvering often involved the abandonment of promises to achieve immediate goals. King James sought to regain the Palatinate through a continental alliance involving France, which required a marriage between Charles and Henrietta Maria, the sister of Louis XIII. However, Louis demanded large concessions for English Roman Catholics, which contradicted promises James and Charles had made to the Commons. Persuaded by Buckingham, the King and Prince threw over their promises to the Commons to accept the French terms, calculating that Parliament could not be summoned to vote for war supplies until the marriage was completed and remonstrances became useless.

In the United States following the Civil War, the Ku Klux Klan and similar secret associations of Southern whites—including the White League, the White Brotherhood, and the Knights of the White Camelia—formed to oppose the Reconstruction measures of the U.S. Congress. This movement resulted in a more or less successful revolution against Reconstruction and the overthrow of governments based on negro suffrage.

## Shifts in Japanese Governance

The transition of power in Japan saw a significant shift in the relationship between the Imperial capital in Kyoto and the Yedo Bakufu. Historically, policies contemplated in Yedo became accomplished facts before reports reached the Imperial capital. However, following his coronation, Emperor Komei departed from this sequence and formally instructed the Bakufu that the traditional policy regarding foreign affairs must be strictly maintained. This marked the beginning of an absorbing object for many high officials in Kyoto: the overthrow of the Yedo Bakufu. In response to changing times, Ieyoshi bowed to this new order, and the Bakufu began submitting foreign policy questions to the Imperial Court before final decisions were made.

Despite some perceived weaknesses in their language, Bakufu statesmen under Abe Masahiro took stalwart constructive actions. They built batteries at Shinagawa, made defensive preparations along the coasts of Kazusa, Awa, Sagami, and Musashi, and rescinded the veto against constructing ocean-going ships. They also commissioned the Dutch at Deshima to procure a library of useful books from Europe and took those with expert Dutch knowledge into official favor.

Later, the Japanese government pursued a policy of developing a mercantile marine, recognizing that transports were as necessary for state safety as soldiers. Through liberal subsidies and grants-in-aid, the fleet grew from forty-six ships (17,948 total tonnage) in 1871 to 6,436 ships (1,564,443 total tonnage) by 1910. This policy allowed Japan to carry large armies to the Asiatic continent in its own vessels during the wars with China (1894-1895) and Russia (1904-1905).

## Military and Physical Construction

The difficulty of maintaining a consistent policy is seen in the British approach to aerial warfare before and during the First World War. One policy focused on restricting expenditure on airships to concentrate resources on aeroplanes, limiting airship construction to experimental levels. By April 1915, the only rigid experimental airship was scrapped due to inferior quality. However, after a change in leadership at the Admiralty, this policy was reversed. Forty million were spent building British Zeppelins, none of which rendered effective fighting service. Meanwhile, the alternative policy of using aeroplanes for aerial observation off warships lagged, leaving the British with no airships and only one aeroplane in the air at the Battle of Jutland.

In physical construction, the expansion of railways since 1830 led to the proliferation of girder bridges. These are considered easier to erect than suspension chains or arches and impose only a vertical load on abutments and piers rather than a horizontal thrust. While they require more material than an arch or chain, the use of mild steel and wrought iron has made their construction economical.

In military fortifications, the ease of overthrowing a structure is illustrated by the Roman experience during the wars with the Jews. After an unexpected fall of a wall, the Romans found that attacking a second, inner wall built by John and his party appeared easier because they could access it through the ruins of the first wall. The Romans believed this second wall was weaker and had been erected so suddenly that it could be quickly overthrown, though the danger of death prevented immediate attempts to scale it.
