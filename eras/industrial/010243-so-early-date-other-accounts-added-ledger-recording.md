# The Ledger and Book-keeping Systems

The ledger serves as the center of all book-keeping systems. While other books may be maintained for practical convenience—referred to as "subsidiary books," "books of entry," or "books of first entry"—they primarily function to record transactions before they are classified into the ledger. Some subsidiary books, known as "auxiliary" or "statistical" books, are used for statistical purposes and do not form the basis for ledger entries.

## Evolution of Ledger Accounts

In the early stages of book-keeping, the ledger was not a complete record of all transactions but contained only those accounts deemed necessary to keep accessible. Initially, many records consisted only of "personal accounts," which tracked transactions with other business houses. 

Early methods for recording the reduction of indebtedness involved listing transactions by date beneath the records of the debt's creation, subtracting the reduction from the total sum. Because this was inconvenient, book-keepers began using two separate accounts: one for the creation of indebtedness and a "contra account" for the reduction or extinguishing of that debt. These were placed on opposite sides of the ledger, leading to the development of the "Dr." (debit) and "Cr." (credit) system used today.

To provide a more complete record of a business's financial position, "real accounts" were added to the ledger. These accounts record the acquisition and disposal of different classes of property. They follow the same principle as personal accounts: expenditure is recorded on the Dr. side and receipts on the Cr. side. The excess of debit entries over credit entries indicates the value of assets remaining as business property.

Finally, "impersonal" or "nominal accounts" were introduced to complete the double-entry record. These accounts track the transfer of money or money's worth that results in a profit or loss rather than a mere reshuffling of assets and liabilities. Profits are recorded on the Cr. side, while losses and expenses are recorded on the Dr. side.

## Double-Entry Principles and Verification

The essential principle of double-entry book-keeping is the creation of a complete record of every business transaction. Because transactions are cross-dealings—where one party receives a benefit and another imparts one—an entry of equal amount must be made on both the debit and credit sides of the ledger.

If clerical work is performed correctly, the aggregate amount on the debit side must equal the aggregate amount on the credit side. A list of all ledger balances used to verify this agreement is called a "trial balance." However, a trial balance is a mechanical test and does not guarantee absolute accuracy. It cannot detect omitted transactions, entries made to the wrong accounts, incorrect monetary values, or fictitious records.

## Modern Ledger Organization and Adaptations

To allow large staffs to work simultaneously, the ledger is often divided into sections:
* **Cash Book:** A separate book combining the cash and bank accounts, often including a third column for discounts to avoid the need for a separate discount journal.
* **Petty Cash Book:** Used for petty payments in businesses that otherwise pay all accounts by cheque and deposit all receipts into a bank.
* **Sales and Purchases Ledgers:** Personal accounts are often separated from real and nominal accounts. Customers' accounts are kept in sales (or sold) ledgers, while trade creditors are kept in purchases (or bought) ledgers.
* **General Ledger:** This contains nominal and real accounts. It may be further split into a nominal ledger and a private ledger. The private ledger, often managed by the principal or a confidential employee, typically holds the trading and profit and loss accounts.

To localize clerical errors, "adjustment accounts" may be used to make these various ledgers self-balancing.

## Specialized Systems and International Variations

### The Slip System and Tabular Book-keeping
The "slip system" involves recording transactions on separate sheets or carbon duplicates rather than in bound books. This allows entries to be sorted and handled separately before being recorded in the ledger. In some cases, these slips themselves constitute the ledger record, though this is only practical when transactions per individual are few. More common adaptations include "card ledgers" (where accounts are on independent cardboard sheets in cabinets) or "loose-leaf ledgers," which allow for the continuous elimination of "dead matter."

"Tabular book-keeping" replaces standard books with those ruled with numerous columns. In books of first entry, this allows a single book to handle multiple classes of transactions (such as different business departments). In ledgers, it allows many personal accounts to be recorded on a single opening if the number of entries per individual is low.

### International Legal Requirements
Book-keeping practices are influenced by national legislation:
* **France:** The Code of Commerce requires a journal, an inventory, and a letter book.
* **Germany:** Accounts must be kept in bound books with consecutively numbered pages; a balance sheet is required annually.
* **Belgium:** Merchants must keep a journal and balance book initialed by a prescribed officer; letters and telegrams must be preserved for ten years.
* **Spain:** The Commercial Code requires an inventory, journal, ledger, letter book, and invoice book.
* **Portugal:** Prescribes a balance book, journal, ledger, and copy-letter book.
* **Holland:** Requires records of commercial transactions, letters received, and copies of letters sent, along with an annual balance sheet.

## Historical Context

While the exact date of the introduction of double-entry book-keeping is unknown, it was likely in use for approximately 200 years before 1494. In 1494, Luca Paciolo published *Everything about Arithmetic, Geometry and Proportion* in Venice, which explained the system. It is noted that the system was not invented by Lucas de Bergo. A ledger from 1697 in the Advocates' library at Edinburgh is one of the earliest extant examples of accounts kept in three distinct columns (date, nature, and money value) and was ruled by hand.
