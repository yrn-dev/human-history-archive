# Standard Pressure and Barometric Measurement

In the field of gasometry, it is essential to establish a standard pressure to which all measurements can be reduced. The most common standard used is a mercury column with a height of 76 cms. (29.922 in.) of pure mercury at 0deg, a value that closely approximates the average height of the barometer.

## Defining Pressure and Gravity
Because the actual force exerted by the atmosphere varies based on the position on the earth's surface and the intensity of gravity, defining standard pressure requires specifying a location. To avoid this, force can be expressed as the pressure in dynes resulting from a column of mercury with a section of one square centimetre supported by the atmosphere. 

The calculation for this pressure is 13.596 $H g$ dynes, where $H$ represents the height in cms. at 0deg, $g$ represents the value of gravity, and 13.596 is the density of mercury. Using this formula, the standard pressure at 0deg is 1,013,800 dynes at Greenwich (where $g$ = 981.17) and 1,013,600 dynes at Paris. Due to the proximity of these values to a mega-dyne (one million dynes), some modern writers have adopted a mega-dyne per square centimetre as the standard pressure for its independence of locality and convenience in calculation.

## Barometer Construction and Temperature Correction
High-quality barometers are constructed using three primary materials: glass for the tube containing the mercury, brass for the case (which holds the engraved scale), and the mercury itself. 

A significant challenge in barometric measurement is that mercury has a considerably greater coefficient of expansion than brass. For example, if an instrument stands at 30 in. at a temperature of 32deg, it will stand at 30.1 in. if the temperature is raised to 69deg. This increase of a tenth of an inch is caused by the expansion of the mercury relative to the brass case, rather than an actual increase in pressure. To ensure exact comparisons between observations made at different temperatures, readings must be reduced to the height they would occupy at a uniform temperature, specifically 0deg cent. or 32deg Fahr.

## Enhancements in Measurement
Various devices have been developed to magnify the variation in the height of the mercury column for more exact readings. One method, suggested by Descartes and implemented by Huygens, involved expanding the barometer tube into a cylindrical vessel at the top and inserting a fine tube partly filled with water. However, water-barometers are often ineffective due to high vapour tension and dissolved gases. 

Success has been found by substituting methyl salicylate for water. This substance is effective due to its freedom from viscosity, low specific gravity (1.18 at 10deg C.), and low vapour tension (with no value given below 70deg C.). In a form patented by C. O. Bartrum, it is claimed that readings can be taken to .001 of an inch of mercury without requiring a vernier.

## Pressure in Ballistics and Other Applications
The study of pressure is also central to ballistics, the science of throwing projectiles. Interior Ballistics specifically analyzes the pressure of powder-gas within the bore of a gun to determine the charge of powder needed for initial velocity without overstraining the weapon.

In experimental settings, such as those conducted by Noble and Abel, a charge of powder (weight $P$ lb) is fired in an explosion-chamber of a known volume ($C$ cub. in.). The pressure ($p$) is recorded in tons per sq. in. using a crusher-gauge. Research into cordite-gas showed that at standard atmospheric pressure and 0deg C., one gramme of the gas occupied 700 ccs. When compressed into 5 ccs. at the temperature of explosion, the pressure reached 16 tons per sq. in., which equals 2440 atmospheres (based on 14.7 lb per sq. in. as one atmosphere).
