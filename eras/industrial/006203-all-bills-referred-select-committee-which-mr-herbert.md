# The Building Societies Act of 1894

The Building Societies Act of 1894 was the legislative result of a period of deliberation following the Liberator disaster. This event created a widespread conviction that action was necessary to prevent similar calamities from occurring in the future.

## Legislative Process and the Select Committee
In response to the disaster, the government of the day introduced a bill intended to address the issue. Simultaneously, several private members prepared their own measures, the majority of which were more stringent than the government's proposal. To resolve these various approaches, all the bills were referred to a select committee. Mr Herbert Gladstone served as the chairman of this committee. The deliberations of this body ultimately led to the passing of the Building Societies Act of 1894.

## Property Returns and Enforcement
During the committee's proceedings, the Rt. Hon. W.L. Jackson (who later became Lord Allerton), a member of the committee, moved for an address to the crown to obtain a return of the property held in possession by building societies. This request marked the first time such a return had been called for. The managers of the societies expressed significant resentment toward the request. Because there were no means of enforcing the return, many large societies failed to provide the information despite frequent applications from the registrar.

## Provisions of the Act
The Building Societies Act of 1894 established new requirements for incorporated societies to ensure transparency. The act mandated that these societies furnish returns in a prescribed form. These returns were required to include specific schedules detailing:
* Mortgages for amounts that exceeded £5000.
* Properties that the societies had taken possession of for more than twelve months due to the default of the mortgagors.
* Mortgages that were in arrear of repayment subscription for more than twelve months.

## Implementation and Impact
The act did not come into operation until January 1, 1895, and the first complete return under its mandates was not due until 1896. This 1896 return revealed that the properties in possession at the time of Mr Jackson's initial request must have been valued at at least seven and a half millions in the assets of the societies.

In the years following the passing of the act, building societies reduced their properties in possession from 14% of all mortgages to 5%. In numerical terms, this was a reduction from 7½ millions to 2½ millions, effectively reducing the original amount to one-third. While this process involved sacrifice for many societies, the overall balance of profit increased rather than diminished. Consequently, although the provisions of the act initially alarmed society managers, they proved to be a blessing in disguise.
