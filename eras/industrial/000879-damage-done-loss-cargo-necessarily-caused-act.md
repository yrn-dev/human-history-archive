# Damage to or Loss of Cargo in General Average

In maritime law, specifically under the York-Antwerp Rules, General Average (G.A.) refers to the system by which losses or expenses incurred for the common safety of a voyage are shared among the interested parties. Damage to or loss of cargo is admitted as G.A. under several specific circumstances involving sacrifice, emergency operations, and necessary port activities.

## Cargo Loss through Sacrifice and Emergency
Damage to cargo is made good as G.A. when it results from a sacrifice made for the common safety. This includes damage caused by water entering a ship through hatches or other openings that were opened specifically to facilitate a jettison for the common safety. 

In the event of a shipboard fire, damage to cargo caused by water or other means—including damage resulting from scuttling or beaching the burning vessel—is allowed as G.A. However, compensation is not provided for separate packages of cargo or bulk cargo that were actually on fire. Similarly, if cargo, ship's stores, or ship's materials are necessarily burnt for fuel during a time of peril for the common safety, they are admitted as G.A., provided that an ample supply of fuel had been originally provided.

Regarding the intentional running of a ship on shore, loss or damage to cargo is allowed as G.A. if the action was taken for the common safety. An exception exists if the ship was intentionally run on shore because it would have inevitably sunk or driven onto rocks or shore regardless of the action; in such cases, no loss or damage to cargo is made good as G.A.

## Damage During Cargo Handling and Refloating
Cargo may sustain damage during necessary operations to save the vessel or its contents. Damage or loss of cargo is admitted as G.A. when it is necessarily caused by the acts of stowing, reloading, storing, and discharging, but only if the costs of those specific measures are also admitted as G.A.

When a ship is ashore and in a position of peril, cargo may be discharged (lightered) to help float the vessel. Any loss or damage sustained by the cargo during this discharge and lightering process is admitted as G.A. This also applies to damage sustained by cargo discharged at a port of refuge to enable necessary repairs to the ship.

## Port of Refuge and Incidental Loss
Under Rule X, expenses for entering a port of refuge are allowed as G.A. if the entry was necessary for the common safety. If the ship sails again with some or all of its original cargo, the expenses of leaving the port are also admitted. When the cost of discharging cargo is treated as G.A. to allow for repairs necessary for the safe prosecution of the voyage, the expenses for reloading and storing the cargo on shore are likewise allowed, along with any incidental damage or loss.

While Rule XII allows for damage during these operations, it is noted that the allowance does not expressly extend to damage sustained while cargo is stored on land. However, if the landing was necessitated by a G.A. act, such as the cutting away of masts, losses may be made good due to the special risks the goods were exposed to. In one legal instance (*Anglo-Argentine &c. Agency v. Temperley Shipping Co.*, 1899), a loss of value for cattle taken to a port of refuge in Brazil—which subsequently could not be legally landed at their destination and had to be taken to Antwerp—was allowed in G.A.

## Valuation and Contribution
The amount to be made good as G.A. for sacrificed or damaged goods is based on the loss sustained by the owner, calculated using market values at the termination of the adventure or the date the vessel arrives.

Contributions to G.A. are based on the actual values of the property at the termination of the adventure, with the amount made good for sacrificed property added back in. Certain deductions are made from the shipowner's passage-money and freight at risk, specifically port charges and crew's wages that would not have been incurred had the cargo and ship been totally lost at the date of the sacrifice. Notably, personal effects and passengers' luggage not shipped under a bill of lading do not contribute to G.A.

## Exclusions and Limitations
Certain types of cargo loss are strictly excluded from G.A.:
* **Deck Cargo:** No jettison of deck cargo is made good as G.A. Any structure not built into the vessel's frame is considered part of the deck.
* **Wreck Removal:** Loss or damage caused by cutting away the remains of spars or other items previously carried away by sea-peril is not allowed.
* **Afloat Operations:** If a ship is already afloat, no loss or damage to cargo caused by carrying a press of sail is made good as G.A.
