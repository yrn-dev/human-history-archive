# Italian Diplomatic Shifts and the Alliance of 1882

## The Requirement for Support
In 1881, Italy found itself in a position where it required external support. This necessity arose specifically due to the French occupation of Tunis. This geopolitical pressure influenced Italy's diplomatic trajectory, leading the nation to seek security through new international partnerships.

## The German-Austrian Alliance
The framework for Italy's eventual support had been established several years prior. In 1879, the European position of the monarchy of Austria-Hungary was placed on a more secure footing through a formal alliance with Germany. This arrangement was the result of a visit to Vienna by Bismarck in the autumn of 1879, where he and Andrassy arranged a treaty. Under the terms of this agreement:
* Germany bound herself to support Austria against any attack originating from Russia.
* Austria-Hungary pledged to assist Germany in the event of a combined attack by France and Russia.

Although Andrassy had resigned by the time the treaty was concluded, it was maintained by his successor, Baron Haymerle, and subsequently by Count Kalnoky after Haymerle's death in 1881. The tsar was informed of this treaty, which served to remove the immediate danger of war between Russia and Austria-Hungary.

## Adhesion of Italy
The existing alliance between Germany and Austria-Hungary was strengthened in 1882 when Italy adhered to the agreement. This move was a direct consequence of the aforementioned French occupation of Tunis in 1881, which had left the Italians in need of support. The alliance was renewed five years later and became the foundation upon which the policy of Austria-Hungary depended.

This diplomatic bond survived various challenges, including national discord and commercial differences that occurred between 1880 and 1890.

## Internal Perspectives and Tensions
The alliance was viewed with great popularity among German Austrians. Some individuals within this group attempted to leverage the alliance to influence internal policy, suggesting that fidelity to the agreement required a ministry in Vienna that supported Germans in their internal struggles against the Slavs. They characterized the arrangement as a national alliance of the Teutonic races.

However, the governments of both Germany and Austria-Hungary refused to countenance this theory. Bismarck was particularly careful to avoid the appearance of exercising influence over the internal affairs of his ally. He recognized that such interference could arouse strong anti-German passions among the Poles and Czechs, who were always inclined toward an alliance with France, thereby jeopardizing the stability of the alliance.

## Related Italian Political Context
During the late 19th century, Italian politics were characterized by the activities of statesmen such as Benedetto Cairoli (1825-1889). Cairoli, a former Garibaldian officer and anti-Austrian conspirator, served as premiership and foreign office leader. His early cabinets were marked by an Irredentist and Francophil policy, which at one point brought Italy to the verge of war with Austria. 

Cairoli's tenure also saw the "clean hands" policy at the Berlin Congress, a move that resulted in Italy obtaining nothing while Austria-Hungary secured a European mandate to occupy Herzegovina and Bosnia. This period of irritation and political instability preceded the broader shift toward the alliance of 1882.
