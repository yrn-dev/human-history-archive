# Bookbinding in England

The art of bookbinding in England has evolved through various stages, ranging from the high-volume production of commercial trade binders to the specialized work of art binders and innovative individual craftsmen.

## The Work of Roger Payne and Contemporaries
Towards the end of the 18th century, a period when decorative binding in England was at a low level, Roger Payne, a native of Windsor, established himself as a bookbinder in London. A splendid workman, Payne introduced several innovations to the craft, including ornamental inside linings (known as "doublures") and richly gold-tooled corner-pieces. He is also credited with inventing the "straight grain" method of graining morocco, which involves graining the leather in only one direction. Payne is said to have cut his own iron binding tools, which are described as exquisitely made; in many of his bindings, he included written descriptions of the loving work he performed on them. Despite being a drunkard, Payne is noted for rendering an immortal service to the art of bookbinding in England.

Other notable figures of the era included John Edwards of Halifax, who in 1785 patented a method to make vellum transparent for use as a covering over delicate paintings. Following a manner used by Samuel Mearne in the 17th century, Edwards painted pictures on the fore-edges of books that remained hidden until the book was opened. Another binder, John Whitaker, utilized calf for his bindings, ornamenting the material using prints from engraved metal plates and strong acids. Because both Whitaker and Edwards favored classical ornaments and borders, their work is often referred to as "Etruscan."

## Commercial and Machine Binding
In large cities such as London, New York, and Paris, the industry is divided between art binders and large trade binders. Trade binders primarily utilize machinery to produce a great quantity of work, though these books are not expected to last for a long duration. 

Most commercial binding machines are products of German or American ingenuity and are typically driven by power. Because these machines must be accurately set to a specific size, they are most effective for large editions of a single book rather than books of varying sizes. A significant distinction exists between hand-sewn books and "cased" books produced by machines. In hand-sewing, the loose ends of the bands are drawn through the boards and strongly fastened. Machine-covering, however, generally only pastes the loose ends of the sewing tapes to the inner surfaces of the boards. Consequently, a cased book is more likely to slip out of its cover once the paste perishes.

## The Modern School of Art Binding
A small class of art binders continues to work according to principles used for first-class work since approximately the 5th century. This school received its initial impetus from William Morris, who created beautiful designs for bindings in both blind and gold. While Morris may not have fully appreciated the specific possibilities or limitations of gold-tooling on leather, his genius guided the spirit of the designs.

The development of this revived art progressed under T.J. Cobden-Sanderson, who is considered the founder of the modern school of design for gold-tooling on book-covers. His individuality and pre-eminence are evidenced by the number of people who imitated his work. Among his successful pupils was Douglas Cockerell, whose work is noted for a scholarly appreciation of ancient methods and originality of treatment. Other contributors to the field include Alfred de Sauty, who developed a style combining delicate pointille work with inlaid leathers.

Women artists in America and England have also found bookbinding to be a lucrative and fitting field. Sarah Prideaux is noted as both a skilled, original binder and a producer of valuable literature on the subject. E.M. MacColl is credited with inventing the small curved gold line produced by a tiny wheel; the graceful curves on her work were designed by her brother, D.S. MacColl. Additionally, Joanna Birkenruth has utilized jewels cut *en cabochon* to recall decorative medieval bindings.

## Related Crafts
The production of bookbinding tools was a specialized skill. William Caslon, who started business in London in 1716, worked as a bookbinder's tool-cutter and an engraver of barrels and gun locks before establishing a famous family of typefounders.
