# Banking and Financial Systems of Germany

## The Imperial Bank of Germany
The Imperial Bank of Germany, also known as the *Reichsbank*, serves as a central financial institution with specific powers regarding note issuance and banking accommodation. The *Reichsbank* possesses an automatic power to augment its note issue on securities, a feature that distinguishes it from the Bank of England. This arrangement with the treasury of the German empire is designed to prevent panic and provide necessary banking accommodation. Consequently, the interest rate charged has generally been 5%, although it reached 7% for a short period in 1899. Between 1881, when circulation first exceeded authorized limits, and the end of 1906, the duty on this extra issue totaled L839,052, providing funds for the relief of taxation.

In 1906, a law was passed allowing the Imperial Bank to issue notes (*Reichsbanknoten*) in denominations of 20 marks (L1) and 50 marks (L2, 10s.). These were added to the existing circulation of 5, 10, 100, and 1000 mark notes. Previously, 20 and 50 mark currency existed only as treasury notes (*Reichskassenscheine*), which were withdrawn following this law.

## Capital, Reserves, and Specie
The capital of the *Reichsbank* was raised to L9,000,000 by a bill in 1899. Its reserve fund, built from surplus profits, reached L3,240,000, exceeding the L3,000,000 requirement set by the act of 1899. An amending act further increased the government's share of profits while diminishing the dividends for stockholders.

Regarding specie, the Bank of Germany sometimes holds more coin and bullion than the Bank of England. Its weekly accounts include silver, though gold and silver are only separated in the annual balance sheet. As of December 31, 1906, the balance sheet showed approximately L24,000,000 in gold and L9,000,000 in silver. In terms of capital, the Bank of Germany's L12,200,000 is exceeded by the Bank of England's L18,000,000, but is higher than the Bank of France's L9,000,000. In 1906, the note circulation of the Imperial Bank of Germany was L69,000,000.

## The German Banking Sector
Beyond the *Reichsbank*, there are approximately 140 banks operating within the states of the German empire. These industrial and credit banks are credited with having an immense influence on the country's industrial development. Five specific banks hold the right of uncovered note-issue:
*   The Imperial Bank of Germany: L23,641,450
*   The Bank of Bavaria: L1,600,000
*   The Bank of Saxony: L838,500
*   The Bank of Wuerttemberg: L500,000
*   The Bank of Baden: L500,000

The total combined capitals and reserves of German banks—including those established for business in the Far East and South America, as well as the Bank of Germany—amount to about L133,000,000. This capital figure is very similar to that of the banks in the United Kingdom. Total resources, including mortgage bonds, notes, and deposits, reach fully L414,000,000. German banks utilize acceptances in a manner not practiced by banks in other countries. The average note circulation of the *Reichsbank* typically ranges between L60,000,000 and L70,000,000.

## International Obligations and Restitutions
Following a period of conflict, Germany was mandated to deliver coal to several nations over a ten-year period. To France, Germany must deliver coal equivalent to the difference between the pre-war output of the Nord and Pas de Calais mines and the production during those ten years, with additional options for 7,000,000 tons per year. Other options include 8,000,000 tons annually for Belgium and an amount for Italy rising from 4,500,000 tons (1919–1920) to 8,500,000 tons (1923–1924). Coke may be substituted for coal at a ratio of three tons to four. Additionally, benzol, ammonia, and coal tar are to be delivered to France over three years. A Commission holds the power to annul or postpone these deliveries if they interfere with Germany's industrial requirements.

Germany is also required to restore several items:
*   The Koran of the Caliph Othman (formerly at Medina) to the King of the Hedjaz within six months.
*   The skull of Sultan Okwawa (formerly in German East Africa) to the Government of His Britannic Majesty.
*   French flags and certain papers belonging to M. Reuher, which were taken by German authorities in 1870, to the French Government.
