# Bricklaying and Wall Construction

Bricklaying is a technical craft requiring specific methods of placement, bonding, and structural preparation to ensure the stability and durability of a building. The process involves the precise arrangement of bricks and the careful application of mortar or cement to create cohesive masses.

## Labor and Placement Techniques
For any wall that exceeds one brick (9 in) in thickness, two men should be employed simultaneously—one working on the inside and the other on the outside. This is necessary because a single man cannot do justice to a wall even 14 in. thick from one side alone. When a wall can only be accessed from one side, the process is referred to as being executed "overhand."

The physical placement of bricks requires specific care:
* **Positioning:** Bricks should not be merely laid; they must be placed with the frog facing upwards, then rubbed and pressed firmly down to force mortar into the joints and ensure absolute adhesion.
* **Wetting:** Bricks must be well wetted before laying, particularly in hot, dry weather. This removes surface dust, ensures more complete adhesion, and prevents the brick from absorbing water from the mortar. This is achieved either by the bricklayer dipping them in water or by sprinkling water on the bricks while they are piled on the scaffold. This practice is even more critical when using quick-setting cements.
* **Circular Work:** For work that is circular on plan, a ranging trammel (a rod on a pivot at the center of the curve with a length equaling the radius), a template, or a gauge mould must be used for every course, as pins and lines cannot be applied.

## Bonding and Structural Integrity
Bonding is the arrangement where bricks in every course cover the joints of the course below. This ensures the mass of bricks acts together as dependently as possible. Proper bonding distributes weight from the topmost brick down through every course over an increasing area.

Different bonding methods are used depending on the wall's purpose and thickness:
* **English Bond:** Considered the most perfect in use, this involves alternate courses of headers and stretchers. In walls with an odd half-brick thickness, the appearance of opposite sides of a course is inverted.
* **Flemish Bond:** Often used for appearance on the face of a wall.
* **Single Flemish Bond:** A compromise where the face is Flemish bond and the backing is English bond, which avoids the use of bats in the wall's interior.
* **Stretching Bond:** Used only for walls half a brick thick, where all bricks are laid as stretchers.
* **Heading Bond:** Used for footings, projecting mouldings, corbels, and work curved too sharply for stretchers; all bricks are laid as headers.
* **Herring Bone or Diagonal Bond:** Used in the interior of thick walls (three bricks thick and upwards) to save labor without losing strength. This should not be used for walls thinner than 27 in.

To maintain these bonds, "queen closers" (portions equal to a half header in width) are placed next to the first header near a quoin or vertical end. "King closers" are used at rebated openings in Flemish bond to add strength.

## Specialized Brickwork and Flues
Certain architectural features require specialized techniques:
* **Arches:** Rough-cut arches use bricks cut with an axe into wedge forms. Gauged arches use soft bricks cut and rubbed to templates to form fitting voussoirs, set in lime putty with joints no wider than 1/32 in. To ensure stability, the sides of the voussoirs are gauged hollow and grouted with Portland cement.
* **Chimneys and Flues:** Flues must be built with precision to maintain capacity. Bends should be gradual curves to minimize resistance to up-draught, with at least one bend of no less than 60° to stop down-draughts. Each fireplace requires a separate flue, though multiple flues can be collected into a "stack" for efficiency and aesthetics. Flues are separated by smoke-proof divisions called "withes," usually half a brick thick. Common flue sizes are 14×9 in. for ordinary grates and 14×14 in. for kitchen stoves.

## Foundations and Footings
Footings are wide courses of brickwork at the base of a wall designed to spread pressure. Offsets of 2¼ in. are made on each side until the width is double the wall's thickness. For example, a wall 13½ in. thick would have a bottom course 2 ft. 3 in. wide. These are typically built with hard bricks laid as headers.

Regarding foundations:
* **Concrete:** Concrete foundations are used on soils like clay that expand when wet and shrink when dry. The London Building Act requires a concrete projection of 4 in. on each side of the brickwork, though 6 in. is often used for easier working. If a concrete wall is used as a substitute for brick, the London Building Act requires an extra thickness of one-third.
* **Damp Courses:** According to 1891 by-laws from the London County Council, every wall must have a damp course of impervious materials extending through its whole thickness at least 6 in. below the lowest floor.

## Historical Examples
The archive records the walls of Babylon as an example of prodigious construction. These walls were 87 feet thick and 350 feet high, forming an exact square with sides 15 miles long. They were constructed of large bricks cemented with bitumen, a glutinous slime that binds more firmly than mortar. These walls were surrounded by a vast brick-lined ditch.
