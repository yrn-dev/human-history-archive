# Mortar Bricks

## Definition and Composition
Mortar bricks are categorized as unburnt bricks and are defined as blocks of artificial stone produced using brick moulds. These bricks are created by moulding a mixture of slaked lime and sand. The resulting blocks are then left to harden in the air.

## The Hardening Process
The hardening of mortar bricks occurs primarily through the conversion of slaked lime (calcium hydrate) into calcium carbonate, a process driven by the action of carbonic acid in the atmosphere. Water evaporation also contributes to this hardening. Additionally, a small amount of lime combines with water and silica to create hydrated calcium silicate, and some hydrated basic carbonate of lime may also form; both of these substances act as cement.

Historically, this natural air-exposure process was lengthy, taking between six and eighteen months to complete.

## 19th Century Improvements and Evolution
During the latter half of the 19th century, various improvements were introduced to increase the strength of mortar bricks and accelerate the hardening process. These included:
* **Material Changes:** The introduction of mixtures containing cement, lime, and sand, as well as mixtures of lime and certain ground blast-furnace slags.
* **Manufacturing Techniques:** The use of hydraulic presses for moulding.
* **Post-Processing:** Treating the bricks with carbon dioxide under pressure, sometimes accompanied by the application of mild heat.

While some of these methods remain in use, a newer type of mortar brick has emerged in recent years that has practically superseded the traditional mortar brick.

## Comparison with Sand-lime Bricks
A distinct process for hardening sand and lime blocks was patented in the early 1880s by Dr. Michaelis of Berlin, leading to the production of "sand-lime" bricks. Unlike mortar bricks, these are made by mixing dry sand with approximately one-tenth of its weight in powdered slaked lime, moistening the mixture, and moulding it under powerful presses (exerting about 60 tons per sq. in.). 

These bricks are then placed in steel cylinders (typically 7 ft. in diameter and 60 to 80 ft. long) and subjected to high-pressure steam (120 to 150 lb per sq. in.) for ten to fifteen hours. This process creates a bond of hydrated calcium silicate that is of a better type than the calcium carbonate filling found in mortar bricks, making sand-lime bricks significantly stronger than any ordinary mortar brick.
