# Syon House and the Bridgittine Order

## The Bridgittine Order
The Bridgittines are an order of Augustinian canonesses established around 1350 by St Bridget of Sweden and received approval from Urban V. in 1370. The order was structured as a "double order," meaning that while the community was governed by an abbess, each convent had a small community of canons attached to it to serve as chaplains. The order saw significant growth in Norway and Sweden, where it contributed remarkably to the promotion of literature and culture in Scandinavia. This influence is evidenced by the head house at Vastein, located by Lake Vetter, which remained active until its suppression in 1595. In total, there were 80 Bridgittine houses across various lands. Currently, the order consists only of women, with approximately six convents remaining on the Continent.

## Syon House at Isleworth
In England, the Bridgittine convent of Syon at Isleworth, Middlesex, was founded in 1415. Royally endowed by Henry V., it grew to become one of the most influential, fashionable, and richest nunneries in the country. The site also served as a location for early scientific observation; Thomas Harriot conducted telescopic observations at Syon House, Isleworth, independently obtaining results similar to those of Galileo.

## Migration and Return
The community of Syon faced significant instability during the English Reformation. During the reign of Mary, the house was among the few religious houses restored, with nearly twenty members of the old community re-established there. However, upon the accession of Elizabeth, the community migrated to the Low Countries. Following a period of many vicissitudes, they moved to Rouen and eventually settled in Lisbon in 1594. While in Lisbon, the community continued to recruit new members from England.

The Bridgittines remained in Lisbon until 1861, at which time they returned to England. Syon House is now established at Chudleigh in Devon. It is noted as the only English community that can claim an unbroken conventual existence since the pre-Reformation era.
