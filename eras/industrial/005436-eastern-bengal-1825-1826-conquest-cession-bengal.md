# Eastern Bengal

## Conquest and Cession
Eastern Bengal was acquired through a process of conquest and cession between 1825 and 1826. This period of acquisition is linked to the broader British expansion in the region, which included the acquisition of Burma through wars in 1826 and 1852. Specifically, the province of Assam, which had remained stubbornly outside the limits of the Mahommedan polity and the Mogul empire despite frequent invasions by Mussulman armies, maintained an uncertain independence until it was invaded by the Burmese toward the end of the 18th century. Assam was finally ceded to the British in 1826.

## Administrative Evolution
The administrative status of the region shifted significantly over time. In 1905, the province of Bengal was reconstituted, leading to the creation of a new province called Eastern Bengal and Assam. This new entity was formed by transferring several areas from Bengal, including the state of Hill Tippera and the districts of Malda, Rajshahi, Dacca, and Chittagong.

The boundary between the province of Bengal and the province of Eastern Bengal and Assam was defined by the Ganges and the Madhumati river. Additionally, the Dwars—a tract of country in north-east India—were integrated into these administrations. Following the Bhutan War of 1864-65, the British took possession of the Western and Eastern Dwars. While the Eastern Dwars were initially assigned to Assam and the Western to Bengal, both divisions were placed within the province of Eastern Bengal and Assam after 1905.

## Geography and Infrastructure
The region's geography is characterized by its proximity to the Himalayas and major river systems. The Eastern Dwars, located in the Goalpara district of Eastern Bengal and Assam, consist of a strip of flat country beneath the Bhutan mountains, though the area is noted as being slightly developed and unhealthy for both Europeans and natives. The Western Dwars, located in the north-east of the Jalpaiguri district, serve as an important center for the tea-planting industry.

Transportation in the region is supported by a railway system centered at the seaport of Calcutta. The Eastern Bengal railway runs north to Darjeeling and operates a service of river steamers on the Brahmaputra. This line connects with the Bengal & North-Western railway via an eastward extension through Tirhoot. The termini for these services are located at Sealdah, an eastern suburb of Calcutta.

## Economy and Commerce
The economy of the region is closely tied to its natural resources and trade. Assam is a primary source of tea, jute, and oil-seeds. The sea-borne trade for Eastern Bengal and Assam is almost entirely concentrated at the port of Calcutta. Principal exports from the broader Bengal region include jute, tea, indigo, lac, hides, opium, rice, and oil-seeds.

Inter-provincial trade is heavily conducted between Eastern Bengal and Assam and other regions such as the Central Provinces and the United Provinces. While the region maintains frontier trade with Bhutan, Tibet, and Nepal, the volume of trade is considered insignificant except for that conducted with Nepal.

## Language and Culture
The linguistic landscape of the region is diverse. Bengali, known natively as *Banga-Bhasa*, is spoken in the Eastern Bengal portion of the province of Eastern Bengal and Assam, as well as on both sides of the Ganges delta. In the Assam Valley, the language spoken is Assamese (natively *Asamiya* or *Ohamiya*). 

Both Bengali and Assamese utilize alphabets derived from early forms of the Nagari character of northern India. The Bengali script, dating from approximately the 11th century A.D., is a cursive script designed for speed. The Assamese alphabet is identical to the Bengali alphabet, with the exception of one additional character used to represent the sound of *w*.
