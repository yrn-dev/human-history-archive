# Marl

Marl is a calcareous clay consisting of a mixture of argillaceous matter and carbonate of lime. Because the term is applied to a wide variety of soils and rocks with a broad range of composition, a strict definition is difficult to establish. Generally, marls graduate into clays as the amount of lime decreases, and they pass into argillaceous limestones as the lime content increases. While popular usage has historically applied the term to various superficial deposits, loams, and rotted rocks of igneous origin—often due to the practice of top-dressing land to increase fertility—characteristic marls are typically regarded as containing between 25% and 75% carbonate of lime.

## Physical and Chemical Properties
Typical marls are earthy and soft, appearing in white, grey, or brownish colors. Many of these substances disintegrate when placed in water and are readily attacked by dilute hydrochloric acid, which causes the carbonate of lime to dissolve and release bubbles of carbon dioxide. 

The lime within marls may exist as a fine, impalpable powder mixed with the clay or in the form of whole or broken shells. Sand is rarely absent, though it is usually not abundant. Some marls contain organic matter, such as humus or plant fragments. Various accessory minerals can be identified through microscopic examination, including:
* **Gypsum:** Found in some marls, sometimes as twinned groups resembling arrow-heads or lozenge-shaped plates. In the marls of Montmartre near Paris, celestine (strontium sulphate) also occurs in concretionary or nodular masses.
* **Other Minerals:** Certain deposits contain small crystals of quartz, lumps of iron pyrites, radiate nodules of marcasite, and large crystals of dolomite or calcite. In Westphalia, the Senonian marls at Hamm produce masses of strontianite reaching up to two feet in length.

## Geological Varieties
Marls are found in various forms across different regions and geological systems:

**Shell Marls:** Found in northern countries and Britain, these are often located in meadows or marshy spots beneath thick layers of peat. They consist of a clay matrix containing numerous shells of fresh-water mollusca. Because the shells are unbroken and the species represented are few, it is concluded that the mollusca lived in the shallow pools and open stretches of clear water that formed the original sites of the deposits. Farmers value shell marls as a source of carbonate of lime, though they are rarely burned to create quicklime.

**New Red Marls:** Located in the Triassic rocks of Britain, these red, green, and mottled clays can exceed a thousand feet in thickness in some areas. Found in the uppermost Keuper division, they are technically ferruginous rather than calcareous clays and likely formed in desert saline lakes. They contain very few fossils and little carbonate of lime, but feature profuse veins and beds of fibrous gypsum. In Cheshire, these deposits are a principal source of rock salt in Great Britain.

**Other Classifications:** In the Rhaetic beds overlying Triassic rocks, "tea-green marls" and "grey marls" occur as calcareous clay seams a few feet thick. In Germany, various types are described, including the white Solenhofen limestone of Bavaria (used as lithographic stone) and the dark, copper-bearing marl slate of the Permian rocks near Mansfeld.

## Use in Brick-Making
In the context of brick production, clays are divided into two types based on chemical composition. The first type consists chiefly of hydrated aluminium silicates with small amounts of carbonate of lime, burning to red, salmon, or buff colors. The second type, known as marls, contains a considerable percentage of carbonate of lime (up to 40%). These marls are distinctive for burning to a sulphur-yellow color.

### Processing and Production
Marls containing 15% to 30% calcium carbonate are used for bricks, though those with over 40% limestone may vary in color. In England, natural deposits of these lime clays have been largely exhausted. In the London area, a fine chalk-clay called "malm" was formerly used; currently, an artificial malm is created by mixing ground chalk with ordinary brick-clay.

The production process for high-quality London facing-bricks involves:
1. **Mixing:** Clay and ground chalk are mixed in water to a cream-like consistency.
2. **Sieving:** The resulting "pulps" are run through a coarse sieve or grating onto a drying-bed.
3. **Tempering:** Once the mass is stiff enough to walk on, fine ashes are spread over it, and the mixture is turned over with spades and tempered with water.

In other regions, marl is mixed with water in a wash-pan, and the fluid is passed through sieves. Coarse sand may be added to the wash-pan if the washed clays are too fine in grain. Alternatively, plastic marls may be squeezed through iron gratings to remove pebbles, or passed through a grinding-mill with heavy iron rollers to crush limestone pebbles. The removal of these pebbles is critical; if left in the clay, they convert to quicklime during firing, which can cause the finished brick to shatter when exposed to weather.
