# The Imperial Styling of 1868 and the Austrian-Hungarian Monarchy

## Change of Imperial Title
In 1868, a significant change in the official styling of the emperor was implemented to reflect the principle of Dualism. By way of a letter sent to Beust on November 14, 1868, the emperor ordered that his title be altered. He was no longer to be styled as "Emperor of Austria, King of Hungary, King of Bohemia, &c.," but instead as "Emperor of Austria, King of Bohemia, &c., and Apostolic King of Hungary." 

This modification in terminology was intended to signify the separation of the two districts over which the emperor ruled. Following this change, the lands under his rule became known as "The Austrian-Hungarian Realm" or "The Austrian-Hungarian Monarchy." The emperor's shorter official styles were established as "His Imperial and Apostolic Royal Majesty" and "His Majesty the Emperor and King."

## Administrative Terminology and Ministries
The shift in the emperor's title led to the adoption of new terminology within the public service. The phrase "Imperial and Royal" (_Kaiserlich und Koeniglich_) began to be applied to branches of the public service belonging to the common ministries. This application first occurred within the diplomatic service. It was not until 1889 that this terminology was applied to the army, which had previously maintained the old style of _Kaiserlich-Koeniglich_. By 1895, the style was applied to the ministry of the imperial house, an office held by the minister for foreign affairs.

The role of the minister for foreign affairs also underwent a change in nomenclature. Originally called the _Reichskanzler_, this title was abandoned in 1871 when Andrassy succeeded Beust. The change was made in deference to Hungarian feeling, as the term _Reichskanzler_ could be interpreted as implying the existence of a single state.

The monarchy maintained a complex system of finance ministries to manage its divided administration. Three distinct ministries of finance existed:
* The _Kaiserlich und Koeniglich_ ministry for joint affairs.
* The _Kiralye_ ministry for Hungary.
* The _Kaiserlich-Koeniglich_ ministry for Austrian affairs; this "K.K." style remained in use within the Austrian half of the monarchy.

## Political Unrest in Bohemia and Galicia
During 1868, the constitution faced challenges from nationalist movements in Galicia and Bohemia. In Galicia, an extreme party led by Smolka sought to imitate the Czechs by refusing to attend in Vienna. While they were outvoted, all parties agreed on a declaration outlining the demands of the Poles. These demands included a significant increase in the powers of the Galician diet and a request that Galician members stop attending the Reichsrath for discussions on matters that the Galician diet should be qualified to handle. The Poles threatened to leave the Reichsrath if these demands were not met.

Simultaneously, Czech leaders in Bohemia were highly active. While the Poles displayed hostility toward Russia—leading the emperor to avoid visiting Galicia—some Czech leaders attended a Slav demonstration in Moscow. In 1868, a "declaration" was presented to the diet at Prague, serving as an official statement of Czech claims. These claims included:
* The full restoration of the Bohemian kingdom.
* The assertion that no foreign assembly was qualified to impose taxes in Bohemia.
* The claim that the diet was not qualified to elect representatives for Vienna.
* The demand for a separate settlement with Bohemia, similar to the one established with Hungary.

This declaration was signed by eighty-one members, including bishops and many feudal nobles. In response, the German majority declared that these members had forfeited their seats and ordered new elections. This led to serious riots and the government's decree of exceptional laws to maintain order. Similar unrest occurred in Moravia, and a revolt broke out among the Bocchesi in Dalmatia.

## Ethnic and Linguistic Conflict in Bohemia and Moravia
The contest between Germans and Czechs was most vehement in Moravia and Bohemia, where the two races were nearly equal in number. A victory for the Czechs would have placed nearly two million Germans in a position of subordination. Over a twenty-year period, there was a constant encroachment of the Czech language over German, partly facilitated by government action.

An 1880 ordinance mandated that business brought before any law court or government office in Bohemia and Moravia be dealt with in the language in which it was introduced, giving Czech a position within the government service. In 1886, this was extended to the high courts of Prague and Bruenn to avoid frequent translations. Further steps included the founding of many Czech elementary and middle schools, and the introduction of Czech classes in German schools. In 1882, Czech classes were started at the university of Prague, an event viewed by Germans as a desecration of the oldest German university.
