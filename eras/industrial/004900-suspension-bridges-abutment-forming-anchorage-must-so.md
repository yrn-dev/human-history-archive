# Suspension Bridge Abutments and Anchorages

In the construction of suspension bridges, the substructure—which comprises the foundations, piers, and abutments—must be engineered to handle specific physical forces. While girder bridges impose only vertical loads on their abutments, suspension bridges create a horizontal pull that necessitates specialized design for the end supports.

## Design and Stability of Abutments
The abutment forming the anchorage of a suspension bridge must be designed to be thoroughly stable under the greatest pull that the chains can exert. Because the principal members of a suspension bridge are in tension, the system requires massive anchorages to resist this pull. In the general substructure of such a bridge, the abutments are responsible for resisting the pull of the suspension chain in addition to other potential forces, such as the thrust of an embankment.

## Anchorage Systems and Components
Suspension chains, which may be constructed from twisted wire strands, wires laid parallel, or links connected by pins, typically pass over lofty piers. From these piers, the chains are led down on either side to anchorages located in rock chambers. 

Specific examples of anchorage components include:
* **Anchor Plates:** In the case of the Clifton bridge, each chain utilizes an anchor plate measuring 5 ft. by 6 ft.
* **Anchor Ties:** In the Tower Bridge in London, chains on the abutment towers are connected to anchor ties via horizontal links carried on rockers.

## Interaction with Piers and Superstructure
The design of the piers is closely linked to the type of suspension bridge adopted. Piers must be carried above the platform, and if the resultant pressure on the piers is not vertical, they must be constructed to meet the inclined pressure. In stiffened suspension bridges, the action of the pier is analogous to that of a pier situated between two arches.

The superstructure consists of the supporting members—such as ropes or chains—and the bridge flooring. To mitigate the inherent defect of flexibility in simple suspension bridges, they can be stiffened with bracing and girders. This flexibility is less serious when the dead weight of the structure is large relative to the temporary or live load.

## Historical Examples and Technical Specifications
Various historical bridges illustrate the application of these principles:
* **The Menai Bridge (1819):** Constructed by T. Telford with a span of 570 ft. and a dip of 43 ft.
* **The Niagara Bridge (1852-1855):** Built by J. A. Roebling with a span of 821 ft. It featured four suspension cables, each 10 in. in diameter, composed of seven strands of 520 parallel wires each.
* **The Clifton Bridge:** This bridge utilizes three chains on each side (alternating one and two links) and carries chains at a 45° angle with the horizontal down to the anchorages.
* **Tower Bridge, London:** A suspension bridge featuring a secondary bascule bridge. It utilizes two main towers in the river and two towers on the shore abutments to carry the suspension chains. The main towers are constructed of a steel skeleton enclosed in brickwork and a facing of Portland stone and granite.
