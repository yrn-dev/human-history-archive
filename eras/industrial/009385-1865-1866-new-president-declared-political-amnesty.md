# Political Amnesties and Governance (1865–1866)

The mid-1860s were marked by significant transitions in governance, financial restructuring, and the application of political amnesties across various nations. These events reflect a broader pattern of states attempting to stabilize after periods of revolution, war, or internal conflict.

## The Concept of Amnesty
An amnesty, or "oblivion" (non-remembrance), is defined as a declaration by those holding sovereign power in a state that specific individuals shall be excused for their political acts. This mechanism implies a power imbalance, as it suggests that those granting the amnesty possess the authority, while those receiving it are either in subjection or lack the political power held by the authors of the decree. 

Historical precedents for this practice include Athens, where an amnesty was declared after Thrasybulus overthrew the Thirty Tyrants, though the Thirty and a few others were excluded. Similarly, Cicero attempted to establish a settlement in the temple of Tellus, utilizing the Greek example of "oblivion" to erase the memory of discord.

## German Political Amnesty of 1866
In the 1860s, the application of amnesty allowed former political exiles to return to their home countries. A notable example is Ludwig Bamberger, a German economist and politician. Born in Mainz and active in the republican party during the revolution of 1848, Bamberger had been condemned to death following a republican rising in Baden and the Palatinate in 1849. 

After spending years in exile in Switzerland, London, Holland, and Paris—where he served as managing director of the bank of Bischoffheim & Goldschmidt until 1866—Bamberger was able to return to Germany due to the amnesty of 1866. Upon his return, he was elected to the Reichstag and joined the National Liberal party, accepting the results of the work of Bismarck.

## United States Financial and Political Transitions
In the United States, the years 1865 and 1866 were characterized by the administrative efforts of the federal government to stabilize the economy and reorganize state governments.

### Treasury and Currency
In 1865, President Lincoln appointed Hugh McCulloch as secretary of the treasury, a position McCulloch maintained under President Johnson until 1869. On December 4, 1865, McCulloch issued his first annual report, which urged the retirement of "greenbacks" (legal tenders) to facilitate the resumption of specie payments. This led to an act passed on March 12, 1866, which authorized the retirement of up to $10,000,000 in six months, and $4,000,000 per month thereafter. Despite this, the act faced strong opposition and was repealed on February 4, 1868, after $48,000,000 had been retired.

### Reconstruction in Louisiana
During this period, the Union military government worked to supersede military rule in Louisiana. By 1864, a constitution had been framed that abolished slavery and repudiated debts incurred as a Confederate state. However, by the spring of 1866, ex-Confederates had successfully gained control of most state offices and local government, though they did not secure the governorship. This prompted the Republican party to become more radical, with leaders seeking negro suffrage and a new constitutional convention to regain control of the government.

## Institutional and Professional Developments
The mid-1860s also saw the establishment of new educational and professional milestones.

### Education
On April 27, 1865, the legislature of New York state incorporated a university named in honor of Ezra Cornell. Cornell, along with Andrew D. White, envisioned a non-sectarian institution that provided practical training in sciences and humanities. The university's trustees first met on September 5, 1865, with Andrew D. White serving as president.

### Legal and Political Careers
Several individuals navigated the political landscape of 1865–1866. Benjamin Helm Bristow served as an assistant United States district-attorney from 1865 to 1866 before becoming the district-attorney for the Louisville district. In New York, Chauncey Mitchell Depew served as secretary of state from 1864 to 1865; in 1866, through connections with William H. and Cornelius Vanderbilt, he became the attorney for the New York & Harlem railway.
