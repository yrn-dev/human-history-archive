# The Nemesis and the Satirical Works of Barthelemy and Mery

Between March 1831 and April 1832, the French satirical poets Auguste Marseille Barthelemy and J. P. A. Mery produced a series of verse satires known as the *Nemesis*. These works were issued on a weekly basis and were directed at the ministers and government of Louis Philippe.

## The Collaboration of Barthelemy and Mery
Auguste Marseille Barthelemy (1796–1867), born in Marseilles, maintained a collaboration with his compatriot and friend J. P. A. Mery (1798–1866) that was so intimate it is considered impossible to distinguish their individual personalities within their joint productions. 

Before their partnership, Barthelemy had established a local reputation as a poet. Upon moving to Paris, he initially gained the favor of the court with his 1825 work, *Le Sacre de Charles X*. However, he soon shifted his energies toward the service of the opposition party.

## Early Political Satires
The joint efforts of Barthelemy and Mery began with a series of clever political satires. In 1825, they published *Les Sidiennes*, followed in 1827 by *La Corbiereide*, *La Peyronneide*, and *La Villeliade ou la prise du chateau de Rivoli*. The latter was particularly successful, requiring fifteen editions within a single year.

The authors continued to produce a rapid succession of satires and political squibs. Notable among these were *Napoleon en Egypte* (1828), which saw nearly a dozen editions in one year, and the *Biographie des quarante de l'academie francaise* (1826).

## Imprisonment and the Revolution of 1830
In 1829, Barthelemy faced legal consequences for the publication of *Fils de l'homme*, a poem concerning Napoleon's son, the duke of Reichstadt. For this work, he was fined 1,000 francs and imprisoned. He was liberated by the Revolution of 1830. Following this event, he and Mery celebrated the triumph of the people with one of their most brilliant joint efforts, titled *L'Insurrection*.

## The Nemesis and Later Career
The period from March 1831 to April 1832 marked the publication of the *Nemesis*. Because of these weekly attacks on the government of Louis Philippe, the small pension Barthelemy had been receiving was stopped. When the *Nemesis* ceased publication, there was strong suspicion that Barthelemy had received payment in exchange for his silence.

In 1832, Barthelemy published an anonymous poem that supported certain government acts that were particularly obnoxious to the Liberal party. This change in political alignment destroyed his influence, and his subsequent writings went unnoticed. For the following few years, he refrained from satirical writing and enjoyed a handsome pension from the government.
