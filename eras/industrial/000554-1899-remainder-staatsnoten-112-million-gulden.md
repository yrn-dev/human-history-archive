# Redemption of the Staatsnoten and Austro-Hungarian Currency Reform

## The Nature of the Staatsnoten
The *Staatsnoten* were government-issued notes created during the war of 1866. The Austro-Hungarian government issued these notes to a total value of 312 million gulden to raise funds, an action that violated the charter of the Austro-Hungarian Bank, which held the sole right to issue notes. Because the bank had been forced to suspend cash payments in 1848—and subsequent attempts to renew them during the wars of 1859 and 1866 had failed—the notes functioned as an inconvertible paper currency.

## Currency Reform of 1892
By 1892, the government undertook a threefold operation to reform the currency: the resumption of cash payments by the bank, the redemption of the *Staatsnoten*, and the substitution of a gold standard for the existing silver standard. 

Prior to this, the currency consisted of paper money issued by the Austro-Hungarian Bank and silver gulden (the "Austrian currency" introduced in 1857). The Austro-Hungarian Bank had originally been entirely Austrian in 1867, but following a settlement in 1877, it was converted into an Austro-Hungarian bank with management divided between the two countries and head offices located in both Vienna and Pest.

## Implementation of the Gold Krone
Laws passed in both parliaments in 1892 introduced a new coinage unit called the krone, which was divided into 100 heller. The krone was valued at approximately 24-25ths of a franc. In terms of the previous currency, the gold krone was equal to .42 of the gold gulden and .5 of the silver gulden, the latter accounting for the depreciation of silver.

The transition began with the issue of nickel coins and silver one-krone pieces, which circulated as half gulden. While the silver gulden remained in circulation, other silver and copper coins were recalled, and the issuance of gold four- and eight-gulden coins was suspended.

## Funding and Redemption Process
The transition to the new coinage required an increased supply of gold. The bank was mandated to buy gold (purchasing over forty million gulden in 1892) and was required to coin any gold brought to it into ten- or twenty-krone pieces. 

To further facilitate this, a loan of 150 million gulden at 4% was arranged through Rothschild. Rothschild paid in gold, consisting primarily of sovereigns and bar gold, which was used to strike new coins valued at over 34 million kronen. Rather than being put into general circulation immediately, these coins were first utilized to pay off the *Staatsnoten*. By 1894, the state had successfully redeemed 200 million gulden of these notes, including all notes for one gulden.
