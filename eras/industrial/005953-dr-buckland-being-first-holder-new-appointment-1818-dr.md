# William Buckland

William Buckland (1784–1856) was an English geologist and divine known for his contributions to the science of geology and his roles within the Church of England. Born on March 12, 1784, in Axminster, he was the eldest son of the Rev. Charles Buckland, who served as rector of Trusham and Templeton in Devon.

## Education and Early Academic Career
Buckland was educated at Winchester and the grammar school of Tiverton. In 1801, he was elected as a scholar of Corpus Christi College, Oxford, where he earned his B.A. in 1804 and was later elected a fellow of the college in 1809. During this period, he was admitted into holy orders.

From his youth, Buckland possessed a strong interest in natural science. This passion was further stimulated by the lectures of Sir Christopher Pegge on anatomy and Dr. John Kidd on chemistry and mineralogy. Under the instruction of Dr. Kidd, who delivered lectures in the dark chambers beneath the Ashmolean Museum, Buckland received his first lessons in geology.

## Geological Appointments and Research
Buckland systematically studied the geological structure of Great Britain, investigating the characters of organic remains and the order of superposition of strata. In 1813, following the resignation of Dr. Kidd, Buckland was appointed reader in mineralogy at Oxford. The significant interest generated by his lectures led to the founding of a readership in geology in 1819, which was specifically endowed by the treasury; Buckland became the first holder of this new appointment.

His professional recognition grew rapidly; he was elected a fellow of the Royal Society in 1818 and served as president of the Geological Society of London twice, in 1824 and 1840. In 1818, he used the term "Kimeridge Clay" to describe the basal division of the Upper Oolites in the Jurassic system. Later in his career, Buckland zealously sought evidence of former glaciation in Britain, welcoming the Glacial theory proposed by Agassiz.

## Ecclesiastical Career and Personal Life
Parallel to his scientific pursuits, Buckland held several positions within the Church of England. In 1825, he was presented to the living of Stoke Charity near Whitchurch, Hants. That same year, Lord Liverpool appointed him to a canonry of the cathedral of Christ Church, Oxford, and he was conferred the degree of D.D. In 1845, Sir Robert Peel appointed him to the vacant deanery of Westminster, and he was subsequently inducted to the living of Islip.

In 1825, Buckland married Mary, the eldest daughter of Mr. Benjamin Morland of Sheepstead House. Mary is noted for having materially assisted Buckland in his literary efforts through her judgment and abilities.

## Major Works and Legacy
Buckland's reputation is primarily based on his published scientific works. His first major publication, *Reliquiae Diluvianae* (1823), examined organic remains found in fissures, caves, and diluvial gravel—including discoveries from the Kirkdale cavern in Yorkshire—to explain their relationship to the Biblical account of the Deluge.

In 1836, he published a treatise on "Geology and Mineralogy" as part of the *Bridgewater Treatises*, a series intended to demonstrate the "goodness, wisdom, and power of God, as manifested in the Creation." The third edition of this work was issued in 1858, edited by his son, Francis T. Buckland.

Beyond his writing, Buckland was recognized as a teacher of the highest order. He presided over the second meeting of the British Association at Oxford in 1832. He also left a legacy at the university of Oxford in the form of large private collections of mineralogy and geology.
