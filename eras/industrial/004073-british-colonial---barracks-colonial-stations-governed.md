# British Colonial Barracks and Imperial Defence

## General Standards for Colonial Barracks
Barracks at colonial stations are governed by the general scale of accommodation established in the *Barrack Synopsis*. However, these standards are modified based on the specific climate of the station, typically resulting in an increase in room height and floor area. In sub-tropical or tropical regions, planning focuses on ensuring the freest possible circulation of air through the buildings. 

To protect walls from the direct rays of the sun, verandahs are utilized. Construction methods vary according to the resources and customs of the specific country. Buildings are generally arranged to catch prevailing winds, and in some instances, inhabited buildings are raised approximately 4 feet off the ground to allow air to circulate beneath the floors. Local domestic arrangements also influence design; for example, in hot climates, kitchens are usually not attached directly to dwelling-houses, and sanitary arrangements vary by the methods adopted.

## Indian Barracks
In India, barracks for British troops are constructed by Royal Engineer officers detailed for military work. They are assisted by military foremen—who have passed through civil engineering colleges—and a native subordinate staff. The scale of accommodation is dictated by Indian army regulations, which provide the private soldier with a more liberal allowance of space than the home government allows for any other colonial stations.

Barrack-rooms in India are airy and lofty, featuring clerestory windows and surrounding verandahs. Roofs are typically double-tiled. Each man is allowed 90 square feet of space in rooms that are 16 feet high. Additionally, an adjoining day room is provided for use as a sitting-room or for meals. 

The facilities for troops in India include:
* **Recreation:** Liberal provision of establishments, including cricket grounds, plunge baths, fives courts, and skittle and bowling alleys.
* **Family and Health:** Separate blocks for married quarters and schools for children. While hospital accommodation is provided on a higher scale than in Britain, hill sanatoria have been used in recent years to improve troop health by providing a change of air for families and men during hot weather.
* **Infrastructure:** Piped water supplies have replaced old wells at many stations, and there is ongoing attention toward improved sanitary and cooking arrangements.

## Imperial Land Defence and Funding
Land defence has been closely linked to the internal growth and development of the empire. Initially, the first American colonies were expected to provide for their own land defence, a practice they followed to some extent, including the taking of Louisburg. In India, the East India Company practiced the principle of local self-defence from the beginning.

In North America, however, local resources proved insufficient for the French wars. Great Britain intervened in 1755 with troops under General Braddock. From that point until the American War, the imperial exchequer bore the cost of North American defence, leading the imperial parliament to claim the right to tax the colonies. In 1765, the Quartering Act was passed, resulting in 10,000 imperial troops being quartered in the colonies.

Following the loss of the American colonies, the imperial authorities accepted the charge for land defences across the empire. Except for the Hudson Bay territories and India—where trading companies paid their own expenses—the taxpayers of the United Kingdom bore the full cost of imperial defence until the end of the Napoleonic Wars. By the mid-19th century, fiscal changes gave self-governing colonies command of their own resources, leading to the expectation that colonial exchequers provide for their own governing expenses, including defence.

## Naval Defence and the Two-Power Standard
Because the empire's main thoroughfares are the oceans, a strong navy is considered the primary requirement for defence. The "two-power standard" became the fundamental axiom, requiring the British navy to exceed the joint strength of the two next powers by a 10% margin.

Until the decade between 1890 and 1900, the colossal expense of this fleet was borne exclusively by United Kingdom taxpayers. Eventually, self-governing colonies began contributing to the imperial navy:
* **Australasian colonies:** Voted £126,000 per annum in 1895, increased to £240,000 in 1902.
* **Cape Colony:** Offered £30,000 a year in 1897, increased to £50,000.
* **Natal:** Voted £35,000 a year.
* **Newfoundland:** Voted £3,000.

Additionally, the Commonwealth provided an Australian torpedo-boat flotilla starting in 1908-1909. Despite these contributions and the local upkeep of fortifications, the majority of the cost remained with British taxpayers. A crisis regarding the increase of the German and allied Austrian fleets in 1909 led to a wave of colonial enthusiasm, including offers of "Dreadnoughts" from New Zealand and other areas.
