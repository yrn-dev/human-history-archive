# Baronet

The baronetage is a hereditary title created by James I as a "new Dignitie between Barons and Knights." The primary objective of its institution was to raise funds for the crown, a practice similar to the sale of peerage dignities under the same sovereign. Specifically, the funds were professedly devoted to supporting troops in Ulster; each grantee was required to pay £1095, which covered the pay of thirty men at 8d. a day for three years.

## Origins and Early Regulations
Instituted in May 1611, the baronetage was initially intended to be select. King James I covenanted that no more than two hundred baronets would be created. To qualify, candidates were required to possess a landed estate worth £1000 a year and have paternal grandfathers who had borne arms, though these qualifications were eventually abandoned.

The title conferred the prefixes "Sir" and "Lady" (or "Dame"). In terms of precedence, baronets were ranked above knights but below the younger sons of barons. To encourage applications after a royal decree on May 28, 1612, had reinforced this lower ranking, James issued a new commission on November 18, 1614. By 1616, the king further incentivized the honor by granting that the heirs apparent of baronets be knighted upon reaching adulthood.

## Regional Baronetages
Following the English institution, the baronetage of Ireland was established on September 30, 1619, with the king pledging a limit of one hundred creations. 

The baronetage of Nova Scotia was devised in 1624 to promote the "plantation" of that province. James I intended to create one hundred baronets, each required to support six colonists for two years (or pay 2000 marks) and pay 1000 marks to Sir William Alexander, who had been granted the province by charter in 1621. Under this scheme, Alexander received a "free barony" of 16,000 acres in Nova Scotia and became a baronet of "his Hienes Kingdom of Scotland."

Charles I carried out this scheme, creating the first Scottish baronet on May 28, 1625. He covenanted that the number of baronets of Scotland or Nova Scotia would not exceed 150 and required a payment of 3000 marks (£166, 13s. 4d.) toward the colony's plantation. In 1629, Charles I granted Nova Scotia baronets the right to wear a badge suspended by an orange tawny ribbon, featuring an azure saltire with a crowned inescutcheon of the arms of Scotland and the motto "Fax mentis honestae gloria." By 1633, the honor was opened to Irish and English gentlemen, though only slightly more than 120 were created in total. Grants of land in Nova Scotia ceased in 1638.

## Heraldry and Precedence
Baronets are entitled to display the red hand of Ulster on an inescutcheon or a canton in their coat of arms. Those of Nova Scotia instead display the saltire of that province. 

Following the union with England in 1707, Scottish creations ceased, and baronetcies of Great Britain were issued to both Scotsmen and Englishmen. After the union with Ireland in 1800, new baronets were created as baronets of the United Kingdom rather than of Great Britain or Ireland. The relative precedence of Nova Scotia and Irish baronets compared to English baronets remained undetermined by the Acts of Union and has been a moot point among heralds.

## Administration and Challenges
The baronetage has historically struggled with the "wrongful assumption" of titles. In 1783, a royal warrant directed that no one be recognized as a baronet in official documents without proof of right, and that future creations register their pedigree and arms at the Heralds' College. However, the first regulation was rescinded due to opposition from the baronets.

The baronetage of Nova Scotia was particularly susceptible to wrongful assumptions because the dignity could descend to collateral heirs male of the grantee as well as those of his body. In 1906, a Home Office departmental committee was appointed to address these assumptions.

In 1897, an order giving the sons of life peers precedence over baronets sparked agitation. In 1898, some baronets formed "the Honourable Society of the Baronetage" to maintain their privileges, though a royal warrant issued on August 15, 1898, confirmed the precedence of the sons of life peers. The society continued as the "Standing Council of the Baronetage," which eventually secured invitations for some representatives to the coronation of King Edward VII.
