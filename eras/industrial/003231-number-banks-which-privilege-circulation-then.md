# The Privilege of Circulation in Banking

The privilege of circulation refers to the legal authority granted to banking institutions to issue their own notes as a medium of exchange. This privilege was subject to significant legislative changes in the 19th century, particularly in the United Kingdom and the United States, as governments sought to regulate the volume of currency and ensure its stability.

## The Bank Act of 1844 in England and Wales

Prior to 1844, nearly all provincial banks in England and Wales, in addition to the Bank of England, possessed the privilege of issue. This changed with the introduction of the Bank Act of 1844. During the parliamentary discussions of the act, banks continued their previous operations; however, once the arrangements were made public, nine banks ceased issuing notes before the act took effect on October 12, 1844. Of these nine, eight were private banks and one was a joint-stock bank. The Western District Joint-Stock Banking Co. was dissolved, one private bank closed, and the remaining seven transitioned to issuing Bank of England notes.

Under the act of 1844, the maximum circulation for English issuing banks was fixed based on the average circulation from the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. At that time, the banks allowed the privilege of circulation in England consisted of:
*   **207 private banks**, with an authorized issue of £5,153,417.
*   **72 joint-stock banks**, with an authorized issue of £3,478,230.

The combined authorized issue for these banks totaled £8,631,647. By October 12, 1844, the actual circulation in England included £20,228,800 from the Bank of England, £4,674,162 from private banks, and £3,331,516 from joint-stock banks.

## Circulation in Scotland and Ireland

In Scotland and Ireland, the privilege of circulation was governed by the acts of 1844-1845. Sir Robert Peel intended this legislation to ensure that bank notes remained uniformly equivalent to coin. To achieve this, any notes issued in excess of the established limits required the issuing banks to hold gold (specie) at their offices. The amount of specie required was determined by the average note-issue of the preceding four weeks.

In Ireland, the limit of authorized circulation established by Peel's Act of 1845 remained in place. Six banks held the privilege of issue at that period, and none have lost it since. By 1906, the Irish joint-stock banks included the Bank of Ireland (est. 1783), Hibernian Bank (1824), Provincial Bank (1825), Northern Banking Co. (1825), Belfast Banking Co. (1827), National Bank (1835), Ulster Banking Co. (1836), Royal Bank (1836), and Munster Bank, Ltd. (1864). However, the Hibernian, Royal, and Munster banks were not banks of issue.

By May 1907, the authorized issue in Ireland was reduced to 12 private banks (£482,744) and 17 joint-stock banks (£1,084,836). The actual circulation of these banks remained far below their authorized limits because their notes were largely unknown outside the immediate neighborhood of their issuance.

In Scotland, the limitation of the power of note-issue to banks that possessed it in 1845 prevented the establishment of any important new banks in the country thereafter. This was because the use of notes in ordinary business was so prevalent that banks without the power of issue could not effectively supply the needs of their customers.

## The National Banking System of the United States

In the United States, the national banking system emerged from the financial needs of the Federal government during the Civil War. Secretary of the Treasury Mr. Chase created a system where banks organized under Federal charters were granted special privileges to issue circulating notes, provided these notes were secured by the deposit of government bonds. This was authorized by the act of February 25, 1863, and supplemented by the act of June 3, 1864.

To ensure the predominance of national banks over entrenched state banking systems, a 10% tax was imposed on the face-value of state bank notes in circulation after July 1, 1866. This drove state banks out of the note-issuing business; some converted to national banks, while others continued commercial operations without the privilege of issue. This led to rapid growth:
*   **1864:** 453 national banks with aggregate capital of $79,366,950.
*   **1865:** 1,014 banks with aggregate capital of $242,542,982.

The volume of this circulation was heavily influenced by the price of bonds. The original maximum circulation was set at $300,000,000, increased by $54,000,000 in 1870, and the limit was removed entirely in 1875. Circulation fluctuated based on bond availability and prices, reaching $362,651,169 on January 1, 1883, and falling to $167,927,574 by July 1, 1891.

## Evolution of Banking Structures

Over time, the nature of banking shifted. In England and Wales, the number of banking offices grew from 976 in 1844 to more than 5,880 by 1906. While the number of individual banks diminished—by 1906, the business was carried on by approximately ten private and sixty joint-stock banks—the number of offices increased. Much of the increase in the Bank of England's circulation since 1844 is attributed to notes held in the tills of these various banking offices.
