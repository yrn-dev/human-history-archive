# Chartered, Private and Joint-Stock Banks

The banking landscape of the United Kingdom and other European regions during the 19th and early 20th centuries was characterized by a transition from small private firms to large joint-stock institutions, governed by a series of legislative acts and evolving rules regarding liability and the issuance of bank notes.

## Legal Evolution of Joint-Stock and Private Banks
In early English banking, the acts of 1694, 1697, and 1708 established a system where no joint-stock bank could be established within England during the continuance of the Bank of England. Furthermore, partnerships exceeding six persons were prohibited from borrowing or taking up money on bills or notes payable on demand or in less than six months. This restriction on partners in private banks remained until 1862, when the limit was increased to ten, though this change did not apply to issuing private banks, which remained restricted to six partners.

The prohibition on joint-stock banks was modified in 1826, allowing co-partnerships of more than six persons to be established beyond 65 miles from London. In 1833, the restriction was removed entirely, allowing joint-stock banks within the 65-mile limit, although they were not permitted to issue notes within that radius.

## Liability and the Companies Act
Until the act of 1862, joint-stock banks did not possess the privilege of limited liability; every shareholder was liable to the extent of their entire means. This deterred many wealthy individuals from becoming partners. The risks of unlimited liability were highlighted by the failures of the Western Bank of Scotland in 1858 and the City of Glasgow Bank in 1878, both of which caused great hardship for overconfident shareholders. 

The failure of the City of Glasgow Bank led to the Companies Act of 1879, which allowed unlimited companies to adopt limited liability. Under this act, a shareholder who paid the nominal amount of their holding was no longer liable for further amounts, unless the company issued bank notes, in which case they remained liable as if the company were unlimited. Most joint-stock banks in the United Kingdom adopted these facilities, except for those already limited by special act or charter.

## The Bank Act of 1844 and Note Issuance
Prior to the Bank Act of 1844, nearly all provincial banks in England and Wales had the privilege of issue. The 1844 act fixed the maximum circulation of English issuing banks based on the average circulation of the twelve weeks preceding April 27, 1844. 

As of October 1844, the authorized issues in England were:
*   **Private banks:** 207 banks with an authorized issue of £5,153,417.
*   **Joint-stock banks:** 72 banks with an authorized issue of £3,478,230.

The actual circulation in October 1844 for England included £20,228,800 from the Bank of England, £4,674,162 from private banks, and £3,331,516 from joint-stock banks. In Scotland, the combined circulation of chartered, private, and joint-stock banks was £2,987,665. In Ireland, the Bank of Ireland had a circulation of £3,597,850, while private and joint-stock banks accounted for £2,456,261.

By May 1907, Irish issues were reduced to 12 private banks (actual issue £122,536) and 17 joint-stock banks (actual issue £437,693). The low actual circulation of these country banks was attributed to their limited geographic reach; their notes were largely unknown outside their immediate neighborhoods and were often difficult to cash, even if payable in London.

## Growth and Consolidation
The banking system shifted toward larger institutions in the latter half of the 19th century. While the number of individual banks diminished—by 1906, English banking was carried out by approximately ten private and sixty joint-stock banks—the number of offices increased significantly. In England and Wales, banking offices grew from 976 in 1844 to 5,527 in 1906.

This growth occurred primarily through the absorption of small banks by three or four large institutions. These large joint-stock banks gained significant influence over foreign exchanges due to their large holdings of bills, sometimes exerting more influence than the Bank of England. They also developed a closer combination with the Bank of England to help maintain the national reserve and stabilize the money market.

## The Clearing House and International Examples
In London, city banks formed a "Clearing House" to settle accounts, with evidence of such a system existing as early as 1773. By 1810, forty-six banking houses used a dedicated house for this purpose. Initially, only private banks were permitted as members; joint-stock banks were admitted in 1854. By 1906, the Clearing House consisted of nineteen banks: one private and eighteen joint-stock.

Outside the United Kingdom, the Austro-Hungarian Bank (founded in 1816 as the Austrian National Bank) served as a joint-stock company by 1899 with a stock of £8,780,000. It held the exclusive legal right to issue bank notes for both Austria and Hungary, with notes required to be covered by two-fifths legal specie (gold and current silver) in reserve.
