# German Street Names and the Czech Town Council

In the history of the Austrian Empire, the use of language in public spaces and government administration served as a primary indicator of political power and ethnic victory. This was particularly evident in the struggle between German and Slav-speaking populations in Bohemia and other territories.

## Language and Public Signage in Prague
In Prague, the victory of the Czechs over German influence was manifested through the systematic removal of all German street names. To ensure the permanence of this change, the Czech town council enacted a by-law that forbade private individuals from putting up tablets displaying the name of the street in German.

This trend of using street names as a "sign of victory" was mirrored in other regions. In Laibach, after the Slovenes won a majority in the town council in 1882, they introduced Illyrian as the official language and required street names to be written in Illyrian. Similar linguistic shifts in the towns of Istria, where Illyrian was used alongside Italian for street naming, resulted in serious riots.

## The Legal Framework of Language Rights
The broader conflict over language was governed by the constitution of 1867. Article 19 of this constitution recognized that all races of the empire possessed equal rights and an "inviolable right" to the use and preservation of their own language and nationality. The state recognized the equality of all "customary" (*landesueblich*) languages in public life, offices, and schools.

However, the application of this law granted significant power to the government, as the state determined which languages were considered "customary." This led to ongoing disputes:
* **German Demands:** Germans sought the recognition of German as a customary language in every part of the empire, including Galicia and Dalmatia.
* **Czech Demands:** In Bohemia, Czechs claimed their language should be recognized as customary even in districts that were almost entirely German, such as Reichenberg.

## Administrative and Educational Struggles
The contest for linguistic dominance was fought with great vehemence in Bohemia and Moravia. Over a period of twenty years, the Czech language encroached upon the German language, often aided by government ordinances. An 1880 ordinance required that business brought before government offices or law courts be handled in the language in which it was introduced. In 1886, this was extended to the high courts of Bruenn and Prague to avoid frequent translations.

Education became a critical point of contention. While a law of 1867 required elementary education in Slav districts to be in Slovenian or Czech, Slavs demanded their own schools even in towns where they were a small minority. This demand was eventually granted by Prazak, the minister of education under Count Taaffe. The introduction of Czech classes at the university of Prague in 1882 was viewed by Germans as a "desecration" of the oldest German university.

## Political Conflict and the Badeni Ordinances
The linguistic struggle reached a peak with the ordinances published in May 1897 by Badeni. These ordinances determined that:
1. All documents and correspondence brought before government officials in Bohemia should be conducted in the language in which they were first introduced, effectively introducing Czech into government offices throughout the kingdom.
2. After 1903, no person could be appointed to a government post in Bohemia without passing an examination in Czech.

These measures caused severe backlash from German Nationalists and Radicals, who utilized obstruction in parliament to prevent any business from being conducted until the ordinances were repealed. The tension remained high until Count Clary withdrew the ordinances in September 1899, though the Czechs continued to demand the restoration of their language in the internal service of Bohemia.
