# Barking Creek and the Roding

## Geography and Location
Barking is a market-town situated in the Romford parliamentary division of Essex, England. It is located on the river Roding, specifically near the point where the river meets its junction with the Thames. The town is positioned 8 miles east of Liverpool Street station and Fenchurch Street station in London. It is served by the Great Eastern and London, Tilbury & Southend railways.

## Infrastructure and Industry
The mouth of the Roding, referred to as Barking Creek, is the site of great sewage works. These facilities receive the Northern Outfall sewer from London. In addition to the sewage works, the area supports chemical works and a shipping trade that primarily deals in fish and timber. 

Other industrial developments within the parish include extensive rubber works and the works of the Gaslight and Coke Company located at Beckton.

## Town History and Landmarks
Barking was historically noted for its nunnery, which was among the richest and oldest in England. Founded around 670 by Erkenwald, the bishop of London, the nunnery was destroyed by the Danes and subsequently restored in 970 by King Edgar. The abbess of the nunnery held the status of a baroness *ex officio*. At the time the monasteries were dissolved, the revenue of the nunnery was L1084. A turreted gateway in the perpendicular style remains from this site.

Other notable landmarks in the town include:
* **The Church of St Margaret:** A Norman structure featuring perpendicular additions and various monuments of interest.
* **Town Hall:** An ancient market-house that serves as the town's hall.
* **Public Spaces:** The town contains recreation grounds and Victoria Gardens, which serve as a public pleasure-ground.

## Demographics and Administration
Between 1891 and 1901, the population of the urban district of Barking town grew from 14,301 to 21,547. Administratively, Barking serves as a suffragan bishopric within the diocese of St Albans.

## Local Tradition and Trade
Historical records and local traditions suggest that illicit traffic was common in the Thames, extending as far up the river as Barking Creek. This context supports theories regarding the use of "ready-made hiding-places," such as dene-holes or caves, by smugglers for the purpose of concealing contraband.
