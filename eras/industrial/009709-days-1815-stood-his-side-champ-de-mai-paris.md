# The Hundred Days and Related Historical Figures (1815)

The period known as the Hundred Days in 1815 was a time of significant political instability and transition in France, marked by the return of Napoleon and the subsequent efforts of various figures to navigate the shifting power structures between the empire and the Bourbon restoration.

## Lucien Bonaparte
Lucien Bonaparte had a complex relationship with the emperor, having lived in Italy for several years, primarily in Rome, where he exhibited marked hostility toward him. In December 1807, the emperor attempted to reach an arrangement where Lucien would become a French prince on the condition that he annul his marriage. Lucien refused this requirement. He resided for a time at his estate of Canino, taking the papal title of prince of Canino, before departing for America. During this journey, he was captured by a British ship and transported to Malta and then to England, where he remained under surveillance until the peace of 1814.

Upon returning to Rome, Lucien offered his assistance to Napoleon during the Hundred Days of 1815. He stood by Napoleon's side at the "Champ de Mai" in Paris and was the final person to defend the emperor's prerogatives during his second abdication. Lucien spent the remainder of his life in Italy and died in Rome on June 29, 1840. His family included six daughters and four sons. His literary works include the two-volume epic *Charlemagne, ou l'Eglise delivree* (1814), as well as *Memoirs* and *La Verite sur les Cent Jours*, though the latter two remained unfinished.

## Financial and Political Administration
The transition of power during this era involved several key statesmen. Joseph Dominique Baron Louis (1755–1837), a financier and statesman born in Toul, served as the minister of finance from 1814 to 1815. Despite having been made a baron of the empire in 1809, Louis supported the Bourbon restoration. He served as a deputy from 1815 to 1824 and again from 1827 to 1832, resuming the finance portfolio in 1815. He later held this position in the Decazes ministry of 1818 and served as the first minister of finance under Louis Philippe in 1831–1832 before becoming a peer of France.

Other officials also navigated the Hundred Days. Bignon, who had been engaged in preparing a constitution for Poland before being interrupted by the events of 1812, returned to Napoleon's service during the Hundred Days. Following the battle of Waterloo, Bignon served as the minister of foreign affairs under the executive commission. In this capacity, he signed the convention of July 3, 1815, which resulted in the handing over of Paris to the allies. Bignon later returned to public life in 1817 as a member of the chamber of deputies until 1830.

## Military and Family Perspectives
The military campaigns of 1815, specifically the Waterloo campaign, are documented in the writings of Marshal Grouchy. His works include *Le Marechal Grouchy du 16 au 18 juin, 1815* and *Fragments historiques relatifs a la campagne et a la bataille de Waterloo*. Grouchy also wrote refutations and observations regarding the accounts of the campaign provided by General de Gourgaud and the Duc de Rovigo.

The political instability of 1814–1815 also affected the Bonaparte family. Maria Annunciata Caroline (1782–1839), born in Ajaccio and wife of Joachim Murat, associated herself with the "equivocal movements" of her husband during this period. Following these events, she retired to Austrian territory under restraint before eventually living in Trieste with her sister, Elisa. Her husband, Joachim Murat, met a tragic end at Pizzo on October 13, 1815.
