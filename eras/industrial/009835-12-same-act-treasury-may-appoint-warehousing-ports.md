# Bonded Warehouses and Port Logistics

## Definition and Purpose of Bonded Warehouses
A bonded warehouse is a facility established by either private enterprise or the state. Its primary function is to serve as a location where goods liable to duty are lodged until the required duty upon them has been paid. 

Before the implementation of this system in England, importers were required to pay duties at the time of importation or provide a bond with security to revenue authorities for future payment. This previous system created several inconveniences:
* Importers often struggled to find sureties.
* Importers were frequently forced to sell goods immediately to raise the necessary funds for duty, often during periods of low market prices.
* The requirement to pay duty in a lump sum increased the price of goods due to the interest on the capital needed for payment.
* Competition was hindered because the importation of heavily taxed articles required large amounts of capital.
* There were difficulties in granting an exact equivalent drawback to exporters for goods that had already paid duty.

## Evolution of the English Warehousing System
To address these difficulties and prevent revenue frauds, Sir Robert Walpole proposed a warehousing system for wine and tobacco in his "excise scheme" of 1733. Although the proposal was initially unpopular, the system was officially adopted in 1803. Under an act of that year, imported goods were placed in warehouses approved by customs authorities, and importers provided "bonds" for the payment of duties upon the removal of the goods; this practice gave rise to the terms "bonding" or "bonded."

The Customs Consolidation Act 1853 removed the requirement for giving bonds and established various provisions to secure the payment of customs duties on warehoused goods. These provisions were later contained in the Customs Consolidation Act 1876, the Revenue Act 1883, and the Customs and Inland Revenue Act 1880. Under the 1876 act, these facilities are known as "king's warehouses," defined as any place approved by the commissioners of customs or provided by the crown for the deposit of goods and the security of the duties due thereon.

## Port Logistics and Cargo Expenses
Under English law, expenses incurred after a ship and cargo have reached safety at a port of refuge are generally not treated as General Average (G.A.). However, if the entry into port was necessitated by a G.A. sacrifice—such as the cutting away of the ship's masts—the expenses for unloading, storing, reloading the cargo, and repairing the G.A. damage are treated as consequences of the original sacrifice and are subject to contribution.

In contrast, if a port is entered to avoid danger (such as a hostile cruiser or a storm) or to repair accidental damage, the G.A. sacrifice is considered ended once physical safety is reached. Subsequent expenditures are viewed as necessities for completing the voyage and are the responsibility of the shipowner. While English average adjusters have modified this by treating unloading expenses as G.A., the legal support for this practice is doubted. Additionally, English practice treats expenditures incurred to protect cargo, such as warehousing it, as a charge to be borne by the cargo for whose benefit the service was provided.

## International Port and Treasury Examples
Various global ports and treasuries have operated under specific administrative frameworks:
* **Cadiz:** The Spanish government declared Cadiz a free warehousing port in 1828 to check a decline in trade caused by the Napoleonic wars and the independence of Spanish colonies. This privilege was withdrawn in 1832.
* **Kyaukpyu:** This port operates under the Indian Ports Act (X. of 1889), with weekly steamer calls between Calcutta and Rangoon via the British India Navigation Company.
* **Japan:** In early Japanese history, the Imibe-uji managed a single store for both administrative and religious articles. During the reign of Richu, a "domestic store" (Uchi-kura) was established for domestic taxes and foreign tribute, with the Koreans Wani and Achi appointed as accountants. Later, in Yuryaku's time, the Okura (Imperial treasury) was added to accommodate increased production of manufactures. Soga no Machi eventually took supreme charge of all three stores (Okura, Uchi-kura, and Imi-kura).
* **Calcutta:** In 1772, Warren Hastings moved the treasury from Murshidabad to Calcutta, establishing the town as the capital of Bengal.
