# Millet

The term "millet" appears in the archive's records in three distinct contexts: as a prominent figure in the French Barbizon school of painting, as a type of cereal grain cultivated in various regions, and as a socio-religious administrative category within the Ottoman Empire.

## Jean-François Millet and the Barbizon School
Jean-François Millet was a leader of the "Barbizon school," a group of painters based in the French village of Barbizon near the forest of Fontainebleau. Along with other leaders such as Corot, Rousseau, and Daubigny, Millet and his contemporaries rejected conventional ideas of "subject" in their art. Instead, they sought inspiration directly from nature, specifically through the depiction of peasant life and landscapes.

Millet spent his early years in Paris before making his home in Barbizon. For many years, he lived in a state of neglect, poverty, and lack of appreciation, struggling before eventually attaining success and recognition. His approach to art was characterized by a preference for "stern truth" over aesthetic feeling. Along with Rousseau, Millet's work is noted for being beautiful and unaffected, though it was initially received harshly due to the 19th-century conflicts between the romantic and classic schools of art. Millet died in Barbizon in 1875.

## Millet as a Cereal Grain
In the context of agriculture, millet refers to various cereal grasses cultivated for food. *Sorghum vulgare*, known in Arabic as *durra* (meaning "pearl"), is a cereal grass grown extensively in semi-tropical and tropical countries. In non-Arabic-speaking regions, this grain is referred to by several names, including African millet, Indian millet, Guinea corn, Kaffir corn, and pearl millet; in India, it is known as *jowari* or *jowaree*. The grain is commonly used to make bread and serves as an important dietary staple.

* **Jíruft and Rudbar:** In these districts, the lands are described as having great fertility, yielding millet in addition to rice, cotton, pulse, dates, henna, Palma Christi, and various beans.

## The Millet System in the Ottoman Empire
The term *millet* also refers to a recognized religious or national community within the Ottoman Empire. The archive describes efforts by the governments of Belgrade and Bucharest to obtain the recognition of separate Servian and Vlach *millets*. 

These efforts were driven by a lack of separate ecclesiastical organization for the orthodox Serbs and Vlachs in Turkey, who previously owed allegiance to the Greek patriarchate. The government of Bucharest sought the establishment of a Rumanian bishopric at Monastir, while the Servians sought the restoration of the patriarchate of Ipek and the appointment of a Servian metropolitan at Usküb. The Vlach *millet* was eventually recognized by the Porte via iradé on May 23, 1905.
