# Bombardon

The bombardon, also known as the bass tuba, is a brass wind instrument used as the bass and contrabass in military bands. In an orchestral setting, it is referred to as the bass tuba. The term "bombardon" is derived from *bombardone*, the Italian name for the contrabass pommer (bombard), which served as the bass for medieval orchestras prior to the invention of the fagotto. Additionally, the term is applied to an organ bass reed stop with a 16 ft. tone.

## History and Development
The bombardon was the first bass wind instrument to be fitted with valves. In its early stages, it was known by several names, including *bass horn*, *clavicor*, or *corno basso*. These names referred more to the instrument's function as a bass than to the specific instrument itself. The original *corno basso* featured a narrow bore and horizontal pistons.

In Prussia, A. Kalkbrenner noted that the keyed bass horn (*corno basso*) was introduced into Jager military bands as the bass in 1829, followed by the bombardon (or valve-ophicleide) in 1831. By 1835, these were superseded in the Guards by the bass tuba, which was invented by J.G. Moritz and Wieprecht.

The bombardon and the euphonium (of which the bombardon is the bass) resulted from applying valves to the bugle family, a process that also produced saxhorns. A primary distinction between tubas, including the bombardon, and saxhorns is the wide conical bore of the former, which allows for the production of fundamental sounds with immense power and a rich, full quality. This distinction led to a classification in Austria and Germany as "whole" instruments (*Ganze Instrumente*), while narrow-bore instruments like saxhorns were termed "half instruments" (*Halbe Instrumente*).

## Design and Construction
Modern bombardons exist in two primary forms:
*   **Upright model:** Used for stationary band music.
*   **Circular model (Helicon):** Worn around the body with the bell resting on the left shoulder, similar to the Roman *cornu*, making it more convenient for marching.

The instrument utilizes a cup-shaped mouthpiece, similar to that of a trumpet. To obtain a complete chromatic scale, the instrument requires seven different lengths of tubing. These are achieved through combinations of pistons or valves. Most bombardons feature three or four pistons that lower the pitch by 1, 1.5, 1.5, and 2.5 tones, though in Belgium, the intervals are 1, 1.5, 2, and 3 tones. In Germany and Austria, a "cylinder action" is frequently used, replacing the pump or piston with a four-way brass cock operated by cranks and a key.

## Technical Characteristics and Performance
The bombardon possesses a harmonic series from the 1st to the 8th and a chromatic compass spanning 3.5 to 4 octaves. Because of the immense length of the air column, the lowest notes produced by the valves are difficult to obtain, as the lips often lack the power to synchronize with such a deep pitch. Consequently, these lowest notes are barely audible unless another bombardon doubles them an octave higher.

Regarding notation, bombardons are generally treated as non-transposing instruments where music is written as sounded, with the exception of Belgium and France, where transposition is common.

## Intonation and Innovations
A known defect of the valve system is that combinations of pistons do not provide absolutely correct intonation because the length of tubing opened is not the theoretical length required. To remedy this, several systems have been developed:
*   **Adolphe Sax:** A six-valve independent system.
*   **Besson:** The *Registre* (providing eight independent positions) and the compensating system *Transpositeur*.
*   **D.J. Blaikley:** The Boosey automatic compensating piston.
*   **V. Mahillon:** Automatic regulating pistons.
*   **Besson Enharmonic:** A system featuring three pistons and six independent tuning slides.
*   **Rudall, Carte & Company:** A new bore (Klussmann's patent) that is conical throughout the open tube and additional lengths.

## Regional Usage
The bombardon is utilized in the military bands of Austria. However, in Germany, it has been replaced by a bass tuba that differs slightly in construction and form from the bass tubas and bombardons used in Austria, France, Belgium, and England.
