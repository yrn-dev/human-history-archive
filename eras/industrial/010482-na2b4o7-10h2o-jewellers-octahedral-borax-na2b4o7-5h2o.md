# Borax

Borax, also known as sodium biborate or sodium pyroborate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7$), is a chemical substance that appears in commercial trade in two primary forms: "common" or prismatic borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot10\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and "jewellers'" or octahedral borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot5\text{H}_2\text{O}$). It is noted that alchemists used the term "borax" in a vague manner, which differs from the specific substance known by the name in modern contexts.

## Chemical Properties and Forms
The two commercial forms of borax differ by their water of crystallization. Prismatic borax is the decahydrate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot10\text{H}_2\text{O}$), which crystallizes in the monoclinic system. These crystals are transparent to opaque and typically white, though they may appear greenish, bluish, or greyish. They possess a hardness of 2–2.5 and a specific gravity between 1.69 and 1.72. In terms of habit and angles, prismatic borax closely resembles pyroxene. It exhibits a perfect cleavage parallel to the orthopinacoid and less perfect cleavages parallel to the prism faces.

The octahedral form, or pentahydrate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot5\text{H}_2\text{O}$), is deposited from supersaturated aqueous solutions when evaporation occurs at high temperatures. It can also be prepared by dissolving borax in water until the solution reaches a specific gravity of 1.246 and then cooling it; the pentahydrate deposits between 56°C and 79°C. Below 56°C, the decahydrate is deposited instead.

## Industrial Applications
Borax is utilized in various industrial and technical processes:
*   **Soldering and Fluxing:** Octahedral borax is preferred for fluxing or soldering because its crystals fuse more easily than the prismatic form and are less likely to split when heated. Borax is used to remove the superficial film of oxide from metal surfaces, as solder only adheres to untarnished metal.
*   **Metallurgy:** Fused borax can dissolve many metallic oxides to form complex borates, which often exhibit characteristic colors. It is used as a component in fluxes for the "dry" or fire assay of lead ores, where it may comprise 15.6% of a flux mixture containing potassium carbonate, sodium bicarbonate, and flour.
*   **Other Uses:** Borax is employed in the glazing of linen, the glazing of pottery, and in the manufacture of glass.

When heated, crystals of ordinary borax swell significantly, losing their water of crystallization and melting into a clear white glass.

## Natural Occurrence and Mineralogy
Prismatic borax is widely distributed as a natural product. Significant deposits are found in Canada, Peru, Transylvania, and Tibet. In Tibet, the material was historically exported to Europe as *tincal*. Specific Tibetan deposits include *sohaga* (impure borax) in the lake-plain of Pugha on the Rulangchu, and *chu tsale* (water borax) at the lakes of Rudok and Tengri Nor.

In the United States, large masses of crystallized borax are found in the bed of Borax Lake near Clear Lake, California. In Lake and San Bernardino counties, isolated crystals have been found weighing one pound each and reaching 7 inches in length. The Mohave desert, bordering Nevada and California, and the Atacama desert in Chile (specifically the province of Atacama) are also major commercial sources of boron compounds. In the Atacama region, borax deposits are described as extensive and productive.

These deposits are generally formed by evaporation from marshes, salines, or salt lakes. The boron in these solutions is likely supplied by solfataras and hot springs of volcanic origin, similar to those that charge the lagoons of Tuscany with boric acid. These natural deposits often consist of mixtures of borates, gypsum, and alkaline salts such as sodium chloride, sulphate, and carbonate.

## Production Methods
While borax occurs naturally, it is mainly derived from the boric acid of Tuscany. This is processed by fusing the boric acid in a reverberatory furnace with half its weight of sodium carbonate, after which the resulting mass is extracted with warm water. Alternatively, boric acid is added gradually to a solution of sodium carbonate in steam-heated, lead-lined pans, and the solution is concentrated until crystallization occurs. Borax can also be produced by fusing naturally occurring calcium borate with a required amount of soda ash, followed by water extraction and concentration.
