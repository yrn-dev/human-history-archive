# Riveting and Hole Production in Engineering

The process of riveting is a fundamental method of connecting metal components in various engineering applications, ranging from the construction of steam boilers to the assembly of bridges and girders. The evolution of how holes are produced for these rivets has been driven by the need to eliminate structural stresses and ensure the safety of the finished product.

## Evolution of Hole Production
Historically, a significant problem existed in riveting when holes were punched. It was practically impossible to ensure that holes in different plates coincided exactly when the plates were brought together. To resolve this, workers used a "drift," which is a tapered rod driven forcibly by hammer blows through the holes of adjacent plates to draw them into alignment. This violent treatment caused permanent strains in the plates and enlarged incipient cracks. Such abuse of the drift tool has been clearly linked to many boiler explosions.

To mitigate these risks, specifications later required that all holes be enlarged by reamering after the plates were positioned. However, this was not a complete safeguard, as the metal removed during reamering was often taken primarily from one side of the hole, leaving the other side torn by the original punch. Consequently, high-class boiler work transitioned to the practice of drilling rivet-holes. While punching and reamering are still used for average bridge and girder work—where service conditions are less severe than in steam boilers—drilling has become the standard for high-pressure environments.

## Materials and Structural Impact
The shift in riveting practices was accelerated by the replacement of malleable iron with mild steel. While steel possesses higher ductility, it is more susceptible to developing cracks if subjected to improper treatment; iron, by contrast, suffers less from irregular or overheating. 

The type of steel used dictates the required hole-production method. Steel is classified by tenacity and elongation:
*   **Soft steel** (tenacity 22½ to 26 tons per sq. in.): Used always for rivets and sometimes for entire bridge superstructures.
*   **Medium steel** (tenacity 26 to 34 tons per sq. in.): Generally used for plates and angle bars, reducing bridge weight by approximately 7% for a given safety factor.
*   **Moderately hard steel** (tenacity 34 to 37 tons per sq. in.): Used for larger members of long-span bridges.
*   **Hard steel** (tenacity 37 to 40 tons per sq. in.): Used only for compression members to minimize the risk of flaws extending.

For medium or moderately hard steel, holes must either be drilled or punched 1/8 in. smaller than the rivet and then reamed out to remove the ring of material strained by the punch.

## Industrial Machinery and Application
Due to the enormous number of rivet holes required in plating and boiler shops, drilling or punching holes individually is considered too slow. Modern machines are designed to produce many holes simultaneously. These machines are specialized; different types are used for shells, furnaces, or specific boiler models. Advanced machines can be adjusted to drill to an exact pitch, removing the need to mark out holes as guides.

In bridge construction, riveting is one of two primary connection methods, the other being pin joints. While pin joints were used in early English bridges (such as Saltash, Newark Dyke, and Chepstow) and remain a distinctive feature of American construction, the general tendency in Europe is to prefer riveted connections. Pins are now primarily used for tension members.

## Other Applications of Riveting and Holes
Beyond heavy structural engineering, riveting and hole production appear in other mechanical and historical contexts:
*   **Bicycle Wheels:** Rims made of aluminium alloy or sheet steel may be jointed by riveting. Holes are drilled near the central plane of the rim for spoke nipples, and holes are also passed through the hub flange to fasten spokes.
*   **Water-Tube Boilers:** In the "Babcock & Wilcox" boiler, cross-boxes are riveted to the bottom of the steam chest. The headers in these boilers feature holes on opposite sides; some connect headers to tubes, while others are oval-shaped to allow for cleaning and the fixing of tubes.
*   **Computing History:** Charles Babbage's "analytical engine" utilized cards with punched holes to communicate arithmetical operations to the machine via levers.
*   **Archaeology:** Pre-Roman "dene-holes" in Kent and Essex exhibit pick-holes near the roof, likely made by horn or bone picks.
