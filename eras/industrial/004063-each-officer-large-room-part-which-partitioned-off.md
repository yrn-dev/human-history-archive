# Officers' Quarters and Partitioning

## Military Officers' Quarters
In military installations, the officers' mess is typically situated a short distance from the barracks for men, often overlooking a cricket ground or parade area. The mess building features an entrance hall equipped with a band alcove for guest nights. The layout includes a mess-room (dining-room) on one side of the hall and an anteroom (reading-room) on the other. To accommodate lantern lights, the kitchen and billiard-room are positioned at the rear. The facility also contains a mess office and various departmental accessories, such as a plate-closet, pantry, cellarage, and a quarter for the mess-man.

The quarters for officers are generally located in a storey above the mess or in wings that extend the building's frontage. Each officer is provided with a large room, a portion of which is partitioned off to serve as a bedroom. Field officers are granted two rooms. Each officer is assigned a soldier servant who has a small room for cleaning purposes. Bathrooms are centrally located and receive hot water from the mess kitchen.

Specific housing is provided for high-ranking officials:
*   **Commanding Officer:** A detached house featuring seven bed- and dressing-rooms, three sitting-rooms, a kitchen, a bathroom, a servants' hall, and other accessories.
*   **Quartermaster:** A smaller house containing four bedrooms, two sitting-rooms, a kitchen, and a bath.

Other married officers of the regiment must arrange their own housing, though they are usually granted a lodging allowance.

## Naval Barracks
Naval barracks, such as those at Devonport, Chatham, and Portsmouth, are designed to maintain the system of board-ship life. These facilities feature large barrack-rooms with caulked floors similar to ship decks, where hammocks are slung for sleeping and stowed during the day so the rooms can function as mess-rooms. While naval officers' messes possess splendid public rooms, their quarters are not as large as those found in military barracks, reflecting the naval officer's familiarity with small cabins.

## Construction and Types of Partitions
Partitions are screens used to divide large floor spaces into smaller rooms. In some instances, they are constructed to support floors above via a system of trussing. Various materials are used for these structures:

### Timber Partitions
Timber partitions are characterized as being substantial, cheap, and light, though they are not vermin-proof, sound-resisting, or fire-resisting. Consequently, they should not be installed in damp areas, such as the lower floors of buildings. There are two primary types of stud or quarter partitions:
*   **Common Partitions:** These act simply as screens and carry no weight. They weigh approximately 25 lb. per superficial foot (including plaster on both sides) and consist of 4 in. X 2 in. upright studs with 4 in. X 3 in. head and sill. Nogging pieces (4 in. X 2 in.) are placed parallel to the head, usually 4 ft. apart, to prevent bending.
*   **Trussed Partitions:** These are built to support their own weight as well as the weight of floors. They feature larger heads and sills calculated based on weight and clear bearing, and they include braces. Two forms of trussing exist: king post and queen post.

### Other Partition Materials
Other methods of partitioning include:
*   **Bricknogged Partitions:** These follow the common stud partition format but use studs placed 18 or 27 in. apart. These spaces are filled with 4-1/2 in. thick brickwork built in cement. This design is used to prevent the spread of fire, block sound, and remain vermin-proof.
*   **Alternative Fills:** Coke breeze concrete may be used to fill the space between studs instead of brickwork.
*   **Additional Materials:** Other partitions include solid hardwood and deal, 4-1/2 in. brick walls, bricks laid on their sides (creating a 3 in. partition), plaster slab, iron and wire, and patent partitions such as hollow brick or coke breeze concrete.

## Other Partitioned Spaces
The use of partitions for dividing space extends to other historical and institutional settings. In monasteries, the dormitory—the monks' sleeping apartment—was often subdivided into partitions or cells for individual monks. These were generally located on the first floor with direct access to the church. In some Elizabethan mansions, the roof space served as a long gallery that was occasionally used as a dormitory.
