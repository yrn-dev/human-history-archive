# Concrete in Bridge and Structural Engineering

Concrete is utilized in various structural capacities, ranging from foundational support and reinforced arches to specialized military fortifications. Its application varies based on the required strength, the nature of the soil, and the specific engineering goals of the project.

## Concrete Arches and Hinged Construction
In Germany and America, concrete and masonry arches have been constructed with spans reaching up to 150 ft. To simplify calculations and allow engineers to work closely to theoretical dimensions, hinges are sometimes introduced at the crown and springings. These can be two-hinged (at the springings) or three-hinged (at both crown and springings). 

In the Württemberg hinged arches, Leibbrand of Stuttgart utilized lead sheets approximately 1 in. thick over the middle third of the depth of the voussoir joints to create virtual articulations. This construction limits the position of the line of pressures and reduces ambiguity regarding stresses. While unhinged arches at Coblentz and Cologne had a stress limit of 50 to 60 tons per sq. ft., the Württemberg hinged arches allowed a limit of 110 tons per sq. ft.

Specific examples of concrete arch construction include:
* **Rechtenstein:** A bridge featuring two concrete arches with a span of 75½ ft. and lead articulations. The arch is 11 ft. wide, with a depth of 2.1 ft. at the crown and 2.96 ft. at the springing.
* **Cincinnati:** A concrete arch with a 70 ft. span and a 10 ft. rise. This structure is 15 in. thick at the crown and 4 ft. at the abutments. It is reinforced by eleven 9-in. steel-rolled joists spaced 3 ft. apart, supported by cross-channel joists at each springing. The concrete mixture used consists of 1 part cement, 2 parts sand, and 3 to 4 parts broken stone.

## Foundations and Soil Stabilization
Concrete is frequently used in foundations to protect buildings from the effects of meteorological influences on soil, such as clays that shrink when dried and swell when wetted. When used to prevent soil from yielding to pressure, concrete is spread widely and its thickness is determined by its ability to transmit the weight of the wall without being crushed or breaking across. In some instances, concrete walls are used as substitutes for brick; in such cases, the London Building Act requires an additional thickness of one-third.

Various specialized concrete foundation methods include:
* **Concrete in a shell:** This method utilizes the ability of strong hydraulic concrete to set underwater. A wooden or iron shell is used to enclose a space, soil is removed via dredging or mechanical excavation, and concrete is shot into the space from a height of about 10 ft. and rammed in 1 ft. thick layers.
* **Piles and Wells:** Concrete is used for reinforced piles in loose or compressible soil. In the case of "wells," a masonry casing is sunk into running sands and the interior is subsequently filled with brick or concrete.
* **Piers and Abutments:** Some piers consist of cast iron cylinders with diameters of 10 ft. or more that are filled with concrete.

## Integration in Large-Scale Bridges
Concrete is often used as a supplementary material in larger masonry or steel structures. In the Adolphe bridge in Luxemburg (erected 1900-1903), which features a span of 278 ft., the central roadway is carried by a flooring of reinforced concrete. This flooring rests on two masonry arch rings, a design that reduced the total masonry required by one-third.

## Military Applications
Concrete has been employed in military defenses, specifically in the form of "pill-boxes" used by German commanders in Flanders. Because waterlogged soil prevented the construction of deep dug-outs or concrete trenches, these small concrete forts were built instead. Often raised only one or two yards above ground level, these structures featured a wooden or steel framework filled with concrete. With walls at least 3 feet thick, pill-boxes were impregnable to ordinary field artillery.
