# Baily's Account of the Rev. John Flamsteed

## Overview and Significance
Published in 1835, *Baily's Account of the Rev. John Flamsteed* is described as a work of fundamental importance to the scientific history of its era. The publication is notable for including a republication of the British Catalogue.

## Francis Baily
The author, Francis Baily, was a scientist whose work spanned several astronomical and physical disciplines. In the field of astronomy, Baily is recognized for his observations of an annular eclipse of the sun on May 15, 1836, at Inch Bonney in Roxburghshire. His vivid description of "Baily's Beads"—a phenomenon resulting from the inequalities of the moon's limb—initiated the modern series of eclipse-expeditions and drew significant attention to the totality of July 8, 1842, which Baily observed at Pavia.

Additionally, Baily contributed to the study of chronology. In 1811, in his first contribution to the Transactions of the Royal Society, he settled the date of a specific eclipse to September 30, 610 B.C. Sir John Herschel later approved these calculations, noting that Baily was the first to solve this disputed question.

Beyond eclipse work, Baily's scientific contributions included:
*   **Earth Density and Shape:** Between 1838 and 1842, Baily used Henry Cavendish's method to determine the mean density of the earth, establishing an authoritative value of 5.66. He also discussed H. Foster's pendulum-experiments, from which he deduced an ellipticity for the earth of 1/289.
*   **Standards of Length:** In 1843, Baily was entrusted with the reconstruction of the standards of length.
*   **Pendulum Research:** He corrected the length of the seconds-pendulum by introducing a previously neglected element of reduction.

Francis Baily died in London on August 30, 1844.

## The Rev. John Flamsteed and the Greenwich Observatory
The subject of Baily's account, the Rev. John Flamsteed, served as the first astronomer royal. Flamsteed initiated a fundamental scheme of practical amelioration at the Greenwich observatory. This organization differed significantly from the approach adopted at Paris.

The primary purpose of Flamsteed's scheme was to achieve a level of predictive power that would allow the positions of the planets, the moon, and the sun to be assigned for an indefinite future without noticeable error. In the original programme, sidereal inquiries were not a primary focus; instead, stars were utilized merely as points of reference.

## Context of Sidereal Science
The work of astronomers like Flamsteed operated within the complexities of celestial measurement. Because stars are not stationary, they require prolonged and toilsive enquiries to determine their movements. These include:
*   **Precessional Movement:** An apparent movement that must be calculated.
*   **Proper Motions:** Detected by E. Halley in 1718 in a few cases and later found to be universal.
*   **Aberration of Light:** Discovered by James Bradley in 1728, causing an annual shifting of the stars.
*   **Nutation:** The complicating effects of the earth's axis on precession, discovered by Bradley in 1748.

Due to these factors, creating a catalogue of "mean" positions for stars for a specific epoch requires extensive preliminary labor, and these positions must be corrected over time both empirically for proper motion and systematically for precession.
