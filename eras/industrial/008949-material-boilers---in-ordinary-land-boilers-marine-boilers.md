# Material of Boilers

The construction of boilers for land and marine use relies on specific materials selected for their tensile strength and suitability for the particular component's function. These materials vary depending on whether the boiler is a standard land or marine type, a water-tube boiler, or a locomotive boiler.

## Ordinary Land and Marine Boilers
In almost all types of ordinary land and marine boilers, the stays and plates are constructed from mild steel. The specific grade of steel used depends on the component:
*   **Shell plates and long stays:** These typically utilize a quality of steel with a tensile strength between 28 and 32 tons per square inch.
*   **Furnaces, flues, short-screwed stays, and flanged plates:** These components use a softer steel, with a tensile strength ranging from 26 to 30 tons per square inch.

The tubes used in these ordinary land and marine boilers are generally made of lap-welded wrought iron.

## Water-Tube and Locomotive Boilers
Material requirements differ for specialized boiler types. In water-tube boilers designed for naval purposes, seamless steel tubes are invariably employed.

Locomotive boilers utilize a variety of materials across their different sections:
*   **Shells:** Generally constructed of mild steel.
*   **Fire-box plates:** These are made of copper, although steel is used in America.
*   **Fire-box side stays:** These are made of special bronze or copper.
*   **Other stays:** These are made of steel.
*   **Tubes:** These are usually brass, consisting of either 70% copper and 30% zinc, or two parts copper to one part zinc by weight. In some instances, steel or copper tubes are used.
*   **Water tubes:** Where these are utilized, they are made of seamless steel.

## Construction and Joining Methods
A critical process in boiler construction is flanging, which involves bending or turning the edges of a plate to create a union with other plates. This method has replaced the older system of using angle iron rings, which were prone to explosions and grooving due to a lack of elasticity. Flanges turned with a large radius provide the necessary elasticity to withstand temperature changes.

Flanging is used in several areas, including:
*   The front and back plates of marine boilers.
*   The fire-boxes of locomotive boilers.
*   The back end-plates of Cornish and Lancashire boilers.
*   The crowns of vertical boilers.
*   The ends of conical cross-tubes and Adamson seams of furnace flues.

While flanging was historically performed by a skilled anglesmith using heat and hammering, modern boiler shops use hydraulic presses with massive dies to bend entire flanges in a single squeeze for plates 4 to 8 feet in diameter. For marine boiler ends that are too large for this method, a special press is used to bend edges in successive heats, and Adamson seams are rolled using a special machine with revolving rollers.

## Structural Support and Fittings
To maintain structural integrity, various stays are used. Screw stays connect the shell of the boiler to the backs and flat sides of combustion chambers, usually featuring nuts inside the chambers. The tops of these chambers are typically supported by strong girders resting on chamber back plates and tube plates, though some use vertical stays attached to riveted T bars or heavy transverse girder plates. Longitudinal stays, secured by double nuts at each end, support the end plates below the combustion chambers and in the steam space.

Every boiler is equipped with essential accessories:
*   **Pressure and Regulation:** A steam-gauge indicates pressure, a stop-valve regulates steam admission, and one or two safety-valves prevent over-pressurization. In locomotive and marine boilers, these safety-valves are held closed by steel springs, whereas stationary boilers use levers with weights.
*   **Maintenance:** Blow-out cocks are used for emptying the boiler. Access for cleaning and examination is provided via man-holes. These are typically oval-shaped so that the doors can be placed on the inside, allowing steam pressure to help keep them shut. These doors are secured by bolts and "dogs" (cross-bars) on the outside.
*   **Water Management:** A glass water-gauge and three try-cocks are used to monitor water levels. Feed-water is admitted via a check-valve and supplied by an injector, an independent steam-pump, or a pump driven by the main engine.

## Marine-Specific Systems
Marine boilers often utilize evaporators to replace water lost through leakages. These evaporators distill fresh water from sea-water using heat from live steam admitted into copper pipe coils.

Additionally, marine boilers face challenges with oil emulsification in feed-water, which can form a film on heating surfaces and prevent water contact. To combat this, a small quantity of lime solution is sometimes mixed with the water; as the temperature rises, the lime precipitates and aggregates the oil globules so they can be removed via cleaning or blowing out.
