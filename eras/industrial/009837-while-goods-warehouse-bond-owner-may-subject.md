# Bonded Warehouses

A bonded warehouse is a facility established by private enterprise or the state used for the lodgment of goods that are liable to duty. These goods remain in the warehouse until the required duty upon them has been paid.

## History and Development in England
Prior to the implementation of bonded warehouses in England, importers were required to pay duties on imported goods at the time of importation. Alternatively, they could provide the revenue authorities with a bond and security for future payment. This earlier system created several inconveniences:
* Importers often struggled to find sureties.
* Importers were frequently forced to sell goods immediately to raise the necessary funds for the duty, often during periods of low market prices.
* The requirement to pay the duty in a lump sum increased the price of goods due to the interest on the capital needed for payment.
* Competition was hindered because the importation of heavily taxed articles required large amounts of capital.
* There were difficulties in granting an exact equivalent drawback to exporters for goods that had already paid duty.

To address these issues and prevent revenue fraud, Sir Robert Walpole proposed a warehousing system for wine and tobacco in his "excise scheme" of 1733. Although the proposal was initially unpopular, the system was adopted in 1803. An act that year mandated that imported goods be placed in warehouses approved by customs authorities, with importers providing "bonds" for the payment of duties upon the removal of the goods. This practice gave rise to the terms "bonding" or "bonded."

## Legal Framework and Administration
The Customs Consolidation Act of 1853 removed the requirement for giving bonds and established various provisions to secure the payment of customs duties for warehoused goods. These provisions were further detailed in the Customs Consolidation Act 1876, the Revenue Act 1883, and the Customs and Inland Revenue Act 1880. 

Under the act of 1876, these facilities are known as "king's warehouses." They are defined as any place approved by the commissioners of customs or provided by the crown for the deposit of goods and the security of the duties due on them.

In certain instances, goods may be moved to a king's warehouse via a "Bill of Sight." This occurs when an importer in England cannot make a perfect entry of consigned goods due to a lack of information. The goods are provisionally landed, and the importer has three days to provide the necessary particulars. If a perfect entry is not made within three days, the goods are taken to the king's warehouse; if a perfect entry is still not made and duties paid within one month, the goods are sold to cover the payment.

## Operations and Market Preparation
While goods are "in bond" within the warehouse, the owner is permitted to subject them to various processes to make them fit for the market. These processes include:
* Roasting coffee.
* Mixing and repacking tea.
* Bottling, mixing, vatting, and racking of spirits and wines.
* Manufacturing certain types of tobacco.

Specific allowances are granted for leakage, evaporation, and waste that arises from these processes.

## Customs and Shipping Regulations
To prevent frauds on revenue, the "Bill of Victualling" (or Victualling Bill) is used. This is an order from a customs export officer for the drawback or shipment of stores from a bonded warehouse required for passengers and crew on an oversea voyage. The bill is created by the master and countersigned by the collector of customs. 

Articles taken on board are not considered stores unless they appear on the victualling bill. If such stores are relanded anywhere in the United Kingdom without sanction from customs officers, they are forfeited. In such cases, the owner and the master are each liable for a penalty of £100 or treble the value of the stores. When a ship has nothing on board but stores, the victualling bill serves as a certificate of clearance.
