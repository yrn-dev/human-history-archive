# Modern and Historical Bridges

Bridges are categorized primarily by the forces acting upon their principal members: arched bridges utilize compression, suspension bridges utilize tension, and girder bridges employ a balance of both compression and tension. The selection of a bridge type depends on cost, available materials, aesthetic preferences, and the need for permanence. While masonry and concrete are more durable than metal, and metal more durable than timber, steel is the most economical choice for long spans, and is imperative for very long spans.

## Timber Bridges

In England, early railways—specifically the Manchester, Sheffield & Lincolnshire and the Great Western—utilized timber bridges of considerable span, featuring either laminated arches (planks bolted together) or braced trusses. Most of these have since been replaced due to decay at the joints.

In America, large-span timber bridges were common between the late 18th and mid-19th centuries. Notable examples include:
* **The Bellows Falls bridge** (built 1785–1792) over the Connecticut, featuring two spans of 184 ft.
* **The Amoskeag bridge** (built 1792) over the Merrimac at Manchester, N.H., which had six spans of 92 ft.
* **The Colossus bridge** (built 1812) over the Schuylkill, a flat arched truss with a span of 340 ft.

While some of these road bridges lasted ninety years with ordinary repairs, they were not heavily loaded. By 1840, American engineers adopted trusses made chiefly of white pine but reinforced with cast-iron shoes and wrought-iron tension-rods. Examples include the Pratt truss (1844), where ties were inclined and struts vertical, and the Howe truss (1830), which used vertical iron ties with timber chords and a lattice of timber struts. Until 1850, these were generally limited to 150 ft. spans. To accommodate increasing railway loads, the Howe truss was later stiffened with timber arches on each side of each girder, though this composite structure was considered fundamentally defective due to indeterminate loading distribution.

Timber was also used for high piers; the Genesee viaduct (1851–1852) featured timber trestle piers 190 ft. high across 10 spans over a total length of 800 ft. These timber framed structures later served as models for metal trusses after 1850.

## Combination and Specialized Structures

Combination bridges utilize both timber and steel, typically using timber for compression members and steel for tension members. These remain in use on the Pacific coast due to the availability of timber and the distance to steel works. An example is the cantilever bridge at Roseburgh, Oregon, which spans a total distance of 584 ft. between anchor piers. This structure uses timber for the floor, railing, floor beams, and most compression members, while steel is used for the pins and the bottom chord panels and struts next to the river piers. Iron is used for tension members, and cast-iron is used for post shoes and chord blocks.

## Historical and Early Bridges

Early bridge construction varied by era and region. In Rome, the first bridge over the Tiber was the timber Pons Sublicius. Later Roman works included the Pons Fabricius (c. 62 B.C.) and the Pons Cestius (c. 46 B.C.). The Pons Milvius was reconstructed in stone in 109 B.C. with arches ranging from 51 to 79 ft. The Pons Aelius (A.D. 134) had eight arches with a maximum span of 62 ft. Trajan constructed a bridge over the Danube in A.D. 104, possibly 3,000 to 4,000 ft. long, and the bridge of Alcantara in Spain, which was 670 ft. long and built of stone blocks without cement.

In Great Britain, the earliest stone bridges were cyclopean structures on Dartmoor, such as the bridge over the East Dart near Tavistock, which used stone slabs (15 ft. by 6 ft.) and is reputed to have lasted 2,000 years. The bridge at Crowland near Peterborough, mentioned in a 943 charter, is one of England's earliest known stone bridges.

In London, the first bridges over the Thames were timber. The "Old London Bridge," begun in stone around 1176 by Peter of Colechurch, carried timber houses and existed until the early 19th century. Its arches spanned 10 to 33 ft., with a total waterway of 337 ft.

## Materials and Erection

The evolution of bridge materials progressed from timber and masonry in classical and medieval times to brick and concrete. Wrought iron appeared in the late 18th century, often combined with cast iron or timber. By the mid-19th century, wrought iron was used for large girder bridges, such as those at Saltash and over the Menai Strait. From 1880 onward, mild steel superseded wrought iron for spans exceeding 100 ft. due to its superior toughness and tenacity. Recently, ferro-concrete or concrete strengthened with steel bars has been introduced for arched bridges.

The erection of bridges involves several methods:
1. Rolling out from one abutment.
2. Floating and raising to the site.
3. Building member by member.
4. Erection on staging or falsework.

Staging may consist of earth banks, iron staging, or timber piles. For example, the Kuilenburg bridge in Holland (span of 492 ft.) was erected on timber staging that required 81,000 cubic feet of timber and 5 tons of bolts to support a 2,150-ton superstructure.
