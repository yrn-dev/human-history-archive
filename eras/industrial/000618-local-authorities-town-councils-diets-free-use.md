# Local Authorities and Diets

In various historical and geopolitical contexts, local authorities—such as town councils and diets—have exercised diverse levels of administrative, linguistic, and political autonomy. These bodies have ranged from highly autonomous "free towns" and powerful regional diets to structured municipal councils under central government oversight.

## Linguistic Autonomy in the Austrian Empire
In the Austrian Empire, the government demonstrated significant liberality regarding the languages used by local authorities, including diets and town councils, which were free to use the language of their choice. While the "external service" (communication between government offices and private individuals) often used the language of the district for practical reasons in remote areas like Bukovina and Galicia, the 1867 constitution formalized these customs as rights. Article 19 of this constitution recognized the inviolable right of every race to preserve and use its own nationality and language, establishing the equality of all "customary" (*landesueblich*) languages in public life, office, and school.

The practical application of this right often became a point of contention. In Laibach, after the Slovenes won a majority in the town council in 1882, they introduced Illyrian as the official language and updated street names to Illyrian. Similarly, in Prague, the Czech town council removed German street names and passed a by-law forbidding private individuals from installing street name tablets in German. However, the central government maintained power by determining which languages were officially considered "customary."

## The Role of Diets in Poland and Austria
The term "diet" refers to different legislative or consultative bodies depending on the region. In Poland, the Diet held immense power over the King, who could not alter laws, levy taxes, or make war and peace without its consent. The Polish system was characterized by the *liberum veto*, which allowed any single member of the Diet to stop a proposal by stating, "I disapprove." A member could also use this veto to dissolve the Diet entirely, leading to a state of political paralysis.

In the Austrian territories, *Landtage* (diets) were instituted in each territory following experiments in government that began in 1860. These diets were elected every six years through four electoral bodies, or *curiae*: the chambers of commerce, the towns, the rural districts, and the "great proprietors" (owners of estates with pre-1848 feudal privileges). The diets served as the mechanism for choosing members of the Chamber of Deputies for the *Reichsrath* (the imperial parliament).

## Free Towns and Municipal Governance
Historically, some towns operated with extreme independence. In Germany, "free towns" such as Augsburg and Lübeck were practically *imperia in imperio*, ruling their populations without outside interference and possessing the authority to make peace and wage war. These towns formed alliances called *Städtebünde* to defend themselves against secular and ecclesiastical potentates. By 1489, their right to be represented in the imperial diet was formally recognized, and by the peace of Westphalia in 1648, they were constituted as the third college of the diet.

In other regions, municipal governance evolved from loose systems to consolidated corporations. In Birmingham, government originally resided in high and low bailiffs. This evolved into a system of thirteen street commissioners before a 1838 charter vested government in a mayor, sixteen aldermen, and forty-seven councillors. By 1851, an act of parliament abolished all other governing authorities in favor of the town council. By 1889, Birmingham became a city, and by 1897, its chief magistrate was granted the title of lord mayor.

## Administrative Structures in Spain and Denmark
In Spain, the *ayuntamiento* (also known as *cabildo* or chapter in some areas) is the term for the town council and the district under its authority. Historically, the *ayuntamiento* consisted of official members and *regidores* (regulators) chosen from the *hidalgos* (nobles) and *pecheros* (commoners who paid a personal tax). These regulators were overseen by a royal judge known as the *corregidor*.

In Denmark, local administration is divided into eighteen counties (*Amter*), each led by an *Amtmand*. Local affairs are managed by the *Sogneraad* (parish council) and *Amstraad* (county council), institutions established in 1841. Provincial town councils are also elected; for example, the council of Copenhagen is elected by individuals with an income of at least 400 kroner. While burgomasters are generally appointed by the crown, those in Copenhagen are elected by the town council, subject to royal approbation.
