# French Cabinet-Making and Furniture

## Overview of the Cabinet-Maker's Art
The term "cabinet-maker" is used to describe the artificer who constructs furniture. While the manufacture of cabinets in the strict sense has become a very occasional part of this work, the term persists. Historically, cabinets were characterized by being supported on a stand and containing a series of pigeon-holes and drawers. These pieces evolved to meet needs of convenience and became universal among the gentle classes, reflecting the tastes of the peoples who adopted them.

The cabinet is believed to have originated in Italy at the beginning of the 16th century. In its earliest rudimentary form, it was an oblong box—with or without feet—small enough to be placed on a chair or table. These early versions were used primarily for the safeguard of money, precious stones, and jewels. One of the earliest recorded cabinets belonged to Francis I of France, which was described as being tooled with mauresque work and covered in gilt leather.

## French Excellence in Cabinetry
As the Renaissance spread, early rudimentary forms were replaced by more architectural and elaborate designs, transforming the cabinet into a sumptuous household adornment. Countries most deeply influenced by the Renaissance excelled in designing these costly pieces; specifically, the cabinets of France, the Netherlands, and Italy were noted as being monumental and especially rich.

French furniture from various periods demonstrates a high level of craftsmanship and the use of diverse materials:
* **Louis XIV Period:** Works from this era include the ebony armoire designed by Berain and executed by Andre Boulle, which featured ormolu mountings and tortoise-shell panels inlaid with other metals and brass. Additionally, "Boulle" work in the style of Berain was used for pine commodes, featuring marquetry of ivory, mother-of-pearl, ebony, brass, tortoise-shell, and green-stained bone.
* **Louis XV Period:** Examples from the end of this period include writing tables featuring Sevres plaques, ormolu mounts, and Riesener marquetry.
* **17th Century:** French or Dutch ebony carved cabinets from the middle of the 17th century featured interiors decorated with coloured woods and inlaid ivory.

## Ornamental Cases and Bookcases
During the period when English designers such as Sheraton and Chippendale were producing elegant glazed bookcases with fret-work frames and satinwood, French cabinet-makers also achieved high success, particularly with small ornamental cases.

These French pieces utilized a variety of timbers, including rosewood, mahogany, and satinwood, as well as other choicer exotic woods. These cases were frequently mounted with gilded and chased bronze and were often inlaid with marqueterie. In the case of dwarf bookcases, a slab of choice marble was frequently used to finish the top.
