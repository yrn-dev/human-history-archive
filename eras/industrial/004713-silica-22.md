# Silica

Silica is a chemical constituent found in various natural minerals, soils, and waters, and it serves as a critical material in industrial construction and manufacturing due to its refractory properties.

## Industrial and Furnace Applications
Silica is categorized as a refractory or fire-resisting material, meaning it is reserved for parts of a furnace in immediate contact with flame and fuel, such as flues, roofs, fire-places, and arches. It is one of the few substances, along with magnesia and alumina, capable of resisting the intense heat produced by gaseous fuel brought to its burning point at high temperatures, such as in the regenerative furnace of Siemens.

In furnace-building, silica is utilized in several forms:
*   **Dinas Clay:** Found in the Vale of Neath in South Wales as a loose disintegrated sandstone, this material is nearly pure silica (containing at most 2.5% bases). It is crushed, mixed with approximately 1% lime, and fired at very high temperatures to create bricks. These are used specifically for fire arches, roofs, and other areas of reverberatory steel-melting furnaces subjected to intense heat. While infusible under ordinary conditions, they may melt after prolonged exposure without corrosion or fluxing.
*   **Ganister:** A slightly plastic siliceous sand found near Sheffield, derived from the Coal Measures of Yorkshire. It is used for the lining of Bessemer steel converters.
*   **Siliceous Bricks:** When combined with a considerable proportion of ferric oxide, these bricks are fire-resisting in boiler flues and brewers' coppers, provided the heated gas contains a large proportion of unconsumed air.

## Chemical Compounds and Synthetics
The electric furnace has enabled the creation of specialized silica-based compounds. Siloxicon, patented by E.G. Acheson in 1903, is a refractory compound of silicon, oxygen, and carbon formed from silica and carbon. It is applied using a bond such as gas-tar or sodium silicate.

In the production of "Blue Brick," a strong vitreous brick used in engineering, the silica present in the clay combines with metallic iron or blue-green ferrous oxide (reduced from ferric oxide by a smoky atmosphere) to form a fusible ferrous silicate. This "slag" fills the pores of the brick to create an impermeable, vitreous layer.

## Construction Materials
Silica is a primary component in the manufacture of sand-lime bricks, a process patented by Dr. Michaelis of Berlin in the early 1880s. These bricks are made by mixing dry sand with powdered slaked lime (averaging ten parts sand to one part lime) and treating the moulded blocks with high-pressure steam (120 to 150 lb per sq. in.) for ten to fifteen hours. This process creates a bond of hydrated calcium silicate, which makes the sand-lime brick stronger than ordinary mortar-bricks. A typical German sand-lime brick consists of 84% silica.

## Natural Occurrence and Geology
Silica appears in various geological formations and minerals:
*   **Marbles and Limestones:** Silicates in marble derive their silica from infiltrated siliceous deposits or sand. In some cases, silica replaces all carbonic acid during recrystallization, resulting in a hard, flinty calc-silicate rock, such as calc-silicate hornfelses.
*   **Earth and Soil:** Analyses of earth from Nutfield, Surrey, show silica levels varying by type; "Blue Earth" contains 62.81% $\text{SiO}_2$, while "Yellow Earth" contains 59.37%. Other analyses indicate lower silica contents ranging from 44% to 50%.
*   **Minerals:** Silica may be found as an impurity enclosed within other minerals, such as barytes (barium sulphate).

## Presence in Brewing Waters
Silica and alumina are found as constituents in waters used for brewing. For example, Burton Water (used for Pale Ale) contains 0.98 grains per gallon of silica and alumina. Dublin Water (used for Stout) contains 0.26 grains per gallon, and Mild Ale Water contains 0.22 grains per gallon.
