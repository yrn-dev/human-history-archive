# AMERICAN COTTON

## Production and Regional Influence
In the United States, cotton cultivation was a defining feature of the South. The cotton-growing South became a center of political tension due to the "institution of negro slavery," which was specifically upheld by the constitution of the "Confederated States" of America following the secession of states including South Carolina, Mississippi, Florida, Alabama, Georgia, Louisiana, and Texas. This region feared the growing predominance of the anti-slavery North in the House of Representatives and the Senate, leading to dreams of expanding slave states into the West Indies and Mexico, reaching as far as Panama.

Beyond the mainland, attempts were made to cultivate cotton on a large scale in the Bahamas. During the American war of independence, colonists arrived with wealth and slave labour, leading to significant efforts in cotton production. By 1783, at Long Island, nearly 4,000 acres of land were under cultivation with 800 slaves at work. However, this industry was plagued by "bad luck," specifically the red bug, which destroyed crops in 1788 and 1794. Consequently, cotton cultivation in the Bahamas was almost abandoned by the year 1800.

## Global Trade and Commercial Infrastructure
The trade of American cotton involved complex international delivery systems. In Liverpool, "American Cotton Delivery Contract Notes" were used to facilitate the trade of American bales. These contracts specified the net weight in pounds and the price per pound, operating under the rules, bye-laws, and Clearing House regulations of the Liverpool Cotton Association, Limited.

The influence of American cotton varieties also extended to other regions. In the southern Mahratta country and the Dharwar collectorate, American varieties of cotton were introduced to the presidency with "much advantage" as part of efforts to improve local fibers.

## Impact of the American Civil War
The American Civil War had profound economic consequences for international markets due to the stoppage of the cotton supply. In Lancashire, this resulted in a "cotton famine" and an industrial crisis of such great destitution that it threatened to overtax the country's benevolence. Relief efforts were significantly led by Lord Derby, who provided munificent personal subscriptions and stimulated public sympathy through speeches, such as one delivered in Manchester in December 1862.

The termination of the American war also impacted global financial stability. In Bombay, a period of prosperity fueled by the foundation of cotton factories was interrupted between 1866 and 1867 by a financial crisis caused by the fall in the price of cotton.

## Cultural Representations
The imagery of American cotton production has been captured in the arts, notably by the American painter Winslow Homer (1836–1910). Homer, who was with troops at the front during the American Civil War, produced a work titled "The Cotton Pickers." His paintings often featured distinctly American types and scenes, including "negroes in the land of cotton."
