# The Aggregate Banking Power of the United States

The aggregate banking power of the United States experienced significant growth during the late 19th and early 20th centuries. According to the annual report for 1907 produced by the comptroller of the currency, the aggregate banking power of the United States rose from $5,150,000,000 in 1890 to $17,824,800,000 in 1907. During this same period, the banking power of foreign countries increased from $10,835,000,000 to $27,034,200,000. For all reporting countries combined, the increase was from $15,985,000,000 to $44,859,000,000.

## National Banking System Growth and Resources

The national banking system was established primarily to address the financial necessities of the Federal government during the Civil War. Secretary of the Treasury Mr. Chase developed a scheme to create a compulsory market for government bonds by granting special privileges to banks organized under Federal charters; these banks could issue circulating notes only when secured by the deposit of government bonds. This plan was authorized by the act of February 25, 1863, and supplemented by the act of June 3, 1864.

To ensure the predominance of national banks over entrenched state banking systems, a 10% tax was imposed on the face-value of state bank notes in circulation after July 1, 1866. This drove state banks out of the note-issuing business, leading some to convert into national banks and others to continue commercial business under state laws without note-issuing privileges. The system saw rapid growth: in 1864, there were 453 national banks with an aggregate capital of $79,366,950, which grew to 1,014 banks with an aggregate capital of $242,542,982 by 1865.

By the early 20th century, the aggregate resources of all classes of banks in the United States had greatly increased. For national banks specifically, the chief account items showed the following growth between 1897 and 1907:
*   **Capital stock:** Increased from $380,090,778 to $807,178,262.
*   **Surplus and profits:** Increased from $382,436,990 to $924,655,010.
*   **Loans:** Increased from $2,231,013,262 to $6,099,897,535.
*   **Deposits:** Increased from $3,324,254,807 to $8,776,755,207.
*   **Total Resources:** Increased from $4,258,677,065 to $11,168,514,516.

## Evolution of Banking Functions and Institutions

While the privilege of issuing notes was exclusive to national banks, this function diminished in importance as other methods of transferring credit developed. This shift allowed for the growth of private banks, state banks, and trust companies alongside the national system. These institutions did not have note-issue privileges but obtained other privileges that were sometimes greater than those held by national banks.

The United States also developed a highly advanced system of clearing cheques, comparable perhaps only to Great Britain. By the year ending September 30, 1907, aggregate clearings reached $154,662,515,258 across approximately 112 leading cities. A large portion of this business was handled by the New York Clearing-House, which in 1906 accounted for 67.2% of the total clearings in 55 of the larger cities. These volumes fluctuate based on business prosperity and stock-exchange transactions.

## Historical Precedents in Federal Banking

Earlier attempts to stabilize national finances include the Bank of North America, authorized on May 26, 1781, with an initial capital of $400,000. It operated under a charter from the state of Pennsylvania due to doubts regarding the continental congress's power to charter a bank. It served as a stabilizing force for Federation finances, loaning $1,249,975 to Robert Morris, the government superintendent of finance. The Bank of North America continued to operate until it was absorbed into the national banking system in 1863.

The second Bank of the United States was authorized in April 1816 following financial difficulties during the war with Great Britain. This bank had a capital of $35,000,000, with the government owning one-fifth of the stock. The president of the United States appointed five of its twenty-five directors. The legality of this institution was upheld by the Supreme Court in 1819 in *McCulloch v. Maryland*, where Chief Justice Marshall ruled that creating the bank fell within the implied powers of the Federal constitution and that states could not levy taxes on the bank's property or circulating notes.
