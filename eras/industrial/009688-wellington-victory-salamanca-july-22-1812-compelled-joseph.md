# The Reign of King Joseph in Spain

## Accession and Early Challenges
In May 1808, Napoleon called upon his brother, Joseph, to take the crown of Spain. This appointment followed a serious blunder by Napoleon, who had deposed the Bourbon king of Spain to promote Joseph from the crown of the Two Sicilies. Napoleon's intent was to unite the kingdoms of Spain and Portugal, the latter of which he had already conquered.

Despite Joseph's benevolent intentions, which he announced in a proclamation dated June 23, 1808, at Bayonne, reconciliation between the French and the Spanish proved impossible. This was largely due to Napoleon's treatment of the *de facto* king, Ferdinand VII. The Spanish responded with patriotic fury, surrounding a French army at Baylen and compelling its surrender. This event forced Joseph to leave Madrid hastily in August 1808.

## Nature of Joseph's Sovereignty
Although Napoleon reinstated Joseph at the close of 1808, the king's sovereignty remained primarily titular. Joseph was kept in a subordinate position, leading him to offer his abdication on four separate occasions. Napoleon ignored these offers and instead ordered Joseph to govern with more energy.

The king's authority was further eroded between February and May 1810, when Napoleon placed the north-eastern and northern provinces under the command of French generals as military districts. These districts operated virtually independent of Joseph's authority. According to the memoirs of his trusted adviser, Miot de Melito, Joseph aspired to be a constitutional king, but the realities of the Peninsula meant he could only succeed by serving as "the mere instrument of a military power."

By the time Joseph's treasury was exhausted to the last extremity, he had ceased to exist as a monarch in reality, retaining only a semblance of authority as a general over a small portion of the French army.

## Efforts to Secure Authority
In April 1811, Joseph traveled to Paris to either offer his abdication or extort better terms for his position. He returned to Spain with a monthly subsidy of 500,000 francs and the promise that the army of the centre—the smallest of the five French armies—would be under his direct control. During this period, in late 1811, Napoleon united Catalonia to France.

## The Victory at Salamanca
The military situation in the Peninsula was characterized by a slow northward push of French armies by a British army under the command of Sir Arthur Wellesley (later the Duke of Wellington). This British force had previously landed in Portugal, defeated the French at Vimiero, and forced their retirement into Spain.

On July 22, 1812, Wellington achieved a victory at Salamanca. This military defeat compelled King Joseph to leave his capital.
