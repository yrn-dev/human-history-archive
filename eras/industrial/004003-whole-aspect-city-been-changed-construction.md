# Urban Transformation through Construction

The physical and visual character of a city, often referred to as its aspect, can be fundamentally altered through strategic construction, land reclamation, and urban planning. Historical records indicate that such changes occur through the introduction of public infrastructure, the modification of natural landscapes, and the expansion of city limits.

## The Development of Baroda
In the city of Baroda, located on the river Viswamitri and serving as a station on the Bombay & Baroda railway, the overall aspect of the city underwent a complete change. This transformation was driven by the construction of handsome public buildings, the widening of existing streets, and the laying-out of parks. Additionally, the city established an excellent water-supply system sourced from the Ajwa lake. 

The city's infrastructure includes a college and numerous schools, as well as several primary hospitals named after the countess of Dufferin, Sayaji Rao, and Jamnabai (the widow of Khande Rao). The area also contains cantonments under British jurisdiction, which are garrisoned by a native infantry regiment and house a population of 4,000. As of 1901, the total population of Baroda was 103,790.

## The Transformation of Yedo
The evolution of Yedo (modern Tokyo) provides an example of how large-scale environmental engineering can change the aspect of an entire region. When Ieyasu first entered Yedo from Sumpu on August 30, 1590, the area was characterized by a very small castle, a few fishing villages, and barren plains. The geography consisted of the moor of Musashi to the northwest and a forest of reeds marking the littoral of Yedo Bay to the southeast.

To transform the site into an administrative center and capital, Ieyasu utilized labor requisitioned from feudatories to perform massive reclamation and construction projects. These efforts included:
* The leveling of hills.
* The reclamation of swamps.
* The building of embankments.

These works provided the necessary sites for the establishment of shops and stores—with owners arriving from Kyoto, Osaka, and other towns—as well as residences for various barons. The process culminated in the rapid construction of a castle of colossal dimensions, which exceeded the Osaka fortress in both magnificence and magnitude. The scale of this construction was so great that the requirement for materials and labor almost overtaxed the resources of the feudatories.

## Comparative Urban Aspects in Cairo
The impact of construction on a city's aspect can also be seen in the contrast between ancient and modern quarters, as observed in Cairo. The city's eastern parts retain an "Oriental aspect," characterized by busy bazaars and scores of narrow, tortuous streets that mirror the Cairo of medieval times. In these areas, the line of fortifications still marks the eastern limits.

Conversely, since the middle of the 19th century, Cairo has more than doubled in size and population. The newer quarters, situated near the river, have been laid out following the fashion of French cities, creating a distinct visual and structural departure from the medieval eastern districts. The city's overall landscape is further defined by the citadel on a spur of the Mokattam hills, the port of Bulak, the palace and gardens of Shubra, and the Nile river.
