# W. H. W.

In the context of the archive's technical and scientific records, the notation "W" and its variants (such as $W_1, W_2, W_x, W_n, W_g$) are used as primary variables to represent weight and load across the fields of bridge engineering and hydrostatic measurement.

## Bridge Engineering and Load Calculations

In the design of bridges, $W$ is utilized to calculate the weight of main girders and the resulting stresses caused by traveling loads.

### Girder Weight Determination
The weight of main girders ($W_g$) is determined by the weight of the live load ($W_l$) and the weight of the platform ($W_f$). The relationship is expressed as $W_g = (W_l+W_f)k/(1-k)$. In provisional designs where $W_g$ is neglected, the actual bridge members must be increased in section by the ratio $k/(1-k)$.

Empirical relations provided by Waddell allow for the comparison of girder weights per foot run ($w_1, w_2$) for different spans ($l_1, l_2$) and live loads ($p, p'$). For example, the ratio of weights for different spans is $w_2/w_1 = ½ [l_2/l_1+(l_2/l_1)^2]$.

Additionally, a partially rational approximate formula for the weight of main girders ($w_3$) involves the total live load per foot run ($w_1$), the weight of the platform ($w_2$), the span ($l$), the average stress on the gross section of metal ($s$), and the depth of the girder at the center ($d$). This is expressed as $w_3 = (w_1+w_2)l^2/(Cds-l_2)$ or $w_3 = (w_1+w_2)lr/(Cs-lr)$, where $r$ is the ratio of span to depth ($l/d$) and $C$ is a constant based on the girder type. For iron braced parallel or bowstring girders, $C$ varies from 1500 to 1800, while for steel, it ranges from 1200 to 1500.

### Bending Moments and Shear
When a series of concentrated loads ($W_1, W_2, W_3$) travel across a girder at fixed distances, they create varying bending moments and shear. The greatest bending moment at a specific section occurs when a load $W_x$ is at that section and a specific condition regarding the sum of the loads ($W_1$ through $W_n$) is satisfied.

As loads move, the bending moments describe parabolas. For three loads, the middle ordinates of these parabolas are $¼W_1l$, $¼W_2l$, and $¼W_3l$. The total moment under a load is the sum of the intercepts of the triangle sides.

Regarding shear, the greatest shear at a section $C$ may occur when $W_1$ is at $C$. If the loads advance so that $W_2$ is at $C$, the shear increases if $Ra/l+d > W_1$, where $R$ is the resultant of the loads on the bridge and $d$ is the reaction at $B$ due to additional loads.

## Hydrostatic Weighing and Relative Density

In physics and chemistry, $W$ represents the weight of a solid or liquid used to determine relative density or specific gravity.

### Hydrostatic Weighing Method
The principle of hydrostatic weighing involves weighing a solid in air ($W$) and then in water ($W_1$). Because $W_1$ is always less than $W$, the difference ($W - W_1$) represents the weight of the displaced water. The relative density is calculated as $W/(W - W_1)$.

For solids lighter than water, a sinker is used. In this case, if $W$ is the weight of the solid in air, $w$ is the weight of the sinker in water, and $W_1$ is the weight of the solid plus sinker in water, the relative density is $W/(W + w - W_1)$.

### Specific Gravity Bottles and Liquids
To determine the relative density of a solid available in small fragments, a specific gravity bottle is used through three operations:
1. Weighing the solid in air ($W$).
2. Weighing the bottle full of liquid ($W_1$).
3. Weighing the bottle containing the solid and filled with liquid ($W_2$).
The relative density is then $W/(W + W_1 - W_2)$.

For liquids, relative density is found by weighing a glass vessel empty ($w$), filled with the liquid ($W$), and filled with a standard substance ($W_1$). The relative density is the quotient $(W - w)/(W_1 - w)$.

### Accuracy and Corrections
To achieve absolute density ($\Delta(0°/4°)$), corrections must be made for the buoyancy of brass weights, the density of air ($\delta$), and the coefficient of cubical expansion of the body ($\alpha$). The high-accuracy formula is $\Delta(0°/4°) = ([\rho][\alpha]W - [\delta]W_1)/(W - W_1)$, where $[\rho]$ is the density of the water.
