# Victorian London: Life in the Industrial Capital

## Overview
During the reign of Queen Victoria (1837–1901), London evolved into the undisputed center of the global economy and the largest city in the world. This era was defined by a jarring dichotomy: the immense wealth generated by the Industrial Revolution and the British Empire existed alongside unprecedented urban squalor.

The city experienced a demographic explosion, growing from roughly 2 million people in 1840 to over 6 million by the century's end. This rapid urbanization outpaced infrastructure, leading to the infamous "Great Stink" of 1858 and recurring cholera outbreaks. While the middle class flourished in leafy suburbs, the working poor inhabited overcrowded rookeries in the East End, where life was a precarious struggle against disease and malnutrition.

Historians continue to debate the "Standard of Living" during this period. Some argue that real wages rose and consumption increased, while others contend that the decline in environmental quality and the psychological toll of urban overcrowding offset any financial gains.

## Key Facts
*   **The Infrastructure Pivot:** To combat cholera and filth, Joseph Bazalgette engineered a massive intercepting sewer system in the 1860s, which fundamentally changed the city's hygiene and public health.
*   **Economic Hub:** London served as the "clearing house" for the world. The City of London became the global center for finance, insurance (Lloyd's), and shipping.
*   **Social Stratification:** The city was geographically divided by class. The West End became the domain of the aristocracy and bourgeoisie, while the East End became the industrial heartland, housing dockworkers and immigrants.
*   **Transport Revolution:** The transition from horse-drawn omnibuses to the "Tube" and steam trains allowed the city to expand outward, creating the first true commuter culture.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Fog was Chemical:** The famous "pea-soupers" were not natural fog but smog—a mixture of water vapor and sulfur dioxide from millions of coal-burning fireplaces.
2. **The Mudlarks:** Impoverished children and adults, known as "mudlarks," scavenged the banks of the Thames at low tide for scrap metal, coal, or jewelry to sell for food.
3. **The First Census:** The 1841 census was the first to provide a comprehensive look at the industrial population, revealing the staggering density of London's slum districts.
