# Louis Charles Auguste Le Tonnelier, Baron de Breteuil

Louis Charles Auguste Le Tonnelier, Baron de Breteuil (1730–1807), was a French diplomatist. He was born on March 7, 1730, at the chateau of Azay-le-Féron in Indre.

## Diplomatic Career
Breteuil began his high-level diplomatic service at the age of twenty-eight when Louis XV appointed him as ambassador to the elector of Cologne. Two years after this appointment, he was sent to St Petersburg. During his tenure there, he arranged to be temporarily absent from his post during the palace revolution that brought Catherine II to the throne.

In 1769, Breteuil was sent to Stockholm. Following his time there, he represented the French government in several major European capitals, serving at Vienna and Naples, and returning to Vienna again until 1783.

## Domestic Service and Ministry
In 1783, Breteuil was recalled from his diplomatic posts to serve as the minister of the king's household. In this role, he implemented significant reforms regarding the administration of prisons. He maintained a close friendship with Marie Antoinette and held strong influence with both the king and queen, particularly the queen.

This position led to a conflict with Calonne, who demanded Breteuil's dismissal in 1787. Despite this, Breteuil's influence remained intact. Following the dismissal of Necker on July 11, 1789, Breteuil succeeded him as chief minister. However, his new ministry was short-lived, ending three days later with the fall of the Bastille.

## Exile and Later Activities
Following the fall of the Bastille, Breteuil joined the first party of *émigrés* and traveled to Switzerland. In November 1790, while at Soleure, Louis XVI granted him exclusive powers to negotiate with European courts. In this capacity, Breteuil attempted to check the diplomacy of the *émigré* princes, which brought him back into opposition with his old rival, Calonne, who held a prominent position in the councils of the princes.

Breteuil was involved in the arrangements for the flight to Varennes. After that event failed, Louis XVI provided him with instructions intended to restore amicable relations with the princes. Breteuil defended the prerogative of Louis XVI and maintained a distrust of the king's brothers. This intransigent stance toward the princes highlighted the divisions within the royal family to foreign sovereigns. Consequently, foreign powers viewed the comte de Provence as the natural representative of his brother and used the contradictory statements made by negotiators as a pretext for not interfering on behalf of Louis XVI. Because of these positions, Breteuil became the target of violent attacks from the party of the princes.
