# The Railway

The railway serves as a critical system for the transport of merchandise, movable goods, and human beings. In legal terms, particularly under English law, a railway company is classified as a common carrier. This means it holds itself out to the public as ready to carry goods for hire and is subject to specific legal liabilities. A railway company is bound to carry the goods of any person offering payment unless there is a valid reason to refuse, such as the carriage being full. The law requires that charges be reasonable and that no undue preference be given to any specific customer or class of customers. While the carrier generally acts as an insurer for entrusted goods, liability is excepted for losses caused immediately by "the king's enemies" or "the act of God."

## The Uganda Railway
One of the most significant engineering projects in the East African protectorate was the construction of the railway from Mombasa to Victoria Nyanza. A preliminary survey for the line was conducted in 1892, with construction beginning in 1896. The railway is a mountain line featuring gradients of one in fifty and one in sixty, spanning a total length of 584 miles with a metre (3.28 ft.) gauge. 

The route begins at Mombasa, crossing to the mainland via a half-mile bridge. It ascends to the Kikuyu Escarpment, reaching 7,600 ft. above sea level at a distance of 346 miles from the start. From there, it descends through ravines via viaducts to a valley floor of 6,011 ft. before ascending the Mau escarpment to the line's highest point at 8,321 ft. The line eventually reaches Port Florence on the Kavirondo Gulf at an altitude of 3,738 ft.

The British government funded the project at a cost of £5,331,000, averaging approximately £9,500 per mile. The first locomotive arrived at Victoria Nyanza on December 26, 1901, and the permanent way was largely completed by March 1903, coinciding with the resignation of the engineer in charge, Sir George Whitehouse.

The railway had profound socio-economic impacts on the region. By eliminating the need for the carriage of goods by men, the railway dealt the final death-blow to the slave trade in that part of East Africa. Furthermore, it enabled the development and occupation of Uganda; previously, this had been nearly impossible due to the prohibitive cost of transporting goods from the coast, which stood at £60 per ton.

## Military and Specialized Railway Use
Railways are frequently utilized for military logistics and medical transport. In wartime contexts, specialized trains are employed for various functions:

*   **Troop and Fatigue Trains:** These may include carriages fitted as repair workshops equipped with grindstones, carpenter's benches, and tools such as saws, axes, and hammers. Other carriages may transport entrenching tools, including pickaxes, spades, and crow-bars.
*   **Ambulance Trains:** These trains transport wounded soldiers. In some instances, wounded men lie on blankets over thick layers of straw. While a standard goods wagon in a troop train can hold forty men or eight horses, it can accommodate a maximum of thirty-six wounded. Seriously injured patients are transferred from these trains to large ambulance motor-cars for transport to city hospitals.
*   **Logistics and Guarding:** Railway troops and Landsturm men often guard lines and stations. Engineers are tasked with repairing infrastructure, such as stone road-bridges that may be blown up to block the line.

## Railway Carriages and Transport
The term "carriage" broadly refers to any vehicular structure used for transporting goods or people. Within this category, railway carriages represent a high end of the spectrum, ranging from basic transport to luxuriously appointed sleeping-cars. 

The development of railways and the expansion of commerce led to an increased demand for carriages. This shift occurred alongside a decline in the pageantry of horse-drawn full-dress carriages, which had reached a peak of artistic excellence and splendour during the early reign of Queen Victoria.

## Regional Examples
Beyond the major lines in East Africa, railways serve as vital links in other regions. In Asia Minor, for example, the town of Buldur (also known as Burdur or Polydorion) is connected to Dineir and Egirdir by a railway, complementing the existing carriage roads used for transporting grain from the Buldur plain.
