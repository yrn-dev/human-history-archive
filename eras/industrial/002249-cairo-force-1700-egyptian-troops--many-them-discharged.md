# Cairo

## Foundation and Early Development
Cairo, originally named El-Kahira (meaning "the Victorious"), was founded in A.D. 968 by Jauhar (Gohar) el-Kaid, who acted as the conqueror of Egypt for the Fatimite caliph El-Moizz. Like the city founded by Amr, Cairo began as the camp of the conqueror. Initially, the city occupied the north-eastern fourth of the present metropolis. Over time, it grew to surpass El-Fostat in size and adopted the name Misr, or Masr, which is the name used for it by modern Egyptians. As Cairo expanded, it eventually included the site of El-Katai to the south, while Fostat declined.

## Medieval History and Expansion
In A.D. 1176, Cairo was the target of an unsuccessful attack by the Crusaders. Following this event, Saladin constructed a citadel on the lowest point of the mountains to the east, overlooking El-Katai. Saladin also established walls around the towns and large gardens within the area now known as Cairo. During the subsequent prosperous rule of the Mameluke sultans, the region was filled with habitations. This period of growth saw the founding of the town of Bulak and the addition of the Hoseynia, a large suburb to the north.

## Foreign Occupations and Political Shifts
Following the Turkish conquest in A.D. 1517, the metropolis experienced decay, although its limits remained the same. In 1798, the city was captured by the French. They remained until 1801, when they were driven out by English and Turkish forces, and the city was handed over to the Turks.

In 1811, Mehemet Ali, who was originally the Turkish viceroy, seized mastery of the country through a massacre of the Mamelukes in a narrow street leading to the citadel. Under Mehemet Ali and his successors, the western part of the city grew. Later, the khedive Ismail destroyed many of the old town's finest houses to create a straight road from the citadel to the Ezbekia gardens. In 1882, Cairo was occupied by the British, and British troops continued to garrison the citadel. Additionally, in September 1882, following the fight at Tel-el-Kebir, English cavalry marched on Cairo, using Bilbeis as their first halting-place.

## Geography and Regional Context
Cairo serves as a central reference point for the geography of the region. For example, the town of Dongola, located on the west bank of the Nile in the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, is situated 1082 meters south of Cairo by river. In lower Egypt, the town of Bilbeis is located 36 miles north-north-east of Cairo by rail. Additionally, the "Cape to Cairo" railway connects the city to southern regions, passing through administration seats such as Kalomo.
