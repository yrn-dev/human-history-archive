# Events and Figures of the Mid-to-Late 18th Century

## Architecture and Urban Development in Buda
The royal palace in Buda serves as the nucleus of the town, situated on the summit of a hill alongside the old fortress. This palace was erected by Maria Theresa between 1748 and 1771. Although it was partly burned in 1849, the structure was restored and underwent large extensions starting in 1894. The palace is surrounded by a magnificent garden featuring steep terraces that descend to the Danube, providing a view of the town on the opposite bank. 

Within the court chapel of the palace, the regalia of Hungary are preserved, including the sword, orb, sceptre, coronation robes, and the crown of St Stephen. The surrounding area of the town contains several other principal edifices, including residences of Hungarian magnates, big hospitals, high and middle schools, and palatial buildings housing various ministries.

## Literary and Artistic Figures
Several notable writers and poets were active during this period. Jean François Marmontel (1723–1799), a French writer born in Cantal, moved to Paris in 1745. Between 1748 and 1753, he wrote a series of tragedies, including *Denys le Tyran* (1748), *Aristomène* (1749), *Cléopâtre* (1750), *Héraclides* (1752), and *Egyptus* (1753). While these were only moderately successful on stage, they allowed him entry into fashionable and literary circles. Marmontel also wrote comic operas, such as *Sylvain* (1770) and *Zémire et Azore* (1771), and contributed a series of articles to the *Encyclopédie* titled *Éléments de Littérature*.

In Germany, the poet Gottfried August Bürger (1748–1794) was born in Molmerswende. After studying theology and later jurisprudence at the university of Halle—where he was influenced by C.A. Klotz (1738–1771)—Bürger eventually entered the university of Göttingen as a law student. By 1771, he had become widely known as a poet, with his first poems appearing in the *Göttingen Musenalmanach*.

## Military and Religious Developments
In the realm of military literature, various instructional works were produced for soldiers and officers. These included a *règlement* for the infantry (1737) and *Des G. F. M. Grafen v. Khevenhüller Observationspunkte für sein Dragoner-regiment*, published in 1734 and 1748. Additionally, the work *Kurzer Begriff aller militärischen Operationen* was published in Vienna in 1756, with a French version titled *Maximes de guerre* appearing in Paris in 1771.

Regarding religious movements, Francis Asbury (1745–1816) was an American clergyman and local preacher who became a regular itinerant minister under John Wesley in 1767. In 1771, Asbury volunteered for missionary work in the American colonies, landing in Philadelphia in October of that year. At the time of his arrival, there were fewer than 300 converts to Methodism in the colonies. Asbury's efforts led to the membership of several congregations more than doubling within a year.

## Political and Regional History
In Belgium, the period following the rule of Archduchess Mary Elizabeth (1725–1741) saw the appointment of Charles of Lorraine as governor-general. During the Austrian War of Succession, the region was conquered by the French, and Marshal Saxe served as governor-general for two years. However, the territory was restored to Austria via the peace of Aix-la-Chapelle in 1748. Under the subsequent long peace and the administration of Charles of Lorraine, Belgium experienced prosperity, the expansion of the canal system, and the founding of the Academy of Science.

In the East Indies, the town of Banjermasin on the island of Borneo served as a trade center for gold, diamonds, iron, weapons, wax, rattans, and benzoin. While the East Indian Company established a factory there in 1700, the settlement was eventually abandoned due to health issues and attacks by natives. The town of Martapura became the seat of the sultan in 1771.
