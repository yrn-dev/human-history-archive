# North American Birds

The study and documentation of North American birds in the 19th century involved a combination of professional naturalist research, government-funded expeditions, and popular nature writing. This period saw the production of detailed catalogs of species, guides to nesting habits, and literary essays on the animals environing human life.

## Ornithological Research and Publications
Significant contributions to the study of North American birds were made by naturalists such as Spencer Fullerton Baird (1823–1887). Baird, an American naturalist and former secretary of the Smithsonian Institution, focused heavily on ornithology after meeting J. J. Audubon in 1838, from whom he received a portion of Audubon's own bird collection. Baird's work included the volume *Birds*, which was part of a series of reports regarding explorations and surveys for a railway route from the Mississippi. Between 1850 and 1860, Baird also provided general superintendence for several government expeditions for the scientific exploration of the western territories of the United States, for which he prepared a manual titled *Instructions to Collectors*.

Other specialized publications from this era focused on the habits of birds. Ernest Ingersoll published *Nests and Eggs of North American Birds* between 1880 and 1881.

## Classification of Species
The classification of North American birds was a subject of debate among ornithologists. A notable example is the *T. sparverius*, commonly referred to in the United States and Canada as the "sparrow-hawk." In his work *North American Birds*, R. Ridgway identified three species: *T. sparverius*, *T. leucophrys*, and *T. sparverioides*. 

Ridgway further divided *T. sparverius* into five geographical races:
* The typical *T. sparverius* found on the North American continent, excluding the coast of the Gulf of Mexico.
* *T. isabellinus*, which inhabits continental America from Florida to French Guiana.
* *T. dominicensis*, found in the Lesser Antilles as far north as St Thomas.
* *T. australis*, located on the South American continent, excluding the Caribbean and North Atlantic coasts.
* *T. cinnamominus*, found in western Brazil and Chile.

While Ridgway recognized these distinctions, other ornithologists, such as Bowdler Sharpe in his *Catalogue*, recognized six species, and the majority of ornithologists found themselves unable to accept the distinctions elaborated by both Sharpe and Ridgway. Later findings in 1881 suggested that *T. australis* and *T. cinnamominus* could not be separated, and that Ridgway's *T. leucophrys* should be termed *T. dominicensis*.

## Popular Nature Writing
Parallel to scientific classification was the rise of popular essays on birds and rural scenes. John Burroughs (1837–), an American poet and writer on natural history, began a series of books in 1871 starting with *Wake-Robin*. Burroughs is regarded as the successor to Thoreau as a popular essayist regarding the animals and plants surrounding human life. Among his chief prose works on this theme is *Birds and Poets* (1877). He also published a volume of poems titled *Bird and Bough* in 1906.
