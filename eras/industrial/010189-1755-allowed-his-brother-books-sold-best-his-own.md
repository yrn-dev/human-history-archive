# Book Collecting and the Evolution of Public Libraries

The practice of book-collecting emerged not as a separate pursuit, but as an added grace in the formation of fine libraries. Between the 16th and 18th centuries, large-scale book buyers typically operated with a public objective, and their zeal was often rewarded by their collections being designated for public resting-places.

## Bequests and Institutional Growth
During this era, numerous prominent figures bequeathed their libraries to educational and public institutions. Sir Thomas Smith (d. 1577) left his books to Queens' College, Cambridge, while Archbishop Williams' library went to St John's College, Cambridge. Archbishop Parker left his collection to Corpus Christi College, though it was subject to severe restrictions. In Oxford, Sir Thomas Bodley refounded the university library during his lifetime, a collection further augmented by liberal contributions from Laud and a bequest from Selden.

Other notable contributions include the library of Archbishop Usher, which was purchased for Trinity College, Dublin, and the founding of a library in Dublin by Archbishop Marsh, who had previously acquired the printed books of Stillingfleet. In the scientific realm, the grandson of Thomas Howard, earl of Norfolk (d. 1646), gave Howard's mathematical and scientific books to the Royal Society. Additionally, the heraldic collections of Ralph Sheldon (d. 1684) were given to Heralds' College, and the library of Pepys was left to Magdalene College, Cambridge. George I also contributed to the university library at Cambridge by purchasing and presenting the books of Bishop Moore, which included a volume of Caxton quartos that were almost all unique.

## Transition to Public Ownership
By the 18th century, there was a strong trend toward public ownership of private collections. While some collections were dispersed—such as the immense accumulations of Thomas Rawlinson (d. 1725), which were sold via a series of auctions, and the printed books of Harley, which were sold to the bookseller Osbourne—others were preserved for the nation. Richard Rawlinson (d. 1755) allowed the books of his brother to be sold, but he bequeathed the best of his own books to Oxford. Similarly, the Harleian MSS. were offered to the nation at a price significantly below their actual value.

The foundation of the British Museum in 1753 was the result of two primary factors: the need for better care of the remaining Cotton manuscripts (which had been vested in trustees for public use in 1702 and partially destroyed by fire in 1731) and a similar offer of the great collections of Sir Hans Sloane, which included approximately 50,000 printed books. The museum opened in 1757.

## 18th Century Book Production and Trade
The 18th century saw notable improvements in presswork, type, and paper in England and France, with Germany and Italy following toward the end of the century. Books became more elegant and neat. Illustration experienced a revival through French *livres-a-vignettes* and the work of French artists like Gravelot in English books. In the final quarter of the century, woodcut illustrations—then called wood engravings—were revived through the work of Bewick.

Binding techniques also evolved. While the best binders of the 18th century were considered inferior to those of the 17th (until the arrival of Roger Payne), the average quality of work improved. Trade bindings shifted away from calf and sheepskin toward paper boards.

The economics of publishing varied by genre:
* **Poetry:** Following the lucrative example of Dryden, Pope and Prior published poetry by subscription at high prices, though Pope also sold single poems for 1s. and 1s. 6d.
* **Novels:** These were typically published in several volumes. Prices remained steady for many years but rose toward the end of the century. For example, Miss Burney's *Evelina* (3 vols., 1778) sold for 7s. 5d., *Cecilia* (5 vols., 1782) for 12s. 6d., and *Camilla* (5 vols., 1796) for L1:1s.
* **Reference Works:** Johnson's *Dictionary* (2 vols. folio, 1755) cost L4:4s. in sheets and L4:15s. in boards.
