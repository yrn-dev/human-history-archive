# The Ashanti Empire

## Origins and Early Expansion
The Ashanti first became known to Europeans in the early 18th century, following a series of successful wars against kingdoms that bordered maritime territory. Osai Tutu is regarded as the real founder of Ashanti power. During his reign, he either built or significantly expanded the city of Kumasi. His military campaigns included the subduing of the neighboring state of Denkera in 1719, as well as the conquest of Banna and the Mahommedan countries of Gaman (Jaman). Through these conquests, he extended the empire's reach to both the east and the west. Osai Tutu was eventually defeated and slain in 1731, after which his successor, Osai Apoko, continued to make acquisitions toward the coast.

## The Reign of Osai Tutu Quamina
In 1800, Osai Tutu Quamina ascended to the throne. Described as an ambitious and enterprising leader, he sought to establish communications with white nations. In 1807, a conflict arose when two chiefs of the Assin, whom Osai Tutu Quamina had defeated in battle, sought refuge with the Fanti, the ruling people on the coast. When the Fanti refused to surrender the fugitives, Osai Tutu Quamina invaded their country, defeated them, and drove them toward the sea.

The Ashanti forces reached the coast near Anamabo, where a British fort was located. Despite mediation efforts by the governor, the townsmen refused to come to terms, resulting in the destruction of the town and the slaughter of 8,000 inhabitants. The Ashanti suffered losses of over 2,000 men and were unable to storm the English fort, though the fort's garrison was reduced from twenty-four men to eight.

A truce was eventually established. The king refused to negotiate with anyone other than the governor of Cape Coast. Colonel G. Torrane, the governor from 1805 to 1807, traveled to Anamabo and concluded an agreement with the Ashanti. This agreement acknowledged the Ashanti conquest of Fantiland and involved the surrender of half the fugitives in the Anamabo fort; one Assin chief escaped, while the other was handed over and executed by the Ashanti. Additionally, the governor agreed to pay rent to the Ashanti for Cape Coast castle and Anamabo fort.

## British Relations and the Battle of Essamako
The British government, recognizing the power of the African kingdom, sought to cultivate intercourse with it. In January 1819, Mr. Joseph Dupuis was sent as consul to Kumasi, arriving on February 28, 1820. Following meetings with the king, a treaty was drafted that acknowledged Ashanti sovereignty over Fanti territory and left the natives of Cape Coast to the mercy of their enemies. However, this treaty was disowned by the governor of Cape Coast, Mr. J. Hope Smith, and the government in London.

In 1821, the British government assumed direct control of its settlements. The first crown-appointed governor, Sir Charles M'Carthy, supported the Fanti. On January 21, 1824, at a place called Essamako beyond the Prah, the Ashanti defeated Sir Charles in battle. The Ashanti force consisted of 10,000 men against Sir Charles's 500. Sir Charles and eight other Europeans were killed; the governor's skull was later used as a royal drinking-cup in Kumasi. It was alleged that the defeat was hastened because the ordnance-keeper brought kegs of vermicelli instead of ammunition.

## Succession and Decline of Coastal Power
Osai Tutu Quamina died on the same day as the battle of Essamako in 1824 and was succeeded by Osai Okoto. His succession was followed by a period of chronic warfare. This continued until August 7, 1826, when the Ashanti suffered a significant defeat at Dodowah. Following this event, Ashanti power over the coastal tribes began to wane. By 1831, the king was forced to purchase peace from the administrator of the Gold Coast, Mr. George Maclean, paying 600 oz. of gold and sending his son to Cape Coast as a hostage. The payment of ground rent for British-held forts also ceased after the battle of Dodowah.

## Later Monarchs and Conflict
Osai Okoto was succeeded in 1838 by Kwaka Dua I, a peace-loving monarch who encouraged trade. Despite this, the Ashanti attempted to reassert authority over the Fanti in the Gold Coast protectorate in 1852. In 1863, war broke out again after the king demanded the surrender of a runaway slave-boy and a fugitive chief, which the British refused. The Ashanti won two battles and retired. During this time, Kwaka Dua noted that while "the white men" brought cannons to the bush, "the bush is stronger than the cannon."

Following the death of Kwaka Dua in April 1867 and a subsequent period of civil war, Kofi Karikari was enstooled. Upon his accession, he declared, "My business shall be war." This led to preparations for an attack on the Fanti tribes, resulting in the war of 1873-74.
