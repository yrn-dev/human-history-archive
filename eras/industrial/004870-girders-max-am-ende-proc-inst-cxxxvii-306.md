# Girders and Bridge Engineering

The design and implementation of girders in bridge construction involve various systems to ensure stiffness, manage load distribution, and resist deformation. These structures range from suspension and cantilever bridges to bascule systems, utilizing a variety of materials and mathematical models to determine strength and weight.

## Stiffness and Suspension Systems
To achieve greater stiffness in bridges, several engineering plans have been adopted. In the Ordish system, oblique chains support girders at intermediate points of the span; a curved chain is used to support these oblique chains and maintain their straightness, as seen in the 1868 bridge at Prague. Another approach, used in an 1860 bridge over the Danube canal in Vienna with a 264 ft. span, employed two parallel chains on each side, positioned 4 ft. apart and connected by bracing to form a stiff inverted arch. Although this bridge carried a railway, it was dismantled in 1884 due to calculation errors.

By approximately 1850, it was recognized that combining supporting chains with suspended stiffening girders could create a bridge capable of carrying railway trains. W. J. M. Rankine demonstrated that the required strength for such a stiffening girder is only one-seventh of that required for an independent girder of the same span carrying the same moving load, excluding the girder's dead weight.

## Cantilever and Large-Scale Bridges
Cantilever bridges are often erected by building out from the abutments, using the completed sections as erecting stages for lifting appliances. Notable examples include:

*   **The Forth Bridge:** Completed in 1889 after a previous stiffened suspension design was abandoned following the 1879 Tay bridge fall. This 5,330 ft. cantilever bridge features two main spans of 1,710 ft. each and utilizes 38,000 tons of steel. It includes a central tower on Inchgarvie (260 ft.), piers at Fife and Queensferry (145 ft.), and six cantilevers of 680 ft. each.
*   **The Lansdowne Bridge:** Completed in 1889 over the Indus at Sukkur, this bridge has a clear span of 790 ft. and a suspended girder 200 ft. in length. Designed by Sir A. Rendel, it supports a single railway line of 5 ft. 6 in. gauge. The back guys are the most strained components, with a provided stress of 1,200 tons.
*   **The Niagara Bridge:** Completed in 1883 for two railway lines, featuring a clear span of 495 ft. between towers and a total length of 910 ft.
*   **The Red Rock Bridge:** A cantilever bridge over the Colorado river with a center span of 660 ft.

## Bascule and Specialized Girders
The Tower bridge utilizes a bascule system where each bascule contains four main girders. These project 100 ft. beyond and 62 ft. 6 in. within the face of the piers, with transverse girders and bracings inserted every 12 ft. The floor consists of buckled plates paved with wood blocks. The system rotates on a forged steel axis 21 in. in diameter and 48 ft. long, featuring eight bearings of live rollers. Each leaf, including 365 tons of ballast, weighs approximately 1,070 tons.

## Structural Analysis and Design
### Bracing and Stress
Three primary types of web bracing developed early in engineering:
1.  **Warren type:** Bracing bars form equilateral triangles.
2.  **Whipple Murphy:** Vertical struts with inclined ties.
3.  **Lattice:** Both struts and ties are inclined, typically at 45° to the horizontal.

Regarding material strength, the method of J.J. Weyrauch and W. Launhardt utilizes Wöhler's law. This involves three types of breaking strength: *statical breaking strength* (t), *primitive strength* (u) for bars loaded and unloaded indefinitely, and *vibration strength* (s) for bars subjected to equal and opposite stresses.

### Weight and Shear Calculations
Empirical relations provided by Waddell and Unwin are used to calculate the weight of main girders. Unwin's formula for the weight of main girders ($w_3$) considers the total live load per ft. run ($w_1$), the weight of the platform ($w_2$), the span ($l$), average stress ($s$), and the ratio of span to depth ($r$).

Shear calculations often involve "influence lines" to determine the effect of unit loads at various positions on the girder. The greatest shear typically occurs when the longer segment divided by the point of consideration is fully loaded while the other is unloaded. The distribution of shear is significantly altered if the load is carried by a rail girder that distributes weight to cross girders.
