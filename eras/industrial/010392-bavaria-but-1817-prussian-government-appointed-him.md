# Bavaria

Bavaria is a German state with a history involving various political transitions, territorial shifts, and contributions to the broader German empire. Its historical trajectory includes early Carolingian rule, conflicts with Prussia, and participation in the development of national cartography.

## Early Governance and the Carolingian Period
In 817, the emperor Louis I divided his dominions among his sons. Louis, surnamed the "German," received Bavaria and neighboring lands, though he did not begin governing until 825. During this period, Louis became involved in wars with Slavonic tribes on his eastern frontier. In 827, he married Emma, the daughter of Welf I, who was the count of Bavaria. 

Following the death of the elder Louis in 840 and subsequent conflicts with his brother Lothair, the treaty of Verdun was concluded in August 843. Under this treaty, Louis received the majority of the Carolingian empire's lands east of the Rhine, including Bavaria, Saxony, Franconia, and Thuringia. Louis established Regensburg as the center of his government.

## Territorial Changes and Political Figures
The region's territories have shifted over centuries through various ecclesiastical and political arrangements. The town of Kissingen, for example, was owned by the counts of Henneberg until 1394, when it was sold to the bishop of Würzburg; it later passed to Bavaria through that bishopric.

In the early 19th century, the grand-duchy of Frankfort was created for Karl Theodor Anton Maria von Dalberg. While Dalberg's territories were augmented, this process involved the cession of Regensburg to Bavaria. By the time of Dalberg's death on February 10, 1817, he held only the archbishopric of Regensburg.

## Conflict with Prussia
The 19th century was marked by significant friction between Bavaria and the rising power of Prussia. In 1866, the Prussians defeated the Bavarians in a battle near Kissingen on July 10, resulting in great slaughter. Following the Prussian victory over Austria at the battle of Sadow in the same year, Prussia established the North German Confederation. Bavaria, along with Austria, Baden, Hesse, and Württemberg, was excluded from this confederation.

## Notable Figures and Intellectual History
Bavaria was the birthplace of Martin Boos (1762–1825), a German Roman Catholic theologian born in Huttenried. After being raised in Augsburg and studying at the university of Dillingen, Boos served as a priest in several Bavarian towns. He later moved to Austria, where he developed a pietistic movement that attracted some fifty or sixty priests and many Catholic laity. After facing persecution and returning to Bavaria, Boos was appointed to a professorship at Dusseldorf by the Prussian government in 1817, and later received a pastorate at Sayn near Neuwied in 1819.

## Cartography and Surveying
By the middle of the 19th century, topographical maps of the German states were largely complete. In 1878, Bavaria entered into an agreement with the governments of Württemberg, Saxony, and Prussia to replace local maps with a unified map of the empire known as the *Reichskarte*. This project consisted of 674 sheets on a scale of 1:100,000 and was completed in 1909. 

Additionally, Bavaria utilized large-scale maps on a scale of 1:25,000, which were referred to in Bavaria as *Positionsblätter* (known elsewhere as *Messtischblätter*). These maps served as the foundation for geological surveys conducted across several German states.
