# Language Ordinances in Bohemia and Moravia (1886)

In the late 19th century, Bohemia and Moravia were the sites of a vehement contest between two nearly equal races. During this period, there was a constant encroachment by the Czech language upon the German language, a process driven in part by the direct actions of the government.

## The Ordinance of 1880
The shift toward the official recognition of the Czech language began with a government ordinance in 1880. This decree determined that all business brought before any law court or government office would be dealt with within that office using the language in which the business was originally introduced. This ordinance applied to the entirety of Moravia and Bohemia, effectively granting the Czech language a formal position within the government service.

## The Order of 1886
Further steps were taken to integrate the Czech language into the legal system in 1886. In that year, an order was issued stating that, "to avoid frequent translations," any business introduced in Czech should be dealt with in that same language within the high courts of Bruenn and Prague.

## Educational Developments
Parallel to these legal changes, the Czech language gained ground through the expansion of educational institutions. A large number of Czech elementary schools were established, and many middle schools were provided to the Czechs. Additionally, Czech classes were introduced into German schools.

A significant point of contention occurred in 1882, when classes in Czech were started at the university of Prague. This development was viewed by Germans as a desecration of what they considered the oldest German university.

## Social and Political Impact
The victory of the Czech language in these administrative and educational spheres carried significant social implications. Because the two races were nearly equal in number, the ascendancy of Czech meant that nearly two million Germans would be placed in a position of subordination.
