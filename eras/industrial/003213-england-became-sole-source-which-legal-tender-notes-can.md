# The Bank of England and Legal Tender Notes

## Establishment and Role of the Bank of England
The Bank of England was originally established to provide the government with a loan of L1,200,000. From the date of its foundation, the bank has served as the banker of the government and has been entrusted with the management of the British national debt. This role includes the registration of all stock transfers and the payment of interest on the stock in which the debt is inscribed, a process that was originally half-yearly but is now quarterly.

The bank's pre-eminence was established early through several key privileges. Until 1826, it held a monopoly as the only joint-stock bank permitted to exist in England and Wales. Additionally, until 1862, it was the only English bank whose shareholders had liability limited to the amount of their holdings. The Bank of England also serves as the banker for other banks within the country and, for many years, maintained control over deposits larger than those of any other individual bank. Currently, stocks inscribed in the bank's books exceed 1250 millions sterling.

## Note Issuance and Legal Tender
From its inception, the Bank of England possessed a practical, though perhaps not absolutely legal, monopoly on issuing notes in London, eventually becoming surrounded by a circle of private banks. Since 1833, the notes issued by the Bank of England have been legal tender throughout England and Wales, with the sole exception of the bank itself.

The Bank Charter Act of 1844 further regulated the note-issue of the country. Under this act, the Bank of England became the sole source from which legal tender notes can be obtained. This position is particularly significant during times of financial pressure. In such instances, the bank becomes the center of interest because a "treasury letter" is required to permit an excess issue of notes when public demand exceeds the limits fixed by the 1844 act. Between 1844 and 1906, the government granted the authority to supply these additional notes on only three occasions at the request of the bank.

## Legal Framework of Notes
In general legal terms, a bank note may be viewed as a promissory note made by a banker and payable to the bearer on demand. A promissory note is defined as an unconditional written promise, signed by the maker, to pay a certain sum of money to a specified person or bearer at a fixed or determinable future time, or on demand. While a bill of exchange is an order to pay, a promissory note is a direct promise to pay.

Bank notes are subject to special stamp regulations and differ from other promissory notes in that they are not discharged by payment; instead, they may be re-issued repeatedly. To protect the currency, the issuance of bank notes is governed by statutory restrictions. Specifically, no bank other than the Bank of England may issue notes in England unless that bank had a lawful note issue in place in 1844.

## Comparative Currency Systems
The system of legal tender in England differs from that of other regions. For example, in certain other jurisdictions, the only legal tenders are the Mexican dollar and the British and Hong-Kong dollar (or other authorized silver dollars of equivalent value). In those areas, small silver and copper coins serve as legal tender for amounts not exceeding two dollars and one dollar, respectively, while paper currency is issued by entities such as the National Bank of China, Limited, the Hong-Kong and Shanghai Banking Corporation, and the Chartered Bank of India, Australia and China.

Similarly, the United States utilized "greenbacks," which were treasury notes first issued in 1862 to fund the Civil War. These were a form of fiat money and inconvertible paper currency because the government had suspended specie payment. These notes were made legal tender through acts in 1862 and 1863, though they depreciated in value to as low as 35 cents on the dollar. Specie payment in the United States was resumed on January 1, 1879.
