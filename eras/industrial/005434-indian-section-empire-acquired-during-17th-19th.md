# The Indian Empire

The Indian empire constitutes one of the three primary sections of the British empire, alongside the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland (including the Isle of Man and the Channel Islands) and the colonial empire. The Indian section is composed of British India and various feudatory native states.

## Acquisition and Governance
The acquisition of the Indian section of the empire took place during the 17th through the 19th centuries. This process began with a royal charter granted to the East India Company by Queen Elizabeth in 1600. Trade served as the most potent bond of union and the most active cause of expansion during the development of the empire; specifically, a trading company won India. By 1815, the East India Company ruled approximately one-third of the Indian peninsula, and it was noted at that time that only the East India Company displayed a spirit of expansion.

Control of the region was transferred from the East India Company to the imperial government in 1858. Subsequently, under the Royal Titles Act in 1877, Queen Victoria was proclaimed empress.

## Territorial Development
The main divisions of the Indian empire generally grew to their present dimensions through a general process of extension. The acquisition of specific centers occurred through various methods, including treaties and conquest:

*   **Madras**: The foundation of Madras at Fort St George represented the first territorial possession of the East India Company in India. It was acquired via a treaty with its Indian ruler in 1639. Madras was established as a presidency in 1683, ceded to France in 1746, and recovered in 1748.
*   **Bombay**: Trade was first established in 1608. The territory was ceded to the British crown by Portugal in 1661, then transferred to the East India Company in 1668. The presidency was later removed from Surat in 1687.

## Role of Trade and Naval Power
The foundations of the British empire were laid upon trade with tropical regions and settlement in temperate regions. The imperial navy provided protection for colonial commerce, serving as one of the strongest material links connecting the crown to its outlying possessions.
