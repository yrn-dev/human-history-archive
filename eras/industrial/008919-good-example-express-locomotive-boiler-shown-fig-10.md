# Express and Locomotive Boilers

## Locomotive Boiler Construction
A locomotive boiler is composed of a cylindrical barrel attached to a fire-box that is nearly rectangular in both its vertical and horizontal sections. The fire-box sides are secured to the shell using numerous stays, typically 1 inch in diameter and spaced 4 inches apart both horizontally and vertically. 

The method of staying the fire-box crown varies by the size of the boiler. In smaller boilers, longitudinal girder stays are used, which are supported at the opposite fire-box plate and the tube plate; some of these girders may be partially supported by slings from the boiler crown. In larger boilers, the boiler crown above the fire-box is flat, and the fire-box crown is supported by vertical stays that connect it to the shell crown. To accommodate the expansion of the copper tube plate, the two or three cross rows of stays closest to the tube plate are designed to move upwards, though not downwards.

Standard tubes in these boilers are usually 1-3/4 inches in diameter. The fire-bars often slope downwards away from the fire door. A fire-bridge or baffle is typically installed just below the lowest tubes, extending approximately halfway from the fire-door side of the fire-box to the tube plate. Some designs incorporate seamless steel water-tubes that extend across the fire-box and are expanded into the side plates. In such configurations, stay bars pass through these water-tubes to support the outer shell side plates. For example, a boiler used by the London & South-Western Railway Co. features 112 water-tubes (2-3/4 inches in diameter) arranged in two clusters of 56, with one set positioned above the fire-bridge and the other near the fire-door.

## Express Locomotive Boiler Specifications
A specific example of an express locomotive boiler, utilized by the Great Northern Railway in England, features a widened fire-box. This particular model has a heating surface of 2500 sq. ft. and a grate area of 30.9 sq. ft. The barrel measures 16 ft. in length between the tube plates and has a diameter of 5 ft. 6 in. In this design, the fire-box crown is supported by vertical stays extending to the shell crown, with the exception of the three rows of stays nearest the tube plates. These specific rows are supported by cross girders that rest on brackets secured to the outer shell.

## Express Water-Tube Boilers
Distinct from locomotive boilers, there is a class of water-tube boilers known as express boilers, which are primarily used in cruisers and torpedo-boat destroyers. These are employed where maximum power is required relative to the total weight of the installation. Express boilers utilize tubes with smaller diameters than other boiler types and are designed for a high rate of fuel combustion achieved through a high degree of "forced draught." The Yarrow express boiler is constructed similarly to the large tube Yarrow boiler, but with tubes that are more closely arranged and smaller in diameter.

## Manufacturing and Pressure
The typical working pressure for locomotive boilers ranges from 175 lb. to 200 lb. per sq. in. 

A critical part of the manufacturing process is flanging, which involves bending the edges of a plate to create a union with other plates. This process is used for the fire-boxes of locomotive boilers, among other components. Flanging replaced the older system of using angle iron rings, which were prone to explosions and grooving due to a lack of elasticity. Modern boiler shops use hydraulic presses with massive dies to bend the entire flanges of fire-box plates and plates up to 8 ft. in diameter in a single squeeze, which saves time and reduces material injury compared to hand-flanging.

## Fuel Adaptations
While coal is the primary fuel, some English railways adapted locomotive engines to use petroleum. The Great Eastern railway adapted 120 engines for use in goods trains and express passenger trains, and the London, Brighton & South Coast railway also began similar adaptations. These were mechanically successful, but the engines eventually returned to coal fuel due to short supplies and the resulting increase in the price of liquid fuel.
