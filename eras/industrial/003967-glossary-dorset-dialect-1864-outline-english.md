# Glossary of the Dorset Dialect and English Philology

The study of the Dorset dialect and the broader classification of the English language involve a combination of literary illustration and philological investigation. The dialect of Dorset is noted for being perfectly distinguishable from the dialects of Somersetshire and Wiltshire, while maintaining many common marks of Saxon origin.

## William Barnes and the Dorset Dialect
William Barnes (1800–1886), known as the Dorsetshire poet, was a central figure in the documentation and literary use of the local speech. Born on February 22, 1800, at Rushay, near Pentridge in Dorset, Barnes was the son of Grace Scott and John Barnes, members of the farmer class. After attending school at Sturminster Newton, he served as a pupil to a solicitor named Dashwood and later worked in the office of one Coombs at Dorchester.

Barnes's intellectual pursuits were diverse; he studied music under an organist and classics with village parsons. His early published work included *Orra, a Lapland Tale* (1822), which he funded using money earned from wood-engraving blocks for *Walks about Dorchester*. In 1823, he took a school at Mere in Wiltshire, where he resided in Chantry House after marrying Julia Miles. During this period, Barnes engaged in assiduous study of authors such as Ovid, Petrarch, Sallust, Herodotus, Buffon, and Burns.

His philological interests led to the publication of *An Etymological Glossary of English Words of Foreign Derivation* in 1829. In 1833, Barnes began writing poetry in the Dorsetshire dialect, publishing works such as "A Bit o' Sly Coorten" and "The 'Lotments" in a local newspaper. His contributions to the study of dialect culminated in the publication of the *Glossary of Dorset Dialect* in 1886. Additionally, Barnes edited a 1867 work by Jacob Poole titled *A Glossary (with some pieces of Verse) of the Old Dialect of the English Colony of Forth and Bargy*.

## Classification of English Dialects
Philological research by Dr. A.J. Ellis and Prince Louis Lucien Bonaparte focused on mapping existing dialects and their relationship to Middle and Old English. Their research identified several primary dialect groups:

*   **South-western:** This dialect occupies Dorset, Wilts, Gloucester, western Hampshire, and Somerset. Along with the Devonian dialect, it is a descendant of early southern English and the older West-Saxon of Alfred. While it may have been spoken everywhere south of the Thames in the 14th century, the influence of London caused its extinction in Kent, Sussex, and Surrey.
*   **Northern:** Located north of a line from the Humber to Morecambe Bay, this dialect is a direct descendant of early northern English and is kindred to Scottish dialects.
*   **East Midland:** Extending from London to south Lincolnshire, this dialect is considered the cradle-land of standard English speech and shows the least variation from it.

Other identified dialects include the Western dialect (representing the dialect of *Piers Plowman* in north Gloucestershire, Worcester, and Hereford), the Eastern dialect in East Anglian counties, and a Midland dialect in Warwick and Leicester shires. Various intermediate dialects also exist, such as the North-western dialect of south Lancashire, Cheshire, Stafford, and Derby, and the North-eastern dialect of north Lincolnshire and Nottingham.

## Dialect Research and Documentation
In modern times, there has been a revival of interest in local forms of English, following the 18th and 19th-century revival of Lowland Scots. This has resulted in a body of literature consisting of local tales, poems, and "folk-lore," particularly in Dorset, Somerset, Devon, Yorkshire, Lancashire, and Cumberland.

The elder Richard Garnett is cited as a pioneer in the philological investigation of these dialects. Further organized research was conducted by the English Dialect Society, founded by the Rev. Professor Skeat. This society published grammars and glossaries of local dialects, including the West Somerset grammar by F.T. Elworthy and the Windhill dialect grammar of Yorkshire by Professor Joseph Wright.

Professor Joseph Wright further synthesized these efforts by constructing the *English Dialect Dictionary*. This work records local words, their senses, geographical range, and pronunciation, utilizing illustrative phrases and quotations. He also produced an *English Dialect Grammar* that examines the phonology of dialects and how modern sounds represent Old English sounds.
