# Barometers

A barometer (derived from the Greek *baros* for pressure and *metron* for measure) is an instrument used to measure the pressure or weight of the atmosphere. These instruments vary in design, ranging from those that use liquid mercury to those that employ exhausted metallic chambers.

## Types of Barometers

The archive identifies several distinct categories of barometric instruments:

*   **Mercurial Barometers:** These typically consist of a tube approximately 36 inches long, containing mercury and hermetically closed at the top.
    *   **Cistern Barometer:** The open end of the tube is placed in a basin, or cistern, of mercury. Atmospheric pressure is measured by the difference in height between the mercury in the tube and the mercury in the cistern.
    *   **Siphon Barometer:** This design dispenses with the cistern by bending the tube round upon itself at the lower end. The diameter remains the same throughout. The height is determined by taking the difference between the readings of the upper and lower limbs.
*   **Aneroid Barometer:** Derived from the Greek *a-* (privative) and *neros* (wet), this type uses no liquid. Instead, it relies on volume changes in an exhausted metallic chamber under varying pressures.
*   **Other Variants:** "Baroscopes" indicate pressure variations without providing quantitative data, while "barographs" automatically record these variations.

## Technical Errors and Corrections

Cistern barometers are subject to two primary types of error: capillarity and the error of capacity.

**Capillarity** occurs when a small-bore glass tube is plunged into mercury; the mercury level in the tube sits below the level of the vessel with a convex surface. This capillary depression is inversely proportional to the tube's diameter. In standard barometers, the tube is about an inch in diameter, which keeps the error due to capillarity below .001 of an inch. To find the true pressure, an addition must be made to the observed height.

**The Error of Capacity** arises because the height of the barometer is the perpendicular distance between the cistern's surface and the column's upper surface. As the barometer falls (for example, from 30 to 29 inches), mercury flows from the tube into the cistern, raising the cistern level. Conversely, when the barometer rises, mercury flows into the tube, lowering the cistern level. Because the graduated scales are usually fixed on brass cases, these constant changes in the cistern level can cause readings to be too high or too low.

The siphon barometer is noted for requiring no corrections for either capacity or capillarity, though its functionality can be hindered by impurities contracted by the mercury in the lower limb.

## Measurement and Standards

Atmospheric pressure is expressed by the perpendicular height of the mercury column rather than the weight of the mercury. For instance, a reading of 30 inches means the pressure can sustain a column of mercury of that height, rather than referring to the weight of mercury filling a tube with a square-inch transverse section.

Different regions use various systems of measurement:
*   **England and America:** English inches.
*   **France and most European countries:** Millimetres (one thousandth of a metre, which equals 39.37079 English inches).
*   **Russia:** Historically used half-lines (one twentieth of an English inch) until 1869, after which the metric scale was adopted.
*   **Other European countries:** Some use the Paris line, which equals 0.088814 inches.

Because scales expand with heat, observations must be reduced to a uniform temperature to be compared accurately. The standard reduction temperature is 32° Fahr. (0° cent.).

## Materials and Thermal Expansion

High-quality barometers are constructed from brass (for the case and engraved scale), glass (for the tube), and mercury. Because mercury has a considerably greater coefficient of expansion than brass, temperature changes affect the reading. For example, an instrument standing at 30 inches at 32° will stand at 30.1 inches if the temperature rises to 69°, even if the atmospheric pressure has not changed.

## Historical Developments and Magnification

Various inventors sought to magnify the variation in the mercury column for more exact measurements:
*   **Bernardo Ramazzini and Sir Samuel Morland:** Suggested the diagonal barometer, where the upper tube is inclined.
*   **Dr. R. Hooke:** Developed the wheel barometer, using a float on the cistern surface to move a wheel.
*   **G. Amontons (1695):** Invented the pendant barometer, featuring a vertical funnel-shaped tube.
*   **Descartes and Huygens:** Proposed expanding the top of the tube into a cylindrical vessel containing a fine tube partly filled with water. While water-barometers failed due to high vapour tension and dissolved gases, the substitution of methyl salicylate (which has low viscosity, low specific gravity of 1.18 at 10°C, and low vapour tension) was successful. A version patented by C. O. Bartrum claims readings to .001 of an inch without a vernier.
