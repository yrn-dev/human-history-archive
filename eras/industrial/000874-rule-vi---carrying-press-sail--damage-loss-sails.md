# RULE VI.--CARRYING PRESS OF SAIL--DAMAGE TO OR LOSS OF SAILS

Within the framework of maritime law and the rules governing General Average (G.A.), Rule VI specifically addresses the loss of or damage to a ship's sails and spars. These rules categorize certain types of damage as acts of sacrifice for the common safety, while excluding others from G.A. compensation.

## Provisions of Rule VI
Under Rule VI, damage to sails, spars, or both is made good as General Average if the damage was caused by efforts to force a ship off the ground or by driving the vessel higher up the ground for the sake of common safety. Such actions are viewed as an abnormal use of the ship's appliances, which is likely to result in damage to the spars and sails.

Conversely, the rule stipulates that when a ship is afloat, any loss or damage caused to the freight, cargo, or the ship itself resulting from "carrying a press of sail" shall not be made good as General Average.

## Related Provisions for Stranded Vessels
Rule VI operates alongside other rules concerning ships that have stranded or are in peril. Rule VII addresses damage to the ship's boilers and machinery when a vessel is ashore and in a position of peril. Damage to these components is allowed in G.A. if it is demonstrated that there was an actual intention to float the ship for the common safety, despite the risk of such damage.

Furthermore, Rule V examines the conditions under which the voluntary stranding of a ship is considered a G.A. act. If a ship is intentionally run on shore to avoid inevitably sinking or driving onto rocks, no loss or damage to the ship, freight, or cargo is made good as G.A. However, in all other instances where intentional stranding is performed for the common safety, the resulting damage is allowed.

## Legal Precedents and Interpretations
The application of these rules is reflected in English law. In the 1895 case of "The Bona," the court of appeal held that G.A. contribution at common law applied to both the coal and stores consumed and the damage sustained by engines while they were being worked to get a ship off the ground.

## Broader Context of General Average
The principles found in Rule VI are part of a wider system of sacrifice and compensation for the common safety. Other related rules include:
* **Jettison and Sacrifice:** Rule II allows for G.A. compensation for damage to cargo and ship resulting from sacrifices made for common safety, including water entering through hatches opened for jettison. However, Rule I specifies that no jettison of deck cargo is made good as G.A.
* **Fire Suppression:** Rule III allows for G.A. compensation for damage caused by water or beaching/scuttling to extinguish a fire, though it excludes compensation for the portions of the ship or cargo that were actually on fire.
* **Wreck Removal:** Rule IV states that loss or damage caused by cutting away the remains of spars or other items previously carried away by sea-peril is not made good as G.A.
* **Lightening and Refloating:** When a ship is ashore, the extra cost of reshipping, lighter hire, and the discharge of bunker coals, ship's stores, or cargo to float the vessel is admitted as G.A. (Rule VIII).

## Expenses and Contributions
When repairs are necessary for the safe prosecution of a voyage, the cost of discharging cargo to enable those repairs—whether caused by accident or sacrifice—is allowed as G.A. under York-Antwerp Rule X (b), regardless of whether the repairs occur at a port of call or a port of refuge. This includes the expenses of storing cargo on shore and reloading it (Rule X (c)), as well as incidental loss or damage (Rule XII).

Contributions to G.A. are based on the actual values of the property at the termination of the adventure, plus the amount made good for sacrificed property. Deductions are made for port charges and crew's wages that would not have been incurred had the ship and cargo been totally lost at the time of the sacrifice. Notably, personal effects and passengers' luggage not shipped under a bill of lading do not contribute to G.A.
