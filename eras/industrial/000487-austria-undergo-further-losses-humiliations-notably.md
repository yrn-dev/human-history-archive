# Austria: Political Transitions and International Relations

The history of Austria is characterized by fluctuating periods of territorial expansion, diplomatic struggle, and systemic losses. From the late middle ages through the early 20th century, the state navigated internal religious conflicts, the pressures of European power dynamics, and the consequences of major global conflicts.

## Early Modern Challenges and Religious Conflict
At the close of the middle ages, Austria had expanded to an area of nearly 50,000 square miles. Despite this growth, the state faced internal weaknesses; its rulers lacked the prestige of the electoral office, and the clergy remained under the allegiance of foreign prelates due to the absence of an internal archbishopric. Unification was eventually aided by the loss of Habsburg possessions on the Rhine and the progress of the Turks in south-eastern Europe, which encouraged the separate states to unite for defense.

Under Emperor Maximilian II (1564-1576), a liberal policy maintained peace, though the government remained in the humiliating position of being a tributary to the Turk. Religious divisions were prevalent: Catholicism was largely confined to the mountains of Tirol, while the majority of Austria and much of Styria were Lutheran. In Moravia, Silesia, and Bohemia, various Christian beliefs competed for dominance.

The accession of Rudolph II (1576-1612), a fanatical Spanish Catholic, shifted this dynamic. He encouraged the Jesuits to pursue the counter-Reformation, though his reign was marked by a lack of effective government. This led to semi-independence in Bohemia and a preference in Hungary for the Turk over the emperor. Following a failed family compact in 1606 to establish Archduke Matthias as head of the family, Ferdinand II (1619-1637) sought to restore the Catholic faith. His efforts to establish Jesuit rule in Bohemia, where he had been elected king in 1618, triggered the Thirty Years' War.

## The Napoleonic Era and the Metternich Diplomacy
In the early 19th century, Austria suffered losses and humiliations, most notably through the treaty of Vienna in 1809. The state found an opportunity for revenge and recuperation following Napoleon's failed Russian campaign in 1812. Under the leadership of Metternich, Austria pursued a policy of recovering lost possessions, though not necessarily through the total overthrow of the French emperor.

Austria initially avoided the March 1813 alliance between Prussia and Russia, instead positioning itself as a mediator while massing 200,000 to 300,000 troops in Bohemia. Following the battle of Vittoria and Napoleon's refusal to abandon his political system in Central Europe, Austria joined the Allies. This decision was finalized in the treaty of Trachenberg on July 12, 1813. Subsequent victories at the battle of Leipzig in October 1813 led to the advance into France and Napoleon's abdication on April 11, 1814.

## 19th Century Power Shifts
By the mid-19th century, Austria sought to restore its influence in Germany and the *status quo ante*. Following the capitulation of Vilagos, which ended the Hungarian insurrection, the Convention of Olmuetz (September 29, 1850) appeared to be a triumph over Prussia. However, Count Beust later characterized this not as a Prussian humiliation, but as an "Austrian weakness," arguing that Austria chose to re-establish a discredited system rather than crushing Prussia, thereby allowing Prussia time to gather strength.

Following the peace of Frankfort, Austria's status declined further, sinking rapidly into the position of a confederate in German policy. During this period, Germany emerged as a united and formidable power, while France was left crippled and humiliated.

## The First World War and Reparations
In the lead-up to the Great War, Austria's relationship with Germany became more consolidated. During the events at Algeciras, Austria's refusal to go beyond certain limits left Germany isolated, though a compromise suggested by Austria allowed Germany to withdraw without a loss of dignity.

Following the war, the Republic of Austria accepted responsibility for losses and damages caused to allied and associated Governments. Under the terms of the peace treaty, Austria undertook the following obligations:
* **Financial Reparations:** While recognizing that Austria's resources were inadequate for complete reparation, the state agreed to compensate civilians and their property. A Reparation Commission was tasked with determining the total liabilities and a payment schedule spanning thirty years, with notification due by May 1, 1921.
* **Maritime and River Assets:** Austria ceded all fishing boats and merchant ships belonging to nationals of the former empire to the Reparation Commission and agreed to deliver 20 percent of its river fleet to compensate for losses in river tonnage.
* **Physical Restoration:** Austria committed its economic resources to the physical restoration of invaded areas, including the replacement of machinery, equipment, and animals destroyed during the conflict.

Regarding war debts, the Republic of Austria remained the only territory of the former empire to carry obligations for the former Austrian Government's war debt.
