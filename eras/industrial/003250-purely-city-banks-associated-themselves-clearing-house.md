# The Clearing House

The "Clearing House" is a system used by banks to settle accounts and clear cheques, facilitating large-scale financial transactions through ledger transfers rather than the physical exchange of notes and specie. This system has been developed extensively in Great Britain, the United States, and Germany to increase business efficiency and reduce the risks and costs associated with money transmission.

## The London Clearing House
Purely city banks in London had associated themselves in a Clearing House by 1776, though evidence from the books of the Grasshopper suggests a less organized system of settlement existed as early as 1773, noting a quarterly charge for the use of a "Clearing-room." In 1810, a dedicated house was taken for this purpose, at which time forty-six banking houses settled their accounts.

The membership of the London Clearing House has evolved over time. In 1844, twenty-six of the forty-nine London banks were connected to it, but membership was restricted to private banks. Joint-stock banks were admitted in 1854. By 1906, the membership consisted of nineteen banks: eighteen joint-stock banks and one private bank. Certain institutions, such as banks in Charing Cross or Fleet Street, did not belong to the body. The Bank of England has also never been a member; while it "clears on one side" by making its claims on clearing bankers through the Clearing House, the claims of those bankers on the Bank of England are sent directly to Threadneedle Street two or three times daily.

In 1858, Sir John Lubbock (Lord Avebury) arranged for the addition of "country clearing." This allowed cheques drawn on distant English villages to be handled as conveniently as those drawn on London, providing significant assistance to business.

## Volume and Impact in the United Kingdom
In the United Kingdom, nearly every large transaction is settled by cheque. This is supported by the fact that every bank in the country maintains an agent in London. The annual circulation handled by the London Clearing House exceeds £12,000,000,000. This system operates on a "well-founded trust," where the reserves or gold stocks of the banks on which cheques are drawn are not questioned.

The volume of clearings has grown substantially over the 19th and early 20th centuries. According to the *Appendix to the Second Report, Committee of House of Commons, Banks of Issue* (1841), the London clearings were:
*   1839: £954,401,600 (29 banks)
*   1840: £978,496,800 (29 banks)
*   1899: £9,150,269,000 (19 banks)
*   1900: £8,960,170,000 (19 banks)
*   1906: £12,711,334,000 (18 banks)

The total yearly turnover in cheques for the entire region—including clearing houses in large towns of Scotland, Ireland, and England, as well as internal bank office settlements—is estimated to be at least twice the amount registered by the London Clearing House.

## Clearing Systems in the United States
The United States has developed a clearing system that is among the most advanced in the world, perhaps second only to Great Britain. As of September 30, 1907, clearing houses existed in approximately 112 leading cities, with aggregate clearings reaching $154,662,515,258. The New York Clearing-House dominates this sector; in 1906, it accounted for 67.2% of the total clearings across 55 of the larger cities.

The volume of these clearings is subject to fluctuation based on the prosperity of the country and the volume of stock-exchange transactions. Membership in the New York Clearing-House has included the sub-Treasury and state and national banks. The Clearing-House Committee in New York wields significant power, as it can refuse emergency aid to banks it deems to be unwisely conducted. This authority was utilized during the panic of 1907 to remove several speculative financial interests from the control of national banks.

Historically, the weekly condition reports of these members served as an index for the money-market and exchanges. However, because trust companies did not provide regular weekly reports, this index was sometimes misleading. In early 1908, the state superintendent of banking announced he would use legal powers to require weekly reports from trust companies to provide a more complete mirror of banking conditions.

## The German Clearing System
In Germany, Hamburg provided the first and for a long time the only example of a clearing system. This was eventually adopted by the Reichsbank, which established its Hamburg office in the building formerly belonging to the Hamburg Clearing House. The Bank of Prussia had also undertaken similar business before being absorbed and developed by the Reichsbank in 1876.

The Reichsbank's clearing system allows for the remittance of money across 443 office locations without charge to the sender or receiver, provided the recipient holds an account at the bank. The sender does not need an account. This system functions as a risk-free, chargeless money-order system, effectively unifying the German Empire for monetary purposes from the Baltic shores and the frontiers of Switzerland to the frontiers of Russia. In 1906, the amount of these transfers exceeded £1,860,000,000.
