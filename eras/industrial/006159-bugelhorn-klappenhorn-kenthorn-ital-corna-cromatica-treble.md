# Bugelhorn, Klappenhorn, and Kenthorn

The bugle, also known as the bugle-horn, keyed bugle, Kent bugle, or Regent's bugle, is a treble brass wind instrument. It is characterized by a conical bore of rapidly increasing diameter and a cup-shaped mouthpiece. Historically, the instrument's name and its characteristic conical bore are derived from the bull's horn. In various languages, the instrument is referred to as *Bugle*, *Clairon*, *Cor à clefs*, or *Bugle à clefs* in French; *Flügelhorn*, *Signalhorn*, *Bugelhorn*, *Klappenhorn*, or *Kenthorn* in German; and *Corna cromatica* in Italian.

## Functional Use and Design
The bugle is primarily utilized as a signal instrument and for military duty. Its design is centered on the conical bore, which allows for its specific acoustic properties as a treble brass instrument.

## Development of the Keyed Bugle
Efforts to improve the instrument led to the development of keyed versions. In 1760, Kolbel, a Bohemian horn virtuoso serving at the imperial Russian court since 1754, applied keys to the bugle (known in Germany as the *Buglehorn* or *Signal*), which he called the *Klappenhorn*. Although Kolbel spent many years attempting to improve the instrument, his experiments with the *Klappenhorn* did not achieve wide adoption or recognition during his own lifetime.

Later developments in keyed brass instruments were spurred by Anton Weidinger, a court trumpeter at Vienna, who created a keyed trumpet in 1801. This invention renewed interest in experimenting with keys across various brass instruments. In 1813, Joseph Weidinger, the twelve-year-old son of Anton, performed a concert in Vienna using the *Klappenwaldhorn* (keyed French horn).

## Technical Mechanics of the Klappenhorn
According to descriptions by Victor Mahillon, who attributes the invention to Kolbel, the instrument featured a single key located on the bell. When this key was opened, it had the effect of raising the pitch of the instrument by a whole tone. By combining the action of this key—which shortened the air column—with the use of harmonic open notes on the normal length of the tube, a diatonic scale could be obtained in the third octave.
