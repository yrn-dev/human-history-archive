# George Boole's Final Years and Research

## Final Research and Publications
During the last few years of his life, George Boole was constantly engaged in extending his researches. His primary objective during this period was the production of a second edition of his work, *Differential Equations*. Boole intended for this new edition to be significantly more complete than the first edition, which had been published in 1859. To facilitate this work, he spent part of his final vacation conducting research in the libraries of the British Museum and the Royal Society.

Despite these efforts, the second edition was never completed. Following Boole's death, his manuscripts were passed to Todhunter. However, these documents were so incomplete that Todhunter found it impossible to use them to publish a second edition of the original treatise. Consequently, Todhunter printed the manuscripts in 1865 as a supplementary volume.

## Academic and Professional Career
George Boole (1815–1864) was an English mathematician and logician born in Lincoln on November 2, 1815. The son of a studious tradesman who provided his first lessons in mathematical science, Boole did not show his extraordinary mathematical powers early in life, initially favoring classics. He began studying higher mathematics at age seventeen, though his progress was hindered by a lack of efficient help.

Boole spent his entire life in the scholastic profession. At approximately sixteen years of age, he served as an assistant-master at a private school in Doncaster. Later, he successfully established a school at Lincoln, which he subsequently moved to Waddington. In 1849, he was appointed as the professor of mathematics at the Queen's College in Cork. In 1855, he married Miss Mary Everest, who later authored several educational works based on her husband's principles.

## Mathematical and Logical Contributions
Boole was recognized by the public as the author of several standard works and numerous abstruse papers on mathematical topics. He completed two systematic treatises during his lifetime: the *Treatise on Differential Equations* (1859) and the *Treatise on the Calculus of Finite Differences* (1860), the latter of which served as a sequel to the former. These works are noted for combining profound philosophical investigation with elementary exposition. In the *Differential Equations* treatise, specifically chapters 16 and 17, Boole provided an account of a general method in analysis and the general symbolic method.

Boole was a pioneer in perceiving that symbols of operation could be treated as distinct objects of calculation, separate from symbols of quantity. His work in logic was characterized by a profound confidence in symbolic reasoning. While he first presented his ideas in the 1847 pamphlet *Mathematical Analysis of Logic*, he later considered this an imperfect exposition. He viewed his 1854 work, *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought, on which are founded the Mathematical Theories of Logic and Probabilities*, as the mature statement of his views.

In his logical system, Boole proposed a deep analogy between algebraical symbols and logical forms. He suggested that logic could be viewed as mathematics restricted to the quantities 0 and 1, where unity (1) denoted the universe of thinkable objects. Using literal symbols (such as x, y, z) to represent elective meanings—similar to adjectives or substantives—he demonstrated that these symbols obey the same primary laws of combination as algebra, allowing them to be multiplied, added, subtracted, and divided.

## Published Works and Recognition
Boole's body of work consists of approximately fifty scattered articles and a few separate publications. His earliest paper, "Theory of Analytical Transformations," appeared in the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* in 1839. Over his career, he contributed twenty-two articles to the *Cambridge Mathematical Journal* and its successor, the *Cambridge and Dublin Mathematical Journal*, and sixteen papers to the *Philosophical Magazine*. 

His research was also published by the Royal Society in six important memoirs within the *Philosophical Transactions*, as well as in the *Transactions of the Royal Society of Edinburgh*, the *Transactions of the Royal Irish Academy*, *Crelle's Journal*, and the *Bulletin de l'Academie de St-Petersbourg* (where he appeared as G. Boldt in 1862). Additionally, he published a paper on the mathematical basis of logic in the *Mechanic's Magazine* in 1848.

For his 1844 memoir, Boole received a medal from the Royal Society, and he was awarded an honorary LL.D. from the University of Dublin. Despite these honors, he did not seek the ordinary rewards associated with his discoveries.

## Death
George Boole died on December 8, 1864, while still in the full vigour of his intellectual powers. His death was caused by an attack of fever that resulted in suffusion on the lungs.
