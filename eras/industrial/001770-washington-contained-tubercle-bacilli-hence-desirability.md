# Tubercle Bacilli

Tubercle bacilli are organisms associated with tuberculosis that can affect both livestock and humans. In the context of bacteriology, these organisms are of particular importance due to their presence in dairy products and their impact on biological tissues.

## Presence in Dairy Products
Milk serves as a medium that is admirably suited for the growth of bacteria and is typically contaminated with these organisms during the ordinary course of supply. Research has indicated a significant prevalence of tuberculosis in cattle; for example, F. Lafar stated that 20% of cows in Germany suffer from the disease. Between 1891 and 1893, 17.7% of cattle slaughtered in Copenhagen were affected.

The contamination of milk supplies is widespread. In Paris, one in every thirteen samples of milk examined contained tubercle bacilli, while in Washington, the frequency was one in every nineteen samples. Because of these findings, the sterilization of milk used for domestic purposes is considered imperative.

## Pasteurization and Control
The pasteurizer, named after the French chemist Pasteur, is used to destroy the tubercle bacillus and other bacilli associated with diseases communicable through milk. The process involves maintaining milk at a temperature of 170 to 180 degrees F for eight to ten minutes. 

Effective pasteurization depends on three primary factors:
1. The temperature to which the milk is raised.
2. The length of time it is maintained at that temperature.
3. The use of mechanical agitation to prevent the formation of a "scalded layer" or scum.

If milk is heated in open vessels, a pellicle or membrane may form on the surface, which can protect the tubercle bacilli and allow them to resist heat at 140 degrees F (60 degrees C.) for longer periods. Consequently, pasteurization is more efficient in closed receptacles. To destroy tubercle bacilli without injuring the consistency or creaming properties of the milk, it is recommended to heat the milk in closed pasteurizers at 140 degrees F for no less than twenty minutes. This standard has been utilized at the Wisconsin University Creamery.

## Pathological Effects in Humans
In humans, chronic inflammation of tuberculous origin affects the spongy ends of long bones as well as the bones of the hands, feet, and vertebrae. In cases of tuberculous ostitis, the bacilli in the spongy tissue cause colorless corpuscles from the blood to collect around them, forming small greyish-white heaps known as tubercles. 

These tubercles can exist in large numbers, leading to a condition called rarefying ostitis. Over time, these may break down into tuberculous abscesses that move toward the surface. Medical treatment for these foci includes placing the affected part on a splint for absolute rest, providing the patient with fresh air, and prescribing iron and cod-liver oil. If an abscess advances toward the surface, surgeons may cut into the part to scrape out the foci and disinfect the area with a strong antiseptic lotion. The use of tuberculin injections may also be considered.

## Bacteriological Properties and Toxins
Tubercle bacilli produce toxins that are classified as intracellular, meaning they are contained within the bodies of the bacteria. Unlike extracellular toxins (such as those from diphtheria or tetanus), which can be isolated by filtering a culture through porcelain, the toxins of tubercle bacilli are released upon the disintegration of the bacteria. These intracellular toxins generally produce fever and interfere with the body's vital processes, which can lead to fatal results.

To study these toxins, scientists have developed methods to break up the bacterial bodies. Koch achieved this by grinding tubercle bacilli in an agate mortar and treating them with distilled water. Another method introduced by Rowland and Macfadyen involves grinding the bacilli in liquid air, which makes the bacterial bodies brittle and easier to break.
