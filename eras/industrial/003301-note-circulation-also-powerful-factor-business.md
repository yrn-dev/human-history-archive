# Note Circulation in International Banking

Note circulation serves as a critical mechanism for conducting business and managing the circulating medium across various national banking systems. The regulation of these issues often involves a balance between the volume of notes in circulation and the reserves—such as specie, gold, or government bonds—held to secure them.

## The Reichsbank of Germany
The Reichsbank is the most powerful banking institution in Germany, managed by a bank directory appointed by the chancellor of the empire. The government maintains complete control through the chancellor, while shareholders participate via a committee requiring members to hold at least three shares. The bank assists business through a clearing system (*Giro-Verkehr*) and its note circulation, which is primarily used for ordinary business payments.

The circulation of notes is governed by the law of 1875 and an amending law of 1899. An uncovered limit, which began at £12,500,000 and reached £14,811,450, was extended by the act of June 5, 1902, to £23,641,450. Notes not represented by specie must be backed by discounted bills (maturing within three months and generally possessing two to three good indorsements), notes of issuing banks, or treasury notes (*Reichskassenscheine*). Additionally, at least one-third of circulating notes must be covered by gold in bullion, foreign coin (reckoned at £69, 12s. per pound fine), money, current German notes, or imperial treasury notes.

The Reichsbank is legally bound to redeem notes in current German money, which may include silver thalers, gold coin, or bar-gold at a rate of 1392 marks per pound fine of gold. While the importance of gold payment is admitted, the bank has not always provided these facilities in practice. In 1906, the average notes issued for the year were £69,000,000, with gold holdings totaling £24,069,000 as of December 30. Unlike the English system, the German law allows the bank to exceed its circulation limits upon payment of 5% on the surplus, a power used annually since 1895.

## Banking Systems in the United Kingdom
In the United Kingdom, the note circulation of the Bank of England is regulated by Peel's act of 1844. While the number of banking offices in England and Wales grew from 976 in 1844 to over 5,880 in 1906, much of the increase in Bank of England note circulation is attributed to the till-money held by these offices.

In Scotland and Ireland, the acts of 1844-1845 require banks to hold specie against any notes issued in excess of the legal limit. Sir Robert Peel designed this to ensure bank notes remained uniformly equivalent to coin and to make the circulating medium fluctuate as if it were purely metallic. However, this system is less applicable to the modern UK medium, which consists mainly of cheques. In Scotland, the limitation of note-issuing power to banks that possessed it in 1845 has prevented the establishment of any important new banks since that time, as the power to issue notes is essential for supplying customer needs in ordinary business. By 1906, ten banks of issue carried out nearly all Scottish business through 1,180 branches.

Historically, the Bank of Dundee exemplified the importance of notes; from 1764 to 1792, its entire business consisted of issuing notes ranging from £23,000 to £56,000. By the time of its 1864 amalgamation with the Royal Bank of Scotland, its note circulation was £41,000.

## The Bank of France and the United States
The Bank of France operates primarily through a massive note circulation, which averaged £186,300,000 in 1906. The limits of this circulation have been extended steadily since 1848, rising from 350 million francs (£14,000,000) to 5,800 million francs (£232,000,000) by 1906.

In the United States, the national banking system emerged from the financial needs of the Federal government during the Civil War. To create a market for government bonds, the act of February 25, 1863, and the act of June 3, 1864, allowed federally chartered banks to issue circulating notes secured by the deposit of government bonds. To discourage state banks, a 10% tax was imposed on the face value of state bank notes after July 1, 1866. This led to a rapid increase in national banks; their number grew from 453 in 1864 (with £79,366,950 aggregate capital) to 1,014 in 1865 (with £242,542,982 aggregate capital).

## Note Circulation in Meiji Japan
Following the Restoration, Meiji statesmen issued government notes to replace unsecured tokens. Initially, these notes fell to a 50% discount, and the government used despotic devices to force their circulation at par. While the government's credit eventually improved—allowing 60 million yen in inconvertible notes to pass without discount by the end of the first five years of the Meiji era—the need for funds for wholesale reforms increased. By 1881 (the fourteenth year after the Restoration), notes in circulation reached 180 million yen and stood at a heavy discount.
