# Flanging

Flanging is a technical process and structural feature used across various fields of engineering and manufacturing. In the context of metalworking, flanging refers to the turning or bending over of the edges of a plate to provide a means of union to other plates.

## Application in Boilermaking
Flanging is a significant operation in boilermaking, though it is scarcely represented in bridge and girder work. It is utilized for tube-plates, end-plates, and furnace flues. Specific examples of flanging include the crowns of vertical boilers, the fire-boxes of locomotive boilers, the front and back plates of marine boilers, the back end-plates of Cornish and Lancashire boilers, and the ends of conical cross-tubes.

In the construction of boilers, flanging serves a critical safety function. It has superseded an older system of union that used angle iron rings (rings forming two sides of a rectangular section). Because those rings had a sharp angular form that lacked elasticity, they were a frequent source of explosions and grooving. In contrast, a flange turned with a large radius provides the elasticity necessary to counteract the effects of temperature changes.

## Manufacturing Methods
Flanging is an operation that must be performed at a red or white heat. The methods of production have evolved from manual labor to mechanized processes:

*   **Hand-Flanging:** In small establishments or in the early days of the practice, a skilled workman known as an angle ironsmith performed the task. This involved heating a length of the edge (approximately a foot or 10 inches) and hammering it around the edge of an iron block of a suitable shape. This process was repeated in successive "heats" until the work was finished.
*   **Machine-Flanging:** Modern boiler shops utilize hydraulic presses with massive dies to bend the entire flanges of fire-box plates for locomotive boilers or plates with diameters of 4, 6, or 8 feet in a single squeeze. For marine boiler ends that are too large for this method, a special press is used to bend edges over in successive heats.
*   **Rolling:** For Adamson seams of furnace flues, a special machine is used where a length of flue is rotated on a table and the flange is turned over between revolving rollers within one minute.

Machine-flanging is preferred over hand-flanging not only because of the enormous saving of time but also because the material suffers far less injury.

## Structural and Mechanical Uses
Beyond boilermaking, the term "flange" refers to various structural components designed for stability, union, or containment:

*   **Girders:** In bridge girders, which typically utilize an I section, the material is arranged into a top flange (boom or chord) and a bottom flange. The top flange is subjected to compression, while the bottom flange is subjected to tension. These flanges resist horizontal tension and compression distributed practically uniformly across their cross sections.
*   **Piers and Abutments:** In constructions using cast or wrought iron, hollow cylindrical pillars may be united by flanges and bolts, as well as by sockets or projections.
*   **Disk Piles:** Used in sand, these piles feature a flat flange at the bottom.
*   **Mechanical Components:** In the mechanism of a Maxim Gun, the extractor features undercut flanges, open at the top and bottom, which hold the rim of a cartridge base.
*   **Wheel Hubs:** In bicycle and tricycle wheels, spoke flanges on the hub are placed far apart to provide lateral stability. The ends of spokes are passed through holes in the hub flange.

## Safety and Material Treatment
Flanging is characterized as a "violent" treatment of metal. Because of this, and because safety is a cardinal matter in certain applications, the method of performing such operations has changed. While flanging is done hot, other violent operations like punching and shearing are performed on cold metal and can leave minute cracks or jagged edges that may extend under stress and lead to fracture. To prevent this, specifications often insist that rough edges be removed—such as by planing a shorn edge or reamering a punched hole—provided at least 1/16 inch of material is removed.
