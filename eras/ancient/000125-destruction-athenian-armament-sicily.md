# The Destruction of the Athenian Armament in Sicily

The destruction of the Athenian armament in Sicily, which reached its melancholy close in September 413 B.C., represents a pivotal catastrophe in the Peloponnesian War. The event involved the total loss of the united armaments led by Nikias and Demosthenes, first within the harbor and subsequently in the vicinity of Syracuse.

## Military Preparations and the Sicilian Campaign
The winter of 414-413 B.C. was characterized by extensive military activity. This period, termed the nineteenth spring of the Peloponnesian war by Thucydidês and the beginning of the Dekeleian war by other historians, saw Athens and its rivals engaging in intense preparations. Demosthenes worked throughout the winter to assemble a second armament for the early spring, while Eurymedon traveled to Syracuse in midwinter with ten triremes. 

To secure the region, Athens sent twenty triremes around the Peloponnesus to Naupaktus to block Corinthian reinforcements from the Corinthian gulf. In response, the Corinthians prepared twenty-five triremes to convoy hoplite transports. Simultaneously, levies of hoplites were conducted in Lacedæmon, Bœotia, Sikyôn, and Corinth for the armament to Syracuse, while preparations were made for the occupation of Dekeleia. In Sicily, Gylippus worked to encourage the island's cities to take a more decisive role in the conflict.

## The Role and Failure of Nikias
The failure of the enterprise and the subsequent destruction of the armament are traced distinctly to the misjudgment of Nikias. Despite his personal bravery—which rose to heroism in the final days in Sicily—and his good intentions, his leadership was marked by severe mental defects. These included apathy, inaction, presumptuous neglect, petty trifling, and an obstinate blindness to obvious necessities. 

These incapacities are evident not only in historical narratives but also in Nikias's own speeches and letters to the Athenians. His flagrant incompetency resulted in the wholesale ruin of two fine armaments, the Athenian maritime empire, and ultimately Athens itself. Consequently, he is viewed with severe condemnation in history, as his personal suffering is not considered a sufficient atonement for the mischief he brought upon his country and army.

## Reaction in Athens
The news of the disaster reached Athens through various channels. According to Plutarch, a stranger arriving at Peiræus first spread the news in a barber's shop. The barber informed the archons and the public, but because the stranger disappeared, the barber was treated as a fabricator of rumors and subjected to torture. More reliable reports eventually arrived via neutrals passing from Megara or Corinth, and later through individual soldiers who had escaped the defeat.

Despite these reports, the Athenian public struggled to believe the entirety of the destruction. In a resolution described as the most unwise ever passed in the Pnyx, the public assembly decided to continue Nikias in command and send fresh forces to Sicily. This decision was fueled by an undeserved confidence in Nikias's caution and ability. The archive notes that the absence of a powerful demagogue, such as Kleon, was detrimental, as such a figure might have exposed Nikias's mismanagement and prevented the resolution. Plutarch further notes that some men had previously opposed sending a second armament due to envy of Nikias's glory and fortune.

## Aftermath in Sicily and Greece
The destruction of the armament in September 413 B.C. caused a profound sensation throughout the Grecian world. In Sicily, the victory was hailed with intoxication. The triumph was achieved through the efforts of nearly all the Grecian cities on the island, aided by Gylippus and Peloponnesian allies, with the exceptions of Agrigentum (which remained neutral) and Naxos and Katana (which were allied with Athens).

Following the victory, the Syracusans waged war against Katana, where some Athenian fugitives assisted the resistance. However, both Katana and Naxos managed to maintain their independence. This was likely due to the exhaustion of the Syracusans, who had endured heavy pecuniary burdens during the siege, and a reluctance among Syracuse's allies to allow the city to become too powerful. After paying their hired auxiliaries and celebrating the triumph, the Syracusans focused on repairing their territory, which had been poorly cultivated during the year of the siege.

The collateral effects of this catastrophe were momentous for all of Greece. In the long term, the conflicts between Thebes, Sparta, and Athens left Greece more disunited and destitute of presiding Hellenic authority than at any time since the Persian invasion. While Athens eventually recovered some maritime power, it remained a shadow of the imperial energy seen under the ancestors of Perikles, leaving the region vulnerable to the rise of Macedonia.
