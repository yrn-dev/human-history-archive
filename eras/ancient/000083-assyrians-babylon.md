# Assyrians and Babylon

The history of the Assyrians and the city of Babylon is characterized by a long-standing struggle for supremacy in Western Asia, marked by shifting power between the "stone city" of Nineveh and the "brick city" of Babylon. While these two centers were at times separate and distinct, they were eventually united and confounded together.

## The City of Babylon
Babylon is described as the first city in Western Asia, even during periods of degradation. It was known for its immense walls, its dimensions, and the laborious cultivation and fertility of the surrounding Babylonia. The city's origins are linked to Sargon of Akkad (3800 B.C.), who is stated to have built sanctuaries there to Ea and Anunit; some sources suggest Sargon may have named the city and made it his capital. After centuries as a provincial town, it became the capital of the first dynasty of Babylon and later the empire of Khammurabi (2250 B.C.), subsequently serving as the holy city of western Asia.

The Babylonian kings possessed an immense command of human labor, reflecting a collective civilization in Asia that lacked individual freedom or development. The city was surrounded by deserts and predatory tribes.

## The Assyrian Empire
The Assyrians, a Semitic people settling near cities such as Assur and Nineveh, were known for their long noses, thick lips, great beards, ringletted long hair, tall caps, and long robes. Initially engaged in mutual raiding with the Hittites, they were once conquered by Sargon I before regaining their freedom. For a period, Tushratta, King of Mitanni, captured Nineveh.

The Assyrians developed military art to a high pitch, utilizing the horse and war chariot. Under Tiglath Pileser I (approximately 1100 B.C.), they conquered Babylon. Later, the "New Assyrian Empire" (or Second Assyrian Empire) was founded by Tiglath Pileser III, who conquered Babylon in 745 B.C. This second empire was characterized by greater consolidation and the introduction of political centralization. Conquered provinces were organized under a bureaucracy where districts paid fixed tribute and provided military contingents. The Assyrian forces became a disciplined standing army, and the state adopted a doctrine of "blood and iron" to reduce the civilized world into a single empire.

## Political and Religious Conflict
A central theme in the politics of this region was the tension between the monarchy and the wealthy priesthoods. To secure a tenure of power in Babylon, a monarch typically had to "take the hand of Bel," a ceremony of adoption by the priesthood of the god Bel-Marduk. This act legitimized the claimant as the inheritor of the old Babylonian empire.

Tiglath-pileser III achieved this legitimacy in 729 B.C. However, Sennacherib, the son of Sargon II, failed to secure the support of the priesthood and never underwent the ceremony. This led to constant revolt in Babylonia, culminating in 689 B.C. (or 691 B.C.) when Sennacherib destroyed the holy part of the city, razing its walls, temples, and palaces. This act shocked the religious conscience of western Asia. Sennacherib was later assassinated by one of his sons. His successor, Esarhaddon, sought to make peace with the god by rebuilding the temple and restoring Bel-Marduk.

## Later Chronology and Decline
The later history of these empires is tracked through several records. The Ptolemaic Canon, the Babylonian Chronicle, and the Assyrian Eponym Lists provide dates for kings from the accession of Nabonassar (747 B.C.) to the conquest of Babylon by Alexander the Great in 331 B.C. Nabonassar, also called Belesis or Baladan, reigned for twelve years and inaugurated a famous astronomical epoch.

Other notable figures include:
*   **Sargon II:** An Assyrian usurper who armed his troops with iron, a technology communicated to the Assyrians by the Hittites.
*   **Sennacherib:** Led an army to Egypt's borders but was defeated by plague.
*   **Assurbanipal (Sardanapalus):** The grandson of Sennacherib, who conquered Egypt in 670 B.C. He later besieged Babylon after a revolt led by his brother, Samas-sum-yukin. While he purified the city, he did not "take the hands" of Bel.
*   **Merodach-baladan:** A Kaldæan prince who entered Babylon and was crowned king, resisting the Assyrians for twelve years before fleeing to the southern marshes.

The Assyrian empire, which lasted between 520 years (according to Herodotus) and 1,360 years (according to Ktêsias), eventually saw the fall of Nineveh to the Medes shortly before 600 B.C. Both the Medes and the Chaldæans of Babylon, who were originally subjects of the Assyrians of Nineveh, eventually acquired independence before being embodied in the Persian empire. The second Assyrian empire lasted 210 years, ending when Cyrus published the edict permitting the sons of Israel to return to their own country after seventy years of captivity at Babylon.
