# The Neo-Babylonian Empire and the Rise of Persia

## The Hegemony of Babylon
Following the fall of Assyria, the seat of empire transferred to Babylonia. Under Nabopolassar and subsequently his son, Nebuchadrezzar II, Babylon regained its status as the mistress of the civilized world. Nebuchadrezzar II reigned for 43 years; fragments of his annals record an invasion of Egypt in 567 B.C., mentioning "Phut of the Ionians." 

During this era, the Semitic peoples—including those of Babylon, Assyria, and Syria—dominated the civilized world. These peoples spoke mutually intelligible languages and controlled global trade. This commercial network was bolstered by the Phoenician mother cities of Tyre and Sidon, which established colonies in Africa, Sicily, and Spain. Carthage, founded before 800 B.C., grew to a population exceeding one million and became for a time the greatest city on earth, with ships reaching the Atlantic and Britain.

## Society and Economy of Babylon
Babylon was regarded as the first city in Western Asia, characterized by its immense walls and the Babylonian kings' command of vast human labor. The civilization was collective in nature, lacking individual freedom or development. 

Economically, Babylon was financially superior to Egypt, though it operated without coined money. Trade was conducted primarily through barter, a system that persisted in other great empires, such as China, well into later eras. For exchange, gold and silver were used in the form of ingots. Bankers existed prior to coinage, stamping their names and the weight of the metal onto these lumps. Travelers and merchants often carried precious stones to pay for necessities, while servants and workers—mostly slaves—were paid in kind rather than money. Notably, hens and eggs were absent from the Babylonian diet, arriving from the East around the time of the last Assyrian empire.

## Religious Evolution and the Marduk Cult
The religious landscape of Babylon underwent a transformation led by the city's priests. They re-edited hymns and myths originally celebrating Bel of Nippur to favor the Marduk cult. In this process, the role of the creator of the world and mankind was transferred from Bel to Marduk. Specifically, the conquest of the monster Tiamat, which symbolized primeval chaos, was ascribed to Marduk. 

As the Neo-Babylonian Empire progressed, Marduk absorbed the attributes and prerogatives of other deities, including Ninib, Nergal, Shamash, Adad, Ea, and Bel. This created an impression that Marduk was the only real deity, with other gods serving as various manifestations of himself, representing a gradual crystallization of a monotheistic tendency centered on the solar deity of Babylon.

## The Fall of Babylon and the Persian Conquest
The final Babylonian king, Nabonidus, is known through a chronological tablet and an inscription detailing his restoration of the Moon-god's temple at Harran. During the sixth century B.C.—a period of intense intellectual activity globally, featuring the teachings of Confucius, Lao Tse, and Gautama Buddha—the Persian and Mede peoples began expanding their influence.

Cyrus, the "king of Anshan" in Elam, revolted against his suzerain Astyages (king of the Manda or Scythians) at Ecbatana in either 549 B.C. or 553 B.C. After the army of Astyages betrayed its king, Cyrus established himself at Ecbatana, ending the Scythian empire (which Greek writers confused with the Medes). By 538 B.C., Cyrus invaded Babylonia. Following a battle at Opis in June, the Babylonians were defeated and the city of Sippara surrendered. 

Nabonidus fled to Babylon, but on the 16th of Tammuz, the soldiers of Cyrus entered the city without fighting. Nabonidus was captured, and Kurdish guards were stationed at the gates of the temple of Bel to ensure services continued without interruption.

## The Persian Empire
Under Cyrus, the rich civilizations of Babylon and Lydia were incorporated into the Persian rule. The Greek cities of Asia Minor and the Phoenician cities of the Levant became tributary states, and Egypt was later subjected by Cambyses. By 521 B.C., Darius I, the third Persian ruler, functioned as the monarch of nearly the entire known world. His decrees were carried by couriers from Upper Egypt to Central Asia and from the Indus to the Dardanelles, creating an empire far larger in extent than any previous world empire.
