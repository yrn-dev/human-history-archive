# The Siege of Syracuse by Nikias

The siege of Syracuse was a central operation of the Athenian expedition to Sicily, characterized by early tactical successes followed by a strategic collapse attributed largely to the leadership of the Athenian general Nikias.

## Initial Operations and Tactical Successes
Upon his arrival in Sicily, Nikias delayed the commencement of the siege, retreating into winter quarters. This inaction provided the Syracusans with the opportunity to send envoys to the Peloponnesus to solicit reinforcements. The envoys received a warm reception in Corinth, where they obtained promises of aid and were accompanied by Corinthian envoys to Sparta to secure Lacedæmonian support.

When Nikias finally began his approach to Syracuse in the early spring, the Syracusan generals attempted to occupy and guard the access roads to the high ground of Epipolæ, which overhung the outer city. However, the Athenians successfully surprised the summit of Epipolæ, a victory essential for the future prosecution of the siege. The Athenians established a central work on the heights known as "The Circle."

The Syracusans attempted to obstruct the Athenians with a series of counter-walls. The first counter-wall, located south of the Athenian circle, was stormed, captured, and destroyed. Nikias subsequently occupied the southern cliff and continued the blockade south of the Circle. A second counter-work, which extended across the marsh south of Epipolæ to the river Anapus, was attacked and taken by the Athenian commander Lamachus. Although Lamachus was killed during the ensuing general battle, the Athenians emerged victorious.

Following these successes, the Athenian fleet entered the Great Harbor, and the southern portion of the blockade wall across the marsh was nearly completed. With the Syracusans offering no further obstruction and falling into despondency, the Athenians faced triumphant prospects, bolstered by the disposition of the Italian Greeks and the Sikels to favor them.

## The Arrival of Gylippus
Despite the apparent success of the siege, Nikias exhibited a comparative languor in his operations and maintained correspondence with individuals inside Syracuse. During this period, the Lacedæmonians appointed Gylippus to lead an auxiliary force to Syracuse. Gylippus was likely chosen for his familiarity with the Italian and Sicilian Greeks; his father, Kleandridas, had been a citizen at Thurii after being banished from Sparta for taking Athenian bribes.

Gylippus requested two triremes from the Corinthians to transport him to Asinê in the Messenian gulf. Although reports of Gylippus's approach reached Nikias, the general despised him and allowed him to advance unobstructed. Gylippus landed at Himera and levied an army, marching across Sicily to Syracuse. 

The impact of Gylippus's arrival was decisive. While Athenian despatches in the spring and summer of 414 B.C. had described the siege as being on the point of a triumphant close, a subsequent communication announced that Gylippus had arrived with a powerful army. This forced the Athenians onto the defensive and halted the progress of the siege.

## Strategic Failures and Aftermath
The failure of the expedition is traced to the remissness and oversight of Nikias. He wasted eight months in trifling measures before beginning the siege, which not only allowed the Syracusans time to seek aid but also led to the wear and tear of the Athenian naval armament. His failure to take precautions against Gylippus, despite being forewarned, shifted the Athenian position from triumph to a state bordering on ruin. This turn of fortune contributed to the demoralization and unmanageable temper of the Athenian soldiery.

Prior to the final collapse, Nikias had attempted to secure winter quarters at Katana, believing he could not maintain his position without more money and cavalry. During this period, he attempted to secure the gates of Messênê through a favorable party within the city. However, Alkibiadês had previously betrayed these proceedings to the philo-Syracusan party in Messênê. Consequently, the opponents of the Athenians rose in arms and held the town by force, forcing Nikias's troops to return to Naxos after thirteen days of fruitless delay.

Despite the exposure of the mendacious promises of Egesta and the futility of seeking local allies, the Athenian government persisted in the siege. This perseverance is described as a "melancholy imprudence," driven by an inability to move past their confidence in Nikias and their ideas of Sicilian conquest. Even the addition of the energetic commander Demosthenês was unable to avert the eventual ruin.
