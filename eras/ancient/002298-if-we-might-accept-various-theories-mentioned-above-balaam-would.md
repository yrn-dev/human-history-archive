# Balaam

Balaam (Hebrew: *Bil'am*) was a Gentile seer and prophet who appears in the biblical history of the Israelites. His narrative occurs during the Israelites' sojourn in the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, at the conclusion of their forty years of wandering, shortly before the crossing of the Jordan and the death of Moses.

## Biblical Narrative and Interactions with Balak
Following the Israelite conquest of Og, king of Bashan, and Sihon, king of the Amorites, Balak, the king of Moab, became alarmed. Balak sent for Balaam to curse the Israelites. According to the accounts, Balaam initially hesitated. In one version of the narrative, God appeared to him in a dream and forbade him from going. However, after Balak sent the princes of Moab to apply further pressure, God permitted Balaam to go on the condition that he spoke only what God told him.

During his journey, Balaam set out with two servants. He encountered the Angel of Yahweh, who was initially visible only to Balaam's ass. The ass attempted to avoid the Angel, provoking Balaam's anger, and was miraculously enabled to speak to him. Once Yahweh enabled Balaam to see the Angel, he was informed that he would have been slain if not for the ass. Though Balaam offered to return, he was told to proceed.

Upon meeting Balak, Balaam warned the king that he could only speak the words provided by God. Despite Balak offering sacrifices and attempting to secure a favorable result by moving to different locations—including the top of Pisgah to view Israel—Yahweh compelled Balaam to bless the Israelites instead of cursing them. The biblical framework for this encounter consists of a narrative and seven oracular poems.

## Diverse Traditions and Interpretations
The figure of Balaam is subject to various interpretations across different sources and traditions:

*   **The Priestly Code:** This tradition presents a different account in which Balaam advises the Midianites on how to bring disaster upon Israel by seducing the people and undermining their loyalty to Yahweh. In this version, Balaam is eventually slain in battle while fighting with the Midianites.
*   **Josephus:** Josephus describes Balaam as the best prophet of his time, though one with a disposition unable to resist temptation. He views Balaam not as an idolater or a false prophet, but as an ill-disposed prophet of the true God.
*   **Philo:** Philo characterizes Balaam as a great magician. In other writings, he refers to him as "the sophist Balaam," using the figure to symbolize a "vain crowd of contrary and warring opinions."
*   **Rabbinical Traditions:** Some Rabbinical sources (Yalkut) identify Balaam with Laban, the father-in-law of Jacob. Other fables suggest he was blind in one eye, was the Elihu mentioned in Job, or served as a governor of an Ethiopian city under Pharaoh before being put to flight by Moses.
*   **Islamic Traditions:** Mahommedan accounts describe Balaam as one of the Anakim (giants of Palestine). These traditions state he read the books of Abraham, through which he learned the name Yahweh, allowing him to predict the future and receive whatever he asked from God. Some conjecture that the Arabic wise man Luqman is identical to Balaam, noting similarities in the names of their fathers and a popular etymology where "Luqman" (meaning devourer or swallower) relates to the name Balaam.

## Scholarly Theories on Origin
Modern critics have analyzed the documents (denoted as J and E) to determine Balaam's origins. Depending on the source, Balaam is identified as:
*   An Edomite or an Ammonite (in source J).
*   An Aramaean or a native of the south of Judah (in source E).
*   A Midianite (in the tradition of the Priestly Code).

Because these groups are either part of the Hebrew stock or closely connected to it, some conclude that Balaam was an ancient figure of traditions common to all Hebrews and their allies, which were later appropriated by individual tribes.

## Geography and Etymology
The etymology of the name Balaam is uncertain. Regarding his location, the text mentions Pethor. While traditionally identified as a place in Mesopotamia or a location on a tributary of the Euphrates (linked to *Pitru* in the inscriptions of Shalmaneser II), some scholars suggest the "River" mentioned is actually a ravine (*nahal*) on the southern frontier of Judea in Egypt or Musri.
