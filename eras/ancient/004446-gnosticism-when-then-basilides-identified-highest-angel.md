# Basilides and His System of Gnosticism

Basilides was a prominent exponent of Gnosticism who lived in Alexandria, likely during the first decades of the 2nd century. While Eusebius's *Chronicle* dates his first appearance to A.D. 133, evidence from Agrippa Castor—who lived under Hadrian (117-138)—suggests his activity may have begun even earlier. Basilides authored an exegetical work in twenty-four books based on a gospel of his own, though the specific identity of that gospel remains unknown. Other associated writings include *Ethika*, *Exegetika* on the prophet Parchor, and works by his son, Isidorus.

## Cosmological Structure and Emanations
The system of Basilides is characterized by a complex structure of emanations from a supreme God, referred to as the *innatus pater*. According to descriptions by the Fathers of the Church, 365 heavens with their respective spirits emanated from this supreme God. This number corresponds to the astronomical conception of the heavens' 365 daily aspects. The supreme God is sometimes called Abraxas (or Abrasax), a name with a numerical value of 365. This numerical association is also found in the name of the Persian god Mithras.

Surrounding the *Pater innatus* is a series of Hypostases. Irenaeus identifies five: *Nous*, *Logos*, *Phronesis*, *Sophia*, and *Dunamis*, while Clemens suggests that *Dikaiosune* and *Eirene* may be added. This arrangement is noted for its similarity to the *Ameshas-spentas* surrounding Ahura-Mazda. 

The world originates from seven powers, which are viewed as the lowest emanations of the supreme God. These seven world-creating angels are identified as the planetary deities of later Babylonian religion. The transition of these deities into daemonic or semi-daemonic forces in Gnostic systems suggests the influence of a stronger monotheistic Iranian religion over Babylonian beliefs.

## Christology and the Process of Redemption
Basilides' views on Jesus of Nazareth were complex and varied across sources. He recognized an earthly Jesus and denied the principle of his sinlessness. In the "double system" shared by the Egyptian school, Jesus was seen as a mere mortal and the son of Joseph and Mary, chosen as an instrument to restore the worship of the supreme Deity. According to this view, the Christ (the first of the aeons and Son of God) descended upon Jesus in the form of a dove at his baptism to direct his actions. When Jesus was delivered to the Israelites, the immortal Christ forsook the earthly body and returned to the pleroma (the world of spirits).

Other accounts present different perspectives:
*   **The Phantasm:** Irenaeus reports that the Saviour appeared only as a phantasm.
*   **The Diakonos:** Clemens refers to the Saviour as *diakonos* or *pneuma diakonoumenon*.
*   **The Nous:** Irenaeus states the supreme God sent his *Nous* (also called Christ) into the world to address the confusion therein.

Because of the belief that the *Diakonos* descended upon Jesus at his baptism, followers of Basilides celebrated the day of the baptism (*epiphaneia*) as a high festival.

## Salvation and Magic
In the system of Basilides, salvation is linked to the restoration of all things and the "great separation of the sexes," where that which was combined for evil is once again separated. 

Among later followers, magic became a central element. They believed that knowing the secret names of the rulers of the various heavens would allow a person to pass through those heavens after death to reach the supreme God. Consequently, redemption was conceived as the revelation of mystic names. It was believed that Christ possessed a mystic name (*Caulacau*) that enabled his descent to earth and subsequent ascent to the Father. This magical tendency is associated with the use of Abraxas gems.

## Distinctions and Influences
Basilides' Gnosticism is described as being closest to Persian dualism in its final form and is closely related to the doctrines of Satornil (Saturninus). He is distinguished from most other Gnostic sects—except perhaps Jewish-Christian Gnosticism—by the total absence of the idea of a fall within the godhead or the figure of the fallen female god, Sophia Achamoth. His work is seen as a development of Iranian dualism that preceded the system of Mani.

Additionally, Basilides is categorized among the Docetes, a group that taught the non-reality of the material body of Christ, granting him only a "seeming" body.
