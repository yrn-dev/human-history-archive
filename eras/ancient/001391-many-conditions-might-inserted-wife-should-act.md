# Marriage and Matrimonial Law

Marriage, also referred to as matrimony, is defined as the act, ceremony, or process that constitutes the legal relationship between a husband and wife, or as a moral, legal, and physical union between a man and a woman for the purpose of establishing a family. Historically, the institution is viewed as having progressed through three stages: a physical stage of unregulated relations, a legal stage where permanence was secured by the husband's property rights in his wife or wives, and a personal or moral stage where the relationship approximates two individuals who are equal before the law.

## Legal Frameworks and Contracts
In many legal systems, marriage takes the form of a contract where the mutual assent of the parties is the indispensable feature. For example, under certain Codes, a marriage contract is required for a woman to be recognized as a wife. These contracts often specify the consequences for either party should they repudiate the other. Such contracts may include specific conditions, such as a requirement that the wife serve as a maidservant to a first wife or to her mother-in-law.

In France, the solemnization of marriage requires the publication of banns for ten days, including two Sundays, listing the names, occupations, and domiciles of the parties and their parents. A three-day interval must follow before the marriage can occur. The ceremony is conducted by a registrar or civil officer who reads the relevant code chapters to the couple and receives their declarations in the presence of four witnesses.

## Property, Debt, and Dower
The financial relationship between spouses varies by legal jurisdiction. In some systems, the couple forms a unit for external responsibility, particularly regarding debt. A husband may be responsible for debts his wife contracted even before marriage; however, a proviso can be inserted into the marriage contract stating the wife shall not be seized for her husband's pre-nuptial debts, in which case the husband is not responsible for her pre-nuptial debts. Both parties remain responsible for all debts contracted after marriage.

A husband may provide his wife with a settlement via a deed of gift, granting her a life interest in part of his property. While she may be permitted to bequeath this to a favorite child, she cannot leave it to her own family. Additionally, a woman may remain a member of her father's house despite being married.

In the United Kingdom, the concept of "dower" refers to a widow's life interest in one-third of her husband's lands. Historically, there were five types of dower, including *ad ostium ecclesiae* (at the church porch) and *ex assensu patris*. The Dower Act 1834 governs women married after January 1, 1834, attaching dower only to lands the husband possessed for an estate of inheritance at his death. This right is subject to the husband's debts and contracts and can be defeated by a declaration in his will.

## Marital Structures and Legitimacy
While monogamy is the general rule in some systems, specific arrangements exist for childless wives. A wife may provide her husband with a maid to bear children; these children are reckoned as the wife's, and she remains the maid's mistress. If a wife does not provide such a maid, the husband may take a concubine. A concubine is a free woman and a wife, though of a lower rank than the first wife. Her children are legitimate, and she can only be divorced under the same conditions as a wife.

Children born to slave girls are free. These children can be legitimized through the father's acknowledgment before witnesses or through adoption, allowing them to share equally in the father's estate. If not adopted, the children of the first wife are given first choice in the division of the estate.

## Legislative Reforms and Special Cases
Various acts have been passed to address specific marital disputes and legalities:
* **The Summary Jurisdiction (Married Women) Act of 1895:** Allows a wife to apply for a separation order and a weekly sum (not exceeding two pounds) if her husband is convicted of assault, deserts her, or is cruel.
* **The Deceased Wife's Sister Marriage Act (1907):** Following the Colonial Marriages Act of 1906, this made marriages with a deceased wife's sister legal in the United Kingdom, though clergymen may still refuse to solemnize such unions.
* **The Marriage with Foreigners Act 1906:** Enables British subjects to marry foreigners abroad by obtaining a certificate from a registrar stating no legal impediment exists.

## Narratives of Marriage and Union
Historical and sacred accounts provide various examples of marital unions. In the account of Adam and his wife, God commanded them to eat of all plants except the tree of knowledge. A serpent persuaded the woman to taste the fruit, claiming it offered knowledge of good and evil and a life not inferior to a god. After eating the fruit, the couple became aware of their nakedness and used fig-leaves to cover themselves.

In the account of David and Saul, King Saul offered his daughter, Michal, to David on the condition that David slay Philistines. David slew many and presented the heads of six hundred Philistines to the king. Because Saul did not wish to appear a liar or treacherous, he gave Michal to David in marriage.

Other records, such as those of Marco Polo, note the practicalities of marriage and inheritance. After Marco's long imprisonment in Genoa, his father Nicolo married a new wife and fathered three sons: Stefano, Maffio, and Giovanni. Upon his release, Marco also took a wife, with whom he had two daughters, Moreta and Fantina.
