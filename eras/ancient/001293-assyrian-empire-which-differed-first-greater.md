# The Assyrian Empire

The Assyrian empire was one of the most powerful entities in the ancient world, characterized by its immense military strength and the dominance of its primary cities, Nineveh and Babylon. Historical accounts of its origins and duration vary significantly. Some authors, such as Ctesias and Justin, suggest a duration of 1,300 years, while Herodotus reduces this to 520 years. Other records indicate the empire subsisted for over 1,450 years. Some sources link the antiquity of the empire to the city of Babylon, which was built by Nimrod, a great conqueror. Most profane authors identify Ninus as the first founder of the Assyrian empire.

## The Second Assyrian Empire
The second Assyrian empire arose under Tiglath-pileser III and was distinguished from the first by its greater consolidation. This era introduced the political concept of centralization for the first time in history. The empire was organized through an elaborate bureaucracy headed by the king, with conquered provinces required to provide military contingents and pay a fixed tribute.

To support this structure, the Assyrian forces were transformed into a disciplined standing army. The empire's policy was to reduce the entire civilized world into a single empire to secure global trade and wealth for Assyria. Tiglath-pileser III achieved this by terrorizing the Medes and Armenia, breaking the power of the Hittites, and securing the Phoenician seaports and high-roads of commerce leading to the Mediterranean. He eventually became the master of Babylonia, attaining the sovereignty of Asia in the holy city of Babylon in 729 B.C.

## Military Expansion and Technology
The Assyrians became the preeminent military power of their time, expounding the "doctrine of blood and iron." This was aided by the introduction of iron from the north; the Hittites, precursors to the Armenians, first possessed iron and communicated its use to the Assyrians. The usurper Sargon II utilized this technology to arm his troops.

The empire's expansion continued under subsequent rulers. Sennacherib led an army to the borders of Egypt, though he was defeated by the plague rather than military force. His grandson, Assurbanipal (also known by the Greek name Sardanapalus), successfully conquered Egypt in 670 B.C., replacing an existing Ethiopian dynasty.

## Political Transitions and Internal Conflict
Tiglath-pileser III died in Tebet 727 B.C. and was succeeded by Ulul, who took the name Shalmaneser IV. Shalmaneser died suddenly in Tebet 722 B.C. during the siege of Samaria. His death led to the seizure of the throne by a general named Sargon, which triggered a Babylonian revolt. The Kald prince Merodach-baladan entered Babylon in Nisan and was crowned king, resisting the Assyrians for twelve years. He was eventually forced to flee to the marshes of southern Babylonia following the overthrow of the Elamites and the failure of his western allies.

Sargon, meanwhile, annexed the future kingdom of Ecbatana and captured the Hittite stronghold of Carchemish in 717 B.C., eventually gaining acceptance as king from the Babylonian priests.

## The Fall of the Empire
The decline of the empire is associated with various internal revolutions and the leadership of princes such as Sardanapalus, whose "dissolute effeminacy" is cited as a factor in the empire's fall. The empire eventually fractured into three considerable kingdoms:
* The kingdom of the Medes, restored to liberty by Arbaces.
* The Assyrians of Babylon, given to the governor Belesis (also known as Nabonassar or Baladan).
* The Assyrians of Nineveh, whose first king was Ninus the younger.

The city of Nineveh was taken by the Medes shortly before 600 B.C. The Medes had originally been subjects of the Assyrian empire but revolted to maintain their independence. While some sources suggest the Median revolt occurred around 711 B.C., others, including Diodorus, state the interval between the revolt and the election of King Dêïokês lasted for "many generations."

## Cultural and Economic Context
During the seventh century B.C., the civilized world was largely dominated by Semitic rulers. The Assyrians, Babylonians, and Syrians spoke mutually intelligible languages. This period saw the trade of the world fall into Semitic hands, with Phoenician cities like Tyre and Sidon establishing colonies in Africa, Sicily, and Spain. Carthage, founded before 800 B.C., grew to a population of over one million and became one of the greatest cities on earth, with ships reaching the Atlantic and Britain.
