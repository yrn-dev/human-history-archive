# Buddhism in Ceylon and the Geography of the Island

The island of Ceylon is distinguished from the neighboring regions of British India primarily by its separate administration and the Buddhistic religion of its population. The island's population is composed largely of 2,000,000 Sinhalese and 954,000 Tamil, along with smaller numbers of Arabs, Malays, Chinese, Eurasians, and others.

## Geography and Topography
The physical landscape of Ceylon varies significantly by elevation. The highest point in the island rises to approximately 9,000 feet above the sea. The slopes of the mountains are densely covered with forest. In contrast, the lower levels of the island possess a climate and cultivation patterns that are quite similar to regions located at the same latitude on the Malay peninsula.

## The Spread of Buddhism to Ceylon
Buddhism was spread to Ceylon through the exertions of Asoka, a zealous Buddhist emperor of the Maurya dynasty who ruled from 264 to 227 or 228 B.C. Asoka, who had previously renounced war after the horrors of his invasion of Kalinga in 255 B.C., sought "conquests of religion" rather than military conquest. He dispatched missionaries to several regions, including Persia, Alexandria, Kashmir, and Ceylon.

The influence of other regions also played a role in the island's religious history. Káshmir was a significant headquarters for a particular school of Buddhist Doctrine and served as a source from which the religion spread. The importance of this connection is evidenced by the account of the Mahawanso regarding the consecration of the great Tope at Ruanwelli by King Dutthagamini (B.C. 157), which notes that 280,000 priests arrived from Káshmir—a number greater than that assigned to any other country except one. This relationship was so profound that Buddhists in Burma looked to Ceylon with a similar feeling of reverence as others once held for Káshmir.

## Buddhist Scholarship and Literature in Ceylon
Ceylon served as a critical center for the preservation and translation of Buddhist scriptures. In the 4th century A.D. (approximately 390 A.D.), a celebrated Buddhist writer and Brahmin by birth named Buddhaghosa traveled from north India to Anuradhapura in Ceylon. He was induced to make this journey by his teacher, Revata, because the commentaries on the scriptures had been preserved in the Sinhalese language.

Under the guidance of Sanghapala and with the consent of the local fraternity, Buddhaghosa spent many years translating these commentaries into Pāli. During his time in Ceylon, he wrote the *Visuddhi Magga* (the Path of Purity), which serves as a summary of Buddhist doctrine, and rewrote a commentary on the principal psychological manual found in the *Pitakas*.

## Core Concepts and Practices
The Buddhist faith, as propagated by Emperor Asoka, is described as purely ethical and independent of ritual or theology, serving as a code of morals for laymen. Asoka himself formally joined the Buddhist community as a layman in the eleventh year of his reign and later "set out for the Great Wisdom" (*Sambodhi*), which is the technical term for entering the eightfold path to Nirvana.

In other Buddhist populations, such as those in Burma, the chief religious principle is the acquisition of merit for the next incarnation through good works in the current life. These acts of religious merit include:
* The bestowal of alms.
* Providing offerings of rice to priests.
* The founding of monasteries and the erection of pagodas.
* The construction of rest-houses or bridges for the convenience of travelers.
