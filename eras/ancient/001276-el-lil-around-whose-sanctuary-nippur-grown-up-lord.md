# El-lil

El-lil (also written as En-lil) was a primary deity of ancient Babylonian religion and the patron deity of the city of Nippur, which grew up around his sanctuary. He is identified as the "older Bel," with "Bel" serving as a title meaning "lord" or "master" *par excellence*. The name En-lil is composed of a first element meaning "lord" and a second element presumably meaning "power" or "might."

## Role and Attributes
In the earliest periods of Babylonian culture, El-lil was regarded as the lord of the ghost-land. His divine gifts to humanity consisted of incantations and spells that compelled spirits, whether evil or good, to obey. The domain governed by El-lil was a mountain, and the creatures he created lived underground.

As the head of an extensive pantheon, El-lil was accorded several high titles, including "father of the gods," "king of heaven and earth," and "king of lands." In later religious developments, a doctrine of a triad of gods emerged to symbolize the three divisions of the universe: water, heaven, and earth. In this system, the province of earth was assigned to Bel (El-lil).

## The Sanctuary at Nippur
The chief temple of El-lil at Nippur was known as E-Kur, which signifies "mountain house." This edifice was of such high sanctity that Babylonian and Assyrian rulers continued to restore and embellish it into the latest days. The E-Kur eventually became the designation for the entire sacred precinct in Nippur, which included a court of chapels and temples dedicated to other gods and goddesses.

The "mountain house" is believed to have been a lofty structure, possibly a staged tower built to imitate a mountain, with the god's sacred shrine located at the top. This tower also bore the specific name "Im-Khar-sag." Some historical perspectives suggest that the memory of this great brick tower at Nippur is preserved in the story of the Tower of Babel.

## Consort and Family
The consort of El-lil was the goddess Nin-lil (also known as Belit, meaning "the lady *par excellence*"). Her name Nin-lil is interpreted as "lady of power." She was also called Nin-Khar-sag, or "Lady of the mountain," a title relating to the Im-Khar-sag tower. Due to her position as the wife of Bel, she held the titles "mother of the gods" and "ruler of heaven and earth." While she possessed her own temple in Nippur and received votive offerings and the dedication of fortresses, she remained overshadowed by her male consort.

Additionally, the moon-god of the city of Ur is identified as the son of El-lil of Nippur, suggesting that Ur may have been colonized from Nippur.

## Political and Religious Evolution
Prior to 3000 B.C., Nippur served as the center of a significant political district, and it was during this early period that El-lil began to be designated as Bel. The influence of Nippur was extensive; for example, Lugal-zaggisi, the founder of the first known empire in Asia, dedicated hundreds of stone vases to El-lil of Nippur to commemorate a kingdom extending from the Mediterranean (the Upper Sea) to the Persian Gulf (the Lower Sea of the Tigris and Euphrates).

However, as Babylon rose politically to become the center of a great empire, Nippur lost its political prerogatives. The attributes and titles of En-lil were transferred to Marduk, who became the "Bel" of later days. This absorption of power occurred at the expense of El-lil's prestige, and after the reign of Khammurabi, the cult of Marduk eclipsed that of Bel.

Despite this decline, the older Bel did not vanish. During the Cassite control of Babylonia (c. 1750-1200 B.C.), the cult of the older Bel experienced a renaissance. Furthermore, because of his place in the triad of gods, Bel maintained a rank independent of political shifts. Nippur remained a sacred city and a place of pilgrimage for both Babylonian and Assyrian rulers, including Assyrian kings up to the days of Assur-baui-pal.
