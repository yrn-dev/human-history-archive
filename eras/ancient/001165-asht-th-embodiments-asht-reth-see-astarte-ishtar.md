# Ashtoreth

Ashtoreth (also appearing as Astarte and Ishtar) is a Semitic goddess who represents the great female principle. In the biblical record, she is known as Ashtoreth, while in Phoenician contexts she is referred to as Astarte. She served as the female counterpart to the Baal of the Phoenicians and Canaanites, as well as the Dagon of the Philistines.

## Nature and Attributes
Ashtoreth is characterized as a great nature-goddess. Her primary attributes are those of reproduction and fertility. In the old Semitic world, she assumed various local forms, leading to her identification and fusion with the deities of other nations. These include the Greek goddesses Aphrodite, Artemis, Diana, Juno, and Venus. As a patroness of the hunt, she was specifically identified with Artemis. Classical writers assigned her the epithets Urania and Caelestis, and her associated star was the planet Venus.

The goddess is associated with several sacred animals. These include the doves of Ascalon and the Phoenician sanctuary of Eryx, as well as the gazelle or antelope, which links her to the Arabian Athtar and the goddess of love. Some scholars, such as Robertson Smith, argue she was originally a sheep-goddess, citing the biblical phrase "Astartes of the flocks" (Deut. vii. 13) to refer to offspring.

## Cult Practices and Symbolism
The cult of Ashtoreth and the Baals was characterized by licentiousness and gross sensuality. These practices were rooted in primitive notions of "sympathetic magic," based on the belief that the powers of nature could be aided or controlled through such means.

In terms of iconography, figurines of the Ishtar-Astarte type are generally depicted as nude. However, some representations include horns; a model of Astarte with ram's horns was unearthed at Gezer. This has led to theories regarding her status as a lunar goddess, supported by the place-name Ashteroth-Karnaim ("Ashteroth of the horns") in Gilead and testimony from late writers like Herodian and Lucian. Other theories suggest the lunar association may have been influenced by the Egyptian goddesses Hathor and Isis.

## Geographic Distribution and Temples
The worship of Ashtoreth was widespread across the ancient Near East and beyond:
*   **Phoenicia and Carthage:** She had temples at Tyre and Sidon, and her worship was later transplanted to Carthage.
*   **Palestine:** The Philistines likely venerated her at Ascalon. Among the Israelites, her cult was significant; King Solomon built a high-place for her in Jerusalem, which remained until the reign of King Josiah.
*   **Syria:** She is linked to the Syrian deity Atargatis (the "fish-goddess"). While the two were historically distinct, they were often fused. A temple at Carnion in Gilead (the Atargateion) is likely identical to the temple of Astarte at Ashtaroth-Karnaim.
*   **Egypt:** Astarte was introduced into Egypt, where she had a temple at Memphis.
*   **Other Regions:** Through Syrian merchants, her cult reached Greece and Italy, with numerous inscriptions found at Delos. It was also introduced to Sicily by mercenary troops and slaves, eventually reaching the northern limits of the Roman Empire.

## Related Deities and Variations
Ashtoreth appears in various compound forms across different cultures. The Moabites formed a compound deity known as Ashtar-Chemosh. In Babylonia and Assyria, the prototype is Ishtar; in Assyria, Ishtar frequently appears as the consort of the chief god, Assur. In Babylonia, priests sought to identify the consort of Marduk, Sarpanit, as a manifestation of Ishtar.

The Aramaean deity Atargatis (a compound of *'At[t]ar-'athe*) is specifically associated with fish and sacred pools in Hierapolis-Mabog and Ascalon. While the South Arabian phonetic equivalent, 'Athtar, is a male deity, the female Astarte of Gabal (Byblus) was known as the *ba'alath* (the feminine form of *baal*), with her actual name often left unpronounced out of reverence.
