# Corinth, Sikyôn, and Megara: Age of the Grecian Despots

During the beginning of the sixth century B.C., the Dorian cities of Corinth, Sikyôn, and Megara—located on or near the Isthmus—underwent a similar transformation in their systems of government. This period was characterized by the rise of despots who overturned existing oligarchical structures, serving as examples of revolutionary influences that affected many cities throughout the Grecian world.

## The Rise of Despotism
Prior to the era of the despots, these cities were governed by oligarchies. In Corinth, this was the oligarchy of the Bacchiadæ. The transition to despotism was driven by a general anti-monarchical sentiment in Greece and widespread dissatisfaction with oligarchical rule. The shift toward despotism indicated an advance in the Greek mind and a tendency toward a better-organized citizenship.

The rise of these rulers was facilitated by social and military changes. Early oligarchies relied on cavalry for military force, but the emergence of free military marines and heavy-armed infantry worked against oligarchical stability. Additionally, these Dorian states were composed of unharmonious elements, specifically the conflict between the Dorian conquerors and the non-Dorian prior inhabitants. This ethnic tension played a role in the revolutions that overthrew the oligarchies in Sikyôn and Corinth, and likely in Megara as well.

## The Despots of the Three Cities
In the seventh century B.C., a despot established himself in each of the three cities:

*   **Sikyôn:** The despotism began with Orthagoras around 680–670 B.C., establishing the Orthagoridæ dynasty. Later, the city saw the violent proceedings of Kleisthenês. While Plutarch lists Æschinês of Sikyôn among despots put down by Sparta, other records suggest the Sikyonian despots were not put down by Sparta.
*   **Corinth:** Kypselus acted as the subverter of the Bacchiadæ oligarchy. He was followed by Periander, under whom Corinth attained great power. The Kypselid dynasty eventually fell after two generations.
*   **Megara:** The city was ruled by the despot Theagenês. The government of Megara during this time is described as disturbed, a condition reflected in the works of the poet Theognis.

There existed a known alliance and sympathy between the despots of Corinth and Sikyôn, though it is unknown if this extended to Megara.

## Power and Influence of Megara
During the seventh and sixth centuries B.C., Megara was more populous and powerful than it would be in later centuries of Grecian history. Its naval force and trade were once comparable to those of Athens, evidenced by colonies established as far as Sicily on one side and the Thracian Bosphorus and Bithynia on the other. Megara also possessed the island of Salamis, which it defended against the full force of the Athenians.

## Corinthian Enterprise and Colonization
Corinth was characterized by early enterprise and commerce. Its influence extended through the foundation of several colonies. Corinth founded Ambrakia and Korkyra; the latter later assisted Corinth in joint settlements at Anaktorium and Leukas. Other colonies associated with this network included Apollonia and Epidamnus.

## Later Political Shifts
In later periods, the political landscape shifted. Corinth, Sikyôn, Phlius, Trœzen, and Epidaurus eventually became secondary states and allies of Sparta, though they later sought to avoid collisions between Sparta and Thebes. Corinth also experienced a period of temporary despotism under Timophanes, though the exact date is uncertain.

In military conflicts involving the Lacedæmonians, Corinth served as a central post for confederates including Athenians and Bœotians. To confine Lacedæmonian forces within the Peloponnesus, a defensive system was established across the isthmus between the two Corinthian ports of Kenchreæ and Lechæum.
