# Lincoln and the Regions of Monmouthshire and Flintshire

## Military History of Lincoln and the Northeast
During the Roman conquest of Britain, the northeast region saw the establishment of a legionary fortress at Lincoln. This was part of a broader strategic effort to subjugate the wilder tribes and hills of Yorkshire and Wales, a process that involved thirty years of intermittent hill fighting between A.D. 47 and 79. The Roman method of conquest relied on placing small detached forts in strategic positions, each garrisoned by 500 or 1,000 men.

Progress in the region was hindered by the revolt of Boadicea, which caused the government to hesitate in moving its garrisons forward, and the withdrawal of the Fourteenth Legion around A.D. 67. However, the decade between A.D. 70 and 80 proved decisive. With the addition of Legio II Adiutrix, three able generals achieved the first conquest of Yorkshire. During this period, the legionary fortress at Lincoln was substituted for a new fortress at York.

## Monmouthshire and Flintshire: Conquest and Industry
Monmouthshire and Flintshire were among the areas early overrun during the Roman expansion. Flintshire, in particular, was noted for its lead mines. In the first two centuries of the Empire, lead was worked in several districts, including Flintshire, Shropshire, Somerset, and Derbyshire.

In later history, Flintshire continued to be a center of mineral extraction and industry. The town of Bagillt, located in the ancient parish of Holywell and 14-1/2 miles from Chester, became important due to its collieries and works producing iron, lead, zinc, and alkali. The great Minera serves as the principal lead mine in the area. Other natural resources in the region include limestone, china stone at Brymbo, sandstone for hones at Cefn Rhiwabon, and millstone grit. Coal mines are also located at Brymbo, Ruabon, and Chirk.

## Geography and Economy of Flintshire
The uplands of the region are uncongenial for the growth of corn; consequently, the land is used for rearing black cattle, sheep, and ponies, which are fattened in the Midlands of England before being sold in London. The tilled land is primarily occupied by turnips and oats rather than barley, wheat, or potatoes.

The county is fairly wooded and supports several farmers' clubs, including the Flintshire and Denbighshire club. The region has been opened up by various railway lines, including the London & North-Western railway (Holyhead line) and its branches in the Clwyd and Conway valleys. Other connections include lines from Wrexham to Connah's Quay and from Denbigh to Ruabon via Corwen and Ruthin. Additionally, the Holyhead road from London runs through the valley of Llangollen.

Other local industries include the production of paving flags at Nantglyn, slabs and slates at Rhiwfelen and Glyn Ceiriog, and woollen manufactures in Llansantffraid (St Bridgit's) and Llangollen. The Ceiriog valley also houses a dynamite factory.

## Notable Figures Associated with the Regions
Several prominent individuals are linked to these areas. Lloyd Kenyon, 1st Baron (1732–1802), who served as the lord chief-justice of England, was born at Gredington, Flintshire. He was educated at Ruthin grammar school before entering Lincoln's Inn in London in 1750.

In the religious history of Lincoln, Edward King (1829–1910) served as the bishop of Lincoln starting in 1885. His tenure was marked by a prosecution for ritualistic practices between 1888 and 1890, a case in which Horace Davey, Baron Davey, served as counsel for the promoters.

In the 17th century, Robert Pierrepont (1584–1643), the earl of Kingston-upon-Hull, was appointed lieutenant-general of several counties, including Lincoln. He was taken prisoner while defending Gainsborough and died in July 1643.
