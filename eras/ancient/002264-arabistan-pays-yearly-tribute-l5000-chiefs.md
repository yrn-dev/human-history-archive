# Tribute and Yearly Exactions in World History

Throughout various eras and civilizations, the practice of tribute—a compulsory payment or service rendered by one party to another—has served as a primary mechanism for imperial administration, political submission, and religious commemoration. These exactions have taken many forms, including currency, agricultural produce, human labor, and ritual offerings.

## Imperial and State Tributes

In the Persian Empire, Dareios established a structured system of twenty provinces, known as satrapies, each governed by a ruler. He appointed specific tributes to be paid yearly based on race, often grouping border populations with the chief races. Payments were standardized by weight: those paying in silver used the Babylonian talent, while those paying in gold used the Euboïc talent. The Babylonian talent was equal to eighty-seven Euboïc pounds. This formalized system contrasted with the reigns of Cyrus and Cambyses, during which tribute was not fixed and was instead provided as gifts.

In the Athenian empire, tribute was initially set at six hundred talents yearly before the Peloponnesian War. This amount was reviewed and the apportionment corrected every fifth year. Over time, the tribute became more burdensome, coinciding with the Athenians transitioning from chiefs to despots and increasing the use of tribute-gathering ships, inspectors, and garrisons.

In Carthage, the state was required to pay an annual tribute to the Romans. During a period of financial crisis caused by the embezzlement of public funds by magistrates and chief men, Hannibal implemented a reformation. By conducting an exact estimate of public revenues and eliminating fraud among the officers managing the funds, he enabled the republic to pay the Roman tribute without imposing new taxes on private citizens.

## Regional and Tribal Exactions

In Persia, the Bakhtiari—a great nomad tribe divided into the Haft-lang and Chahar-lang divisions—occupy a province extending from Chaharmahal in the east to near Shushter in the west. This province is under the governor-general of Arabistan and pays a yearly tribute of approximately L5000.

In ancient Nubia, after the capture of Dongola by Moslems in 652, the country was placed under a tribute known as *bakt*. This required the yearly sending of 400 men to Egypt. While this tribute was paid when enforceable, the Nubians occasionally regained the upper hand, such as in 737 when King Cyriacus marched into Egypt to address grievances of the Copts. By the close of the 10th century, the Nubians appear to have regained almost complete independence.

In the region of Elis, the Lepreates had previously agreed to pay one talent yearly as tribute to the Olympian Zeus (administered by the Eleians) in exchange for aid against Arcadian enemies. This payment was suspended during the Peloponnesian War by agreement of the confederacy, but a dispute arose when Elis attempted to resume the collection after the war, leading the Lepreates to seek the protection of Sparta.

In Japanese tradition, the tribe led by Yachimata no Orochi (the "eight-headed serpent") invaded a province and took possession of the hills and valleys of the river Hi. This tribe demanded a yearly tribute of females from the local population.

## Religious and Ritual Tributes

Tribute has also been utilized for religious and commemorative purposes. The philosopher Epicurus left property and funds to trustees to maintain several yearly tributes: an offering in commemoration of his parents and brothers, expenses for his own birthday celebration on the 7th of Gamelion, and a social gathering on the 20th of every month in honor of himself and Metrodorus.

## Tributes in Judea

Under the decrees of Caius Cæsar, the country of the Israelites (excepting Joppa) was ordered to pay a yearly tribute for the city of Jerusalem. This payment was exempt during the seventh year, known as the sabbatical year, because the land was not sown and no fruit was received from trees. The tribute was paid in Sidon during the second year of the sabbatical period, consisting of one-fourth of what was sown. 

Additionally, the Israelites were required to pay tithes to Hyrcanus and his sons. Hyrcanus, as ethnarch, also received a tribute from those occupying the land and from exports to Sidon totaling twenty thousand six hundred and seventy-five modii annually, excluding the sabbatical year. A further decree allowed the Israelites to deduct a corus from their tribute every second year the land was let during the sabbatical period, and stipulated that the tribute should not be let to farm nor remain permanently fixed.
