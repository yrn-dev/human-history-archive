# Noise and Thunder

In the human record, noise and thunder are documented across linguistic studies, historical military accounts, and mythological narratives, appearing as both natural phenomena and products of human conflict.

## Linguistic Composition and Terminology
In certain linguistic structures, specifically those characterized by polysyntheticism, complex terms are formed through the combination and contraction of juxtaposed words. An example of this process is found in the formation of the word *odots*, meaning "thunder," which is created from the combination of *odei* ("cloud") and *ots* ("noise"). Similarly, the term *belaun*, meaning "knee" or the "front of the foot," is derived from the combination of *belar* ("forehead") and *oin* ("foot").

## Thunder in Mythology and Nature
In Greek mythology, thunder is closely associated with the deity Zeus. To strengthen his position against the Titans, Zeus released the Cyclôpes from Tartarus, and in return, they supplied him with lightning and thunder. The resulting combat lasted ten years and convulsed all nature; the shock and noise were felt by Gæa, Pontus, and the distant Oceanus. Zeus later used thunder to strike down the Titans into Tartarus in a fit of wrath following the death of Zagreus, the "horned child." Zagreus had been predestined to succeed Zeus in supreme dominion and in the handling of the thunderbolt.

In historical accounts, thunder is frequently linked to violent storms that influenced the outcome of events. During a campaign against King Porus, Alexander encountered a terrible storm of rain accompanied by lightning and thunder; despite several of his men being struck dead, he continued his crossing of the river Hydaspes. In another instance, a violent storm with thunder and lightning intervened during a battle between Timoleon's Greeks and the Carthaginians. The storm pelted the Carthaginians in their faces, and the noise of the wind and hail rattling against their armor prevented them from hearing the orders of their officers.

## Noise in Warfare and Conflict
The archive contains numerous accounts of the "terrific noise" associated with modern and ancient warfare. In the context of aerial combat, pilots have reported a "constant turmoil of thunder and lightning" caused by explosions and impacts. The "eternal din" of rifle fire and the rattle of machine guns are described as sounds that can overstrain the strongest nerves.

On the ground, the sounds of war are described as a "small inferno," consisting of the "thunderous roar" of bombs and the steady "pup-pup" of machine guns. In some instances, the sound of artillery is audible from great distances; for example, the "dull but distinct thunder of guns" has been heard from sixty kilometres or more away.

In ancient military strategy, noise was used as a tactical tool. Alexander is recorded as having caused "a great noise and disturbance" in his camp daily to lead the enemy to disregard his actual movements.

## Auditory Perception and Experience
The experience of sound varies based on the observer's perspective. The blind bard Homer is noted for being "audile" rather than visual, describing the world through sounds such as the noise of a chariot passing through a gate, the "twanging arrow," and the "far-thundering sea."

In other contexts, noise is described as a disruptive or atmospheric element, such as the "broken noises" of a restaurant or the "hullabaloo" of the ocean, where great waves are described as breaking "thundering" over vessels.
