# Twentieth and Twenty-first Years of the War—Intrigues of Alcibiades

## Peloponnesian Naval Maneuvers at Rhodes
During the twentieth and twenty-first years of the war, the Peloponnesians sought to expand their influence by sailing to Rhodes. This decision followed an invitation from several principal men on the island. The Peloponnesians aimed to secure Rhodes due to its strength in land forces and its significant number of seamen. Furthermore, by gaining control of the island, they believed they could maintain their fleet through their own confederacy, thereby removing the necessity of requesting funds from Tissaphernes.

The Peloponnesian fleet, consisting of ninety-four ships, set sail from Cnidus during the winter. They first arrived at Camirus in the Rhodian country. Because the mass of the inhabitants had not been privy to the intrigue, they were greatly alarmed and fled, particularly since the town lacked fortifications. Subsequently, the Lacedaemonians assembled the inhabitants of Camirus along with those from Ialysus and Lindus. Through persuasion, the Rhodians were led to revolt from the Athenians, and the island transitioned to the control of the Peloponnesians.

## Athenian Response and Continued Conflict
The Athenians received alarm regarding the situation at Rhodes and dispatched their fleet from Samos in an attempt to forestall the Peloponnesians. Although they came within sight of the island, they arrived too late to prevent the transition. The Athenian fleet sailed to Chalce and then returned to Samos. Following these events, the Athenians continued to wage war against Rhodes, operating from Samos, Cos, and Chalce.

## Related Military and Political Contexts
The broader conflict of this era included significant events such as the revolt of Ionia and the intervention of Persia. In other theaters of Hellenic history, the war in Sicily resulted in a total destruction for the conquered, who saw their army and fleet destroyed, with very few returning home. This is described as the greatest Hellenic achievement of the war and of Hellenic history, being simultaneously most calamitous for the defeated and most glorious for the victors.
