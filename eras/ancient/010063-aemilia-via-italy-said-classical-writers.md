# BONONIA

Bononia, known in the modern era as Bologna, served as the chief town of ancient Aemilia in Italy. Its origins are subject to varying accounts; classical writers attributed the city to Etruscan origins, claiming it was founded from Perusia by Ocnus or Aucnus under the name Felsina. However, recent excavations have revealed the existence of approximately 600 ancient Italic (possibly Ligurian) huts and cemeteries dating from 800 to 600 B.C. (Umbrian period), which immediately preceded the Etruscan civilization that existed from roughly 600 to 400 B.C. Evidence of the Etruscan presence is further supported by an extensive necropolis discovered on the site of the modern cemetery, as well as others found on the Arnoaldi Veli property and in the public garden.

## Roman Integration and Infrastructure
Bononia first appears in the historical record in 196 B.C., at which time it was already possessed by the Boii and had likely undergone a name change. It became a Roman colony in 189 B.C. and later acquired Roman citizenship in 90 B.C. 

The city's strategic importance was solidified by its position as the center of the road system of north Italy. In 187 B.C., it was connected via the Via Aemilia to Placentia and Ariminum. In the same year, a road was constructed to Arretium, and another road to Aquilelia was likely established around 175 B.C. This network of roads made Bononia a vital meeting point, a characteristic that persists in the modern era, as Bologna remains an important railway center.

## Imperial Period and Administration
During the Roman Empire, Bononia continued to grow in significance. In 43 B.C., Mark Antony utilized the city as his base of operations for his campaign against Decius Brutus and settled colonists there. The Emperor Augustus later added more colonists and constructed a new aqueduct sourced from the Letta, a tributary of the Rhenus; this aqueduct was restored to use in 1881.

The city faced a significant fire in A.D. 53, after which the emperor Claudius provided a subvention of 10 million sesterces (L1,087,500). Due to its prominence, Bononia is considered to have been one of the most important cities of ancient Italy.

## Late Antiquity and Beyond
Bononia demonstrated resilience during the period of general ruin in the 5th century, successfully resisting Alaric in 410. Following this period, the city belonged to the Greek exarchate of Ravenna. While no Roman remains exist above ground today, various discoveries have been made within the city walls.

## Related Sites on the Via Aemilia
The Via Aemilia hosted several other notable stations and towns:
* **Forum Cornelii:** A station said by the 5th-century writer Prudentius to have been founded by Sulla, though its membership in the *Tribus Pollia* indicates it had Roman citizenship before the Social war. The poet Martial published his third book of epigrams while staying there. In the Lombard period, the name Imolas began to appear. The town later passed to Girolamo Riario in 1480 as a dowry for Caterina Sforza and was incorporated into the States of the Church by Caesar Borgia in 1500.
* **Forum Gallorum:** Located between Mutina and Bononia, this site is either occupied by or lies near the modern town of Castelfranco nell' Emilia. In 43 B.C., it was the site where Hirtius and Octavian defeated Mark Antony.
