# Mythology and the Religion of Babylonia and Assyria

The study of ancient Near Eastern mythology and religion, specifically that of Babylonia and Assyria, is documented through a vast array of cuneiform texts, monographs, and archaeological findings. These sources provide a framework for understanding the spiritual development of these civilizations from their earliest origins until the eventual extinction of their old cults.

## Chronological Development of Religion
The development of Babylonian-Assyrian religion is categorized into four distinct periods:

1.  **The Oldest Period:** Spanning from approximately 3500 B.C. to the era of Khammurabi (circa 2250 B.C.).
2.  **The Post-Khammurabic Period:** Occurring within Babylonia following the reign of Khammurabi.
3.  **The Assyrian Period:** Beginning around 2000 B.C. and continuing until the destruction of Nineveh in 606 B.C.
4.  **The Neo-Babylonian Period:** This era began with Nabopolassar (625-604 B.C.), the first independent ruler to lead Babylonia into a short-lived period of prosperity. This political independence ended with the conquest of Babylon and Babylonia by Cyrus in 539 B.C.

While political independence ceased in 539 B.C., the religion continued undisturbed for several centuries. The period is generally carried forward to 331 B.C., the date of the Greek conquest of the Euphrates valley, which introduced new influences that gradually led to the disappearance of the ancient cults.

## Key Scholars and Literature
Numerous academic works have analyzed the religious and mythological structures of these regions. Morris Jastrow, a Professor of Semitic Languages at the University of Pennsylvania, authored *Religion of the Babylonians and Assyrians* (also titled *Religion Babyloniens und Assyriens*), and contributed research on proper names and the figures of Bel and Belit. Other significant contributors include L. W. King, who wrote *Babylonian Religion and Mythology* (1899), *Babylonian Magic and Sorcery* (1896), and *The Seven Tablets of Creation* (1902).

A. H. Sayce provided early insights in *The Religion of the Ancient Babylonians* (1887) and later *Religions of Ancient Egypt and Babylonia* (1902). T. G. Pinches also contributed to the field with *Religion of Babylonia and Assyria* (1906) and "The Babylonian Chronicle."

## Specialized Religious Subjects
The archive's sources indicate that Babylonian and Assyrian religious study is divided into several specialized areas:

*   **Divination and Astrology:** Research by A. Boissier focuses on Assyrian presages and Assyro-babylonienne divination. Virolleaud has documented Chaldean astrology, while J. A. Craig has analyzed astrological-astronomical texts.
*   **Magic and Spirits:** The study of the supernatural includes R. C. Thompson's work on the *Devils and Evil Spirits of Babylonia* and the reports of magicians and astrologers from Babylon and Nineveh. Ch. Fossey has written on Assyrian magic, and K. L. Tallqvist examined the Assyrian incantation series *Maqlu*.
*   **Hymns and Prayers:** G. A. Reisner documented Sumerisch-babylonische hymns, and J. A. Knudtzon focused on Assyrian prayers dedicated to the sun god.
*   **Mythological Epics:** Paul Haupt has written on the Babylonian Nimrod epic.

## Primary Source Materials
Much of the knowledge regarding these religions is derived from cuneiform texts. Key collections include the series of cuneiform texts from Babylonian tablets housed in the British Museum (specifically parts v, xii, xv, xvii, xviii, and xxi). Additionally, H. C. Rawlinson edited *Selections from the Miscellaneous Inscriptions of Western Asia*. Archaeological authorities providing the physical context for these findings include A. H. Layard (*Nineveh and Babylon*), E. de Sarzec and L. Heuzey (*Decouvertes en Chaldee*), and H. V. Hilprecht (*The Babylonian Expedition of the University of Pennsylvania*).

## Comparative Mythology
Beyond the Near East, the archive contains references to broader mythological studies. Daniel Garrison Brinton (1837-1899), an American archaeologist and ethnologist, attempted to scientifically analyze the mythology of American Indians in *The Myths of the New World* (1868) and wrote *Religions of Primitive People* (1897). Other general works on the evolution of religion and primitive culture include those by E. B. Tylor, J. G. Frazer (*The Golden Bough*), and Jacob Grimm (*Teutonic Mythology*).
