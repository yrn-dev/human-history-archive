# FROM THE BLOCKADE OF POTIDÆA DOWN TO THE END OF THE FIRST YEAR OF THE PELOPONNESIAN WAR

## Origins of the Conflict
The movements leading toward the Peloponnesian War were precipitated by contests between Athens and Corinth. These conflicts occurred first indirectly at Korkyra and subsequently in a distinct and avowed manner at Potidæa. These events triggered significant movements within the Lacedæmonian alliance.

## The Blockade of Potidæa
The blockade of Potidæa represented a major financial and military commitment for Athens. To maintain the siege, Athens employed a force of heavy infantry numbering three thousand men. Each soldier drew two drachmae per day—one drachma for himself and one for his servant. This force was maintained at this specific number until the end of the siege. Additionally, sixteen hundred men served with Phormio, who departed before the blockade concluded.

The cost of the Potidæa operation, combined with the maintenance of the Athenian fleet, severely exhausted the city's revenues. During the period when the war began, Athens manned one of the largest fleets in its history, with a total of two hundred and fifty vessels on active service in a single summer. This fleet was distributed as follows: one hundred ships guarded Salamis, Euboea, and Attica, while another hundred cruised around the Peloponnese, with the remaining ships deployed at Potidæa and other locations. All ships were paid at the same rate as the infantry.

## Operations of the First Year
The first year of the Peloponnesian War was characterized by a lack of decisive results for either belligerent party. Military actions included the invasion of Attica and flying descents conducted around the coast of the Peloponnesus. While both sides inflicted mutual damage—with Attica suffering the greatest measure of loss—neither side achieved their primary objectives.

The Peloponnesians were particularly disappointed by the lack of progress regarding their most pressing wish: the relief of Potidæa. The Athenians did not find it necessary to relax the blockade of the city, leaving the position of the city unchanged.

## Strategic Outcomes
By the close of the first year, following an attempted surprise of Platæa by the Thebans, the relative strength of the warring parties in Greece remained unaltered. The results of the year's operations served to justify the anticipations of Archidamus and Periklês, while simultaneously disappointing the Corinthians and other ardent instigators of the war.
