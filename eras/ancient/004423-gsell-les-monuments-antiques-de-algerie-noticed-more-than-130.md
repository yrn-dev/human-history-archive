# Early Christian Basilicas and the Works of S. Gsell

## Development of Christian Architecture
The architecture of buildings expressly erected for Christian worship began in the 2nd or 3rd century A.D. These early structures likely consisted of an oblong interior that ended in an apse. Following the era of Constantine, the number of faithful increased enormously, leading to the addition of side aisles. This evolution caused the structures to resemble the civil basilica. Evidence for this architectural transition was discovered at S. Saba on the Aventine in Rome, where a small, early church without aisles was found beneath the floor of the existing basilica.

## Basilicas in North Africa and the East
In the Roman province of North Africa, there are abundant remains of early Christian churches. S. Gsell, in the work *Les Monuments antiques de l'Algerie* (published in Paris, 1901), noted more than 130 examples of these churches. One such example is the basilica adjoining the Forum of the Roman city of Timgad in North Africa.

Other notable basilican churches in the East include:
* The monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai, constructed by Justinian.
* The church of Dana, located between Antioch and Bir, which dates to the same period as St Catherine.
* St Philip at Athens.
* Bosra in Arabia.
* Xanthus in Lycia.
* The church of St Demetrius at Thessalonica.

## Basilicas in Europe
Basilicas of a strictly early Christian date are no longer found in Germany, Spain, or France. However, there are later examples of the basilican form:
* **France:** The "Basse Oeuvre" at Beauvais is described as very plain and may date from Carolingian times.
* **Germany:** An unaltered basilica from the time of Charles the Great is located at Michelstadt in the Odenwald. Additionally, the fine-columned basilica of St Mauritius near Hildesheim dates from the 11th century.
* **Modern Era:** The basilican form has been revived in the modern basilica at Munich.

## Scholarly Authorities and Bibliography
Various authorities provide documentation on these architectural and historical subjects. For North Africa, S. Gsell's *Les Monuments antiques de l'Algérie* is a primary source. Gsell also published a "Chronique archeologique africaine" in the *Revue africaine* of Algiers starting in 1893, as well as in the *Melanges d'archeologie et d'histoire de l'Ecole francaise de Rome* from 1895 onwards. Additionally, Gsell authored a complete *Essai sur le regne de l'empereur Domitien* in 1894.

Other significant works on early Christian and Byzantine architecture include:
* H. Leclercq's *Manuel d'archéologie chrétienne* (1907).
* Texier and Pullan's *Byzantine Architecture* (also cited as *L'Architecture b. 73*, 1864).
* Couchaud's *Choix d'eglises byzantines* (also cited as *Églises byzantines en Grèce*).
* The works of the count de Vogue, specifically regarding architecture in central Syria.
* Dom R.P. Cabrol's *Dictionnaire d'archéologie chrétienne*.
* C. Bayet's *L'Art byzantin*.
