# Ruins of Babylon and Related Antiquities

The ruins of Babylon and the surrounding regions of Mesopotamia have been the subject of extensive study and documentation by various explorers, historians, and archaeologists. These sites provide insight into the intellectual, social, and political history of the early Babylonians and Assyrians.

## Historical and Social Context of Babylon
The early Babylonians were distinguished not only by the astronomical acquisitions of their priestly caste but also by their persevering industry, dense population, and the fertility of their country. The specific characteristics of Babylon were heavily determined by its location on the Euphrates. 

Babylon remained a center of great intellectual activity even under Assyrian monarchs. Sardanapalus, an Assyrian ruler who became "Babylon-ized," established a library consisting of clay tablets, a writing medium used in Mesopotamia since early Sumerian days. This collection is considered one of the most precious stores of historical material in the world. Later, Nabonidus, the final monarch of the Chaldean line, furthered these literary and antiquarian interests, commissioning inscriptions to commemorate the calculated accession date of Sargon I.

## Political Transition and the Persian Conquest
During the reign of Nabonidus, attempts were made to centralize the empire by bringing various local gods to Babylon and establishing temples for them. This action caused conflict with the priesthood of Bel Marduk, the dominant Babylonian god. The priesthood sought an alternative ruler and found one in Cyrus the Persian, who had previously conquered Croesus of Lydia.

In 538 B.C., after a battle outside the city walls, the gates of Babylon were opened to Cyrus, and his soldiers entered without fighting. According to the Bible, the crown prince Belshazzar, son of Nabonidus, was feasting when a hand wrote the mystical words “Mene, Mene, Tekel, Upharsin” on the wall. The prophet Daniel interpreted this as a sign that God had finished Belshazzar's kingdom and given it to the Medes and Persians. The Bible records that Belshazzar was killed that night. Nabonidus was taken prisoner, and the transition was peaceful enough that the services of Bel Marduk continued without interruption.

## Archaeological Exploration and Documentation
The ruins of the region have been noted for centuries. Among the most significant ruins of Assyria are the tower of Belus at Babylon and the hall of Chosroes at Ctesiphon. These sites were visited by the traveler Pietro della Valle. In more recent history, the ruins of Nineveh—once the greatest of the Assyrian cities—were located on the eastern bank of the Tigris, opposite the modern town of Mosul. While Herodotus and Strabo described Nineveh as destroyed, Tacitus and Ammian Marcellinus mentioned it as subsisting. Although its ruins were remarked upon by Thevenot and Niebuhr, they were not carefully examined until the work of Ainsworth, Rich, and others.

Claudius Rich is cited as providing the best modern account of Babylon through his *Memoir on the Ruins of Babylon* (1816) and *Collected Memoirs* (1839). Other significant scholarly contributions to the study of Babylon and Nineveh include:
* A. H. Layard, *Nineveh and Babylon* (1853)
* C. P. Tiele, *De Hoofdtempel van Babel* (1886)
* A. H. Sayce, *Religion of the Ancient Babylonians* (1887)
* C. J. Ball in *Records of the Past* (1890)
* F. Delitzsch, *Im Lande des einstigen Paradieses* (1903)
* F. H. Weissbach, *Das Stadtbild von Babylon* (1904)
* F. Hommel, *Grundriss der Geographie und Geschichte des alten Orients* (1904)
