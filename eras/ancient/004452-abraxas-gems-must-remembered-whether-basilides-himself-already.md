# Basilides and the Gnostic System

Basilides was a prominent exponent of Gnosticism who lived in Alexandria, likely as early as the first decades of the 2nd century. While Eusebius's *Chronicle* dates his first appearance to A.D. 133, other evidence suggests his activity may have begun earlier; Agrippa Castor, who lived under the reign of Hadrian (117-138), wrote a polemic against him. Basilides authored an exegetical work consisting of twenty-four books based on a gospel, though the specific gospel is unknown. Further writings associated with him include the *Ethika*, *Exegetika* on the prophet Parchor, and works by his son, Isidorus.

## Theological Framework and Dualism
The system of Basilides is characterized as a form of Gnosticism closely related to that of Satornil (Saturninus) and represents a development of Iranian dualism, which later influenced the religious system of Mani. Basilides is distinguished from most other Gnostic sects—with the possible exception of Jewish-Christian Gnosticism—by the complete absence of the idea of a fall within the godhead and the figure of the fallen female god, Sophia Achamoth.

His doctrine involves a sharply defined dualism, though this is scarcely evident in the accounts provided by the Fathers of the Church. In his system, the supreme God is known as the *innatus pater*. This supreme God is surrounded by a series of Hypostases, numbering between five and seven. According to Irenaeus, these include *Nous*, *Logos*, *Phronesis*, *Sophia*, and *Dunamis*; Clemens suggests that *Dikaiosune* and *Eirene* may also be included. This structure is reminiscent of the *Ameshas-spentas* surrounding Ahura-Mazda.

## Cosmology and the Name Abraxas
The cosmology of Basilides involves the emanation of 365 heavens and their accompanying spirits from the supreme God. This structure corresponds to the astronomical conception of the heavens and their 365 daily aspects. Consequently, the supreme God is referred to by the name Abraxas (or Abrasax), a name that contains the numerical value 365. This numerical connection is also found in the name of the Persian god Mithras, suggesting that speculations regarding the Perso-Hellenistic Mithras were transferred to the Gnostic Abraxas.

The world originates from seven powers, which are viewed as the lowest emanations of the supreme God. These seven world-creating angels are considered an oriental speculation, specifically linked to the planetary deities of later Babylonian religion. The transition of these deities into daemonic or semi-daemonic forces in Gnostic systems indicates the influence of the stronger Iranian monotheistic religion over Babylonian beliefs.

## Redemption and Mystic Names
Among the later followers of Basilides, magic became a determining factor. These followers maintained the names of the rulers of the various heavens as weighty secrets, believing that knowledge of these names would allow a person to pass through all the heavens to the supreme God after death. In this context, redemption was conceived as the revelation of mystic names. This belief system is associated with the use of Abraxas gems and the name Abraxas.

This perspective extended to the figure of Christ, who was believed to possess a mystic name (Caulacau) that enabled him to descend through the heavens to earth and subsequently ascend to the Father. It remains undecided whether Basilides himself introduced this magic tendency to Gnosticism.

## The Saviour and Salvation
In the Basilidian system, the supreme God sends his *Nous* (also called Christ) into the world to address the extreme confusion present there. Clemens refers to the Saviour as *diakonos* or *pneuma diakonoumenon*. There are conflicting accounts regarding the Saviour's relationship to Jesus of Nazareth:
* Irenaeus suggests the Saviour appeared only as a phantasm.
* The *Excerpta ex Theodoto* claims the *Diakonos* descended upon Jesus at his baptism in the form of a dove.

Because of this, followers of Basilides observed the day of the baptism of Jesus (the *epiphaneia*) as a high festival. Basilides himself acknowledged an earthly Jesus but denied the principle of his sinlessness.

Salvation in this system is viewed as the restoration and fulfillment of all things through the "great separation of the sexes." This process ensures that that which was combined for evil is once again separated.
