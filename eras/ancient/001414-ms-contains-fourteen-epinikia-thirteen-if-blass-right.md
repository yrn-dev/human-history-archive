# The Bacchylides Papyrus

The Bacchylides papyrus is the oldest known Greek manuscript, dating to the age of Alexander the Great. Discovered in Egypt by natives and reaching the British Museum in the autumn of 1896, the manuscript was initially found in approximately 200 pieces. It was reconstructed by F. G. Kenyon, who served as the editor of the *editio princeps* in 1897.

## Composition and Structure
The manuscript is divided into three distinct sections. The first section consists of 22 columns of writing, ending after the first eight verses of Ode xii. The second section comprises columns 23 through 29; column 23 is largely missing, containing only the final letters of two words. This section includes the remains of Odes xiii and xiv, breaking off before the conclusion of Ode xiv. The third section consists of columns 30 through 39, beginning with the mutilated opening verses of Ode xv (the *Antenoridai*) and ending after verse 11 of the final dithyramb, *Idas*.

The columns typically contain 34 or 35 lines, though the number varies between 32 and 36. Due to a lacuna between the end of column 29 and the start of column 30, it is uncertain if a collective title was prefixed to the dithyrambs. Additionally, material was likely lost after the final column.

## The Epinikia
The poems in the manuscript are categorized into two classes: Odes of Victory (*epinikion melos* or *epinikos hymnos*) and dithyrambs. The *epinikion* is a form derived from the *humnos*, which was originally a song praising a deity. Following the precedent of Stesichorus (c. 610 B.C.), who wrote hymns for heroes, the custom of writing hymns to celebrate the victories of living men emerged in the second half of the 6th century B.C. This coincided with the peak popularity of the four great Greek festivals.

The manuscript contains either thirteen or fourteen *epinikia*. The discrepancy arises from a theory by Blass, who suggests that Odes vi and vii (as numbered by Kenyon) are actually parts of a single ode written for Lachon of Ceos. These odes are distributed among various festivals:
*   **Olympian festival:** Four odes (or three, according to Blass).
*   **Isthmian festival:** Three odes.
*   **Nemean festival:** Three odes.
*   **Pythian festival:** Two odes.
*   **Petraia:** One ode, celebrating a Thessalian festival of a local character.

The *Petraia* is placed last in the manuscript. The other *epinikia* are arranged casually, following no specific chronological, alphabetical, or contest-based sequence, though the four great festivals all precede the *Petraia*. The first ode, which celebrates the victory of the Cean Argeios at the Isthmus, may have been placed first because the poet included early legends of his native island.

## Bacchylides and His Style
Bacchylides of Ceos (c. 504-430) was the nephew of Simonides and the youngest of the three great lyric poets. Before the 1896 discovery, he was known only through scanty fragments. Bacchylides is characterized as the "Cean nightingale," noted for writing simply and elegantly. While considered inferior to his rival Pindar ("the Swan of Dirce") in terms of splendor of language and originality, Bacchylides' *gnomai* (maxims) appealed to philosophical readers, including the emperor Julian.

A distinguishing feature of Bacchylides' work is his tendency to dwell on the specific incidents of a victory. For example, while Pindar's first *Olympian* and Bacchylides' fifth ode both celebrate the victory of the horse Pherenicus, Bacchylides provides a more vivid and full description of the winner's run (vv. 37-49) compared to Pindar's slight and general reference.

## Dithyrambs and Other Works
The manuscript contains six extant dithyrambs, which are odes for the festivals of heroes or gods. These poems provide valuable information regarding legendary history and the court life of the period. The last extant dithyramb is *Idas*. It is believed that Bacchylides wrote at least three additional poems of this class—focusing on Philoctetes, Laocoon, and Cassandra—which would have followed *Idas* in alphabetical order.
