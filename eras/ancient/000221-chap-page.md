# Historical Figures and Cultural Concepts

## Ancient Greek Military and Political Affairs
In 338 B.C., the Tarentines sought assistance from Sparta, their mother city, due to pressure from the Messapians. King Archidamus, the son of Agesilaus, responded to this request by sailing to Italy with a mercenary force. His operations concluded when he was defeated and killed near the time of the battle of Chæronea. 

Regarding the nature of Lacedæmonian warfare, Agis II is attributed with the saying that the Lacedæmonians do not ask how many their enemies are, but rather where they are. In the realm of philosophy, Zeno, who was from Cyprus, founded the Stoic school of Philosophy.

## The Great Kaan and the Mongol Empire
Cublay Kaan (Kúblái) held significant puissance as the Great Kaan. His reign involved conflicts with his uncle, Nayan, who led a revolt against him. The Great Kaan marched against Nayan and fought a battle against him, eventually causing Nayan to be put to death. In this execution, the shedding of royal blood was avoided. 

The geography of the era and the travels of the Polo brothers are documented in various sources. The brothers set forth from Constantinople to traverse the world, passing through Soldaia and beyond to Sarai, the city of Bolghar, and the city of Bocara after crossing a desert. Their accounts mention the Bamboo Palace, the Oirad Tribe, and the annual migration of Kúblái to Shangtu (properly Chandu). Other noted elements of this region include the Mare’s Milk Festival, the presence of Lamas—some of whom were married—and ascetics known as "Sensin."

## Norse Kings and Mythology
The saga of King Harald Grafeld and Earl Hakon, son of Sigurd, highlights the intersection of faith and tradition. Although Hakon was a Christian, he appeared to favor the old religion and spared the temples of Odin. Consequently, he is assigned a place in Valhal, the blessed abodes where he lives forever with the bright gods. 

Norse poetic tradition mentions the Fenriswolf, who is prophesied to devour the race of man from shore to shore. The records of this epoch are preserved through the works of skalds such as Einar Helgason Skalaglam, Eyvind Skaldaspiller, Kormak Agmundson, and Glum Geirason.

## Religious and Philosophical Terminology
The term "dogma" has been recorded in several pre-Christian or extra-ecclesiastical contexts. Marcellus of Ancyra identified three primary usages:
*   **Medical:** A distinction between empirical and dogmatic medicine.
*   **Philosophical:** Referring to first principles, whether practical or speculative. Cicero’s *Academics* contains strong statements regarding the inviolability of such dogmas, though these are presented as maxims of "wisdom" rather than a specific school.
*   **Legal/Political:** Referring to decrees of a legislative assembly, an emperor, a church gathering, or Old Testament law.

In the context of Islamic fraternities, various roles and lifestyles exist. All members are considered dervishes, whether they are lay or cloistered. Those granted miraculous powers by God due to their great faith are called *walis*, while begging friars are known as *fakirs*. These groups follow a theology that is a form of Sufi'ism and allow for diverse lifestyles, including solitary, monastic, secular, celibate, married, wandering, stationary, and ascetic forms.

## Miscellaneous Historical Notes
In ancient Egypt, Serapis was created as a synthesis of Apis and Osiris. In the Greek world, Hiero exercised a hereditary priesthood, a role originally brought to Gele by Têlinês. 

Regarding literary history, the "Similitudes" (chapters xxxvii-lxxi) were written between 95 B.C. and 64 B.C. This dating is based on the fact that the writer was unaware of Rome, but complained about the slaying of the righteous, an event that did not occur under the Maccabean princes before 95 B.C.
