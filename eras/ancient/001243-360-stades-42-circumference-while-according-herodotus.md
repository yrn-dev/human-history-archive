# Ancient City Measurements and Fortifications

The study of ancient urban centers often relies on the accounts of historians such as Herodotus and Ctesias, whose reports provide varying estimates regarding the scale and defenses of major cities. These measurements, while sometimes appearing exaggerated, offer insight into the architectural ambitions and strategic layouts of antiquity.

## The Fortifications of Babylon

Babylon was constructed on both sides of the river in a square configuration. The city was protected by a double row of lofty walls, though Ctesias suggests there was a third. There are significant discrepancies between historical sources regarding the circumference of the outermost wall:
* **Ctesias:** 360 stades (42 m.).
* **Herodotus:** 480 stades (56 m.), which would encompass an area of approximately 200 sq. m.
* **Q. Curtius:** 368 stades.
* **Clitarchus:** 365 stades.
* **Strabo:** 385 stades.

Even the smaller estimate provided by Ctesias implies an area of about 100 sq. m. Regarding the dimensions of the walls, Herodotus recorded a height of approximately 335 ft. and a width of 85 ft., while Ctesias estimated the height at about 300 ft. These figures are contextualized by the fact that the ruined wall of Nineveh remained 150 ft. high during the time of Xenophon.

The city's defenses included 250 towers, with spaces between them wide enough to allow a four-horse chariot to turn. The walls were constructed from bricks made using clay dug from the moat. Babylon featured 100 bronze gates, complete with bronze posts and lintels. The two inner enclosures were decorated with enamelled tiles depicting hunting scenes. Additionally, two walls ran along the quays and banks of the Euphrates; each of these had 25 gates corresponding to the number of streets they accessed. The two parts of the city were connected by a 30 ft. broad movable drawbridge supported on stone piers, with ferry-boats operating between the gate landing-places.

## The Geography of Carthage

Founded by the Phoenicians around 822 B.C., Carthage was located on the north coast of Africa in the Sinus Uticensis (Gulf of Tunis). The city's area at its greatest extent formed a triangle defined by the small harbour of Goletta, the Arab village of Sidi-bu-Said, and Cape Gamart. The highest point in this area was Sidi-bu-Said, situated on a cliff approximately 490 ft. high.

The city's layout included the citadel, Byrsa, and the chief cemetery on Cape Gamart. The harbours were located near the modern hospital of the Khram, about three-fifths of a mile south of Byrsa. A tongue of land, referred to as *ligula* or *taenia*, extended from the harbours to the mouth of the Catadas. According to Strabo, the peninsula of Carthage had a circumference of 360 stades (41 m.), while Polybius estimated the isthmus connecting the peninsula to the mainland at 25 stades (about 15,000 ft.).

## The Defenses of Athens

Following the Persian wars, Athens underwent a period of architectural perfection under the designs of Pericles, Cimon, and Themistocles. The city's defenses were reconstructed to embrace a larger area than the previous circuit. The walls enclosed several quarters: Diomea to the east, Collytus to the north, Melite to the west, and Limnae to the south and south-west.

Key gates included the Dipylon Gate (north-west), the Peiraic and Melitan gates (west), the Itonian gate (south), the Acharnian gate (north), and the Diocharean and Diomean gates (east). Thucydides recorded the circuit as 43 stades (about 5-1/2 m.), excluding the section between the junctions of the long walls to the Peiraeus; however, it is noted that the total circumference likely did not exceed 37 stades. Archaeological evidence at the Dipylon suggests the walls were constructed hastily, as sepulchral tablets were found built into the masonry.

## Other Ancient Structures

Historical records also describe massive works in Egypt, most notably the lake of Mœris. Herodotus viewed this lake as superior to the labyrinth and the pyramids. It was reportedly dug by King Mœris to regulate the irregularities of the Nile's inundations. According to some accounts, the lake had a circumference of 3,600 stadia (about 180 French leagues) and a depth of 300 feet. In the center of the lake stood two pyramids, each 300 feet high and topped with a colossal seated statue. However, other sources, such as the geographer Pomponius Mela, suggest a much smaller circumference of twenty thousand paces, or seven to eight French leagues.
