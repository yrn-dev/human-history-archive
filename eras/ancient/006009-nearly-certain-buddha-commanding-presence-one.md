# Buddha

## Identity and Origins
In European contexts, the term "Buddha" designates the last historical Buddha, whose family name was Gotama. He was the son of Suddhoda, a chief of the Sakiyas, which was one of the republican clans existing in India at the time. According to Buddhist theory, a "Buddha" is a figure who appears in the world from time to time to preach the true doctrine; once this teaching is corrupted or lost over time, it is not restored until a new Buddha appears.

While some scholars, such as Professor Wilson of Oxford in 1854, once argued that Buddha was an imaginary being and his life a myth, it is now generally admitted that a basis of truth exists beneath the mass of miraculous tales and legendary accounts that have gathered around his history.

## Chronology
The dates of Buddha's life are approximate and vary by region. In Europe, the generally accepted dates are calculated using the accession of Asoka to the throne (approximately 270 B.C.) and the interval between that event and Buddha's death, which the Ceylon Chronicles list as 218 years. This places Buddha's death at 488 B.C. and his birth at 568 B.C., based on the fact that he was eighty years old when he died. However, in Siam, Burma, and Ceylon, the dates are accepted as being roughly half a century earlier, specifically 623 B.C. for his birth and 543 B.C. for his death.

## Personal Presence and Early Disciples
Buddha is described as having a commanding presence and a deep, rich, thrilling voice, characteristics often found in successful leaders of men. He possessed deep earnestness and a thorough conviction regarding the truth of his gospel. 

His success in persuading his early followers is attributed in part to his personal influence and the prior relationship he had with five students who had undergone a similar culture. While later books claim these disciples were converted immediately, the most ancient Pali record indicates that although their reverence for him was rekindled upon his arrival, they only accepted his plan of salvation in its entirety after Buddha spent five days talking to them, both separately and together.

## Teachings and Philosophy
Buddha taught exclusively through conversation, utilizing the literary habit of his day by embodying doctrines in set phrases known as *sutras*. These served as *memoria technica*—catchwords that could be easily remembered and later expanded upon. Unlike the Brahmins, who used the dead language of Sanskrit, Buddha purposely used Pali, the ordinary conversational idiom of the time.

His teaching is characterized as subtle and metaphysical, described as being more difficult to understand than the Hebrew command to accomplish righteousness and fear God, or the Greek injunction to know and see rightly and fearlessly. This complexity meant that the teaching was beyond the understanding of even his immediate disciples, leading to its corruption and coarsening once his personal influence was removed.

A central element of his teaching was the "Eight-fold way," also known as the Noble or Aryan Path. This path emphasized:
* Mental uprightness.
* Right speech and right aims.
* Right conduct and honest livelihood.
* An appeal to generous, self-forgetful ends and a quickening of the conscience.

## Legacy and Evolution
Following Buddha's death, his sayings were collected by disciples into the Four Nikayas (collections), which reached their final form approximately fifty or sixty years later. A supplementary Nikaya was added containing verses and sayings ascribed to the disciples. These were handed down by memory for a generation or two, accompanied by running commentary, before a schism occurred in the community about one hundred years after his death.

Over time, the simple facts of Gautama's life were often replaced by fantastic legends and wonder stories. In Tibet, this evolution resulted in the establishment of a "god-king" known as the Dalai Lama (the "living Buddha") and the construction of huge temples with golden idols of Gautama Buddha, featuring priests, incense, and prostrations—elements contrary to Buddha's original practice of using only huts and avoiding the creation of priests.

In Japan, Buddhism gained a firm footing among the upper classes during the first century after its introduction, largely because Empresses were drawn to the decorative aspects of the creed. However, the general population continued to worship the Kami. For example, during a destructive earthquake in 599, prayers were offered to the Kami of earthquakes rather than the "Merciful Buddha." It was not until 642, during a devastating drought, that a Buddhist service involving the Mahayana Sutra and images of the Four Heavenly Kings and bosatsu was held, though rain only fell after the Empress worshipped the four quarters at a river source.
