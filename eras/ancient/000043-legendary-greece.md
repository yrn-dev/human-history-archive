# Legendary Greece

Legendary Greece represents the mythical world and the starting point of Grecian history. It is characterized by a period of heroic races—those who piloted the adventurous Argô, slew the monstrous boar of Kalydôn, and besieged Thêbes and Troy—before the transition into historical Greece.

## Nature of the Legendary Record
The legendary world of Greece is presented with a degree of symmetry and coherence that was not originally present. The original primitive elements—such as old ballads, stories sung at festivals, and religious narratives maintained by the Exegêtês of temples to explain local customs—have been lost. What remains is an aggregate result formed by confluent streams of fable, systematized by subsequent poets and logographers.

Early agents of this systematization included the Hesiodic poets, though they are hardly preserved. Much of the information regarding Grecian mythology derives from prose logographers such as Akusilaus, Hellanikus, Pherekydês, and Hekatæus. These writers operated during a time when the concept of Hellas as a single whole composed of fraternal sections was deeply rooted, favoring the idea of a few great families branching from a common stem over the idea of distinct indigenous origins for separate districts. Modern knowledge of this era is primarily filtered through Apollodôrus, various scholiasts, dramatic and Alexandrine poets, and their Latin imitators, with occasional glimpses provided by the *Odyssey* and the *Iliad*.

## Social and Political Structure
In legendary Greece, the king held an overruling personal ascendency. While the *boulê* (Council) and the *agora* (Assembly) existed, they were subordinate to the king and served primarily as media for the promulgation of his intentions. For example, the *agora* was summoned by Telemachus in Ithaka, and the *Iliad* depicts the *agora* as a place of submission. Justice was administered by the king or chiefs within the *agora*, though Hesiod recorded complaints of unjust judgment in his own case. This structure stands in contrast to historical Greece, where the Council and Assembly became the paramount depositaries of power.

Socially, legendary Greece was driven by the omnipotence of personal feeling toward individuals, the king, or the gods. Strong family ties existed, including respect for the wife, brothers, and kinsmen. Hospitality was a core value, particularly the reception of the suppliant and the stranger. However, the era was also marked by unrestrained ferocious and aggressive passions. Homicide was not treated as a crime against society, as it was in historical Greece, but was instead appeased through *poinê* (valuable compensation) paid to the kinsman of the murdered man. Other darker aspects of this period included the mutilation of dead bodies and the plight of orphans.

## Occupations and Economy
The Greeks of the Homeric era included various social classes, such as slaves and *thêtes*. Their commerce and navigation were limited, though they interacted with the Phœnicians, Taphians, and Kretans.

## Migrations and the Transition to History
The closing events of legendary Greece are defined by the "Return of the Herakleids" (also known as the Dorian conquest of Peloponnêsus) and subsequent emigrations. These events provide the legendary explanation for how the Hellenic aggregate was distributed:
* **The Peloponnêsus:** Shared unequally among the Dorians, Achæans, Arcadians, and Ætolo-Eleians.
* **Asia Minor and the Ægean Islands:** Settled by the Ionians, Æolians, and Dorians.

Specific migrations include the Æolic migration under the Pelopids and the Ionic emigration, which branched off from the history of Athens. The Athenian lineage involved Thêseus and Menestheus; later, the sons of Thêseus were displaced by the Neleids. Following the death of Kodrus—after which Athens had no more kings—a quarrel among the sons of Kodrus led to the emigration of Neileus. Dorian emigrations led to the establishment of colonies in Asia, including Thêra, Kôs, Knidus, and Karpathus, as well as the founding of Rhodes by Althæmenês.

## The Intermediate Darkness
Following these migrations, there exists a period of "intermediate darkness" or a "blank" lasting nearly three centuries before the dawn of authentic chronology and the first recorded Olympiad. Some scholars, such as J. Beloch, have questioned the historical value of the Dorian invasion, suggesting these legends were attempts to reconcile the political geography of Homer with that of historic Greece or to justify political ambitions during rivalries between Dorian Sparta and non-Dorian Athens and Thebes. However, others argue that the Homeric poems represent a specific phase of prehistoric culture—the "Achaean" phase—which was closed by a general catastrophe.
