# Josephus Makes An Attempt Upon Sepphoris But Is Repelled

## Strategic Fortifications in Galilee
Josephus took extensive measures to provide for the safety of Galilee against external violence, specifically anticipating a Roman assault. He oversaw the construction of walls in several locations, including Bersabee, Selamis, Jotapata, Taricheae, Tiberias, Caphareccho, Japha, and Sigo, as well as Mount Tabor. His fortification efforts extended to the caves near the lake of Gennesar in Lower Galilee, and to various sites in Upper Galilee, including Jamnith, Meroth, and the Rock of the Achabari. In Gaulonitis, he fortified Gamala, Sogane, and Seleucia.

Regarding the city of Sepphoris, Josephus allowed the inhabitants to build their own walls, as he perceived them to be wealthy and ready for war without needing further injunctions. A similar arrangement occurred in Gischala, where John the son of Levi built a wall with Josephus's consent. To complement these defenses, Josephus assembled an army of more than one hundred thousand young men from Galilee, arming them with collected old weapons.

## The Conflict with Sepphoris
The city of Sepphoris, described as the metropolis of Galilee, was eventually assisted by Roman auxiliaries consisting of six thousand footmen and one thousand horsemen under the command of Tribune Placidus. The foot soldiers were stationed within the city as a guard, while the horsemen camped in the great plain. The Roman cavalry became a significant nuisance to Josephus and his men by continually marching through and overrunning the adjoining country, intercepting those who ventured abroad and plundering areas outside the city's liberty.

In response, Josephus marched against Sepphoris. His objective was to capture the city—which he had previously encompassed with a strong wall—before the inhabitants could revolt from the rest of the Galileans, believing that if they did, the Romans would find it much more difficult to take. However, Josephus proved too weak for the task. He failed both in his attempt to force the city and in his efforts to persuade the people of Sepphoris to surrender to him.

## Consequences of the Failed Attempt
The failure of Josephus's attempt provoked the Romans to apply the law of war to the region. Driven by anger over the attempt, Roman forces engaged in a campaign of perpetual destruction, burning locations in the plain by day and night. They seized cattle, killed anyone capable of fighting, and took the weaker population into captivity as slaves. This resulted in Galilee being filled with blood and fire. During this period of calamity, the only refuge available to the people was to retire to the walled cities that Josephus had previously constructed.

## Related Military Actions and Internal Strife
Parallel to the struggles at Sepphoris, Josephus dealt with internal revolts. When the people of Tiberias revolted, Josephus recovered the city with seven of his guards and empty ships. During this action, a man named Clitus was identified as the chief author of the revolt. While Josephus intended to slay nobody, he ordered a guard named Levius to cut off both of Clitus's hands. Levius refused out of fear of the enemy. Consequently, Clitus, fearing Josephus's passion, agreed to cut off his own left hand with his sword to satisfy Josephus's demand for punishment.

Josephus also retook Gischala, which had revolted alongside Sepphoris. Although he allowed his soldiers to plunder the city, he later collected the plunder and restored it to the inhabitants. He performed the same act for the people of Tiberias and Sepphoris, intending to provide "good instruction" through the plundering while regaining their goodwill by returning their money.

## Final Retreat
The military situation in Galilee shifted further with the arrival of Vespasian. Vespasian marched to the bounds of Galilee and pitched camp, displaying his army to intimidate the enemy and encourage repentance. Josephus was stationed at a city called Garis, located near Sepphoris. Upon hearing that the Romans were near and would fight hand-to-hand, the spirits of the soldiers sank; many dispersed and fled before the enemy was even in sight. Recognizing that he lacked a sufficient army to engage the Romans and seeing that the majority of the people were willing to come to terms, Josephus despaired of the war's success. He fled to Tiberias with the few remaining men who had stayed with him.
