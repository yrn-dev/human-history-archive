# Ravenna

Ravenna is an Italian city of significant historical, architectural, and artistic importance, serving as a key site for the development of early Christian and Byzantine art and a strategic location during various imperial conflicts.

## Architecture and Early Christian Basilicas
Ravenna is home to several remarkable basilican churches. While these structures are smaller in dimension than those found in Rome, they are noted for having better proportions and design. The city's cathedral was once a noble basilica with double aisles, erected by Archbishop Ursus in A.D. 400, though it was later destroyed to make way for a building described as tasteless.

Among the most significant remaining structures are the two basilicas dedicated to S. Apollinare. The earlier of the two, S. Apollinare Nuovo, was originally an Arian church built by Theodoric between 493 and 525. It measures 115 feet in breadth and 315 feet in length, featuring a 51-foot-wide nave separated from single aisles by colonnades of twenty-two pillars. These pillars support arches with small prismatic blocks bearing sculptured crosses. The church is renowned for its mosaic ornamentation, specifically a continuous band of saintly figures—males on one side and females on the other—processing toward the Virgin Mother and Our Lord. The later and smaller S. Apollinare in Classe (A.D. 538-549), measuring 104 feet by 216 feet, shares a design so similar that it is believed both churches were created by the same architect.

Unlike the basilicas in Rome, which often utilized materials from desecrated public buildings and temples—resulting in a lack of architectural unity and the use of columns of varying heights and orders—the basilicas of Ravenna exhibit greater harmony of proportions and unity of design because the city lacked such a quarry of ready-worked materials.

## Byzantine Influence and Art
Ravenna contains several buildings that are considered truly Byzantine, including the church of San Vitale. The city is particularly noted for its mosaics. The mausoleum of Galla Placidia features mosaic work from the 5th century, and many magnificent examples from the 6th century remain in the city. One such 6th-century mosaic was transported from Ravenna to Germany two generations ago.

In terms of architectural features, the presence of galleries is a distinguishing factor. While galleries are a characteristic feature of Eastern churches (intended to provide privacy for women), they are very rare in the West. In the West, such features are questioned in antiquity at Rome, but they are never questioned at Ravenna.

## Political and Military History
Ravenna has frequently been a focal point in the struggle for imperial control. During the reign of Julian, the city surrendered without resistance to Severus, who had passed the Alps and received the support of Italian cities. This surrender, along with the capture of the Adriatic fleet, contributed to the inevitable ruin of Julian.

In the conflicts involving Justinian, the general Belisarius was involved in the recovery of Rome, Naples, and Sicily, eventually leading to the surrender of Ravenna. Later, the city served as a center of power during the period of the Exarchs, coinciding with the Lombard conquest of Italy and the distress experienced by Rome.

## Other Historical References
The city appears in various other historical and biographical contexts. The writer Plutarch mentions having seen a statue or bust of Marius at Ravenna, though he provides few other details regarding his travels in Italy. In the 17th century, the Italian poet Giambattista Marini (1560-1625), a leading figure of the "Secentisti" school, visited Ravenna while accompanying his patron, Cardinal Aldobrandini, from Rome.
