# The Hellenic People in Early Historical Times

The Hellenic people, also known as the Greeks, occupied a central territory in early historical times located south of Mount Olympus and south of the line connecting Mount Pindus with the city of Ambrakia. From this central stock, numerous outlying colonies were established. While the recorded Olympiads provide a starting point for historical discernment, delineations of Greece as far back as 1183 B.C.—the supposed epoch of the Trojan War—remain uncertified due to a lack of authentic materials.

## The Hellenic Aggregate and Unity
The "Hellenic aggregate" was held together by three primary factors: a fellowship of blood, a common language (which was essentially one but featured various dialects), and shared religious sentiments, sacrifices, and localities. The Greeks frequently consulted oracles, a habit of the Greek mind, and participated in sacred games, such as the Olympic games.

A significant feature of the early Hellenic mind was the habit of common sacrifice. This evolved into "Amphiktyonies," which were exclusive religious partnerships that fostered sympathies and a primitive feeling of brotherhood among the people. One such organization was the Amphiktyonic Council, consisting of twelve constituent members who originally met at Thermopylæ. These Amphiktyons were responsible for the superintendence of the temple of Delphi. The Amphiktyonic oath promoted mutual protection and abstinence from injury, though the council's interference in general Grecian affairs was rare and occasional, and many Hellenic states did not participate.

## Social and Political Organization
In early Greece, political sovereignty was attached to each separate city, a concept essential to the Hellenic mind. Each city maintained an international relation with others. City government was viewed as the essential standard of living, while village residence was considered an inferior scale. Although village residents were numerous in early Greece, many eventually coalesced into cities; Sparta was a notable exception, retaining its village trim even at the height of its power.

The term "barbarian" was used as an antithesis to the "Hellens." Among the non-Hellenic populations, the historical Pelasgians and Leleges are noted for speaking barbarous languages. While some alleged ante-Hellenic colonies from Egypt and Phœnicia have been mentioned, these are considered neither probable nor verifiable.

## Regional Divisions and Groups
The Hellenic people were divided into several groups, including the Doric, Ionic, and Aeolic settlements. These names correspond to territorial, linguistic, and political divisions, particularly on the west coast of Asia Minor. An "Achaean" group was also recognized, with kinship between the Achaean colonies of Magna Graecia and those of Ionia.

In European Greece, the distribution of these groups was more complex. Between the 8th and 6th centuries, Dorian states were governed by exclusive aristocracies. This led to conflicting theories regarding their origin: some suggested the Dorians were not true "sons of Hellen" but were Achaean exiles or of northerly ancestry who gained predominance through violence. Conversely, Dorian aristocracies claimed they were the only genuine Hellenes, viewing anti-Dorian Athenians as "Hellenized barbarians" of Pelasgian origin.

## Geography and Regional Peoples
The geography of Greece—characterized by irregular waters, rivers that ran dry in summer, and frequent marshes—made land transport difficult and rendered sea communication essential for colonies and islands.

North of the Peloponnesus, the population included:
*   **Thessalians:** A powerful group when unanimous, though their cities were a disorderly confederacy. Their population included a villein race known as the Penestæ.
*   **Tributaries of Thessaly:** These included the Achæans, Perrhæbi, Magnêtes, Malians, and Dolopes, all of whom were Amphiktyonic races.
*   **Other Groups:** The Phocians, Lokrians, Ætolians, and Akarnanians. The Ozolian Lokrians, Ætolians, and Akarnanians were regarded as the rudest of all Greeks.
*   **Bœotians:** Organized as a confederation of cities, including Orchomenus, with early legislation established in Thebes by Philolaus and Dioklês.

## Origins and Legends
There are conflicting accounts regarding the most ancient district of Hellas. Aristotle claimed it was near the river Achelôus and Dôdôna, inhabited by the Selli (or Helli) and people then called "Græci." However, the general feeling among Greeks connected the origins of the Hellenes and Deukaliôn to Achaia Phthiôtis, between Mount Œta and Mount Othrys.

The legend of the Dorian invasion, appearing in the 7th-century poet Tyrtaeus, describes the Spartan Heracleids moving from northern Doris to the Peloponnese. Herodotus provides a more complex trajectory for the Dorians, tracing them from Phthiotis to Histiaeotis, then to Pindus (where they were a "Macedonian people"), then to Parnassian Doris, and finally to the Peloponnese.

## Historical Timeline
The history of the Greeks is generally divided into two periods:
1.  **776–560 B.C.:** A preparatory period that is very little known. During this time, the movements of different Hellenic portions were isolated.
2.  **560–300 B.C.:** A period where collective character became more evident.

The first historical incident to trigger a common Hellenic feeling of active obligation was the destruction of Kirrha by the Amphiktyons in defense of the Delphian temple.
