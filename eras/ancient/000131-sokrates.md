# SOKRATES

Sokratês was an Athenian figure whose life and intellectual contributions are primarily known through the accounts of his students, Plato and Xenophon. While their depictions of him are generally accordant, they serve different purposes: Xenophon’s "Memorabilia" seeks to vindicate Sokratês against his accusers and unfavorable opinions, while Plato’s dialogues often use Sokratês as a spokesman for Plato's own speculative theories.

## Life and Character
Sokratês was characterized by a life of constant publicity and indiscriminate conversation. He believed himself to be on a special religious mission, prompted by a "dæmon" or genius and an oracle from Delphi which declared that no man was wiser than he. This religious conviction merged with an intellectual impulse, leading him to test the false conceit of wisdom in others. He viewed the belief that one possesses knowledge when they do not as a deep mental defect, which Xenophon describes as being near to madness.

In his youth, Sokratês served as a soldier. During the campaign of Potidaea, he demonstrated great courage in an obstinate engagement, defending Alkibiades and saving his life and arms. Sokratês later encouraged the generals to award the prize for valor to Alkibiades to foster the young man's love for glory. He also showed courage at the battle of Delium, where he retreated on foot while being defended by Alkibiades. Regarding his education, contemporary witness Ion of Chios suggests that Sokratês received instruction from Archelaus.

## Philosophical Innovations and Method
Sokratês is credited with opening ethics as a new subject of scientific discussion. He shifted the focus of philosophical study away from the nature of the universe—the "history of nature" pursued by previous philosophers—and confined it to human affairs, society, and man. His ethical theories were centered on human security and happiness.

To explore these subjects, Sokratês developed a specific dialectical method. This involved:
*   **Inductive discourses:** Using specific examples to reach general conclusions.
*   **Definitions:** Seeking precise meanings of concepts.
*   **Cross-examination:** A colloquial process of questioning used on both noted men and the general public.
*   **Division:** The process of logically distributing subject matter into genera and species.

This method laid the foundation for formal logic, which was later expanded by Plato and systematized by Aristotle. Sokratês used these tools to examine the contrast between special professions and the general duties of social life, and to discuss whether virtue is teachable.

## Conflict and Trial
Sokratês' habit of questioning others and his influence on the youth earned him numerous enemies. He frequently addressed young men who, after engaging in his conversations, brought home new ideas that displeased their fathers. This led to the general accusation that he was corrupting the youth.

One specific instance of this friction involved Anytus, a rich leather-seller and political leader. Anytus became incensed when Sokratês, recognizing intellectual promise in Anytus' son, attempted to dissuade the father from raising the boy in the leather-selling trade. Anytus, who valued narrow practicality and had suffered property losses during the dominion of the Thirty, became one of Sokratês' primary accusers.

During his trial, Sokratês delivered a defense (reproduced in Plato's "Apology") in which he predicted that if the dikasts put him to death, a great number of young men would take up the vocation of cross-questioning and cause the city more trouble than he ever had.
