# Canterbury and Rochester

Canterbury (*Cantwaraburg*) and Rochester (*Hrofes ceaster*) are two historic locations in the county of Kent. Despite their English names, both were undoubtedly Roman places.

## Roman and Early Origins
In the time of the Romans, Britain contained a number of fortresses (*castetta*) and twenty-eight cities (*civitates*). The terms *ceaster* (from the Latin *castra*) and, less frequently, *burg* were applied to these Roman cities and fortresses. While many of these sites were situated in territories later occupied by invaders, their history during the intervening period is often obscure. 

Some sites, such as Chester, appear to have been deserted for three centuries following destruction in the early 7th century. However, Canterbury and London are noted as having recovered a certain level of importance relatively early, specifically within two centuries after the invasion. It is likely that the cultivation of land was not interrupted for more than a few years, making the use of existing village sites convenient even if the original buildings had been destroyed.

## Ecclesiastical History
Kent is unique among English counties for comprising two entire bishoprics. The see for East Kent, Canterbury, was founded in 597, while the see for West Kent, Rochester, was founded in 600. 

Historically, these dioceses were organized into archdeaconries. By 1291, the archdeaconry of Canterbury was co-extensive with its diocese and included several deaneries: Canterbury, Charing, Elham, Lympne, Ospringe, Sandwich, Sittingbourne, Sutton, Westbere, Bridge, and Dover. The archdeaconry of Rochester was similarly co-extensive with its diocese, including the deaneries of Rochester, Shoreham, Malling, and Dartford. 

Over subsequent centuries, these boundaries shifted. In 1845 and 1846, a new archdeaconry of Maidstone was created, absorbing several deaneries from both Canterbury and Rochester. Further subdivisions occurred in the mid-19th century, including the creation of North and South Malling, East and West Charing, North and South Lympne, and East and West Dartford. Additional deaneries, such as Gravesend, Cobham, Greenwich, Woolwich, and Croydon, were established between 1862 and 1873. By 1906, several of these subdivisions were abolished, and Shoreham and Tunbridge were transferred to the Rochester archdeaconry.

## Political and Military History
Both cities have held parliamentary representation. In 1295, Canterbury and Rochester (along with Tunbridge) were represented in parliament. While other towns like Maidstone and Queenborough later acquired representation, the representation for Canterbury and Rochester was reduced to one member each by the act of 1885.

Rochester has been a focal point of significant military conflict. During the rebellion of Odo of Bayeux, the city was captured by William Rufus. In 1213, the king captured Rochester Castle, which was subsequently captured by the dauphin of France in 1216, leading nearly all of Kent to submit to him. The castle was also captured by Gilbert de Clare during the wars of Henry III with his barons.

Canterbury is noted for the murder of Thomas à Becket in 1170 and for the plundering of the archbishop's palace by rebels during the peasants' rising of 1381.

## Geography and Economy
The region between Rochester and Canterbury in East Kent is characterized by a significant amount of fruit land. This area is home to cultivators who specialize exclusively in fruit, managing their land with technical knowledge and undivided attention. The acreage includes cherry, apple, pear, plum, and damson trees, as well as soft fruits such as strawberries, raspberries, currants, and gooseberries. Advanced growers in the region employ systematic spraying and grease-banding of apple trees to prevent insect attacks and focus on selecting varieties of pears and apples based on size, flavor, and keeping qualities to compete with imports from Canada and the United States.
