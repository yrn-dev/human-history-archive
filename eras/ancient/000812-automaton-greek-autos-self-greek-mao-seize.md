# AUTOMATON

An automaton (derived from the Greek *autos*, meaning self, and *mao*, meaning to seize) is defined as a machine that is self-moving, or one where the principle of motion is contained within the mechanism itself. While this technical description encompasses devices such as watches and clocks, the term is generally used to describe contrivances designed to simulate the motions of animal life for a period of time. When such a machine represents human actions and figures, it is sometimes specifically referred to as an *androides*.

## Early History and Medieval Accounts
Records of the construction of automata date back to early history, including references to the moving figures of Daedalus and the tripods of Vulcan. In 400 B.C., Archytas of Tarentum is said to have created a wooden pigeon capable of flight.

During the Middle Ages, numerous instances of automata construction were recorded, though the historical accuracy of some accounts is limited. Examples include:
* **Regiomontanus**: Said to have created an iron fly that could flutter around a room and return to his hand, as well as an eagle that flew before Emperor Maximilian during his entry into Nuremberg.
* **Roger Bacon**: Reported to have forged a speaking brazen head.
* **Albertus Magnus**: Said to have possessed an *androides* that served as a doorkeeper; this device was reportedly broken to pieces by Aquinas.

Later instances, such as a figure constructed by Descartes and automata exhibited by Dr. Camus, are also noted, though not much is accurately known about them.

## 18th Century and Later Developments
The 18th century saw a significant impulse in the construction of these figures, largely due to the work of the mechanician Jacques de Vaucanson. Vaucanson exhibited three notable figures: a tambourine-player, a flute-player, and a duck. The duck was capable of drinking, eating, and exactly imitating the natural voice of the fowl. Because the means of producing these results were clearly visible, other inventors were encouraged to create similar devices.

Subsequent notable constructions include:
* **Knauss**: Exhibited an automaton in Vienna that could write.
* **The Droz family**: A father and son who constructed several ingenious mechanical figures capable of playing music and writing.
* **Frederick Kaufmann and Leonard Maelzel**: Created automatic trumpeters capable of playing several marches.

The Swiss have been particularly celebrated for their mechanical ingenuity, producing many of the curious toys found in industrial exhibitions, such as singing and flying birds.

## Philosophical and Linguistic Context
In the context of Greek terminology, the word for spontaneity is *to automaton*, translated as "the Self-moved."
