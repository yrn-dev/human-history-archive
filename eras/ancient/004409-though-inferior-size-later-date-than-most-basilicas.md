# The Basilica of S. Clemente and the Evolution of Basilican Architecture

The basilica of S. Clemente is noted for being later in date and inferior in size compared to most other basilicas. Despite these factors, it is considered unsurpassed in interest because it has more perfectly retained its original church-fittings and ritual arrangements than any other example.

## Structure and History of S. Clemente
The current building of S. Clemente was erected upon the foundation of an earlier church. This previous structure was abandoned and filled with earth for unrecorded reasons at an unknown date. The most probable explanation is that the earlier church was completely overwhelmed during the ruin of the city in 1084, when Robert Guiscard burnt all public buildings from the Capitol to the Lateran. Due to this destruction, it was deemed more convenient to build a new edifice at a higher level rather than repair the old one.

The existing building is preceded by an atrium, which serves as the only remaining perfect example in Rome. In the center of this atrium is a *cantharus*, a fountain used for ablutions. The atrium is accessed via a portico constructed from earlier fragments that were put together carelessly.

## Interior Ritual Arrangements
The interior of S. Clemente features several specific ritual elements:
*   **The Chorus Cantorum:** Occupying approximately one-third of the nave, this area is enclosed by a low marble screen about 3 feet high. This 9th-century work was preserved from the old church and newly arranged. The white marble slabs feature low relief patterns and 13th-century glass mosaic ribbons. These screens stand free of the pillars to allow for a passage.
*   **The Ambos:** On the ritual north stands the octagonal gospel-ambo, featuring a double flight of steps leading westwards and eastwards. Opposite this, to the south, is the square epistle-ambo, which contains two marble reading-desks facing east and west for the reading of the gradual and the epistle.
*   **Additional Fittings:** To the west of the gospel-ambo is the great Paschal candlestick, which is decorated with mosaic and possesses a spiral shaft.
*   **The Sanctuary:** The sanctuary is divided from the choir by another section of the marble screen and is raised two steps above it. The altar is situated beneath a lofty *ciborium* supported by marble columns.

## Comparative Basilican Architecture
The basilican form has appeared in various contexts across the empire. In Rome, early civil basilicas included the Basilica Porcia, erected by M. Porcius Cato in 184 B.C., and the Basilica Julia, which was the most extensive by the age of Augustus. Other significant Roman examples include the Basilica Ulpia, built by Trajan around A.D. 112, and the vaulted basilica of Maxentius or Constantine from the beginning of the 4th century.

In the Christian context, other notable Roman basilicas include:
*   **S. Maria Maggiore:** Dedicated in 365 and reconstructed in 432 A.D., this three-aisled basilica features Ionic pillars of grey granite and mosaic pictures of Old Testament history.
*   **S. Sabina:** Dating from about A.D. 430, it is noted for preserving a primitive aspect, including unique carved wooden doors of early Christian date.
*   **S. Lorenzo fuori le Mura:** Originally constructed in A.D. 432, this church was later disorientated in the 13th century by Honorius III, who replaced the apse with a twelve-bay nave.

## Regional Variations and Civil Prototypes
Basilicas were nearly universal in provincial cities. In Britain, the Callevan Forum contained a remarkably large basilica used for business and meetings. In the East, notable examples include the church of St Demetrius at Thessalonica and the monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai. In Italy, the basilicas of S. Apollinare Nuovo (493-525) and S. Apollinare in Classe (538-549) in Ravenna are recognized for their superior design and proportions.

While early Christian churches share similarities with columned civil basilicas, they differ in key respects. Most early Christian basilicas in the west lacked galleries over side aisles and were dominated by a semi-dome apse terminating the central nave—a feature not found in normal columned civil basilicas, with the possible exception of the 4th-century remains at Silchester.
