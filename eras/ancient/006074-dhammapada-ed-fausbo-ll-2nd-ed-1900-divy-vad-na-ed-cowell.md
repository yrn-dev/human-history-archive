# Dhammapada and Divyavadana

The *Dhammapada* and the *Divyavadana* are significant Buddhist texts preserved in the archive's records. While the former serves as a foundational guide for ethics and self-training, the latter is a collection of legends concerning the figures of the Buddhist order.

## The Dhammapada

The *Dhammapada* is described as a text book used within the Buddhist community. It is included in a supplementary collection known as the *Khuddaka Nikaya*, which was compiled after the *Nikayas* had been closed. This collection provides insight into the literary life of the Ganges valley during the 4th or 5th century B.C., as well as the intellectual state of the Buddhist community following the earliest period.

The text consists of 423 verses gathered from various older sources. These verses are organized around twenty-six selected points of Buddhist ethics or self-training, with each point featuring between ten and twenty stanzas. The *Dhammapada* lacks a strict internal connection between its verses, other than their general relation to the same subject. Furthermore, the collector of the text did not adhere to a consistent number of lines or a single metre for the stanzas.

In terms of scholarly editions and translations, a second edition of the *Dhammapada* was edited by Fausböll in 1900. A translation by Max Müller was published as part of the "Sacred Books of the East" series in 1881.

## The Divyavadana

The *Divyavadana*, also referred to as "The Heavenly Legend," is a chief example of a type of Buddhist romance literature known as *Avadana*. These works are represented by a large number of Sanskrit (Nepalese) collections. Although the *Divyavadana* is of a later date than most canonical Buddhist books, it is held in veneration by the orthodox. Its position within Buddhism is compared to the role the *Puranas* hold in relation to Brahminism.

The *Divyavadana* is a collection of legends focusing on the leading disciples of the Buddha and important members of the order over a period of three centuries. While the work was put into its present form during a comparatively late period—dated to approximately the 2nd or 3rd century of our era—it contains some very ancient fragments. The legends within the collection are of varying dates.

An edition of the *Divyavadana* was produced by Cowell and Neil in 1882. Additionally, Cowell and Max Müller included it in the *Buddhist Mahayana Texts* published in 1894 as part of the "Sacred Books of the East."
