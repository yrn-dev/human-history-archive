# THE GRECIAN MYTHICAL VEIN COMPARED WITH THAT OF MODERN EUROPE

## The Nature of Grecian Mythes
In the early stages of Grecian development, mythes constituted the entirety of the mental stock. These narratives arose from a state of mind characterized by a tendency toward universal personification, where a personifying faith supplied the gaps left by an absence of positive knowledge. To the early Greeks, the gods and heroes—whose primary agency was cast back into the past—were not poetical fancies but serious realities. 

The Homeric age was defined by a paramount religious point of view and an easy faith in plausible, popular stories, with poets receiving their material through the divine inspiration of the Muse. In this era, actual history was considered uninteresting. Religion, patriotism, the divine, and the human were woven into an indivisible web where truth and reality were not intended to be distinguishable.

## Transition to Scientific Thought
The Grecian intellect eventually underwent an expansive transition toward positive and present facts. This shift was influenced by the opening of Egypt to Grecian commerce around 660 B.C., leading to historical, social, and geographical progress. By 500 B.C., a scientific point of view began to develop, creating a dissent with the religious feeling of the multitude. 

This period saw the commencement of physical science with figures such as Pythagoras, Xenophanês, and Thalês, who conceived of impersonal nature as an object of study. This created a scission between the multitude and superior men, including Hippocratês, Anaxagoras, and Socratês; the latter was treated by the Athenians in a manner that reflected this conflict. Consequently, the mythes were accommodated to new standards of ethical and intellectual judgment. While tragic poets like Æschylus maintained the grandeur of the mythical world, Euripidês was accused of introducing exaggerated rhetoric and refinement, and logographers like Hekatæus began to rationalize the mythes.

## Comparison with Teutonic and Scandinavian Epics
There is a remarkable analogy between the old heathen versions of Teutonic and Scandinavian narratives and Grecian mythical narrative. The *Volsunga Saga*, the *Niebelungen Lied*, and songs from the *Edda* relate to a common fund of superhuman personages and fabulous adventures, such as Sigurd, Brynhilde, Gudrun, and Atle. 

A specific point of similarity is the presence of a curse of destiny. Just as the Labdakids of Thêbes and Achilles suffered despite their qualities, the family of the Volsungs faced ruin despite being protected by the gods. Both the Grecian and the Teutonic/Scandinavian epics share a heroic character and a self-expanding subject. However, the German epic is later and more elaborated, including figures like Dieterich of Bern and presenting characters and circumstances as Christian, whereas the *Edda* contains only heathenism.

## Mythical Tendencies in Modern Europe
While the old mythes eventually became unsuitable for the evolving European sentiment, the demand for mythical narrative persisted, though it was forced into new channels. In modern Europe, this manifested as the saintly ideal (Legends of the Saints) and the chivalrous ideal (Romances of Arthur and Charlemagne). These were often accepted as realities of the fore-time.

The shift in sentiment is evidenced by the twelfth-century French *Chansons de Geste*, which transformed Apollo into an evil dæmon and patron of the Mussulmans. Similarly, the prophet Mahomet anathematized the old Persian epic, with the Koran stating that some men buy "frivolous" tales to turn men away from the way of God.

## Historical Perception and Methodology
The approach to mythical history varied across cultures. In England, history was conceived down to the seventeenth century as beginning with Brute the Trojan, a belief defended with tenacious faith. However, the standard of historical evidence was raised for English history in a way it was not for Grecian history.

Regarding the Grecian mythes, two methods of treatment exist: omitting them entirely (as is done with old British fables) or recounting them as mythes. The latter is preferred because the mythes are an important chapter in the history of the human mind. Ancient chronologers, such as Herodotus and Hellanikus, did not distinguish between real and fictitious persons in the mythical world; they assumed the entire sequence of characters was real to calculate the number of centuries separating them from the gods. In their system, three succeeding persons of a lineage—grandfather, father, and son—counted for one century.

## Examples of Mythical Faith
The influence of the mythical vein is seen in the gratitude of the Megalopolitans toward Boreas for protection against King Agis, and in the account of the Ten Thousand Greeks in Armenia. When Boreas blew "parching and freezing" winds in their faces, a prophet recommended a sacrifice to him, after which the painful effects of the wind appeared to cease.

The tale of Troy further illustrates the magnitude of the Grecian mythical world, involving Dardanus, the walls built by Poseidôn, and the expedition under Agamemnôn. While the Trojan war is essentially legendary, it remained a central item of national faith, with figures like Alexander and the Romans treating the site of Ilium with marked respect.
