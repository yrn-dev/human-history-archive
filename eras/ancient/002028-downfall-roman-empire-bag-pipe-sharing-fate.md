# The Bag-pipe

The bag-pipe is a complex reed instrument of great antiquity that serves as a link between the primitive organ and the syrinx. Its primary function is to provide a reservoir for wind-supply, allowing the performer to regulate sound-producing pressure using their arm. The instrument consists of an air-tight leather bag with three to five apertures, each containing a fixed stock or short tube. These stocks serve as air-chambers to protect the reeds and act as sockets for three types of pipes: a valved insufflation tube (blow-pipe) for filling the bag, a melody-pipe known as the "chaunter" (which uses a double-reed and may feature a bell, keys, or lateral holes), and "drones," which are cylindrical pipes that produce a single fixed note via a beating-reed.

## History and Ancient Origins
Evidence suggests the bag-pipe originated in remote antiquity. While not found on known Egyptian or Assyrian monuments, it can be traced through ancient Persia, Chaldaea, and Greece. In Persia, terracotta figures from the 8th century B.C. discovered at a *tell* in Susa depict musicians with bags under their arms and pursed lips, though the insufflation tubes are missing. 

In ancient Greece, the instrument was likely known as the *askaulos*, though it does not appear in classical literature or the lists of pipes provided by Athenaeus or Pollux. It remained a rustic instrument used by peasants and shepherds. A description by Dio Chrysostom (circa A.D. 100) mentions a performer playing a pipe with his mouth on a bag placed under his arm-pits. The Roman version of the instrument was known as the *tibia utricularis*.

## Transition and the Middle Ages
Following the downfall of the Roman empire, the bag-pipe shared the fate of other instruments and likely lingered for a period among jugglers, actors, and itinerant musicians. It later reappeared in the early middle ages in a primitive guise under the name *chorus*. 

Evidence of the instrument's survival is found in a 6th-century A.D. illustration of a Persian bag-pipe on the great arch at Takht-i-Bostan, dating to the reign of Chosroes II. Although the representation is crude, it confirms the instrument's existence during the centuries between the Susa figurines and the early medieval period. By the 9th to 13th centuries, drawings in monuments and manuscripts frequently depict bag-pipes, though these often lack drones. For example, a 12th-century psalter in the Hunterian collection at the University Court, Glasgow, depicts King David surrounded by musicians; one plays a bag-pipe with a well-defined stock and long chaunter, but without drones or an insufflation tube.

## Regional Varieties and Classification
Bag-pipes are categorized into two main classes based on how the bag is inflated: by mouth (blow-pipe) or by a small bellows worked by the arm or elbow.

**Class I (Mouth-inflated):**
*   **Highland bag-pipe:** Features a conical wooden chaunter (14 to 16 inches) with seven front holes and one back thumb hole. It produces nine notes.
*   **Other varieties:** Includes the old Irish bag-pipe, the *cornemuse*, the Breton *bignou* (or *biniou*), the Calabrian bag-pipe, the Greek *ascaules*, the *tibia utricularis*, and the *chorus*. The Calabrian variety uses a goatskin bag with hair left on and features two drones and two chaunters.

**Class II (Bellows-inflated):**
*   **Northumbrian or Border bag-pipe:** Distinguished by a chaunter stopped at the lower end, rendering the pipe silent when all holes are closed. It has seven finger-holes, one thumb hole, and four drones tuned by stoppers.
*   **Lowland bag-pipe:** Differs from the Highland version primarily by the use of bellows.
*   **Other varieties:** Includes the *musette* (French bag-pipe), the union pipes of Ireland, and the *surdelina* of Naples. The *surdelina* (or *sampogna*), described by Mersenne, was a complicated instrument with two drones and two chaunters, all featuring double reeds.

## Specialized Designs and Cultural Use
Certain regional variations offered unique musical capabilities. In Magdeburg, Praetorius noted a *sackpfeife* larger than the *schaeferpfeife* and pitched a third lower. This instrument featured two chaunters in one stock, allowing the performer to play simple two-part melodies.

In the British Isles, the bag-pipe spread from England to Ireland and Caledonia, where it became a highly esteemed military instrument and a staple of Celtic life. It was utilized at funerals, weddings, and festivals. In the 18th century, it was used to lighten manual labor; for instance, in 1786 in Skye, parties of road-makers were accompanied by bag-pipers. Additionally, the instrument was used in the old mysteries at Coventry in 1534.
