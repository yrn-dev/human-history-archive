# MANNERS AND CUSTOMS IN REMOTE ANTIQUITY

The study of manners and customs in remote antiquity relies upon a diverse array of sources, ranging from ancient chronicles and records to archaeological relics and oral traditions. These sources provide insight into the social structures, spiritual beliefs, and daily interactions of early civilizations across the globe.

## Social and Cultural Traditions in Japan
In the study of early Japan, historians rely on the Records and Chronicles compiled in the eighth century, which describe customs from twelve or thirteen hundred years prior to their writing. While some suggest these accounts may be romance or reflect the ideas of the narrators rather than the era they describe, they are generally viewed as having a foundation of truth regarding institutions and manners. 

Early Chinese writers referred to the Japanese using an ideograph signifying "dwarf," pronounced *Wa*, though the reasoning for this epithet is not evidenced. Legends from the Records also detail mythological conflicts and the origins of humanity. One such account describes the deity Izanagi blocking the "even pass of hades" with a huge rock to separate himself from Izanami. Following this separation, Izanami threatened to kill a thousand people daily in his land, to which Izanagi responded that he would retaliate by causing fifteen hundred to be born.

## Ancient Civilizations of China and Greece
In China, tradition dates the Golden Age to three thousand years before Christ, although a survey of early civilization finds evidence of an advanced state by the tenth century B.C. Some students of history have suggested a migration from Babylonia based on shadowy coincidences in language and customs, but authentic Chinese records contain no mention of such a migration.

In ancient Greece, the theater served as a significant cultural institution. Athenians derived great pleasure from dramatic performances—both comic and tragic—that addressed public affairs. Poets used the stage to instruct the public in their duty, soothe passions, and justify or condemn conduct, thereby gaining influence in public counsels. Additionally, certain festivals in various locations practiced "consecrated scurrility" (*τωθασμὸς*), where abusive language and insulting gestures were part of the ritual. A similar custom of using opprobrious epithets toward strangers during harvest and vintage seasons has been observed in Gerace, the site of ancient Epizephyrian Lokri.

## Customs of Hospitality and Social Interaction
In primitive times, the virtue of hospitality was highly regarded, though some historians argue it arose from necessity rather than generosity. Because public inns were few or nonexistent in remote antiquity, hospitality was reciprocal; entertaining a stranger secured the right to be received in return if one traveled to the stranger's country. This right was considered sacred and inviolable, extending to the children and posterity of those involved.

## Burial Practices and Beliefs
Burial customs in remote antiquity often reflected the belief that the deceased were traveling toward a specific region where their future would be spent. 
* **Pacific Islands:** Samoans and Fijians laid the dead facing the far west.
* **South America:** The Guarayos turned the head east toward the "happy hunting grounds" of their god, Tamoi.
* **Christian Tradition:** The church followed an ancient custom of laying the dead facing east, as this is the attitude of prayer and the direction of the heavenly Jerusalem.
* **Scandinavia:** In barrows, such as the one found at Goldhavn in 1830, skeletons were discovered seated on low stone benches facing south or east. This arrangement suggests the dead were continuing the drinking bouts they had enjoyed during their earthly lives.
