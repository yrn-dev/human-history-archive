# Buddhist Asceticism

Buddhist asceticism is characterized as a philosophical and ethical product, distinct from other forms of ascetic practice. Its primary objective is the attainment of deliverance from life and the inevitable suffering associated with existence.

## The Four Noble Truths and Salvation
The core of Buddhist asceticism is centered on the understanding of the four Noble Truths. According to the Blessed One (Buddha), the failure to understand and grasp these truths is the reason individuals wander upon a "weary path of transmigration." These four truths concern:
*   Sorrow
*   The cause of sorrow
*   The cessation of sorrow
*   The path leading to that cessation

The Buddha taught that once these truths are grasped, the craving for existence is rooted out and the factors leading to renewed existence are destroyed, thereby ending the cycle of birth. For the elect disciple who possesses this way of Truth, the state of suffering is ended. Such a person is no longer liable to be reborn as a ghost, an animal, or in any "place of woe," and is instead assured of final salvation.

## Doctrines of Transmigration and Karma
While the Buddha rejected the ritualistic magic of brahmin schools, the soul-theory of the Jains, and the animistic superstitions of the general population, he retained a belief in transmigration. This is the belief that after the death of the body, the soul transmigrates into other bodies, which may be those of gods, beasts, or men.

In India, this belief was integrated with the ethical doctrine of *Karma*. Under this system, a person's physical advantages or social position in life are viewed as the result of their actions in a previous birth. For example, a person born blind is explained to have had a "lust of the eye" in a previous birth, while someone with unusual powers of hearing is explained to have loved listening to the preaching of the law in a previous existence.

## Philosophical Concepts
In comparative philosophical analysis, the Buddhist "Absolute" is described as something that can only be characterized by denying everything that is asserted of the finite. Additionally, Buddhist practice is noted for a universal charity that embraces all creatures equally.

## Practices and Priesthood
In certain regions, such as China, Buddhist temples often feature legends at their gates stating, "No meat nor wine may enter here," and the diet served in refectories is strictly vegetarian. 

The process of entering holy orders involves a severe ordeal of endurance for the novice. During a long ritual, between three and nine pastilles are placed on the novice's bald scalp and lighted, burning into the skin to create permanent scars. The novice is supported by priests who encourage them to endure the excruciating pain. Once fully qualified, a priest receives a diploma that entitles them to a day and a night of board and lodging from priests at any temple throughout the empire.

## Context of Asceticism
Broadly, asceticism is defined as the theory and practice of self-mortification and bodily abstinence, usually for religious reasons. The term is derived from the Greek *askeo* ("I practise"), evoking the metaphor of the ancient wrestling-place (*palaestra*), where victory went to those who best trained their bodies. 

True asceticism is based on a moral contrast between what is "right" and what is "pleasant." While primitive asceticism often involved non-moral taboos or self-torture that appeared valueless for character training, great religious reformers—including the Buddha, Moses, Paul, Mani, Mahomet, St Francis, and Luther—sought to direct the ascetic instinct toward higher aims and personal holiness rather than mere ritual purity.
