# ATARGATIS

Atargatis is a Syrian deity generally described as the "fish-goddess." She was known to the Greeks by the name Derketo, and also as Deasura or Dea Syria. 

## Etymology and Identification
The name Atargatis is a compound of two divine names. The first part is a form of the Himyaritic *'Athlar*, which is the equivalent of the Phoenician Astarte and the Old Testament Ashtoreth (with the feminine ending omitted, similar to the Assyrian Ishtar). The second part is the Palmyrene name *'Athe*, meaning *tempus opportunum*, a term that appears in many compounds. 

Due to the first half of her name, Atargatis has frequently been identified with Astarte. While the two deities likely share a common origin, their cults are historically distinct. However, in many instances, they were fused to the point of being indistinguishable. An example of this fusion is found at Carnion in Gilead, where an Atargateion (temple of Atargatis) is mentioned in 2 Macc. xii. 26; this site is probably identical to the famous temple of Astarte at Ashtaroth-Karnaim.

## Attributes and Functions
Atargatis is generally presented as the wife of Hadad (Baal), and together they serve as the protecting deities of the community. She is associated with several diverse roles and attributes:

*   **Civic and Social:** In her capacity as *polionchos*, she wears a mural crown. She is regarded as the founder of religious and social life, the ancestor of the royal house, and the inventor of useful appliances.
*   **Fertility and Nature:** She is the goddess of generation and fertility, a role evidenced by the prevalence of phallic emblems. In this capacity, she is identified with the Greek Aphrodite. Ultimately, she is viewed as a great Nature-Goddess, analogous to Rhea and Cybele.
*   **Cosmic Elements:** She typifies the universal mother-earth and the function of water in the production of life. Influenced by Chaldaean astrology, she is also associated with the power of destiny.

## Cult and Worship
The home of the goddess was not Palestine, but Syria proper, specifically at Hierapolis (also called Bambyke or Edessa), where she possessed a great temple. From Syria, her worship spread to Italy, Greece, and the furthest west. 

The expansion of the cult was largely driven by Syrian merchants, leading to its presence in major seaport towns; numerous inscriptions at Delos testify to its importance there. The cult also reached Sicily and the northern limits of the Roman empire, likely introduced by mercenary troops and slaves. 

Descriptions provided by Apuleius and Lucian mention beggar-priests who traveled to great cities collecting money while carrying an image of the goddess on an ass.

## Legends and Mythology
The legends surrounding Atargatis are often astrological and are used to explain specific religious practices, such as the abstinence from fish and the worship of doves. One derivation of her name, *ates Gatidos* ("without Gatis"), refers to a queen who forbade the eating of fish.

Other myths include:
*   **The Transformation:** Diodorus Siculus records that Atargatis fell in love with a youth worshipping at the shrine of Aphrodite. After becoming the mother of the Assyrian queen Semiramis, she flung herself into a pool at Hierapolis or Ascalon out of shame and was transformed into a fish.
*   **The Egg:** Another story claims she was hatched from an egg found by fish in the Euphrates. The fish placed the egg on the bank, where it was hatched by a dove. In gratitude, Atargatis persuaded Jupiter to place the fish in the Zodiac.
