# Pharisees and Spiritual Lawyers in Historical and Literary Context

The terms "Pharisee" and "spiritual lawyer" appear across various historical, religious, and literary records. While the former refers to a specific sect within the Israelites, the latter appears in later European morality plays as a symbol of specific moral failings.

## The Pharisees of the Israelite People

The Pharisees were one of two celebrated sects that arose in Jerusalem following the return of the exiled nation to the promised land and the restoration of ancient religious records by Ezra. They were characterized by their austerity of manners, which allowed them to draw the body of the Jewish people into their party.

### Doctrines and Beliefs
Unlike the Sadducees, who adhered strictly to the literal sense of the Mosaic law and rejected the immortality of the soul, the Pharisees added the authority of tradition to that of Scripture. Through these traditions, they incorporated speculative tenets from the religion or philosophy of eastern nations. Their beliefs included:
*   **The Afterlife:** They accepted a future state of rewards and punishments and the immortality of the soul. According to Josephus, the Pharisees taught that the souls of good men move from one body to another, while the souls of the bad are subject to eternal punishment or an eternal prison.
*   **Fate and Providence:** The Pharisees held views on fate or Divine Providence. While some perceived this as an absolute fatality that denied human freedom—potentially allowing individuals to excuse sins by ascribing them to fate—others, including Josephus, maintained that their views were consistent with the freedom of human actions.
*   **Traditions:** They were fond of unwritten traditions, which were viewed by some, including the Sadducees and later figures like Jesus, as "commandments of men" added to the written law.

### Practices and Conflicts
The Pharisees were known for the meticulous observance of rules regarding a pious career. This included the "tradition of the elders," such as the requirement to wash hands, cups, pots, brazen vessels, and tables before eating. These practices were sometimes viewed as "bargaining righteousness" or formal religion. In the political sphere, the Pharisees held significant influence over the multitude, to the extent that Sadducean magistrates were often forced to adopt Pharisaic notions to remain acceptable to the public.

## The "Spiritual Lawyer" in Morality Plays

In a different historical context, the term "spiritual lawyer" appears in the 16th-century works of the author known as "foul-mouthed Bale." In his morality play, *The Thre Laws of Nature, Moses and Christ, corrupted by the Sodomytes, Pharisees and Papystes most wicked* (printed in 1538 and 1562), Bale used specific costumes to represent various vices.

In this dramatic work, the character of "Covetousness" is directed to be dressed as a "Pharisee or spiritual lawyer." This pairing suggests that in the context of the play's religious and political aims, the figure of the spiritual lawyer was synonymous with greed and the hypocrisy associated with the Pharisees. This play was part of a broader body of work by Bale that served as a polemic against the Roman Catholic Church.

## False Doctrine and the "Popish Doctor"

Within the same morality play by Bale, the concept of "False Doctrine" is personified. The stage directions specify that the character representing False Doctrine should be dressed as a "popish doctor." This imagery was used to align theological errors with the clergy of the Roman Catholic Church, reflecting the author's strong bias against Roman Catholic writers.

## Legal Standards of Candor

While the "spiritual lawyer" of the 16th century was a literary symbol of covetousness, modern legal archives define the professional requirements of lawyers regarding truthfulness. According to the FRPC 4-4.1, a lawyer is prohibited from knowingly making false statements of material fact or law to a third person. 

Misrepresentations in a legal context can occur through:
*   Affirming a statement known to be false.
*   Using partially true but misleading statements.
*   Omissions that are equivalent to affirmative false statements.

Furthermore, FRPC 4-8.4(c) prohibits conduct involving deceit, fraud, or dishonesty, while FRPC 4-8.4(d) prohibits conduct prejudicial to the administration of justice, regardless of whether that conduct occurs within a judicial proceeding.
