# FROM THE CAPTURE OF OLYNTHUS TO THE TERMINATION OF THE SACRED WAR

The period spanning the capture of Olynthus and the conclusion of the Sacred War was characterized by the expansion of Philip of Macedonia's power and the struggle of Athens to maintain its influence in the Hellenic world. Philip had previously aggrandized himself in Thrace and Macedonia at the expense of Athens through the acquisition of Pydna, Potidæa, and Amphipolis. During the Social War, he had enlisted Olynthus as both an ally and an enemy of the Athenians, initiating a conflict regarding Amphipolis that lasted twelve years without a formal peace.

## The Olynthian War
The Olynthian war began in 350 B.C. when the Olynthians, fearing Philip, changed their sentiments and sought peace with Athens. Philip's hostility toward Olynthus ripened into war, fueled in part by the fact that Philip's fugitive half-brothers had obtained shelter in the city. Philip utilized corruption and the fomenting of intestine discord to undermine the Olynthian confederacy.

Between 350 and 347 B.C., Philip attacked the Olynthians and Chalkidians. He subdued thirty-two Chalkidic cities before marching directly against Olynthus. Some of these towns, including Torônê and Mekyberna, were reportedly betrayed by corrupt citizens without resistance. During his advance, Philip attempted to force a passage across the river Sardon; he was initially repulsed and forced to swim back across the river. During this engagement, an Olynthian archer named Aster wounded Philip in the eye, causing a permanent loss of sight that the Greek surgeon Kritobulus could not remedy.

## Athenian Involvement and the Olynthiacs
As the danger to Olynthus increased, the city concluded an alliance with Athens. This period is marked by the Olynthiac orations of Demosthenes, an opposition speaker who sought to alert Athens to the practical danger Philip posed. Demosthenes addressed the question of the Theôric Fund to secure assistance for Olynthus. 

Athens provided significant aid, including ten thousand mercenaries, four thousand citizens, and fifty triremes. While this resulted in partial success, it created an exaggerated confidence in Athens, leading the city to lose sight of the continuing danger. Internally, the Athenian political climate was volatile; Demosthenes faced insults from Meidias during a Dionysiac festival in March 349 B.C. and was reproached for his absence from the battle of Tamynæ, leading him to serve in Eubœa as a hoplite.

## The Eubœan Conflict
Concurrent with the Olynthian war, a revolt broke out in Eubœa around January or February 349 B.C. Philip engaged in intrigues on the island, and Plutarch of Eretria requested Athenian aid. Despite Demosthenes' dissuasions, Athens sent aid under Phokion. Although Plutarch proved treacherous, Phokion achieved a victory at Tamynæ. Hostilities in Eubœa continued through 349 and 348 B.C., forcing Athens to divide its efforts between maintaining Eubœa and supporting Olynthus.

## Diplomatic Intrigues and the Fall of Olynthus
Philip employed sophisticated diplomacy to neutralize Athenian resolve. In 348 B.C., during a truce of the Olympic festival, Philip's cruisers captured an influential Athenian named Phrynon. After Phrynon paid a ransom, Athens sent Ktesiphon as a public envoy to help recover the funds. Philip received the envoys courteously and restored the ransom, effectively bribing Phrynon and Ktesiphon, who returned to Athens as partisans of Macedonia.

As Philip approached Olynthus, he issued a peremptory summons demanding the inhabitants evacuate the city or he would leave Macedonia. The Olynthians rejected the notice and decided to defend the city to the last, relying on the presence of Athenian citizen-armaments. However, their defense was undermined from within. A "philippizing" party, led by Lasthenes and the venal Euthykrates, brought about the banishment of the chief opponent, Apollonides, and nullified measures for an energetic defense.

## The Sacred War
While the Olynthian conflict unfolded, the Hellenic world was further fractured by the Sacred War. This conflict is described as rending the "entrails of the Hellenic world" and proving profitable only to Philip. The war created complications and embarrassments for the Greek city-states, which Philip, as an indefatigable aggressor, exploited to further his own power.
