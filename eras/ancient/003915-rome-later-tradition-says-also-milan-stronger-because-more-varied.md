# Rome

## Historical and Religious Traditions
In the study of early Christian history, the movements of Barnabas are often obscured by the "mists of history," though certain records provide glimpses of his work. While tradition suggests he may have been one of the seventy and potentially traveled to Alexandria, the evidence regarding his visits to Rome is considered stronger because it is more varied. This varied evidence includes the *Homilies* and *Recognitions* of Clement, the late Cypriot *Encomium*, and the early *Actus Petri Vercellenses*. Later tradition also suggests he may have visited Milan. Some have inferred his connection to Rome through the Western ascription of the epistle to the Hebrews to him, though this is debated as it may be a mere inference based on the text's description of the author as a "son of exhortation."

## Imperial Legacy and the Catholic Church
Although the Latin-speaking Roman Empire ceased to exist in the fifth century, its prestige and tradition persisted through the Latin-speaking half of the Catholic Church. This institution survived the fall of the empire by appealing to the wills and minds of men, utilizing a system of missionaries, books, and teachers that proved stronger than legions or law. During the fourth and fifth centuries, as the empire decayed, Christianity expanded across Europe and eventually "conquered its conquerors," the barbarians.

The Patriarch or Pope of Rome, claiming leadership over the entire Christian church, utilized moral force to achieve results that armies could not; for instance, the patriarch intercepted Attila when he seemed inclined to march on Rome. Following the disappearance of the emperors, the Pope began to annex imperial claims and titles, including the title of *pontifex maximus*, which was the most ancient title held by the Roman emperors as the head sacrificial priest of the Roman dominion.

## The Legend of Roman Supremacy
The collapse of the Roman social and political structure varied by region. In Britain, the Anglo-Saxons practically wiped out the use of Latin. In the east, the Roman structure was cast off by the older and stronger Hellenic tradition, while in the west, it fragmented into separate lives. Despite this physical destruction, the tradition of the supremacy of the Cæsars and the world empire of Rome did not perish. Instead, the legend of a splendid and serene Roman world-supremacy expanded in the imagination of mankind because it was removed from the possibility of verification.

## Architectural and Artistic Transitions
Roman artistic influence evolved significantly over time. Early Roman forms were characterized by a certain bluntness and weariness, which were later replaced by a new energy of workmanship that explored fresh fields of beauty. This included brightly illuminated lattices of carved ornament, which were often gilt and colored, appearing more as color arrangements than as sculpture. 

While Rome lavished wonderful skill on plaster work, this art became extremely rude under the Byzantines. However, examples of Byzantine tradition plaster work can still be found in various locations, including the tympana of the baldachin at Sant' Ambrogio in Milan, which contain modelled figures.

## Rome in the Post-Imperial Era
Following the decline of the imperial system, the common lot of people faced squalor and insecurity, though local variations existed between the rule of "violent bullies" and "good-tempered freedom." During this period, the Roman landscape changed as pre-existing buildings were adapted for defense. The great tomb of Hadrian and the Colosseum—formerly the arena for great gladiatorial shows—were both converted into fortresses. In these decaying and insanitary cities, small groups of artisans continued to serve nearby cultivating villages under the protection of local nobles.

## Comparative Honors
In the eyes of the ancients, Roman honors were held in high esteem, though they were sometimes compared to Greek achievements. Cicero noted that the consular dignity in its original splendor was highly honorable to the ancient Romans. However, he also observed that the Greeks viewed a victory at Olympia as almost more great and glorious than receiving the honor of a triumph at Rome.
