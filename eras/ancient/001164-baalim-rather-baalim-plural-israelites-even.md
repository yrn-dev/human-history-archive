# The Baalim

The term "Baal" is a Semitic word that primarily denotes an inhabitant, owner, or lord. Within the context of Semitic religious and family relations, the term was specifically used to describe the relationship between a deity and a worshipper, or a husband and his wife. When applied to a deity, the term did not signify that the god was the lord of the worshipper, but rather that the deity was the ruler of, or the possessor of, a specific district or place.

## Usage in the Old Testament
In the Old Testament, the word is regularly written with the article as "the Baal." Because the Baals associated with different sanctuaries or tribes were not necessarily viewed as identical entities, the term frequently appears in the plural form as "Baalim," or "the Baalim."

Evidence exists that the Israelites applied the title of Baal to Yahweh. This is seen in several proper names, including:
*   **Jerubbaal** (Gideon)
*   **Eshbaal** (a son of Saul)
*   **Beeliada** (a son of David), a name that appears as Eliada in 2 Samuel v. 16, suggesting that "El" (God) was considered equivalent to "Baal."
*   **Be'aliah**, which translates to "Yahweh is baal or lord."

However, there were periods when the name Baal became exclusively associated with idolatrous worship. During such times, the word was viewed with abhorrence as an "unholy word." To reflect this, the word *bosheth* (meaning "shameful thing") was substituted for *baal* in compound proper names, resulting in forms such as Mephibosheth and Ishbosheth.

## The Baalim of Carthage
In the religion of Carthage, which followed Phoenician traditions, there existed a hierarchy of deities. At the lower level was an army of minor deities known as *baalim* and *alonim*. Above these minor gods stood a trinity of great deities:
*   **Baal-Ammon (or Moloch):** Identified by the Romans as Saturn or Cronus.
*   **Tanit:** The Phoenician Astarte and virgin goddess of the moon and heavens, known in the Roman period as Juno Caelestis.
*   **Eshmun:** The protector of the acropolis, generally identified with Aesculapius.

Other specialized cults existed alongside this structure, including those of the Tyrian Melkarth (analogous to Hercules), Iolaus or Tammuz-Adonis, and the god Patechus (or Pygmaeus), a repulsive monster whose images were placed on ship prows to intimidate enemies. Inscriptions also record other minor deities, such as Baalat haedrat ("mistress of the sanctuary"), Rabbat Umma ("the great mother"), Illat, Sakon, Tsaphon, Sid, Aris, and Ashtoreth.

## Idolatry and the Israelites
Historical accounts describe instances where Midianite women attempted to entice the Israelites toward idolatry and lewdness. This conflict highlighted a contrast between the worship of the God of Israel and the worship of idol gods. The worship of the true God was linked to living according to the holy laws provided by Moses, whereas the worship of false gods was associated with "impure laws" derived from demons. This distinction provided a substantial reason for the strict concern within the law of Moses to protect the Israelites from pagan idolatry.
