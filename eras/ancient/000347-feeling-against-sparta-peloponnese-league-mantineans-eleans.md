# Feeling against Sparta in the Peloponnese

The political landscape of the Peloponnese was characterized by significant instability and shifting alliances, primarily driven by a growing apprehension toward Lacedaemonian (Spartan) influence. This sentiment led to the formation of a strategic league comprising the Mantineans, Eleans, Argives, and Athenians.

## Origins of Anti-Spartan Sentiment
The movement against Sparta was initiated by the Mantineans, who were the first to defect due to fear of the Lacedaemonians. Having utilized the war against Athens to subject a large portion of Arcadia, the Mantineans feared that Lacedaemon would interfere with their conquests once she had the leisure to do so. Consequently, they sought an alliance with Argos, a sister democracy and historical enemy of the Lacedaemonians.

This defection prompted other states in the Peloponnese to consider following the Mantinean example. A primary cause of this general panic was a specific clause in the treaty between Lacedaemon and Athens, which allowed both parties to add to or remove terms at their own discretion. This provision excited suspicions that Lacedaemon and Athens might combine against the liberties of the other Peloponnesian states, as any alterations to the treaty were not conditional upon the consent of the allies. This atmosphere of distrust created a widespread desire among various states to align themselves with Argos.

## The League of the Mantineans, Eleans, Argives, and Athenians
To counter Lacedaemonian power, the Athenians, Argives, Mantineans, and Eleans—acting for themselves and their respective empires—entered into a treaty intended to last for one hundred years. The terms of this alliance were designed to ensure mutual security and independence:

*   **Mutual Non-Aggression:** It was forbidden for any party or their allies to carry on war against the other members of the league.
*   **Mutual Defense:** If an enemy invaded Athenian territory, the Argives, Eleans, and Mantineans were required to provide relief to the best of their power. If an invader plundered the territory and then departed, the offending state would be declared an enemy of all four cities, and no member could make a separate peace without the agreement of the others.
*   **Independence and Restoration:** The treaty mandated that all cities in the Peloponnese, regardless of size, remain independent according to their own customs. Additionally, the Lacedaemonians were required to restore any children held in their custody to their respective cities.
*   **External Threats:** The contracting parties agreed to unite to repel any powers from outside the Peloponnese that invaded the region.

## Oaths and Formalization
The alliance was formalized through rigorous oaths taken by the Senate and magistrates of each city. In Athens, the Prytanes administered the oath; in Argos, the Eighty; in Mantinea, the Theori and Polemarchs; and in Elis, the Demiurgi and Thesmophylaces. The oath required participants to stand by the alliance and its articles "justly, innocently, and sincerely."

To ensure the treaty's permanence, the articles and oaths were inscribed on stone pillars in the Athenian citadel, the Argive market-place (in the temple of Apollo), and the Mantinean market-place (in the temple of Zeus). Furthermore, a joint brazen pillar was to be erected at the Olympic games. The oaths were scheduled for renewal periodically: the Athenians would visit Elis, Mantinea, and Argos thirty days before the Olympic games, while the other three cities would visit Athens ten days before the feast of the Panathenaea.

## Military Actions and Internal Friction
Despite the formal league, internal tensions persisted. Corinth, while an ally of the Argives, refused to accede to the full alliance, remaining content with a limited defensive agreement and subsequently turning its attention back toward Lacedaemon.

The league engaged in active military operations, including a campaign against Orchomenos. After a force of one thousand Athenian heavy infantry and three hundred horse arrived under Laches and Nicostratus, the allies—encouraged by the Athenian ambassador Alcibiades—besieged Orchomenos. The city eventually capitulated, agreeing to join the league, provide hostages to the Mantineans, and release hostages previously lodged there by the Lacedaemonians.

Following the fall of Orchomenos, the league split over its next target. The Eleans urged an attack on Lepreum, while the Mantineans, supported by the Athenians and Argives, favored Tegea. This disagreement led the Eleans to leave the campaign in a rage.

## Diplomatic Shifts and the Role of Lykomedes
The relationship between Athens and its Peloponnesian allies was further strained by events at Oropus. Athens became discontented when its allies neglected a summons during an emergency at Oropus, despite Athenian troops having protected the Peloponnese against the Thebans.

Lykomedes, an ambitious Mantinean leader, sought to exploit this discontent to disengage the Arcadians from both Thebes and Sparta. He entered negotiations with Athens, arguing that removing Arcadian ties to Thebes served the interests of both Athens and Sparta. Although Lykomedes was killed by Arcadian exiles before he could return home, an alliance between Arcadia and Athens was eventually established, despite facing opposition.
