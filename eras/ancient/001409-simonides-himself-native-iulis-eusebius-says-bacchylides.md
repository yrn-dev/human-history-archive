# Bacchylides

Bacchylides was a Greek lyric poet born at Iulis on the island of Ceos. He is identified as the nephew of the poet Simonides, who was also a native of Iulis. Bacchylides is regarded as the youngest of the three great lyric poets, a group that includes Simonides and Pindar.

## Chronology and Life
Based on the writings of Eusebius, Bacchylides "flourished" in Ol. 78. 2 (467 B.C.). Because the term used for flourishing typically referred to a person's physical prime around the age of forty, it is estimated that he was born circa 507 B.C. (or approximately 504 B.C.). He was roughly fifteen years younger than Pindar and forty-nine years younger than his uncle, Simonides.

The dating of his work is supported by fragments of the Olympic register; his earliest approximately dated Ode (xii) may belong to 481 or 479 B.C., while the latest (vi) is fixed to Ol. 82. 1 (452 B.C.). Further records from Eusebius and Georgius Syncellus suggest he was "of repute" between 431 B.C. (Ol. 87. 2) and 428–425 B.C. (Ol. 88), indicating he likely survived into the beginning of the Peloponnesian War.

## Literary Works and Style
Until 1896, Bacchylides was known only through scanty fragments. The discovery of an Egyptian papyrus from the age of Alexander the Great revealed nineteen poems, consisting of six odes for festivals of heroes or gods and thirteen or fourteen epinicia (Odes of Victory).

The epinikion is a form derived from the humnos (a song praising a deity). While Stesichorus first composed hymns for heroes, the custom of celebrating victories of living men arose in the second half of the 6th century B.C. Bacchylides' style in these works is characterized by a focus on the specific incidents of a victory. For example, in his fifth ode celebrating the horse Pherenicus, Bacchylides describes the winner's run more vividly and fully than Pindar does in his first Olympian, which celebrates the same victory.

The papyrus also contains six compositions classified by Alexandrian scholars as "dithyrambs." Originally choral hymns to Dionysus, the dithyramb evolved to celebrate any god or hero and became a vehicle for mythical narratives. Bacchylides' dithyrambs—including works on Io, Idas, and Theseus—are noted for their epic narrative, picturesque detail, and use of short speeches. One such work, Ode xvii, relates the voyage of Theseus to Crete; although it was a paian for the Delian Apollo, it was categorized as a dithyramb. Additionally, Bacchylides wrote a poem regarding the competition among suitors for the hand of Marpêssa.

## Patronage and Rivalry
Bacchylides, Pindar, and Simonides all visited the court of Hiero I of Syracuse (478–467 B.C.). Hiero was a munificent patron of poets, though he was also described as cruel, rapacious, and jealous. Evidence suggests that Hiero preferred the odes of Bacchylides over those of Pindar. In 468 B.C., Bacchylides alone was commissioned to celebrate Hiero's victories, although Pindar composed an ode for a different Syracusan victor that same year.

This dynamic led to a tradition, noted by Alexandrian scholars, that Pindar viewed Bacchylides as a jealous rival. It is suggested that Pindar believed Bacchylides disparaged him to their common patron and that Bacchylides' skill was the result of study rather than innate genius.

## Critical Reception and Legacy
The Alexandrian scholars included Bacchylides in their "canon" of nine lyric poets, alongside Pindar, Simonides, Sappho, Alcaeus, Alcman, Ibycus, Stesichorus, and Anacreon. His work was studied by the poet Horace and enjoyed by the emperor Julian, who was attracted to Bacchylides' excellent gnomai (philosophical maxims).

Ancient critics offered varying assessments of his merit. In the treatise "On the Sources of Elevation in Style" (ascribed to Cassius Longinus), the author describes Bacchylides as "faultless" with a style of "perfect elegance and finish." However, the author places him in a "second class" of poets, arguing that while Bacchylides avoids glaring faults, he does not reach the "loftier heights" or "greatness" associated with Pindar.
