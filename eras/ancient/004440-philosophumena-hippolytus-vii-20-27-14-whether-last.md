# Philosophumena of Hippolytus

The *Philosophumena* is a work attributed to Hippolytus that contains accounts of various Gnostic systems. Within this text, specifically in sections vii. 20-27 and x. 14, Hippolytus provides an account of the teachings of Basilides.

## Account of Basilides
The account of Basilides found in the *Philosophumena* is characterized as being essentially different from other available records of his school of thought. While other sources—such as the *Syntagma* of Hippolytus (preserved in Philaster, Epiphanius, and Pseudo-Tertullian), the writings of Irenaeus, and notices in the *Stromateis* of Clemens Alexandrinus—offer a different perspective, the *Philosophumena* presents a version of Basilides' system with a pronounced monistic tendency.

## Scholarly Controversy and Sources
There has been a long-standing controversy regarding which account represents the original system of Basilides: the one found in the *Philosophumena* or the one provided by Irenaeus and the *Syntagma* of Hippolytus. 

Recent scholarly opinion tends to favor the latter and decide against the account in the *Philosophumena*. This conclusion is based on the analysis of the composition of the work; it appears that Hippolytus utilized a compendium by a Gnostic author as his primary source. This source author is believed to have introduced additional elements into most of the systems he treated.

## Context of Basilides
Basilides was a prominent exponent of Gnosticism who lived in Alexandria, likely as early as the first decades of the 2nd century. While Eusebius's *Chronicle* dates his first appearance to A.D. 133, other evidence suggests his activity may have begun earlier. Specifically, Agrippa Castor, who lived during the reign of Hadrian (117-138), wrote a polemic against him.

Basilides is known to have written an exegetical work consisting of twenty-four books based on a gospel of his own, though the identity of this gospel remains unknown. Other associated writings include the *Ethika*, *Exegetika* on the prophet Parchor, and works by his son, Isidorus.
