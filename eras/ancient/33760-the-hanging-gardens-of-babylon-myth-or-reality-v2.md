# The Nineveh Hypothesis: Shifting the Geography of the Hanging Gardens

While traditional historical narratives place the Hanging Gardens within the walls of Babylon, the silence of the Babylonian records is deafening. Despite the city's meticulous administrative archives, no contemporary Babylonian text mentions such a wonder. This discrepancy has led modern historians to explore a provocative alternative: that the "Hanging Gardens" were not in Babylon at all, but were actually located in Nineveh, the Assyrian capital.

This perspective shifts the focus from the Neo-Babylonian Empire of Nebuchadnezzar II to the Neo-Assyrian Empire, specifically the reign of King Sennacherib (r. 705–681 BCE). By re-examining the evidence, we find that the "myth" of the gardens may simply be a case of ancient geographical misidentification.

## The Evidence at Nineveh

The primary argument for the Nineveh hypothesis rests on the detailed records left by Sennacherib. Unlike the vague descriptions provided by later Greek historians, Sennacherib’s own inscriptions describe a magnificent palace complex featuring an innovative irrigation system. He speaks of "a palace without equal," surrounded by gardens that mirrored the mountains of Armenia.

Archaeological surveys at Nineveh have revealed the remains of massive aqueducts and canals designed to bring water from the hills to the city. This sophisticated hydraulic engineering—utilizing stone arches to transport water over long distances—would have been necessary to sustain a lush, elevated garden in the arid climate of Upper Mesopotamia.

## The Greek Confusion

If the gardens were in Nineveh, why did the Greeks credit Babylon? Historians suggest that the confusion arose from the shared cultural and linguistic landscape of Mesopotamia. To a Greek traveler or historian writing centuries later, "Babylonia" often served as a general term for the entire region. 

Furthermore, the description of the gardens as "hanging" likely refers to the tiered terraces and vaulted arches used to support the soil, a technique well-documented in Assyrian architecture. Over time, the grandeur of Sennacherib’s achievements was erroneously attributed to the more famous city of Babylon, transforming a feat of Assyrian engineering into a Babylonian legend.

## Key Facts
* **The Traditional View:** Attributed to Nebuchadnezzar II in Babylon to please his homesick wife, Amytis.
* **The Revisionist View:** Attributed to Sennacherib in Nineveh, based on archaeological and textual evidence.
* **Hydraulic Engineering:** The gardens required advanced irrigation, such as the Archimedes' screw or similar lifting mechanisms.
* **The Literary Gap:** No Babylonian tablets from the 6th century BCE mention the gardens.
* **Scholarly Debate:** While the Nineveh theory is gaining traction, some historians still argue the gardens were a poetic idealization rather than a physical place.

## Did You Know?
* Sennacherib’s aqueduct at Jerwan is one of the oldest known examples of a bridge-aqueduct in history.
* The term "Hanging Gardens" is a mistranslation; the original Greek word *kremastos* can mean "overhanging" or "terraced."
* Nineveh was once the largest city in the world before its fall in 612 BCE.
