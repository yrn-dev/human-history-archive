# Egyptians

## Physical Characteristics and Origins
The Egyptians are described as an African race, possessing physical peculiarities and a complexion that resemble various tribes found in the central and eastern parts of Africa. Their language also shares greater analogies in grammatical construction with African idioms than with those of other regions. Some observers have noted that Egyptians are dark-skinned and have curly hair.

There is a historical connection between the Egyptians and the people of Colchis. The Egyptians believed the Colchians were a portion of the army of Sesostris, while the Colchians maintained a stronger remembrance of the Egyptians.

## Customs and Daily Life
The Egyptians practiced several distinct customs that differed from other nations. They were among the few races—alongside the Ethiopians and Colchians—to practice circumcision from the earliest times. Other groups, such as the Phenicians and Syrians in Palestine, as well as the Macronians and Syrians near the rivers Parthenios and Thermodon, are said to have learned this practice from the Egyptians or Colchians.

In terms of personal grooming, Egyptian priests shaved their heads, unlike priests in other lands who wore long hair. Conversely, while most people cut their hair short during mourning, Egyptians let the hair on their heads and chins grow long.

Their dietary and labor habits were also unique. Egyptians made their bread from maize (also called spelt), kneading the dough with their feet. They used their hands to work with clay and gather dung. In their maritime practices, they fastened the rings and ropes of sails inside the ship rather than outside. Their system of writing and reckoning with pebbles moved from right to left, which they considered the "rightwise" direction. They utilized two types of characters for writing: common and sacred.

## Social and Political Structure
Egyptian society was organized into hereditary professions or castes, including a military order and a priesthood. The population of the towns was large, and the people were characterized by a profound submission. This submission was evident in the destructive toil imposed upon them to create the country's great monuments.

The Egyptian kings were drawn from different parts of the country. In early times, Thebes and Upper Egypt held more importance than Lower Egypt, though this shifted by the time of Herodotus. Historical records of Egypt before the reign of Psammetichus are not known. Under Psammetichus, Greeks were first introduced into Egypt, and Greek mercenaries became important to the Egyptian kings, leading to the creation of a caste of interpreters.

Later rulers included Nekôs, the son of Psammetichus, who was defeated by Nebuchadnezzar at Carchemisch; Psammis, the son of Nekôs; and Apriês. Amasis eventually dethroned Apriês with the help of native soldiers. Amasis encouraged Grecian commerce and established a religious and factory center for Greeks at Naukratis, leading to a period of prosperity.

## Religion and Beliefs
The Egyptian conception of history did not distinguish between human kings and gods; both were viewed as essential elements of the same series. Their religious life was centered on a powerful desire for immortality, creating an "immortality religion" unlike any other. This craving for a life of compensation intensified as the nation fell under foreign conquerors and the gods lost political significance.

The Egyptians practiced the worship of animals. One such deity was Apis (called Epaphos by the Hellenes), who appeared at long intervals. His appearance prompted the Egyptians to wear their finest garments and hold festivities.

## Historical Accounts and External Relations
Egypt maintained commercial links with Assyria through the Phenicians. However, the archive notes that much of the archaeology and history of Egypt provided by Grecian sources, such as Diodorus and Plato, is blended with Grecian myths, philosophy, and romance. For instance, the eponymous kings Ægyptus and Neileus are likely Grecian inventions.

Other historical accounts describe a period of conflict involving Moses. When the Egyptian king rejected the words of Moses, the Egyptians were struck by grievous plagues. One such plague turned the Egyptian river into bloody water, making it undrinkable and causing bitter torment to those who tried to drink it, while the water remained sweet and natural for the Hebrews.
