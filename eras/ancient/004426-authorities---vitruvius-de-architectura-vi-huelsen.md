# Vitruvius and the Ancient Basilica

The knowledge of the ancient basilica as a civil structure is derived primarily from the writings of Vitruvius, supplemented by existing remains, inscriptions, and incidental notices from classical writers. While some writers debate whether the term "basilica" referred to a specific architectural form or merely the use of the building, the evidence suggests a normal form existed, despite various deviations in specific examples.

## Relation to the Forum
Vitruvius treats the basilica as an adjunct to the forum. In early classical times in Italy and Greece, political, commercial, and legal business, as well as pageants and shows, took place in the open forum. As populations grew and business increased, additional accommodations were required. While theaters and amphitheatres handled games and performances, and markets served buyers and sellers, the basilica served as a covered forum. 

This structure provided shelter and quiet for merchants to transact business. It also housed legal tribunals, though it was not exclusively a law court. Magistrates presiding over these tribunals sometimes utilized platforms that were rectangular or curved in plan as part of the building's permanent fittings.

## Architectural Specifications
According to Vitruvius, the normal plan of a basilica is a rectangle. The length should be no less than twice and no more than three times the width. In cases where the site requires a greater length, the surplus is designated as *chalcidica*, which are open vestibules.

The interior is organized into a central space and side aisles. These aisles are one-third the width of the central space. The columns separating the nave from the aisles are equal in height to the width of the aisles. 

## Roof and Lighting
The aisles are covered by a flat roof known as a *contignatio*, which serves as a terrace for walking. To conceal those walking on the terrace from the merchants below, a breastwork or parapet called a *pluteum* surrounds the inner side of the terrace. 

A second row of columns stands atop this parapet; these columns are three-quarters the height of the lower columns. The spaces between these upper columns, above the *pluteum*, are left open to allow light into the central space. Vitruvius refers to the roof covering this central space as the *mediana testudo*. Vitruvius does not mention an apse or a permanent tribunal in these specifications.

## Comparative Examples and Variations
The ground plan of the basilica at Pompeii illustrates the Vitruvian description, although its superstructure did not follow the Vitruvian scheme. The Pompeii example includes a portico (chalcidicum), a hall of basilica, aisles, an altar, a tribunal, and offices.

While later basilicas might vary in their architectural schemes, the majority of earlier basilicas followed the essential columned structure described by Vitruvius, featuring a central space with galleries (generally in two stories) and clerestory lighting.

## Other Architectural Notes
Beyond the basilica, Vitruvius provides specifications for other structures. The *ephebeum*, a large hall in the ancient Palaestra used for the exercises of youths aged sixteen to eighteen, was furnished with seats. Vitruvius specifies that the length of the ephebeum should be one-third larger than its width.
