# The Nineveh Hypothesis: Relocating the Hanging Gardens

While traditional historical narratives place the Hanging Gardens within the walls of Babylon, a growing body of scholarly evidence suggests we may be looking for them in the wrong city. For centuries, the gardens were attributed to King Nebuchadnezzar II, who supposedly built them to soothe his homesick wife, Amytis of Media. However, the glaring absence of any mention of these gardens in the extensive Babylonian records of the era has led historians to reconsider the site's true location.

The alternative theory proposes that the "Hanging Gardens" were actually located in Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire, and were the creation of King Sennacherib.

## The Silence of Babylon vs. The Records of Nineveh

The primary tension in the "myth or reality" debate is the lack of archaeological evidence in Babylon. Despite extensive excavations, no structures matching the descriptions of the gardens have been found. Conversely, the archives of Nineveh contain detailed accounts of Sennacherib’s massive irrigation projects.

Sennacherib, who reigned from 705 to 681 BCE, described a "wonder" of a palace complex that included a sophisticated system of canals and aqueducts. These systems brought water from the mountains to the city, feeding lush terraces and exotic flora. To a Greek traveler or a later historian, the descriptions of Nineveh’s botanical achievements would mirror the legendary accounts of the Hanging Gardens.

## The Confusion of the Classics

How did the gardens migrate from Nineveh to Babylon in the historical record? Most historians believe this was a result of "geographic blurring" by later Greek and Roman writers. By the time writers like Berossus and Diodorus Siculus were recording these tales, the distinction between the various Mesopotamian empires had faded. 

Because Babylon was the most famous city of the region, it became the default setting for the era's architectural marvels. The "Hanging Gardens" likely became a composite memory—a blend of Sennacherib’s actual engineering feats in Nineveh and the general grandeur associated with the Babylonian empire.

## Key Facts
* **Traditional Attribution:** Historically credited to Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon (c. 605–562 BCE).
* **The Alternative Theory:** Proposed by Dr. Stephanie Dalley, suggesting King Sennacherib of Nineveh (c. 705–681 BCE) was the true builder.
* **Engineering:** If they existed, they required advanced hydraulic systems, such as Archimedes' screws, to lift water to upper terraces.
* **The Source Gap:** No contemporary Babylonian cuneiform tablets mention the gardens.
* **The Nineveh Evidence:** Assyrian reliefs depict lush, terraced gardens and complex waterworks.

## Did You Know?
* **Botanical Diversity:** Ancient accounts claim the gardens featured plants from all over the known world, creating a prehistoric "botanical garden."
* **The "Hanging" Misnomer:** The gardens weren't actually hanging; the Greek word *kremastos* likely referred to "overhanging" terraces or balconies.
* **Aqueduct Innovation:** Sennacherib's aqueduct at Jerwan is one of the oldest known examples of a stone bridge designed to carry water.
