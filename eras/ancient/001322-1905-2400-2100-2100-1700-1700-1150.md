# Chronological and Calendar Data (1700–2100)

The period between 1700 and 2100 is documented across various archival records, encompassing astronomical calculations, calendar adjustments, historical dynastic theories, and regional demographic data.

## Calendar Systems and Equations
The Gregorian calendar was designed to correct errors found in the Julian year by suppressing three intercalations every 400 years. This adjustment was intended to restore the vernal equinox to March 21, as it had been during the council of Nicaea. To achieve this, Gregory directed that October 5 be reckoned as October 15. 

Between 1582 and 1700, the difference between the old (Julian) and new (Gregorian) styles was ten days. Because 1700 was a leap year in the Julian calendar but a common year in the Gregorian, the difference increased to eleven days during the 18th century. In the 19th century, the difference became twelve days because 1800 was also a common year in the new calendar. From 1900 to 2100 inclusive, the difference is thirteen days.

The archive also details the use of "equations" to manage epacts:
*   **Solar Equation:** Occurs in years including 1700, 1800, 1900, 2100, 2200, and 2300. This event diminishes epacts by unity.
*   **Lunar Equation:** Occurs in years including 1800, 2100, 2400, 2700, 3000, 3300, 3600, and 3900. This event augments epacts by unity.

When both equations occur simultaneously—as they did in 1800, 2100, and 2700—they compensate for one another, and the epacts remain unchanged. For example, while the solar equation in 1900 shifted the line of epacts to B, the simultaneous equations in 2100 meant that line B continued to serve for three centuries, from 1900 to 2200.

## Historical and Dynastic Chronologies
The archive contains various scholarly estimates regarding early dynastic periods, specifically for Dynasties I, II, and III. These estimates vary significantly by source:
*   **Winckler (1905):** Dates Dynasty I from approximately 2400–2100 B.C., Dynasty II from approximately 2100–1700 B.C., and Dynasty III from approximately 1700–1150 B.C.
*   **Oppert (1888):** Places Dynasty I at 2506–2202 B.C., Dynasty II at 2202–1834 B.C., and Dynasty III at 1834–1257 B.C.
*   **Sayce (1899/1902):** Dates Dynasty I to 2478 (or 2460)–2174 B.C., Dynasty II to 2174–1806 B.C., and Dynasty III to 1806–1229 B.C.
*   **Delitzsch (1891/1907):** Estimates Dynasty I at approximately 2399–2094 (or 2420–2120) B.C., Dynasty II at 2094–1726 (or 2120–1752) B.C., and Dynasty III at 1726–1150 (or 1752–1176) B.C.

Other records mention Babylonia, noting that cadastral surveys for taxation existed in the age of Sargon of Akkad (3800 B.C.) and that clay tablets in the British Museum date back to 2300 or 2100 B.C.

### British Peerage
Records regarding the Grimston family note that Samuel (1643–1700), the only surviving son of Sir Harbottle, died in October 1700, causing the baronetcy to become extinct. In 1700, William Luckyn (grandson of Mary, Sir Harbottle's eldest daughter) succeeded to the estates of Sir Samuel Grimston and adopted the name Grimston. This William Luckyn Grimston (1683–1756) was later created Viscount Grimston and Baron Dunboyne in the Irish peerage in 1719.

### The West Indies
The era of the buccaneers effectively ended around 1700. French and English buccaneers had been unified in aggression for 75 years, but this bond broke in 1689 due to war between their respective countries. Following a short peace from 1697 to 1700, hostilities resumed in 1700. The accession of the French claimant to the throne and the subsequent rise of Spain closed the career of the buccaneers.
