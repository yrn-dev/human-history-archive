# BAPTISM

Baptism is a rite of initiation and purification with origins and practices that evolved across different eras and denominations. Its early history involves a transition from the practices of John the Baptist and Jewish proselyte baths to the established sacraments of the Christian Church and later sectarian developments.

## Origins and Early Development
The origins of Christian baptism are linked to the baptism of John. For publicans and soldiers, John's baptism served as a purification from the past, similar to the bath of a proselyte, but it further assured them of a place in the kingdom of God and raised them as children unto Abraham without requiring circumcision. For Jews, however, John's baptism aligned with a practice used by those wishing to consecrate their lives through stricter practice and study of the law.

During the apostolic age, the rite became identified with Jesus of Nazareth. Formulas used included "into Christ Jesus," "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ," or "of the Lord Jesus Christ." Paul interpreted these as being equivalent to "into the death of Christ Jesus," symbolizing the faithful being raised from death into everlasting life. This likeness to Christ's death and resurrection was intended to ensure a real union between the believer and Christ.

## The Baptistery and Administration
In the early Church, the baptistery was a separate hall or chapel connected to the church where catechumens were instructed and the sacrament administered. These buildings were commonly circular, though some had twelve or eight sides. A typical baptistery consisted of an ante-room (*proaulios oikos*) for instruction and confession of faith, and an inner apartment containing the baptismal font (*kolumbethra* or *piscina*). 

The font, originally made of stone and later sometimes of metal, was designed for trine immersion, with three steps leading down to the floor. A gold or silver dove was often suspended over the font, and walls were frequently decorated with scenes from the life of John the Baptist. Because the bishop typically baptized all catechumens in his diocese and the rite was performed only three times a year, baptisteries were often large—sometimes large enough to host synods and councils—and were generally attached to cathedrals rather than parish churches. During months when no baptisms occurred, the bishop's seal was used to seal the doors.

Baptisteries became prominent after Constantine made Christianity the state religion in the 4th century. By the 6th century, fonts began to be built in the church porch or within the church itself. After the 9th century, few were built, with notable later examples found in Lucca, Parma, Padua, Florence, and Pisa. An example of an octagonal baptistery is found south of the basilica of St John Lateran, featuring a baptismal basin sunk below the floor.

## Rituals and Formulas
Early baptismal practices included several features: the use of fonts, exorcism, the status of the baptizer, and the *disciplina arcani*. The *Teaching of the Apostles* (c. 90-120) prescribed baptism in living water in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Spirit, though it allowed for other water or warm water if necessary.

The use of the trinitarian formula and trine immersion was not uniform. In the 3rd century, baptism in the name of Christ remained widespread; Pope Stephen declared such baptisms valid. Similarly, Pope Nicholas (858-867) allowed baptism *tantum in nomine Christi*. Conversely, Basil condemned baptism into the Lord alone as insufficient, and some Armenian fathers specified that baptism "into the death of Christ" was the only essential element.

## Confirmation and Regional Variations
In the West, the rite of confirmation (the reception of the Spirit) was separated from the baptism of regeneration and reserved for adults. The Roman church practiced this earlier than the English church, which confirms at sixteen or fifteen. In this tradition, god-parents take baptismal vows vicariously for infants. In the East, however, the complete rite is performed over the child from the start.

Different regions handled the baptism of those previously baptized by heretics differently. The Roman church decreed in the 3rd century that such persons should only have hands laid upon them. In contrast, the East and Africa insisted on repeating the entire rite of baptism and confirmation. The Cathars of the middle ages rejected water baptism as a Jewish rite, instead practicing "spiritual baptism" through the laying on of hands and the *traditio precis*.

## Later Sectarian Developments
In England, two distinct groups of Baptists emerged: the General Baptists (Arminian, influenced by Mennonite Anabaptists) and the Particular Baptists (Calvinist, springing from the Independents). Both groups rejected infant baptism. While they initially rarely practiced immersion, they eventually reached an agreement on the proper mode, subjects, and administrator of baptism, leading to a 1644 Confession of Faith by the Particular Baptist churches of London.
