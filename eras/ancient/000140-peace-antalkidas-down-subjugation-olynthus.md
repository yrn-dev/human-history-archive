# From the Peace of Antalkidas to the Subjugation of Olynthus

The period beginning with the Peace of Antalkidas represents a significant and mournful era in Grecian history, characterized by the shifting power dynamics between Sparta, Persia, and the various autonomous cities of Greece.

## The Peace of Antalkidas
The Peace of Antalkidas, also referred to as a convention, is described as an edict drawn up, issued, and enforced by Persia upon Greece. This arrangement signaled a degradation of Pan-hellenic dignity, as Sparta entered into a separate partnership with Persia, increasing its submission to the Persian king as a means of securing help. This subservience followed a history of Spartan applications for Persian aid, including an active partnership against Athens after the Athenian catastrophe at Syracuse.

The convention included a promise of universal autonomy for the cities, a concept popular to the Grecian ear. However, the Spartans did not intend to grant general autonomy. Instead, they used the peace to isolate Athens and make immediate points against Thebes and Corinth. Through this peace, Sparta became the practical mistress of the Corinthian isthmus and Corinth.

## Spartan Hegemony and Aggressions
In the eight years following the peace (387–379 B.C.), Sparta utilized its position to aggrandize itself at the expense of autonomous cities. Isokrates denounced the Lacedæmonians as traitors to the freedom and security of Greece, accusing them of assisting foreign kings and Grecian despots—specifically Artaxerxes, Amyntas, and Dionysius—to absorb the free Hellenic world. During this time, Sparta undertook unjust aggressions against Mantinea, Phlius, Thebes, and Olynthus.

In the case of Phlius, the surrender to Agesilaus occurred nearly simultaneously with the fall of Olynthus. The proceedings in Phlius were conducted by a "Hekatontarchy," whose power was analogous to the Lysandrian Dekarchies or the Thirty at Athens.

## The Subjugation of Olynthus
The subjugation of Olynthus by Sparta is viewed as a signal misfortune for the Grecian world. Olynthus had led a growing confederacy in the Chalkidic peninsula, serving as a bulwark for Greece against Thracian and Macedonian tribes. This confederacy was characterized by equal and generous principles, employing only the minimum compulsion necessary to overcome the instinct of town-autonomy.

The intervention of Sparta crushed this liberal promise. Between 382 and 379 B.C., Sparta was at the maximum of its power and had its hands free, as it maintained a garrison in the Kadmeia of Thebes. The urgency of the reluctant Akanthians, who begged Sparta to act before the Olynthian confederacy could fully realize its benefits, facilitated this destruction.

## The Decline of Spartan Influence
Sparta's triumphant position was short-lived. Only a few months after the submission of Olynthus, Theban exiles retook the Kadmeia. Xenophon notes that the Lacedæmonians were punished for violating their oaths to leave cities autonomous. The Theban faction that had originally introduced the Spartans into the citadel to ensure the city's enslavement was eventually put down by seven assailants among the exiles.

By 371 B.C., Spartan ascendency had effectively vanished. Following the battle of Leuktra, Sparta was driven to contend for its own home and family, potentially due to the dispositions of its Helots and Periœki in Laconia. Her influence over Greeks outside of Laconia became extinct. This vacuum of power led Athens to invite various Peloponnesian cities to enter a common league based on the peace of Antalkidas and the peace sworn at Sparta. While cities like Corinth joined, the Eleians refused because their sovereignty over the Skilluntians, Triphylians, and Marganeis was not recognized.

## Contemporary Perspectives and Legal Guarantees
The conduct of Sparta during this era drew sharp criticism. While Lysias expressed his censure through surprise, Isokrates spoke plainly against Spartan ambition. Additionally, the peace of Antalkidas served as a legal guarantee for certain cities. For example, a speaker from Platæa complained that Thebes had violated the terms of the peace of Antalkidas when Platæa was taken by the Thebans during a time of peace.
