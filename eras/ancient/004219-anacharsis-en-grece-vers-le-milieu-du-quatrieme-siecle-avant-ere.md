# Anacharsis en Grece, vers le milieu du quatrieme siecle avant l'ere

## Overview of the Work
*Voyage du jeune Anacharsis en Grece, vers le milieu du quatrieme siecle avant l'ere chretienne* is a four-volume work published in 1787 by Barthelemy. The author spent thirty years working on the text, having begun the project in 1757. Rather than intending to create a register of accurately ascertained facts, Barthelemy sought to provide his countrymen with knowledge of Greek civilization in an interesting format. While modern scholarship has superseded many of the details found within the *Voyage*, the work is noted for its charm of style, a quality that distinguishes it from similar efforts such as W. A. Becker's *Charicles*.

## Narrative and Structure
The work follows a hero, a young Scythian who is a descendant of the famous philosopher Anacharsis. The narrative posits that this young man traveled to Greece in his early youth to seek instruction. After touring the islands, colonies, and republics of Greece, he is said to have returned to his native land. The book is presented as having been written by the hero in his old age, following the period when the Macedonian hero overturned the Persian empire.

The structure of the work mimics that of modern travelers. The hero provides accounts of the antiquities, government, and customs of the regions he is supposed to have visited. To supplement the narrative, Barthelemy included a copious introduction to provide necessary historical details. Additionally, the work contains various dissertations covering several specialized topics:
* The literature of the Athenians.
* The music of the Greeks.
* The manners, customs, ruling passions, pursuits, and economy of the surrounding states.

## The Historical Anacharsis
The archive contains separate accounts regarding the historical figure of Anacharsis, the Scythian. According to Herodotus, Anacharsis visited many lands and displayed great wisdom. However, his adoption of foreign customs led to his death. While sailing through the Hellespont, Anacharsis stopped at Kyzicos and witnessed a magnificent festival in honor of the Mother of the gods. He vowed that if he returned safely to his own land, he would hold a night festival and perform the same rites.

Upon returning to the region of Hylaia in Scythia—an area characterized by a variety of trees and located beside the racecourse of Achilles—Anacharsis attempted to perform these ceremonies using images hung about himself and a kettle-drum. A fellow Scythian reported this to King Saulios. Upon witnessing these acts, King Saulios killed Anacharsis with an arrow. Consequently, the Scythians of the time claimed not to know Anacharsis because he had gone to Hellas and adopted foreign customs. Genealogical records from Tymnes, the steward of Ariapeithes, indicate that Anacharsis was the son of Gnuros, grandson of Lycos, and great-grandson of Spargapeithes. He was also the uncle on the father's side of King Idanthyrsos; thus, Anacharsis was killed by his own brother, as Idanthyrsos was the son of Saulios.

## Philosophical Interactions in Athens
Other accounts describe Anacharsis's time in Athens, where he is said to have conversed privately with Thales and Solon. Upon meeting Solon, Anacharsis requested friendship. When Solon suggested that such friendships were best made at home, Anacharsis replied that Solon, being at home, should therefore enter into friendship with him.

During his stay, Anacharsis observed Solon composing laws to restrain the greed and crimes of citizens. Anacharsis mocked this effort, comparing written laws to spiders' webs that catch the weak but are broken through by the strong. Solon countered that he had designed the laws so that it was to every citizen's advantage to abide by them. Anacharsis also expressed surprise during a public assembly that, in Greece, ignorant men decided public affairs while wise men were the ones speaking upon them.
