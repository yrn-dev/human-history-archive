# Military Organization and Logistics in Ancient and Modern Warfare

## The Assyrian Military
In the second Assyrian empire, the military was characterized by a standing army, which is believed to have been first organized in Assyria. The army was raised, at least in part, through conscription. The military aristocracy of Assyria served as a social counterpart to the priesthood found in Babylonia.

Under the leadership of various kings, the army underwent successive improvements. Chariots were eventually superseded by cavalry. Tiglath-pileser III introduced high boots and saddles for riders, and Sennacherib established a corps of slingers. The Assyrian military structure included a commander-in-chief, known as the *tartan*, who held a rank second only to the king. During marches, the army transported battering-rams, baggage-carts, and tents.

## Logistics and Transport in Classical Antiquity
Logistics and the management of baggage were critical components of ancient campaigns. During the retreat of the Ten Thousand Greeks following the death of Cyrus, the troops faced severe food shortages. Lacking bread, they killed baggage animals for food and used baggage carts, arrows, and discarded wooden Egyptian shields as fuel for fires to cook their meat.

In other conflicts, baggage and equipment were primary targets of capture. During a Roman campaign in Epirus, Roman forces seized the tents, slaves, and baggage of their enemy. In the pursuit of Darius III after the battle of Arbela, the fleeing caravan abandoned women and baggage. Darius himself was eventually found dead in a deserted mule-cart, which had been left behind by Bessus and his captains.

Technical equipment was also transported via carts. During the attack on the fort at Delium, Boeotian forces—which included slingers and darters from the Malian Gulf—utilized a specialized engine to set fire to the fort's walls. This engine, consisting of a scooped-out beam plated with iron and a cauldron filled with pitch, sulphur, and lighted coals, was brought to the wall upon carts.

## Modern Military Logistics and Transport
In more recent conflicts, such as those involving German armies in the west, logistics became highly systematized. Supply hubs, such as the station at Dun, functioned as centers of animation where storehouses and baggage-rooms were packed with tinned foods, meat, wine, sacks of flour, oats, and ready-baked loaves.

Supply columns utilized requisitioned farmers' carts drawn by horses, typically two per cart. These columns followed specific roads marked by placards and were guided by pickets to prevent congestion at cross-roads. Wagons were meticulously marked to identify their unit; for example, one wagon was labeled as belonging to the 1st ammunition column of the 3rd (Mortar) Battalion of the Foot Artillery Regiment.

Various specialized columns were employed:
* **Ammunition Columns:** Both infantry and artillery ammunition columns were used. Artillery columns utilized teams of six horses.
* **Food Columns:** Known as *Fuhrparkkolonne*, these were composed of farmers' carts.
* **Medical and Support:** The army utilized dressing wagons and field kitchens, the latter referred to as *Erbsenkanone* ("pea-shooter").

During halts, soldiers utilized shelter-tents for encampments. Military police were employed to preside over crossings and keep notes on marching columns.
