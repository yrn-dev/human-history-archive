# Architecture and Early Christian Basilicas

The study of early Christian and Byzantine architecture is documented through a wide array of historical authorities and archaeological surveys. Key scholarly references include the works of Vitruvius (*De Architectura*), H. Leclercq (*Manuel d'archéologie chrétienne*), and C. Bayet (*L'Art byzantin*). Further specialized research into Byzantine art and history is found in publications such as *Byzantinische Zeitschrift* and *Oriens Christianus*.

## The Basilican Form and Regional Examples

The basilica is a prominent architectural form in early Christian history. In the East, several notable basilican churches are highlighted:
* **Mt. Sinai**: The monastery of St Catherine, constructed by Justinian.
* **Thessalonica**: The "very noble" church of St Demetrius.
* **Other Eastern Sites**: Basilicas located at Dana (between Antioch and Bir), St Philip at Athens, Bosra in Arabia, and Xanthus in Lycia.

Descriptions and views of these structures are preserved in the works of Couchaud (*Choix d'eglises byzantines*), Texier and Pullan (*Byzantine Architecture*), and the count de Vogue.

In North Africa, specifically within the Roman province, there are abundant remains of early Christian churches; S. Gsell has noted more than 130 examples in *Les Monuments antiques de l'Algerie*.

## Architecture in Western Europe

Basilicas of a strictly early Christian date are not currently found in Germany, Spain, or France. However, there are later examples of the basilican form in these regions:
* **France**: The "Basse Oeuvre" at Beauvais is described as very plain and may date from Carolingian times.
* **Germany**: An unaltered basilica from the time of Charles the Great exists at Michelstadt in the Odenwald. Additionally, the basilica of St Mauritius near Hildesheim, featuring fine columns, dates to the 11th century.
* **Modern Revivals**: The basilican form has been revived in a modern basilica located at Munich.

## Architectural Terminology and Components

In the field of architecture, the term "frustum" (derived from the Latin for "a piece broken off") is used to describe the drum of a column. In a broader geometric sense, a frustum refers to the part of a solid figure—such as a pyramid or cone—that lies between two parallel planes or is cut off by a plane parallel to the base.
