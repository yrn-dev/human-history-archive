# Spain: Early History and Occupations

## Early Tribal Composition
From the 5th century B.C., the geography of Spain is represented as being occupied by a congeries of tribes, primarily distinguished as Celts, Celtiberi, and Iberi. These groups lacked cohesion and were frequently at war with one another, moving constantly unless they were temporarily united to face a foreign enemy. As Mediterranean civilization advanced, the ruder tribes were driven toward the north.

In the southern region of Baetica, the tribes possessed a literature, written laws, and poems of ancient date, though none of these works have survived. Evidence of their existence is limited to marks on pottery, legends on coins, megalithic monuments, and inscriptions. These inscriptions, found across six geographical districts, utilize alphabets of Levant origin that differ slightly. While they still await an interpreter, they suggest that a general language was once spoken throughout Spain and extended a short distance onto the northern slope of the Pyrenees. Some scholars have noted that ancient Spanish toponymy—such as Iriberry (Iliberis) and Oloron (Iluro)—bears a strong resemblance to Basque place-names, leading to the theory that a people speaking a language akin to Basque were extended throughout Spain and possibly into Sardinia and Sicily.

## Phoenician and Carthaginian Influence
The south-western portion of Spain, between the Mediterranean and the Anas river (Guadiana), was occupied by the Turduli and Turditanians. These Iberian tribes are described as the most improvable and civilized section of the population, facilitating commercial relations with settlers on the isle of Leon. This region, known to the Greeks in the 6th century B.C. as Tartessus, was highly productive in wine, fish, cattle, corn, iron, and silver. For several centuries, the Phoenicians held a monopoly over the Tartessian trade, using Gadês as a center to establish relations with amber-gatherers from the Baltic and tin-mines in Cornwall.

The city of Cadiz (Gades) is traditionally attributed to Phoenician merchants from Tyre as early as 1100 B.C. By 501 B.C., it was occupied by the Carthaginians, who used it as a base to conquer southern Iberia and later to equip Hannibal's armaments against Rome. Similarly, Cartagena (Carthago Nova) was founded around 243 B.C. by the Carthaginian Hasdrubal. Its harbor and gold and silver mines provided immense wealth to the Carthaginians.

## Roman Conquest and Administration
Roman occupation began in various stages. Corduba, likely of Carthaginian origin, was occupied by Marcus Marcellus in 152 B.C., becoming the first Roman *colonia* in Spain. Due to the high number of noble colonists, it was titled *Patricia*. In 45 B.C., after the battle of Munda, Caesar took the city and massacred 20,000 inhabitants to punish its resistance. Under Augustus, Corduba became a municipality and the capital of the Romanized province of Baetica; during the time of Strabo, it was the largest city in Spain.

In the north, the Iberian tribe of the Astures maintained independence from the Carthaginians, extending their territory as far south as the Douro. They were noted for their gold and horses. Around 25 B.C., the Romans subjugated the district south of the Cantabrians, naming it Augustana with its capital at Asturica Augusta (modern Astorga). However, the warlike mountaineers of the northern districts, the Transmontana, remained hostile to Roman rule.

Other cities also flourished under Rome. Cadiz became a *municipium* under Augustus with the name *Augusta Urbs Gaditana*. Cartagena, taken from the Punic army by P. Scipio in 210 B.C., became the colony *Colonia Victrix Julia Nova Carthago*.

## Visigothic and Moorish Periods
Roman rule ended with the Visigothic conquest in the late 5th century. The Visigothic kings maintained the importance of Corduba from the 5th century until the beginning of the 8th century. In A.D. 425, the Goths pillaged and nearly destroyed Cartagena.

In 711, Roderick, the last Visigothic king, was defeated and died. By 713, the Moors had captured all of Spain except for Asturias and Galicia. Under Moorish rule, Corduba was initially an appanage of the Damascus caliphate, but in 756, Abd-ar-Rahman I established it as the capital of an independent caliphate. The city reached its peak in the mid-10th century under Abd-ar-Rahman III. Native chroniclers claim that at this time, Corduba contained 600 mosques, 900 baths, a university, numerous libraries, and 200,000 houses within its walls.

## The Christian Reconquest
The Christian reconquest began in the remoter fastnesses of the north. Pelayo the Goth, a surviving Christian leader, took refuge in the cave of Covadonga with 300 followers. The Asturians chose him as king in 718. Although Galicia was lost in 734, the levies of Pelayo prevented the Moors from penetrating the northern mountains. Following Pelayo's death in 737, the Asturians continued their resistance, eventually helping the people of Castile, Leon, and Galicia recover their liberty.

To fight the Moors, military religious orders were founded, including the orders of Calatrava, Alcantara, and St James of Compostella (the latter traditionally attributed to Ramiro II of Leon in the 10th century). In 1236, Ferdinand III of Castile captured Corduba, and in 1243, Ferdinand II of Castile destroyed an independent Moorish principality in Cartagena, which was finally conquered by James I of Aragon in 1276.
