# Ezra-Nehemiah and the History of the Israelites

The books of Ezra and Nehemiah serve as a historical narrative and a direct sequel to the books of Chronicles. Together with Chronicles, they form a series of historical texts that document the experience of the Israelites during and after their captivity in Babylon.

## The Babylonian Captivity and Return
The Babylonian Captivity refers to the deportation of the Israelites to Babylon by Nebuchadrezzar. This occurred in three separate stages. The first took place in 597 B.C. during the time of Jehoiachin, resulting in the partial despoiling of the temple in Jerusalem and the removal of leading citizens. Eleven years later, during the reign of Zedekiah, a fresh rising of the Judaeans led to the city being razed to the ground and a further deportation. A third captivity is recorded five years after that event.

Following the Persian overthrow of Babylonia, Cyrus granted the Israelites permission to return to their native land in 537 B.C. While some sources suggest a massive number returned, other records—including Ezra 2:61, 1 Esd. 5:40, and Nehemiah 7:66—agree that the general sum was approximately 42,360 people. These returnees were led by Zorobabel. While a remnant returned, an immense number of the ten tribes remained beyond the Euphrates; these individuals continued to be regarded as observers of the laws of Moses.

## The Roles of Ezra and Nehemiah
Under the protection of Darius, and encouraged by the prophets Zechariah and Haggai, the Israelites completed the construction of the temple, which had been delayed for many years due to the cabals of enemies. 

Subsequently, Artaxerxes sent Ezra to Jerusalem. Ezra is credited with restoring the observation of the law and public worship. Following Ezra, Nehemiah was sent to the city. Nehemiah's primary contribution was the construction of walls around Jerusalem to fortify the city against neighbors who were jealous of its reviving greatness. It is noted that the prophet Malachi was either a contemporary of Nehemiah or prophesied shortly after him. Some historical accounts suggest that Ezra arrived on the seventh year of Xerxes, while Nehemiah arrived on the twenty-fifth, leaving an eighteen-year interval between their arrivals.

## Composition and Structure of the Texts
The books of Ezra and Nehemiah share a similar structure, utilizing a combination of first-person memoirs and compiler narratives. The memoirs of Ezra are found in Ezra vii. 12-ix. (which includes the decree of Artaxerxes) and likely form the basis for Ezra x. and Neh. viii.-x. The memoirs of Nehemiah are located in Neh. i. 1-vii. 73a, xii. 31-41, and xiii. Additionally, a narrative written in Aramaic is found in Ezra iv. 8-vi. 18.

The compiler of these works, as well as the compiler of Chronicles, often viewed the past through the lens of their own era. In the Chronicles, for example, pre-exilic Judah is depicted as already possessing the fully developed ceremonial system and principles of the compiler's own day. Because of this focus on ecclesiastical aspects, the work has been referred to as the "Ecclesiastical Chronicle of Jerusalem."

## Canonical Placement and Tradition
In the Jewish canon, the Old Testament is divided into three parts: the Torah (Law), the Prophets, and the Writings (Hagiographa). Ezra and Nehemiah are placed within the "Writings," where they are grouped together with Daniel as a single book. Historically, the Israelites treated the books of Samuel, Kings, Ezra, Nehemiah, and Chronicles each as a single book; they were not divided into two separate books until the 16th century due to Christian influence.

The final form of the Old Testament Scripture, including the compilation of the psalter (which contains psalms written after the return from captivity, such as the 126th and 137th), was set forth after the return from Babylon and before the time of Ptolemaeus Philadelphus.
