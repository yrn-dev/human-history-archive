# Early Princes

The history of early princes and rulers is documented through various archaeological monuments, inscriptions, and historical accounts across different ancient civilizations, including those of Mesopotamia and Greece.

## Early Rulers of Mesopotamia
The earliest approximately dated monuments from Lagash (Tello) provide evidence of early political structures and leadership. Records from this region mention a "king of Kengi" and Me-silim, the king of Kis. Me-silim had dealings with Lugal-suggur, who served as the high-priest of Lagash, as well as the high-priest of a neighboring town provisionally transcribed as Gis-ukh. According to Scheil, Gis-ukh is represented by Jokha, located west of the Shatt el-Hai and south of F[=a]ra. This identification is supported by a seal-cylinder where two of its rulers are titled kings of T[=e].

Over time, the high-priests of Lagash assumed kingship, and Ur-Nin[=a] founded a dynasty there. Archaeological finds in a building Ur-Nin[=a] attached to the temple of Nin[=a] include onyx plates and terra-cotta bas-reliefs depicting the king and his sons. Additionally, onyx heads of lions, which resemble Egyptian work, were found; these were dedicated to the goddess Bau as "booty."

The grandson of Ur-Nin[=a], E-anna-du, expanded his power to become the master of all southern Babylonia. His dominion included the cities of Larsa (?), Ur, and Erech, as well as "the district of Sumer." E-anna-du also annexed the kingdom of Kis, though Kis regained its independence following his death. Under his rule, Gis-ukh became a tributary state, requiring each person to pay a specific amount of grain into the treasuries of the god Ingurisa and the goddess Nin[=a].

The victory of E-anna-du is commemorated by the "Stele of the Vultures," currently housed in the Louvre. This monument depicts various war scenes:
* The king in a chariot holding a curved weapon made of three metal bars bound by rings.
* Kilted followers wearing helmets and carrying lances marching behind the king.
* Vultures feeding on the bodies of defeated enemies.
* The construction of a tumulus over those slain on the side of Lagash.

## Early Spartan Princes
In the internal history of Sparta, the earliest determinable event is the introduction of the Lykurgean discipline. Regarding external events, King Têleklus is credited with the conquest of Geronthræ, Pharis, and Amyklæ. Amyklæ was located approximately two and a half miles from Sparta. Têleklus was eventually slain during the first quarrel with the Messenians.

Historical records suggest that early Spartan kings may have undertaken expeditions against the Kynurians, the Argeians, and locations such as Kleitor (on the northern side of Arcadia) and Helos on the sea-coast. However, evidence suggests that if Helos and Kynuria were conquered by these early rulers, they had to be conquered a second time by kings who succeeded Têleklus. Other areas assumed to have been conquered early include Belemina, Sellasia, the upper valley of the Eurotas, and the valley of the Œnus.

## Historical Interpretation of Monuments
The use of monuments to deduce the history of early princes is subject to scholarly caution. In the case of early Assyrian history, while cuneiform inscriptions may provide positive knowledge of a period otherwise in darkness, monuments of art alone are considered insufficient for historical inferences. For instance, sculptures depicting a king receiving captives or taking a city by assault are not automatically accepted as commemorations of real, positive conquests without corroborative proof. Historians emphasize the need to discriminate between mythical and historical subjects.
