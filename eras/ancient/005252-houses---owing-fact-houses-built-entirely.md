# Houses

The construction and design of houses have varied significantly across different eras and regions, influenced by available materials, social hierarchies, and environmental conditions.

## Early Secular and Religious Buildings
In certain early periods, houses were constructed entirely of perishable materials such as wattle and wood. Because of this, knowledge of these structures relies almost exclusively on literary evidence. Walls were typically built using posts set at short distances with the gaps filled by clay-daubed wattlework, or by laying stout planks either horizontally or vertically. These buildings were generally rectangular, with gable sides that were likely shorter than the other walls. Roofs were high-pitched and covered with reeds, hay, straw, or tiles.

Internal partitions were rare. In wealthy establishments, separate buildings within a single enclosure served the purpose of rooms. Key structures in such a complex included the *heall* (hall), used for reception and dining, and the *brydbur* (lady's bower), which functioned as a bedroom for the mistress and master. Other necessary buildings included a bakehouse, kitchen, farm buildings, and quarters for attendants. The entire group of buildings was situated within an enclosure known as a *tun*, which was surrounded by a stockade called a *burg* (potentially resting on an earthwork). Peasant homesteads were similarly surrounded by a fence, or *edor*.

While two-storeyed houses were not evident in early times, they became common during the 10th and 11th centuries. Windows were simple openings in the roof or walls, as glass was not utilized for this purpose until the latter part of the 7th century. For heating, open fires in the center of the building were most common, though stoves were also known. Wealthy homes may have featured painting and carving for ornamentation.

Regarding religious structures, stone was first used for churches starting in the 7th century, with masons initially imported from Gaul. However, wood continued to be used for many churches and secular buildings until a much later date.

## Roman-Era Housing in Britain
In Silchester, private houses followed two primary designs: a row of rooms with a corridor, sometimes with additional rooms at the ends, or three such rows forming three sides of a large open square yard. These were detached houses, each situated in its own garden rather than in terraces. These types are recognized as the normal patterns for country houses throughout Roman Britain.

While these structures may have been Celtic types modified for Roman use—similar to Indian bungalows—their internal fittings were Roman, including mosaics, frescoes, and hypocausts. The largest house in Silchester, which featured a special annexe for baths, is generally considered to have been an inn or guest-house for those traveling between the west and London. Because the town contained only seventy or eighty houses of significant size and left large spaces unbuilt, Silchester resembled a village of scattered cottages rather than a town with continuous streets.

## Urban Housing and Architecture in Other Regions
In various global contexts, housing materials and layouts reflected local needs:

*   **Ashanti Kingdom:** In Kumasi, the king's palace was originally built of red sandstone, modeled after Dutch buildings at Elmina. After it was destroyed by the British in 1874, a new royal palace was constructed, though it was made of clay rather than brick.
*   **Bucharest:** Due to frequent earthquakes, many houses in Bucharest were built of wood. This made the city susceptible to fire; in 1847, a quarter of the city was destroyed. Older districts also contained antique one-storeyed houses surrounded by acacias and poplars.
*   **Rome:** In ancient Rome, houses were often crowded and heavy, leading to frequent collapses. The Roman Crassus exploited this by employing over five hundred slaves who were builders and architects to buy up houses on fire or those adjacent to fires at low prices.
*   **Germany:** In certain parts of the country, such as the area around Wittlich, houses in both towns and villages are almost entirely stone. The streets in these areas are very narrow, creating an impression that they were designed for protection against foreign invasion.

## Specialized and Monumental Structures
Some structures served specific religious or civic functions. In Jerusalem, King Solomon built the temple on a strong hill. Over time, the people expanded the site by adding banks and filling valleys with earth to level the ground with the city's narrow streets, utilizing stones measuring forty cubits in magnitude.

In Nidaros, King Magnus Olafson built the king's house and Olaf's church. Later, King Harald constructed a stone hall and the Mary Church, the latter of which was built so strongly with lime that it was difficult to demolish.
