# Bordeaux

Bordeaux, known in antiquity as *Burdigala*, is a major city in south-western France and the capital of the department of Gironde. It is situated on the west bank of the Garonne river, approximately 60 miles from the sea, within a plain that includes the Medoc wine-growing district. The city is positioned where the Garonne forms a semicircle, separating the main city on the left bank from the suburb of La Bastide on the right bank.

## Early History and Roman Period
Bordeaux was originally the chief town of the Bituriges Vivisci. During the Roman Empire, it developed into a flourishing commercial center. By the 4th century, it was designated as the capital of Aquitania Secunda. Ausonius, a native of the city and a 4th-century writer, described Bordeaux as a four-square city protected by walls and lofty towers, noting its status as one of the most significant educational centers in Gaul. Following the disintegration of the Roman Empire, the city suffered a period of decline and did not regain its prosperity until the beginning of the 10th century.

## English Rule and Administration
From 1154 to 1453, Bordeaux and Guienne were possessions of the English kings. During this nearly three-hundred-year period, the city served as the seat for the court of Edward the Black Prince, and his son Richard was born there. The English administration supported the city's trade and liberties. In 1235, Bordeaux was granted the right to elect its own mayors, who were assisted by a municipal council known as a "jurade."

The city's regional influence grew as it led a federation that included other important towns, such as Libourne and St Emilion. Extensive commerce flourished between Bordeaux merchants and English ports, specifically Chester, Bristol, Dartmouth, Exeter, Hull, and London. This period of English rule ended following the battle of Castillon in 1453; after a three-month siege, Bordeaux submitted to Charles VII.

## French Governance and Internal Conflict
Under French rule, the city's privileges were initially curtailed, though Louis XI partially restored them and established the parlement of Guienne in the city. In 1548, the inhabitants rose up by force of arms against the imposition of a salt-tax, a rebellion that was met with merciless severity by the constable Anne de Montmorency.

The city later became a center for the reformed religion, though nearly three hundred inhabitants were killed during the massacre of St Bartholomew. The 17th century was marked by instability; Bordeaux served as a primary support for the Fronde, and royal troops were dispatched in 1653 and 1675 to suppress insurrections against royal measures.

## Revolution and Modern Era
During the French Revolution, Bordeaux served as the headquarters for the Girondists. Consequently, the city suffered severely during the Reign of Terror, comparable to the experiences of Marseilles and Lyons. Under the rule of Napoleon I, the city's commerce was greatly reduced. In 1814, the city declared for the house of Bourbon, and Louis XVIII later bestowed the title of duc de Bordeaux upon his grand-nephew, the comte de Chambord. In 1870, as German forces approached Tours, the French government was transferred to Bordeaux.

## Geography and Urban Layout
The city is characterized by a crescent of busy quays featuring mansions, factories, and lofty warehouses. At the center of these quays is the Place des Quinconces, which is planted with plane trees and features two rostral columns acting as lighthouses, as well as statues of Montesquieu and Montaigne. A monument to the Girondin deputies proscribed in 1793 is located on its west side. The Place des Quinconces opens to the south-west into the Place de la Comedie, home to an 18th-century Grand Theatre.

The city's interior consists of narrow, winding streets near the center, while the outer areas feature low white houses rather than the high tenements found in other large French towns. A semicircle of boulevards surrounds the city, beyond which lie the suburbs of Begles, Talence, Merignac, Cauderan, and Le Bouscat. The city is connected by the Pont de Bordeaux, a stone structure from the early 19th century measuring 1,534 feet in length, and a railway bridge.

## Commerce and Industry
Bordeaux is the fourth largest port in France. Its harbor is formed by the basin of the Garonne and is divided by the Pont de Bordeaux. The northern portion is accessible to vessels drawing 21 to 26 feet, and the harbor can accommodate between 1,000 and 1,200 vessels. A 25-acre floating basin on the left bank handles the largest vessels.

The city's trade is primarily maritime, with major relations with the United States, Portugal, Argentina, Spain, and Great Britain. The total yearly value of imports and exports averages between 25 and 26 million sterling, with exports accounting for 13.5 million. About one quarter of these exports are sales of "Bordeaux" wines. Other significant exports include sugar, brandy, chemicals, rice, hides and skins, woollen and cotton goods, pitwood, fruit, salt-fish, oil-cake, and vegetables. Chief imports include coal, rubber, wine, rice, timber, fish, wool, hardware, chemicals, and agricultural machinery.

The most important industry is the construction and refitting of ships, including merchant vessels, torpedo-boats, and ironclads. The city also manufactures railway carriages and supports the wine trade through cooperage, wine-mixing, and the production of bottles, capsules, corks, wooden cases, and straw envelopes.
