# Perfections of the Deity in Respect of Creation and Providence

The conceptualization of the moral perfections of the Deity, specifically regarding the acts of creation and providence, has been explored through various philosophical, theological, and historical lenses. These perspectives range from the metaphysical inquiries of English divines and Greek philosophers to the mythological frameworks of ancient civilizations.

## Philosophical Inquiries into Divine Rectitude
In the early 18th century, the English divine and philosopher John Balguy authored an essay titled *Divine Rectitude: or, a Brief Inquiry concerning the Moral Perfections of the Deity, particularly in respect of Creation and Providence*. In this work, Balguy sought to demonstrate that the moral principles intended to direct human life also underlie the ways and works of God. He argued that goodness within the Deity is not merely a disposition toward benevolence. Instead, it is defined as a regard for harmony, beauty, and order—elements that are not simply relative to human capacities and faculties.

This inquiry aligned with the theories of Clarke and Cudworth, which posit that virtue consists of conformity to reason. Under this framework, acting according to "fitnesses" arises from the eternal and immutable relations between agents and objects.

## Metaphysical and Classical Perspectives
The nature of the Deity and the process of creation were central to the meditations of Plato. He contemplated the first self-existent, necessary cause of the universe, though he struggled to conceive how a purely incorporeal Being could execute a perfect model and mould "rude and independent chaos" with a plastic hand. To address these difficulties, Plato considered the divine nature through a threefold modification: the first cause, the soul or spirit of the universe, and the reason, or *Logos*. In this system, the *Logos* was viewed as the Governor and Creator of the world, and the Son of an Eternal Father.

In contrast to Plato's supposition that matter was eternal, Oriental and Jewish perspectives viewed matter as a creation of God, who alone was eternal. For instance, the *Zend Avesta* teaches that Ormuzd created the universe by the Word (*honover*), which is more ancient than the world itself.

## Ancient and Regional Conceptions of Creator Deities
Various ancient cultures attributed the creation and animation of the world to specific deities:

*   **Peru:** The Incas recognized several creator-gods. Viracocha was described in an old hymn as the "world-animator" and "world-former" and was associated with water. Pachacamac, whose name means "world-animator," was accorded theoretical supremacy; some Peruvian philosophers claimed he desired only worship of the heart, though he was also linked to the cosmic significance of subterranean fire. A third creator, Manco Capac ("the mighty man"), and his wife Mama Oello ("the mother-egg") were later represented as the sun and the moon. Peruvian tradition also includes a story of a "bad son" of the creator-god who attempted to undo the creation, leading his father to hurl him into the sea.
*   **Assyria:** In the "Creation tablet," the term *An-sar* was used to personify the heavens collectively as the "host of heaven," distinguished from *Ki-sar*, the "host of earth." Assyrian scribes likely attempted to identify the specific Assyrian deity, *Asur*, with this creation deity, *An-sar*.
*   **Egypt:** The Egyptians believed the Divinity placed their kings upon the throne, viewing the monarchs as visible representations of the Deity. They believed kings embodied the most noble characteristics of the Supreme Being, specifically the will and power to do good for others.

## The Absolute and Moral Attributes
In later metaphysical analysis, such as that discussed by Lotze, the "universal substance" or "absolute" is initially viewed without the attributes of a personal Deity. In this context, the absolute serves as a metaphysical postulate. For this notion to become a "living power," it must be linked to the experience of the significance of creation, the value of the good and the beautiful, and the worth of personal holiness. To describe the universal substance as the perfect realization of the good, the beautiful, and the holy requires an internal feeling of these real values.
