# SICILIAN AFFAIRS

## Overview of Sicilian and Greek Relations
The history of the Grecian communities in Sicily has often remained distinct from the events occurring in Eastern Greece and the Peloponnesus. However, during the period surrounding the Athenian expedition against Syracuse, the histories of the Peloponnesian Greeks and the Sicilian Greeks became intimately blended. Prior to this, the connection between the two regions had been merely occasional and produced little reciprocal effect.

## The Athenian Expedition and its Aftermath
The Athenian involvement in Sicily culminated in a siege of Syracuse, an ambitious project that resulted in a fatal hazard for Athens. This expedition ended in a lamentable fate for the Athenian armament, during which Nikias and Demosthenes perished along with nearly their entire force. This event marked a significant turning point that changed the face of the Peloponnesian war.

## The Reign of Dionysius the Elder
Following the destruction of the Athenian armament, Dionysius, known as the Elder, served as the despot at Syracuse. During the first eleven years of his reign, he engaged in a great war against the Carthaginians. At one point, Dionysius was hard pressed and actually besieged. However, a sudden turn of fortune occurred when the victorious Carthaginian army stationed before Syracuse was utterly ruined by a terrible pestilence. This collapse was further compounded by ignominious treason committed by the Carthaginian commander, Imilkon. The period of Sicilian affairs spanning from the destruction of this army by pestilence down to the death of Dionysius the Elder is dated between 394 and 367 B.C.

## Subsequent Governance and the Role of Dion
Following the death of Dionysius the Elder, Sicilian affairs continued under Dionysius the Younger and Dion. Dion's government was characterized by the use of pure force, a decision described as a deplorable mistake that was mischievous to both himself and Syracuse. This approach led Dion into a cycle of decline from which there was no possibility of amendment. During his rule, Dion made a martyr of Herakleides and likely would have created others had his life not been arrested early. Despite these failures, the philosopher Plato mourned Dion's death with sympathy and esteem, attributing the failure of Dion's government to factors other than Dion himself. Plato had previously exercised positive action on the destinies of Syracuse while serving as a counsellor to Dionysius II.

## Carthaginian Interventions and Influence
For two centuries, leading up to the conclusion of the second Punic war, the Carthaginians frequently intervened in Sicilian affairs on an extensive scale, powerfully affecting the destinies of the Sicilian Greeks. Carthage was a wealthy city with an oligarchical government and extensive colonial possessions along the coast of Africa (both east and west of the city), as well as the Balearic isles and Sardinia. While Carthage had few settlements in Spain at this time, its dominion over other dependencies was often harsh and extortionate.

In Sicily, the Carthaginian province was viewed more as a dependent ally than a colonial possession. This distinction is evident in early treaties with Rome; while Carthage restricted or interdicted Roman traffic with Africa and Sardinia, it granted Romans ample license for intercourse with the Carthaginian province in Sicily. Carthage viewed the relationship between itself and its Sicilian province as similar to the relationship between Rome and the cities of Latium.

## Later Sicilian Affairs
The records of Sicilian affairs extend through the close of the expedition of Timoleon, covering the period from 353 to 336 B.C. These events took place against a broader backdrop of Greek instability. Between 404-403 B.C. and 360-359 B.C., Greece became more disunited and destitute of presiding Hellenic authority than at any time since the Persian invasion. The mutual weakening of Athens, Sparta, and Thebes left the region vulnerable to the rise of the aggressor from Macedonia.
