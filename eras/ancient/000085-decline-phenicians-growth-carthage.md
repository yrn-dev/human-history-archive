# Decline of the Phoenicians and Growth of Carthage

The historical trajectory of the Phoenicians is marked by a transition of power from the eastern cities of Tyre and Sidon to their western colony, Carthage. While the original Phoenician settlements were primarily driven by commercial interests, the rise of Carthage introduced a new era characterized by the combination of trade with territorial empire and political dominion.

## The Phoenician Commercial Network
The Phoenicians, members of the Semitic family of the human race, established a vast maritime network that flourished in the earlier periods of Greek history. Their activities included caravan trade by land and extensive naval voyages, including the circumnavigation of Africa. Their settlements were founded with views that were purely commercial, focusing on pacific interchange and metallurgy. 

Key early colonies included Utica and Gadês. Gadês, founded by Archaleus (a version of the name Hercules), served as a hub for commerce extending toward Britain on one side and Africa on the other, situated in the productive region known as Tartêssus. In Iberia, Phoenician settlers were distributed between the town of Malaka and the mouth of the Anas (Guadiana). Strabo notes that in the interior of this region, there were no fewer than two hundred towns that became "Phenicized." Additionally, the Tyrians established numerous settlements along the western coast of Africa, extending beyond the strait of Gibraltar.

## The Rise of Carthage
Carthage was founded by the Tyrians, with authorities placing its establishment at either 878 B.C. or 819 B.C. Although it was not the oldest Tyrian colony—Utica is cited as being 287 years older—Carthage eventually outstripped other settlements in power and wealth. It acquired a federal preëminence over all Phoenician colonies on the African coast.

By the sixth century B.C., while Tyre and Sidon were in a period of decline, Carthage experienced significant growth. During this century, Carthage engaged in considerable traffic with Tyrrhenian towns on the southern coast of Italy and expelled Phôkæan settlers from Alalia in Corsica. Unlike the purely commercial focus of Tyre, the Carthaginians combined their trade with large schemes of conquest. They reduced independent Phoenician establishments in the gulf of Tunis to a state of dependence and planted many new townships of their own.

At the height of its dominion, the Carthaginian empire included the original Phoenician foundations of Hippo, Adrumêtum, Leptis, and Utica. It also encompassed a dependent territory covering half the space between the greater and lesser Syrtis, featuring three hundred towns inhabited by a mixed population known as Liby-Phenicians. The city of Carthage itself was said to contain 700,000 inhabitants. Its power extended to foreign dependencies in Spain, Sardinia, the Balearic isles, and Sicily.

## Conflict and Interaction with the Greeks
The growth of Grecian marine and commerce led to inevitable collisions with the Carthaginians. The earliest known collision occurred when the Phôkæans of Ionia established the colony of Massalia around 600 B.C., an achievement made possible only after a naval victory over the Carthaginians. The Carthaginians were highly jealous of commercial rivalry, particularly regarding their lucrative mine-working in Spain and traffic with the Latins and Tuscans in Italy.

In Sicily, the Greeks began establishing colonies such as Naxos (735 B.C.) and Syracuse (734 B.C.). The Phoenicians, who already held various islets and promontories for trade with the Sikans and Sikels, found their trade disrupted by these newcomers. Because the Tyrians were simultaneously hard-pressed by the Assyrians under Salmaneser and Carthaginian power had not yet peaked, the Phoenicians retreated from their petty settlements and concentrated in Malta and three towns near Lilybæum: Panormus, Motyê, and Soloeis.

Later, wars between Carthage and Grecian colonies in Sicily began shortly after 500 B.C. and continued with fluctuating success for two and a half centuries. Despite these conflicts, trade often persisted; for example, after a bloody war in 398 B.C., Carthaginian merchants quickly returned to dwell in Syracuse and other Greco-Sicilian cities.

## Relations Between Tyre and Carthage
Despite the shift in power, relations between the mother city of Tyre and its colony Carthage remained constantly amicable. Carthage continued to send Theôri as a tribute of religious recognition to the Tyrian Hêraklês. 

The bond between the two was evidenced during critical political moments. When the Persian empire was expanding, the Tyrians refused to provide their fleet to Kambysês for the conquest of Carthage, likely saving the colony from subjugation. Later, during the siege of Tyre by Alexander the Great, the wives and children of the Tyrians were sent to Carthage for shelter.
