# Ritual and Prohibited Fires

The use of fire in ritual, funerary, and sacrificial contexts has been a recurring feature of various global cultures, often becoming a point of contention for religious and legal authorities seeking to suppress perceived paganism or barbarism.

## European Bonfires and Christian Suppression
In Europe, the practice of lighting large open-air fires, known as bonfires, has ancient origins. These fires served multiple purposes: as funeral piles for corpses, fires of bones, signals of alarm (such as those used in England to warn of the Armada), or celebrations of national rejoicing. Peasants historically lit these fires on specific days of the year, accompanying them with dancing or leaping over the flames.

This custom is reflected in antiquity, such as in Ovid's *Fasti*, which describes shepherds leaping over heaps of burning straw during the feast of Pales (the Palilia). Because of these heathen associations, early Christian synods attempted to suppress the practice. In A.D. 680, the third council of Constantinople issued its 65th canon, commanding an end to the "ridiculously and foolishly" performed custom of leaping over fires kindled before houses and shops on new moons. Later, in A.D. 742, the *Synodus Francica* under Pope Zachary forbade all observations of the Pagans, specifically naming "those sacrilegious fires which they call *Nedfri* (or bonefires)."

Despite these prohibitions, the early Church eventually compromised, incorporating bonfires into Christian festivals. For example, in Cornwall, bonfires are lit on the eve of St. Peter's day and St. John the Baptist. In Cornish, midsummer is called *Goluan*, a term meaning both "festivity" and "light." Some of these traditions involved burning effigies or pretending to burn living persons, and there are beliefs that actual human sacrifices once occurred in these fires.

## Sacrificial Fires in Carthage
In Carthage, the practice of offering human sacrifices to the gods was viewed by some as a sacrilege (*Sacrilegium veriùs quàm sacrum*). This "barbarous custom" persisted until the city's ruin, though it was briefly suspended due to fear of Darius I, the king of Persia, who forbade both the eating of dog flesh and human sacrifice.

The practice resumed later. During a conflict in Sicily, the general Hamilcar (son of Hanno) continually sacrificed living men by throwing them onto a flaming pile throughout a day-long engagement. Following a defeat by Gelon, the tyrant of Syracuse, Hamilcar threw himself into the fire to avoid disgrace. St. Ambrose described this act as an attempt to extinguish the "sacrilegious fire" with his own blood. Consequently, Gelon included a specific article in the peace terms mandating that no further human sacrifices be offered to Saturn.

## Funerary Fire in China
The Penal Code of China generally forbade cremation and water burial. However, an exception was made for children whose father died at a great distance and who were unable to transport the corpse back to the native district for ordinary burial. In such cases, consuming the corpse with fire was permitted. This practice was based on the belief that fire establishes immediate communication with the spirit-world, allowing the deceased's spirit to reach its native place—a journey considered impossible if the corpse remained intact.

## Concepts of Fire in the Qur'an
The Qur'an presents "the Fire" (or "hell-fire" and "the flame") as a place of punishment and a destination for specific groups of people.

### Companions of the Fire
The text identifies several groups as "companions of the Fire" or "owners of hell-fire":
*   **Pagans and Idolaters:** It is stated that it is not fitting for the Prophet or believers to pray for the forgiveness of idolaters or Pagans, even those of near kin, once it is clear they are companions of the Fire.
*   **Disbelievers:** Those who reject Faith, deny revelations, or belie the Signs of Allah are described as owners of hell-fire or companions of the flame.
*   **Hypocrites:** Allah has promised the fire of Hell to the rejecters of Faith and to hypocritical men and women.

### Deliverance and Contrast
The Qur'an contrasts the fate of the disbelievers with that of the believers. Those who turn to Allah in repentance, serve and praise Him, fast, prostrate in prayer, enjoin good, forbid evil, and observe the limits set by Allah are promised glad tidings. Such "good people" are rewarded with eternal homes in gardens with flowing rivers. Furthermore, the text notes that by Allah's grace, believers were saved from the "brink of a pit of fire" (or "abyss of fire") after He joined their hearts in love.
