# BATTLE OF SALAMIS AND THE RETREAT OF XERXES

## Prelude and Athenian Evacuation
Following the battle of Thermopylæ, the Greeks were gripped by surprise and terror. With no ulterior plan of defense and no position capable of defending Attica, the Peloponnesians focused on fortifying the Isthmus of Corinth. This left the Athenians in a hopeless situation, as they had not yet taken measures to remove their families from Attica. Consequently, the Athenians abandoned Attica, moving their property and families to locations including Ægina, Salamis, and Trœzen, a process marked by unavoidable suffering and hurry.

During this crisis, the Athenian leaders showed energy and unanimity. Themistoklês proposed the restoration of Aristeidês from exile. Meanwhile, Xerxes occupied Attica and Athens. During the Persian march from Thermopylæ to Attica, the Persian army pillaged the temple at Abæ and ravaged Phocian townships. A Persian division was detached to attack the temple of Delphi, but this detachment ended in ruin, flight, and failure. In Athens, Xerxes was accompanied by the Peisistratids; while the acropolis initially held out, it was eventually taken and sacked. The Peisistratids later made an atoning visit to the ruined site.

## Strategic Disputes and the Greek Council
The Persian fleet entered the road of Phalêrum, where Xerxes reviewed his forces. Despite the prudent counsel of Queen Artemisia, Xerxes resolved to fight a naval battle at Salamis. 

Among the Greeks, however, there were bitter differences of opinion. A combined Greek fleet gathered at Salamis, but many, specifically those from Corinth and states behind the Isthmus, wanted to retreat to the Isthmus, which would have meant abandoning Ægina and the cities of Megara. Themistoklês strongly insisted on fighting in the narrows of Salamis. 

The dispute led to multiple synods of Grecian chiefs. In the first, Themistoklês persuaded Eurybiadês to reopen the debate, but the Peloponnesians continued to adhere to the resolution to retreat. In a second instance, Themistoklês threatened to retire with the Athenian squadron unless the battle was fought at Salamis. Eurybiadês adopted this measure, though Peloponnesian chiefs later refused obedience. By the third synod, the majority remained opposed to Themistoklês. The deadlock was broken when news arrived—brought by Aristides the Just—that the Persians had sailed around Salamis and cut off the route of retreat. Aristides, despite having been a bitter antagonist of Themistoklês in the past, used his eloquence and sanity to help Themistoklês hearten the hesitating commanders.

## The Naval Engagement
At dawn, the Greek ships pulled out to battle. The Greek fleet was primarily manned by freemen fighting for their homes. In contrast, the Persian fleet was more composite and less united, though it was approximately three times the size of the Greek force. The Persian fleet featured Phœnicians on one wing and Ionian Greeks from the Islands and Asia on the other; while some Ionians fought stoutly, others were mindful of their Greek heritage.

Xerxes watched the combat from a golden throne situated on a high cliff overlooking his host. Accounts differ on the exact location: Phanodemus places him above the Temple of Herakles where the strait is narrowest, while Akestodorus places him on the mountains called Horns near the Megarian frontier. Scribes stood near the king to record the events.

The battle raged confusedly through the early hours. Eventually, it became evident to Xerxes that his fleet was attempting to flee, and this flight turned into a disaster.

## Aftermath and Retreat
The defeat at Salamis, occurring in 480 B.C., cleared a "terrific cloud" from Greece. In Sicily, Gelo had previously sent an envoy named Kadmus to Delphi to monitor the invasion; had the Persians succeeded, Gelo intended to offer submission and presents to the invader to protect Sicily. However, the victory at Salamis (alongside the victory at Himera against the Carthaginians) brought prosperous hopes to both regions.

The retreat of the Persian seamen was marked by extreme terror. It is reported that they mistook the cliffs of Cape Zôstêr for enemy ships and redoubled their haste, though some sources characterize this as a silly exaggeration. Following the battle, Xerxes is said to have ordered the beheading of some of the Phœnicians.

In the years following the conflict, Themistoklês eventually sought refuge with King Artaxerxes, the son of Xerxes. In a letter to the king, Themistoklês claimed that while he had harmed Artaxerxes' house by defending against the invasion, he had done the king's father a greater good during his retreat. He specifically cited a warning he sent to Xerxes to retreat and claimed—falsely—that he was responsible for the bridges remaining unbroken.
