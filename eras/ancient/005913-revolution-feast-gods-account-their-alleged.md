# Revolution and Feasts

The archive contains various accounts of political revolutions, social upheavals, and the celebration of religious or cultural feasts across different eras and civilizations.

## Political Revolutions and Governance
Historical records detail several instances of revolutionary change and the subsequent establishment of government. In Thebes, Pelopidas served as Bœotarch without interruption from the time of the revolution until his death; Plutarch notes this appointment lasted thirteen years. 

In France, the "social gales of 1848" represented a period of revolutionary attempt that ultimately did not succeed. The republic established in 1848 faced difficulties with crude labour experiments. During this time, the nephew of Napoleon—a man who had posed as a revolutionary and published *Des Idées Napoléoniennes*—entered the country and was elected President. Although he took an oath to be faithful to the democratic republic and to oppose those attempting to change the government, he became Emperor of the French in December 1852.

Other shifts in governance are noted among the Israelites. Before their captivity and the dissolution of their polity, they were governed by kings—specifically Saul and David—for a period of five hundred and thirty-two years, six months, and ten days. Prior to these kings, they were governed by monarchs and judges for more than five hundred years following the deaths of Joshua their commander and Moses. Later, they utilized an aristocratical form of government mixed with an oligarchy, led by high priests, until the posterity of the Asamoneans established a kingly government.

## Religious and Cultural Feasts
The archive documents a wide array of festivals, ranging from sacred rites to civic celebrations.

### Israelite and Hebrew Observances
The feast of unleavened bread was a significant festival celebrated in the first month (called Nisan by the Israelites and Xanthicus by the Macedonians). The celebration included the Passover sacrifice on the fourteenth day of the month. Following their delivery from captivity during the times of Darius and Cyrus, the people celebrated this festival with great magnificence and whole burnt-offerings to God, thanking Him for returning them to the laws and the land of their fathers.

Earlier, during the time of Moses, the Hebrews observed a period of preparation while Moses ascended Mount Sinai to converse with God. During this time, they pitched their tents at the lowest parts of the mountain, feasted, and kept themselves pure by not accompanying their wives for three days.

### Greco-Roman and Pagan Festivals
In Athens, feasts were established in honor of Bacchus, who was brought from Egypt. These were divided into the "less feasts" (Lenea), celebrated in the open field during autumn, and the "great feasts" (Dionysia), solemnized in the spring within the city. These events featured dramatic representations, shows, and games, as well as poetry competitions where tragic or comic pieces were judged by arbitrators.

In Norway, records describe a feast held by bondes on the first winter-day's eve. This event involved drinking and "remembrance-cups" to the old gods, known as the Asas. The festivities included the slaying of horses and cattle, with blood sprinkled on altars to pray for good seasons. When King Olaf accused them of these heathen forms, a head-man named Olver of Eggja defended the bondes, claiming the gatherings were merely usual social meetings and friendly drinking parties.

### Christian Observances
The Feast of the Assumption of the blessed Virgin Mary is celebrated by the Christian Church on August 15th. This festival commemorates the ascent of the mother of Christ into heaven. According to accounts by Gregory of Tours, Jesus appeared to the Apostles and committed Mary's soul to the Archangel Michael, later carrying her body in a cloud to heaven. Other accounts, such as those by St John of Damascus, state that the body remained in the grave for three days without corruption and that patriarchs, including Adam and Eve, appeared at the death-bed.

## Literary Censorship and Allegations
In the 18th century, the works of Pope were subject to censorship. While a pirated edition was published by Edmund Curll in 1721, an authorized edition appeared in 1723. However, authorities removed two specific works: "Account of the Revolution" and "The Feast of the Gods." These sections were excised due to their alleged Jacobite tendencies. These specific pieces were later printed at the Hague in 1727, though Pope claimed he had no knowledge of their contents.
