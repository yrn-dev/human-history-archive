# Kapilavastu and the Birth of the Heir

## Geography and the Sakiya Clan
Kapilavastu (also referred to as Kapilavatthu) served as the capital of the Sakiya clan. The Sakiya territory spanned approximately two to three thousand square miles, extending into the lower slopes of the mountains in what is now Nepal, as well as areas on the British side of the frontier. The city is located at approximately 27° 37' N. by 83° 11' E., situated some days' journey north of Benares.

The wealth of the Sakiya people was primarily derived from well-watered rice-fields, with the Himalayas visible from their lands. Their water supply was drawn from the river Rohini (known today as the Kohana). While the use of this river occasionally caused disputes during droughts between the Sakiyas and the neighboring Koliyans, the two clans were at peace during the era of the Buddha's birth. This peace was further cemented by marriage; Suddhoda had two daughters of a Koli chieftain, whose capital was located 11 miles east of Kapilavastu, as his principal wives.

## The Birth of Gotama
For a long period, Suddhoda's wives remained childless. Rejoicing occurred when the elder sister, Maha Maya, promised her husband a son at approximately forty-five years of age. Following the custom of the time, she began a journey from Kapilavastu to her parents' home in Devadaha, the capital of the Koliyas, to be confined.

During this journey, the party halted under the shade of lofty satin-trees in a pleasant riverside garden known as Lumbini. It was in this grove, located not far from Devadaha, that the future Buddha, Gotama, was unexpectedly born. This site has been rediscovered and is marked by an inscribed pillar erected by Asoka. Ancient Pali ballads, such as the *Song of Nalaka* in the *Sutta Nipata*, describe the "Wisdom-child" as a precious, unmatched jewel born in the Sakiya land for the joy and weal of the world of men.

## The Birth of the Heir to Kapilavastu
In later accounts, Gotama is described as having experienced a series of visions. After the final vision, he spent an afternoon in his riverside pleasure-grounds and bathed before preparing to return home in his chariot. During this time, a messenger informed him that his wife, Yasodhara, had given birth to a son, who was Gotama's only child.

While Gotama viewed the child as a "new and strong tie" that he would eventually have to break, the people of Kapilavastu were greatly delighted. The child was the only grandson of the raja and the young heir to the lineage. Gotama's return to the city was marked by an ovation, featuring shouts of joy and triumph and the accompaniment of musicians.

## Departure from the Palace
During the celebrations, Gotama's attention was drawn to a stanza sung by his cousin, a young girl. She sang of the happiness of the father, mother, and wife of such a son and husband. In this context, the word "happy" carried a double meaning, referring also to being saved or delivered from the chains of rebirth. In response to this reminder of his highest hopes, Gotama gave the girl his collar of pearls. Although the girl interpreted this as the start of a courtship and imagined becoming his principal wife, Gotama took no further notice of her.

That evening, festive Natch dances were performed by dancing-girls, but Gotama remained indifferent and fell into an uneasy sleep. At midnight, he awoke with a feeling of overpowering loathing. Described in the Sinhalese chronicle as being roused "like a man who is told that his house is on fire," he decided to leave. He instructed his charioteer, Channa, to saddle his horse. Before departing, Gotama gently opened the door to the room where Yasodhara slept with her hand on the head of their child, hoping to take the babe in his arms.
