# ARGONAUTIC EXPEDITION

The Argonautic expedition was a legendary voyage of the ship *Argô* and its crew, known as the Argonauts. Composed of the bravest and noblest youths of Greece, the expedition is regarded as one of the capital exploits of a heroic and superhuman race. The voyage is documented in various ancient sources, including the *Odyssey*, the works of Hesiod and Eumêlus, and poems by Epimenidês and Antimachus.

## Origins and the Quest for the Golden Fleece
The expedition was prompted by the circumstances of Jasôn, the son of Æsôn, grandson of Krêtheus, and great-grandson of Æolus. Pelias, the ruler of Iôlkos, had been warned by an oracle to beware of a man appearing before him with only one sandal. During a festival for Poseidôn, Jasôn arrived in this exact condition, having lost a sandal while wading through the swollen current of the river Anauros.

To avert the danger predicted by the oracle, Pelias tasked Jasôn with a desperate mission: to retrieve the Golden Fleece and bring it back to Iôlkos. The fleece belonged to a ram that had carried Phryxos from Achaia to Kolchis, where Phryxos had dedicated it as an offering to the god Arês.

## The Crew and Notable Companions
The Argonauts included several celebrated heroes of Greek legend. Among them were:
* **Hêraklês and Hylas**: Hêraklês was a member of the crew, and his actions later included the slaying of Zêtês and Kalaïs at Tênos.
* **Thêseus**: The Attic hero, known for his conquest of the Minotaur, was also present on the expedition.
* **The Sons of Boreas**: Zêtês and Kalaïs, the winged sons of Boreas and Oreithyia, participated in the voyage and engaged in the pursuit of the Harpies.
* **The Dioscuri**: Kastôr, a master horse-master, and Pollux, a premier pugilist, were both enrolled. During the voyage on the coast of Asiatic Thrace, Pollux encountered Amykus, the king of the Bebrykes. Amykus was a gigantic pugilist who challenged Pollux; according to some narratives, Pollux vanquished and killed him, while other versions suggest Amykus was merely conquered and forced to renounce his brutal conduct.

## The Voyage and Adventures
The journey of the *Argô* was marked by numerous perils and encounters. The crew visited Lêmnos and faced adventures at Kyzikus in Bithynia. In the vicinity of Cyzicus, the narrative mentions a fountain called Artacia, which corresponds to a real fountain known in historical times.

The Argonauts encountered Phineus, who had been blinded by his own father-in-law, Phineus's second wife Idæa had falsely accused Phineus's sons, Plexippus and Pandiôn, of attempting her chastity, leading Phineus to put out their eyes. The Argonauts punished Phineus for this cruelty during their voyage.

The crew also faced the dangers of the Symplêgades before arriving at Kolchis. Upon arrival, Æêtês imposed specific conditions as the price for the Golden Fleece. Despite these terms, the Argonauts eventually fled Kolchis with the fleece, aided by Mêdea. When Æêtês pursued them, Mêdea saved the Argonauts. The return journey to Iôlkos is described as circuitous and perilous.

## Historical and Geographical Analysis
The Argonautic legend has been subject to various interpretations as geographical knowledge increased. Over time, the "fabulous geography" of the tales was modified, and epical localities were transposed. The voyage eventually became specifically attached to Kolchis, with some versions linking Æêtês to Circê.

The scholar Strabo attempted to find a basis of actual fact within the legend. He proposed that the Golden Fleece was a symbol for the immense wealth of Kolchis, specifically the gold-dust washed down by its rivers. In Strabo's view, Jasôn's voyage was actually a military expedition involving a considerable army that plundered the country and made extensive conquests in the interior. Strabo characterized Jasôn, along with Hêraklês and Dionysus, as having traversed wider spaces of ground than any moderns could equal.

## Literary Sources
The expedition has been a recurring theme in ancient literature. The Krêtan prophet Epimenidês composed a poem of 6,500 lines regarding the building of the *Argô* and Jasôn's sailing to Kolchis. Antimachus touched upon the expedition in his poem *Lydê*, and the logographers Hekatæus and Pherekydês are believed to have related the story at considerable length.
