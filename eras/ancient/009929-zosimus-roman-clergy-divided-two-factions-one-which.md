# Zosimus and Roman Ecclesiastical Factions

## The Historian Zosimus
Zosimus is a historical source who preserved the memory of the transition of power in Britain following the dissolution of the artificial fabric of civil and military government. He accurately observed that the letters of the emperor Honorius were addressed to the cities of Britain. Additionally, Zosimus and some of his followers are noted for mistaking the Danube for the Tanais, which led them to place a specific field of battle in the plains of Scythia.

## Factions in the Roman Clergy
The Roman clergy were frequently characterized by deep divisions and the emergence of rival factions, particularly during the election of popes.

### The Election of Boniface I
Following the death of Pope Zosimus, the Roman clergy split into two factions. One faction elected a deacon named Eulalius, while the other elected the priest Boniface. To maintain public order, the imperial government ordered both competitors to leave the town and reserved the final decision for a council. However, Eulalius broke his ban. Consequently, the emperor Honorius decided to recognize Boniface, and the planned council was countermanded. Despite this, the faction supporting Eulalius continued to foment disorders for a significant period, necessitating further intervention by secular authorities.

### The Conflict of Damasus I and Ursinus
Earlier, during the papacy of Damasus I (366–384), similar factionalism occurred. Upon the death of Pope Liberius in 366, a considerable party nominated Damasus as successor. However, "irreconcilables" from the party of Liberius, who viewed Damasus's previous political shifts as "trimming," set up a rival deacon named Ursinus. This resulted in a serious conflict involving rioting and hand-to-hand fighting. During these encounters, the Liberian Basilica (S. Maria Maggiore) was partially destroyed, leaving 137 dead bodies inside. While the government of emperor Valentinian was generally averse to ecclesiastical affairs, the prefect of Rome supported Damasus. This led to the expulsion of Ursinus and his leading supporters from Rome, central Italy, and in some cases, their internment in Gaul. The opposition continued through riots and calumnious lawsuits, including one brought by Isaac, a relapsed and converted Jew.

## Later Papal Successions and Reforms
The instability of the papacy continued through various eras due to the influence of secular powers and internal clerical disputes.

### Boniface II and Boniface III
Boniface II (pope from 530 to 532) was a Goth whose election was influenced by the Gothic king and the nomination of his predecessor, Felix IV. He faced opposition from the Roman electors, who supported a priest of Alexandria named Dioscorus; however, Dioscorus died one month after his election. Boniface II attempted to establish a custom of nominating his own successor, but the senate of Rome and the clergy forced him to cancel this arrangement. Later, Boniface III (pope from February 15 to November 12, 606) obtained recognition of the "headship of the church at Rome" from Phocas, which compelled the patriarch of Constantinople to momentarily abandon his claim to the title of oecumenical patriarch.

### Evolution of the Election Process
For centuries, the chair of St. Peter was disputed through popular elections marked by venality, violence, and blood. These schisms were often transient as long as the civil magistrate determined the final appeal. However, once the maxim was established that the vicar of Christ was not amenable to any earthly tribunal, vacancies in the holy see often led to wider controversy and war. Factions consisting of the people, nobles, and inferior clergy would often hold double elections in different churches. This instability persisted until Alexander the Third abolished the "tumultuary votes" of the people and clergy, restricting the right of election solely to the college of cardinals. This change assimilated the orders of deacons, priests, and bishops, granting the parochial clergy of Rome the first rank in the hierarchy.
