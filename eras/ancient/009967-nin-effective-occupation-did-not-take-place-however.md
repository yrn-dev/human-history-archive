# NIN

The term "nin" appears across various historical and linguistic contexts within the archive, ranging from ancient Mesopotamian deities and rulers to Japanese nomenclature and the description of uninhabited territories.

## Ancient Mesopotamia and Babylonia
In the context of early Mesopotamian history, "Nin" is associated with both royalty and divinity. In Lagash (Tello), a dynasty was established by Ur-Nin[=a]. This ruler constructed a building attached to the temple of Nin[=a]. Later, E-anna-du, the grandson of Ur-Nin[=a], rebuilt the town of Nin[=a], a location that likely provided the name for the later city of Nineveh. E-anna-du's military campaigns included the subjugation of Gis-ukh, where a levy of grain was required to be paid into the treasury of the god Ingurisa and the goddess Nin[=a].

In Babylonian religion, the element "Nin" is used in the names of goddesses to denote power or status. Nin-lil, whose name means "lady of power," is the consort of Bel (also known as En-lil) and is associated with Nippur. She holds the titles "mother of the gods" and "ruler of heaven and earth." She is also referred to as Nin-Khar-sag, meaning "Lady of the mountain," a name related to Im-Khar-sag ("storm mountain"), which was the name of the sacred edifice or staged tower dedicated to Bel at Nippur.

## Japanese History and Nomenclature
In Japanese history, the term "nin" appears in both the names of emperors and the description of geography.

### Emperor Nintoku
The emperor Nintoku ascended the throne with the support of the Takenouchi magnates. According to the Chronicles, the ideograph "nin" in his posthumous name signifies "benevolence" (while "toku" signifies "virtue"). Upon taking the throne, Nintoku established his capital at Naniwa (Osaka). After observing from a tower that no smoke rose from the roofs of houses—which he interpreted as a sign of poverty—he remitted all taxes and forced labor for three years. Consequently, his palace of Takatsu fell into a state of practical ruin, with tradition stating that inmates had to move from room to room to avoid leaking rain.

### The Bonin Islands
The term "nin" is also found in the name *Bunin-jima*, which foreigners corrupted into "Bonin." This name was derived from the islands being without (*bu*) inhabitants (*nin*). According to Japanese annals, these islands were discovered toward the end of the 16th century and added to the fief of the Daimyo Ogasawa Sadayori, leading to the name Ogasawara-jima. 

Despite these claims, effective occupation did not occur for a significant period. Communications ceased in 1635 due to the Japanese government's veto on constructing sea-going vessels. While fitful communication was restored in 1728 by the Ogasawara family representative, it was interrupted again until 1861. During the interim, the islands were visited by Captain Beechey and later settled in 1830 by a small party of British, American, and Danish citizens, along with Hawaiian natives. These settlers hoisted the British flag on Peel Island (Chichi-jima). By 1853, Commodore Perry found thirty-one inhabitants on Peel Island; by 1875, Russell Robertson found the colony had grown to sixty-nine people. Robertson noted the settlers lived comfortably on cultivated land but lacked laws, religion, education, and a system of government. Although the settlers regarded themselves as a British colony and spoke English, the British government renounced all claims to the islands in 1861, recognizing Japan's right of possession.
