# History of South Central Africa

The history of the territory in South Central Africa is described as recent and slight. Early European presence was characterized by vague Portuguese wanderings during the 16th and 17th centuries. The first educated European explorer to penetrate the region was the Portuguese official Dr. F.J.M. de Lacerda e Almeida, who traveled from Tete on the Zambezi to the vicinity of Lake Mweru.

## The Era of David Livingstone
The real history of the region began with the arrival of David Livingstone. In 1859, Livingstone penetrated up the Shiré river and discovered Lake Nyasa. He conducted subsequent journeys to Lake Mweru, Lake Bangweulu, and the southern end of Tanganyika. These explorations opened this part of South Central Africa and established British interests in the area. Livingstone died at Lake Bangweulu in 1873.

## Missionary and Colonial Influence
Following the death of Livingstone, various missionary societies entered the territory to begin the evangelization of the country. These missionaries, along with a small number of Scottish settlers, provided steady opposition to Portuguese attempts to extend their control into the region from the Zambezi and the adjoining provinces of Moçambique.

From these missionary societies, a trading entity known as the African Lakes Trading Corporation was formed. This corporation entered into conflict with Arabs who had established themselves at the northern end of Lake Nyasa.

## Conflict and the British Protectorate
A struggle for the possession of the country between Britons and Arabs began around 1885 and continued until 1896. During this unofficial war, the African Lakes Corporation enlisted volunteers, including Mr. (later Sir) Alfred Sharpe and Captain (later Sir F.D.) Lugard, both of whom were wounded in operations that did not achieve complete success.

In 1889, Mr. (later Sir) H.H. Johnston was dispatched to the region with two primary objectives:
1. To attempt to arrange a settlement for the dispute between the African Lakes Corporation and the Arabs.
2. To ensure that friendly native chiefs were protected from Portuguese aggression beyond a specific point.

The treaties and efforts resulting from Johnston's mission led to the creation of a British sphere of influence and protectorate north of the Zambezi.
