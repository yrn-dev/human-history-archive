# Incantation

Incantation refers to the use of magic formulae, charms, and ritualized speech across various cultures and historical periods. These practices have been employed for a wide range of purposes, including the healing of illness, the expulsion of demons, the pursuit of favorable natural outcomes, and the execution of religious sacrifices.

## Mesopotamian Traditions
In the Babylonian-Assyrian religious system, incantation texts were gathered into series that reflected a movement toward the centralization of worship. Originally, incantations were addressed to deities such as Nusku, the god of fire, and Ea of Eridu, the god of the watery element. Over time, these were transferred to Marduk, the patron deity of Babylon. This transition was achieved by establishing Ea as the father of Marduk and Nusku as a messenger between the two.

To increase the effectiveness of these magic formulae, practitioners introduced a large number of old local deities to add their power to the chief gods. This process incorporated the astral system, utilizing the names of stars and assigning attributes to gods that suggested their seats were in the heavens. These incantations often transitioned into prayers and hymns; the term *shiptu* was used to describe this connection. 

Furthermore, older incantations associated with Ea were re-edited to grant Marduk supreme power over sorcerers, witches, and demons. While the theoretical substratum of the religion changed, the ritual elements accompanying incantation formulae—such as ritualistic purification, the sprinkling of water, libations, and animal sacrifice—remained connected to older, popular religious elements.

## Ancient Near East and Persian Practices
In the history of the Israelites, King Solomon is credited with possessing a science useful and sanative to men, which included the skill to expel demons. Solomon composed incantations used to alleviate distempers and established methods of exorcism to drive away demons permanently. These methods remained in use in later periods; for example, a man named Eleazar performed cures in the presence of Vespasian by reciting Solomon's incantations and using a ring featuring a "Foot" mentioned by Solomon to draw demons out through the nostrils of the afflicted.

Among the Persians, incantations were an essential component of animal sacrifice. According to Herodotus, Persians did not use altars, fire, music, or libations. Instead, a sacrificer led an animal to an unpolluted place, wore a tiara wreathed with a myrtle branch, and prayed for the well-being of the king and all Persians. A Magian was required to be present to chant a theogony over the boiled flesh of the victim, which served as the incantation for the rite.

## Primitive and Early Human Use
For primitive humans, incantations and charms were used to address issues of great importance where cause and effect were not easily linked. This included attempts to ensure that fish were plentiful or that game was abundant. Incantations were also a response to the "feverish exercise" of the mind when facing illness and death without a manifest cause.

In the Neolithic period, these beliefs often manifested as a fear of tribal gods, leading to practices of sacrificial propitiation. This included human sacrifices at seedtime or the sacrifice of slaves and wives during the burial of chieftains, driven by the belief that the gods were "athirst."

## Other Cultural Applications
In Japan, incantations were sometimes associated with political intrigue and accusations of malice. In 1155, following the death of Emperor Konoe, Bifuku-mon-in persuaded Emperor Toba that the premature death of her son had been caused by the incantations of the cloistered sovereign, Sutoku. This accusation contributed to the exclusion of Sutoku's offspring from the throne and subsequent political struggle.

Additionally, various forms of divination have been linked to the use of charms. Some traditions involved sticking up sticks while murmuring incantations; the direction in which the sticks fell—attributed to the operation of devils—was then noted to provide counsel.
