# The History of Bookselling and Bookshops

The trade in books is an ancient practice that evolved from the oral recitation of works to the establishment of dedicated commercial shops and professional guilds. Early poets and orators often recited their works in public to encourage listeners to acquire written copies. These copies were frequently taken down *viva voce*, and transcripts were then sold to wealthy individuals. In the biblical book of Jeremiah, the prophet is depicted dictating to a scribe named Baruch; such scribes served as the earliest booksellers, providing copies of texts as they were demanded.

## Ancient and Classical Book Trade
In ancient Greece, the demand for books was stimulated by the founding of the Alexandrian library around 300 B.C. This period saw the activity of Athenian booksellers, who were known as *biblion kapaeloi*. Notable figures of the era engaged with the trade in various ways; for example, Aristotle possessed a somewhat extensive library, and Plato is recorded to have paid one hundred minae for three small treatises written by Philolaus the Pythagorean.

In Rome, particularly toward the end of the republic, libraries became a fashionable addition to household furniture. This trend supported a flourishing trade managed by booksellers referred to as *bibliopolae* or *librarii*. These professionals operated shops known as *taberna librarii*, which were located chiefly in the Vicus Sandalarius and the Argiletum. To advertise their stock, booksellers placed lists of available books on the side posts or the doors of their shops. The cost of these works varied; for instance, Martial notes that a copy of his First Book of Epigrams could be purchased for five denarii. During the era of Augustus the great, the Sosii were prominent booksellers. Legal developments also emerged during this period; according to Justinian, a law was enacted that granted scribes property rights over the materials they used, which may represent the earliest origin of modern copyright law.

## Medieval Bookselling and the University System
Following a period known as the dark ages—during which distinct accounts of book dealers are rare, though Peter of Blois mentions purchasing a book from a public dealer (*a quodam publico mangone librorum*)—the trade was re-established in the 12th century. This resurgence occurred primarily in Bologna and Paris, driven by the needs of universities and lawyers.

These medieval dealers were called *stationarii*, a term possibly derived from the open stalls where they conducted business, although *statio* was a general low Latin term for a shop. According to the statutes of the universities of Bologna and Paris, *stationarii* sold books on commission. They are sometimes distinguished from *librarii*, a term that originally referred to copyists before it was expanded to include those who traded in books. 

Beyond the sale of books, these dealers provided parchment and other writing materials—occupations that retained the name "stationery." They also performed related tasks such as decorating and binding books and likely employed professional transcribers, as copyists were a recognized profession in large cities and university centers.

## Related Commercial and Literary Contexts
The term *taberna* (shop) also appears in other Roman cultural contexts. In Roman literature between 80 and 170 B.C., a species of comedy dealing with the lives of the lower classes was termed *tabernariae*. This name was derived from *taberna* and was considered appropriate by some due to the extant titles of the plays, while others suggest it referred to the use of provincial scenes. The playwright Titinius wrote plays that were all *tabernariae*.

Additionally, the intersection of commerce and intellectual recording is seen in the accounts of Sokratês. While many *Socratici viri* composed dialogues in the style of Plato, a leather-cutter named Simon took memoranda of conversations that took place in his own shop, which he later published as dialogues.
