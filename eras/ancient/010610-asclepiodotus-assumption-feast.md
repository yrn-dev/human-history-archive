# ASCLEPIODOTUS AND THE FEAST OF THE ASSUMPTION

## Asclepiodotus
Asclepiodotus was a Greek military writer who flourished during the 1st century B.C. The available historical record regarding his life is minimal, noting only that he was a pupil of the Stoic Poseidonius, who died in 51 B.C. 

Asclepiodotus is credited as the supposed author of a treatise concerning Graeco-Macedonian tactics titled *Taktika Kephalaia*. However, it is probable that this work is not his own original composition, but rather a skeleton outline of lectures delivered by his master, Poseidonius, who is known to have written on the subject of tactics.

## The Feast of the Assumption
The Feast of the Assumption of the blessed Virgin Mary—also referred to in Latin as *festum assumptionis, dormitionis, depositionis, pausationis B. V. M.* and in Greek as *koimaesis* or *analaephis taes theotokou*—is a festival of the Christian Church. It is celebrated annually on the 15th of August to commemorate the miraculous ascent of the mother of Christ into heaven.

### Origins and Authenticity
The beliefs underlying the festival originate from apocryphal sources. These include the *de transitu Mariae*, written around A.D. 400 and assigned to Melito, bishop of Sardis, as well as the *eis taen koimaesin taes uperagias despoinaes*, which is ascribed to the Apostle John. 

The authenticity of these texts has been viewed differently over time. Pope Gelasius I (492-496) included them in a list of condemned apocryphal books within the *Decretum de libris recipiendis et non recipiendis*. Conversely, they were accepted as authentic by Gregory of Tours (who died in 593 or 594) and by the pseudo-Dionysius in *de nominbus divinis c. 3*, whose writings likely date from the 5th century.

### Accounts of the Miracle
In his work *De gloria martyrum*, Gregory of Tours provides an account of the event: while the Apostles watched over the dying Mary, Jesus appeared with angels and entrusted Mary's soul to the Archangel Michael. The following day, as her body was being carried to the grave, Christ appeared again and carried the body in a cloud to heaven, where the soul and body were reunited.

This narrative is further expanded upon by St John of Damascus in the homilies *In dormitionem Mariae*, which the Roman Church continues to read as the lesson during the octave of the feast. This expanded account includes the following details:
* The patriarchs, along with Adam and Eve, appear at the death-bed to praise Mary, as they were rescued from the curse of God through her.
* A Jew who touches the body loses both of his hands, though the Apostles subsequently restore them.
* The body remains in the grave for three days without corruption before being taken up into heaven.
