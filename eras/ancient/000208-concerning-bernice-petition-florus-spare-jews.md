# Bernice's Petition To Florus

## The Violence of Florus
The procurator Florus instigated a period of severe brutality against the inhabitants of Jerusalem. Provoked by local events, Florus commanded his soldiers to plunder the Upper Market-place and kill those they encountered. The soldiers expanded this operation, forcing their way into houses to slay the inhabitants. During this period, citizens fled through narrow lanes to escape the soldiers, and many quiet people were captured and brought before Florus, where they were first chastised with stripes and then crucified.

The total number of people destroyed that day, including wives, children, and infants, was approximately three thousand and six hundred. This event was marked by a specific Roman barbarity: Florus ordered men of the equestrian order to be whipped and nailed to the cross before his tribunal. Although these men were by birth Jews, they possessed Roman dignity.

## Bernice's Intercession
During this time, Bernice had come to Jerusalem to perform a vow she had made to God. Such vows were customary for those who had suffered from distresses or distempers; they required the individual to abstain from wine and shave their head for thirty days before offering sacrifices.

Deeply affected by the wicked practices of the soldiers, Bernice frequently sent her guards and the masters of her horse to Florus to beg him to cease the slaughters. She eventually appeared before Florus's tribunal barefoot to beseech him to spare the Jews. Despite the nobility of her status and the magnitude of the slaughter, Florus refused to comply with her requests, as he was focused solely on the financial advantage he could gain through plundering.

The violence of the soldiers escalated to the point that they tormented and destroyed people in Bernice's presence. Bernice herself narrowly escaped being killed by fleeing to the palace, where she remained all night with her guards for protection against the soldiers.

## Diplomatic Efforts and Accusations
Following these events, Florus attempted to provoke a war by sending false accusations to Cestius, claiming that the Jews were revolting from the Roman government and had authored the disturbances in which they were actually the sufferers. In response, Bernice and the governors of Jerusalem wrote to Cestius to report the illegal practices Florus had committed against the city.

Upon receiving these accounts, Cestius consulted his captains. While some suggested he lead his army to punish a revolt or settle Roman affairs, Cestius instead sent a tribune named Neopolitanus to investigate the intentions of the Jews. Neopolitanus eventually met King Agrippa at Jamnia while the king was returning from Alexandria.

## Efforts to Prevent War
The Jewish multitude sought permission from the high priests and King Agrippa to send ambassadors to Nero. They feared that silence would create a suspicion that they had caused the slaughters or were disposed to revolt, making them appear as the initiators of the war. 

Agrippa, viewing the appointment of accusers against Florus as dangerous, gathered the multitude in a large gallery. He placed Bernice in the house of the Asamoneans—located over the gallery at the passage to the upper city where the bridge joined the temple—so that she could be seen by the people. Agrippa and Bernice wept together, and their tears helped repress much of the people's violence.

The people maintained that they did not wish to fight the Romans, but only Florus. Agrippa warned them that their current actions—specifically the failure to pay the tribute due to Caesar and the cutting off of the cloisters that joined the temple to the tower Antonia—were equivalent to making war against the Romans. He advised them to pay the tribute and rebuild the cloisters to prevent a revolt, noting that the tribute was not paid to Florus and the citadel did not belong to him.

The people followed this advice, joining the king and Bernice in the temple to rebuild the cloisters. Meanwhile, senators and rulers collected the deficient sum of forty talents in tribute. Although Agrippa attempted to persuade the multitude to obey Florus until Caesar sent a successor, the people became provoked, cast reproaches upon the king, and eventually excluded him from the city, with some throwing stones at him. Consequently, Agrippa sent the rulers and men of power to Florus in Cesarea to appoint a tribute collector while he retired to his own kingdom.
