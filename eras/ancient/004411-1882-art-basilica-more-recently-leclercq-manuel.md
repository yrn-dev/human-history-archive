# Basilica

The basilica is an architectural form that evolved from early civil structures into the primary design for Christian worship. Its development reflects a transition from simple interior spaces to complex structures capable of accommodating large numbers of the faithful.

## Origins and Early Roman Development
The prototypes for Roman basilicas are believed to have originated in Athens, though it is unclear if the importation was direct or mediated through other Greek cities. The earliest known example is the basilica at Pompeii, which may date back to the 2nd century B.C. In Rome, basilicas appeared nearly at the beginning of that same century. The first Roman example, the Basilica Porcia, was erected in 184 B.C. by the censor M. Porcius Cato, who had recently visited Athens and been impressed by the city's beauty. Other notable Roman examples include the Basilica Julia and the Basilica Ulpia.

## Evolution of Christian Basilicas
Buildings expressly erected for Christian worship first appeared in the 2nd or 3rd century A.D. These early structures likely consisted of an oblong interior that terminated in an apse. Following the time of Constantine, the population of the faithful increased enormously, leading to the addition of side aisles. This modification gave the churches an appearance similar to that of the civil basilica. Evidence for this architectural evolution was discovered at S. Saba on the Aventine in Rome, where a small, early church without aisles was found beneath the floor of the existing basilica.

## Notable Examples in Italy
Outside of Rome, some of the most remarkable basilican churches are located in Ravenna. While smaller than the Roman basilicas, these structures are noted for superior design and proportions.

### The Cathedral of Ravenna
Erected in A.D. 400 by Archbishop Ursus, the cathedral was a noble basilica featuring double aisles. However, this building was destroyed to make way for a subsequent structure described as tasteless.

### The Basilicas of S. Apollinare
Two churches dedicated to S. Apollinare in Ravenna exhibit such similar designs that they are believed to have been created by the same architect:

*   **S. Apollinare Nuovo:** Originally an Arian church erected by Theodoric between 493 and 525, it measures 315 feet in length and 115 feet in breadth. The nave is 51 feet wide and is separated from single aisles by colonnades of twenty-two pillars. These pillars support arches with intervening small prismatic blocks bearing sculptured crosses. The church is renowned for its mosaic ornamentation, specifically a continuous band of saintly figures—males on one side and females on the other—processing toward the Virgin Mother and Our Lord.
*   **S. Apollinare in Classe:** Built later, between A.D. 538 and 549, this church is smaller, measuring 216 feet by 104 feet.

## Scholarly Resources and Documentation
Comprehensive lists of early Christian basilicas outside of Rome are documented in Kraus's *Realencyklopaedie der christlichen Alterthuemer* (1882, art. "Basilica") and H. Leclercq's *Manuel d'archéologie chrétienne* (1907). Leclercq's work, specifically the appendix "Essai de Classement des Principaux Monuments," provides a detailed classification and a complete bibliography for the study of these structures. Other authorities on the subject include Vitruvius, Huelsen, and various specialists in Byzantine and early Christian architecture.
