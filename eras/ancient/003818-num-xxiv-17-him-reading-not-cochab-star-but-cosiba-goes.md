# Num. xxiv. 17 and the Figure of Bar Cochba

The biblical passage Num. xxiv. 17 has been the subject of varying interpretations, particularly regarding the terminology used to describe a coming leader and the subsequent application of this text to historical figures.

## The Oracles of Num. xxiv
The section of the Bible encompassing Num. xxiv. 14-19 contains an announcement regarding the coming of a king, who may possibly be David, destined to conquer Moab and Edom. This is part of a larger sequence of oracles. Scholars identify the history of Balaam, found in most of Num. xxii.-xxiv., as being written by a source referred to as "E." This writer, believed to have lived in the northern kingdom, preferred the name *Elohim* ("God") over "Jehovah" and recorded traditions current in northern Israel.

Other oracles in this sequence include the ruin of Amalek (xxiv. 20), the destruction of the Kenite state by Assyria (xxiv. 21 f.), and the arrival of ships from the West to attack "Eber" and Assur (xxiv. 23 f.), which some suggest refers to Alexander the Great's conquest of Persia.

## Bar Cochba and the Messianic Interpretation
In Christian sources, the name Bar Cochba (also written as Barcochebas or Bar-Cochab), meaning "son of a star," is attributed to Simeon, a leader of the revolt against Rome during the reign of Hadrian (A.D. 132-135). While Roman historians do not use this name, it holds significant religious weight in other traditions.

The Rabbi 'Aqiba recognized Simeon as the Messiah and applied the prophecy of Num. xxiv. 17 to him. In doing so, 'Aqiba did not read the word *Cochab* ("a star"), but instead read it as *Cosiba* ("goes forth from Jacob"). Through this reading, Bar-cochab became a Messianic title for the "man of Cozeba."

## Rabbinic Perspectives and the Revolt
Following the failure of the revolt, Rabbinic sources shifted the characterization of the leader. In these texts, he is called Bar (Ben) Coziba, which translates to "son of deceit." This name, derived from the Hebrew root *KZB* ("to be false"), is thought to reflect a later verdict of condemnation.

During the initial stages of the insurgency, the Romans paid little attention to the movement. The insurgents were able to persecute Christians who refused to join the revolt and struck coins in the name of Eleazar the priest and Simeon, described as the "prince of Israel." However, Roman troops eventually collected and reduced the various fortresses occupied by the Israelites. The revolt concluded with the fall of Beth-thar (Bethar). Historical accounts also mention extraordinary stories regarding the prowess of Barcochebas and the rigorous training ordeals he imposed upon his soldiers.
