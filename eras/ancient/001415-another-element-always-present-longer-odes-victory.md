# The Gnomic Element in Greek Odes of Victory

In the study of ancient Greek lyric poetry, specifically the *epinikion* (ode of victory), the "gnomic" element is a recurring feature found in longer compositions. This element consists of the inclusion of maxims or moral sentiments, which serve to provide didactic or meditative reflections within the celebratory framework of the poem.

## The Nature of the Epinikion
The ode of victory, known as the *epinikion (melos* or *epinikos (humnos*, is a poetic form derived from the *humnos*, which was originally a song used to praise a deity. The evolution of the form began around 610 B.C. with Stesichorus, who composed hymns honoring heroes rather than gods. By the second half of the 6th century B.C., as the four great Greek festivals—the Olympian, Pythian, Nemean, and Isthmian—reached the peak of their popularity, the custom evolved into writing hymns to celebrate the victories of living men.

The general structure of these odes often uses a particular victory as an occasion to introduce heroic legends related to the victor's city or family. These narratives serve to lift the poem into an ideal region, granting it a significance that transcends the local or temporary nature of the event.

## Contrasting Gnomic Styles: Pindar and Bacchylides
While the gnomic element is a constant in longer victory odes, it is executed differently by the two primary poets of the genre, Pindar and Bacchylides.

Pindar, a Boeotian of Dorian stock, is characterized as the most bold, fervid, and sublime of the lyric poets. His approach to the gnomic element is didactic; he packs his *gnomai* (maxims or moral sentiments) into epigrams that are sometimes obscure and terse. In doing so, Pindar adopts a commanding voice, reminiscent of Delphic wisdom.

In contrast, the moralizing found in the work of Bacchylides is described as an utterance of quiet meditation. His style is less about commanding authority and more reflective, occasionally recalling the strain of Ionian gnomic elegy. This differs from Pindar's method of selecting vivid, forceful scenes from legends; Bacchylides instead employs a gentle flow of simple epic narrative, relying on the interest of the story as a whole.

## Other Poets and Technical Development
The culmination of lyric poetry is marked by Pindar and his contemporary, Simonides. Simonides, an Ionian from the island of Ceos, was the earliest recorded writer of *epinikia*. While Pindar's references to the specific events of a race are often slight and general, fragments of Simonides' work suggest that he dwelt more on the actual incidents of the victory. This tendency to focus on the vivid details of the win is also shared by Bacchylides. For example, while Pindar's first *Olympian* and Bacchylides' fifth ode both celebrate the victory of the horse Pherenicus, Bacchylides provides a much fuller and more vivid description of the winner's run.

## Context of the Victories
The victories celebrated in these odes often involved chariot races. These contests featured chariots drawn by two or four horses (*bigæ* and *quadrigæ*), or sometimes mules (*apēne*). Pindar, for instance, celebrates a triple victory for Psaumis, who won with a four-horse chariot, a mule-drawn chariot, and a single horse. 

The races were highly technical, involving twelve laps around a Stadium. The charioteers started from the *Carceres* and had to navigate a boundary turn; the art of the race depended on taking the best ground at this turn to avoid either dashing the chariot to pieces by driving too close or being cut off by an antagonist by driving too wide.
