# Religion and Scholarship of Ancient Babylonia

The study of ancient Babylonian religion and its historical chronology has been documented through various scholarly works and archaeological memoirs. Research in this field involves the analysis of cuneiform texts, the study of national deities, and the establishment of dynastic timelines.

## Scholarly Contributions and Literature
A significant body of literature exists regarding the ruins and religious practices of Babylon. Early foundational works include C. J. Rich's *Memoir on the Ruins of Babylon* (1816) and *Collected Memoirs* (1839), as well as A. H. Layard's *Nineveh and Babylon* (1853). Further specialized studies include C. P. Tiele's *De Hoofdtempel van Babel* (1886) and A. H. Sayce's *The Religion of the Ancient Babylonians* (1887), which was based on the Hibbert Lectures and later superseded by *Religions of Ancient Egypt and Babylonia* (1902).

Other notable contributions to the field include:
*   **L. W. King**: Author of *Babylonian Religion and Mythology* (1899), *The Seven Tablets of Creation* (1902), and *Babylonian Magic and Sorcery* (1896).
*   **R. C. Thompson**: Author of *The Reports of the Magicians and Astrologers of Nineveh and Babylon* (1900) and *Devils and Evil Spirits of Babylonia* (1903-1904).
*   **Morris Jastrow, jun.**: Author of *Religion of Babylonia and Assyria* (1898) and the expanded *Religion Babyloniens und Assyriens* (1904).
*   **A. Boissier**: Author of *Documents assyriens relatifs aux presages* (1894-1897) and *Choix de textes relatifs a la divination assyro-babylonienne* (1905-1906).

Additional research has been published in the *Mittheilungen der deutschen Orientgesellschaft* (1899-1906) and by authors such as F. Delitzsch in *Im Lande des einstigen Paradieses* (1903) and F. H. Weissbach in *Das Stadtbild von Babylon* (1904).

## Religious Concepts and Texts
The religion of the region is explored through various specialized texts and monographs. These include cuneiform texts from Babylonian tablets held in the British Museum (1901-), and *Selections from the Miscellaneous Inscriptions of Western Asia* edited by H. C. Rawlinson (1891). 

Specific areas of religious study include:
*   **Divination and Magic**: Works by Ch. Fossey on Assyrian magic (1902) and K. L. Tallqvist on the *Maqlu* incantation series (1895).
*   **Astrology**: Virolleaud's *L'Astrologie chaldeenne* (1906-) and Craig's *Astrological-Astronomical Texts* (1892).
*   **Hymns and Prayers**: G. A. Reisner's *Sumerisch-babylonische Hymnen* (1896) and J. A. Knudtzon's *Assyrische Gebete an den Sonnengott* (1893).

Regarding the nature of deities, A. H. Sayce noted in his Hibbert Lectures that a certain national god is almost identical in character to the early Yahweh of Israel, functioning essentially as a national god.

## Chronology of Dynasties
The dating of Babylonian dynasties varies significantly among different scholars. For the first three dynasties (Dyn. I, II, and III), the following timelines have been proposed:

*   **A. H. Sayce**: In 1899, Sayce dated Dyn. I from 2478 to 2174 B.C., Dyn. II from 2174 to 1806 B.C., and Dyn. III from 1806 to 1229 B.C. In 1902, he revised the start of Dyn. I to 2460 B.C.
*   **Oppert (1888)**: Proposed Dyn. I (2506-2202 B.C.), Dyn. II (2202-1834 B.C.), and Dyn. III (1834-1257 B.C.).
*   **Winckler**: Provided several estimates, including a 1905 range of approximately 2400-2100 B.C. for Dyn. I, 2100-1700 B.C. for Dyn. II, and 1700-1150 B.C. for Dyn. III.
*   **Delitzsch**: In 1907, suggested Dyn. I began c. 2420 B.C. and ended c. 2120 B.C.
*   **Lehmann-Haupt**: In 1903, proposed Dyn. I (2296-2009/8 B.C.), Dyn. II (2008/7-1691 B.C.), and Dyn. III (1690-1115 B.C.).
*   **Hommel**: In 1886, proposed Dyn. I (2403-2035 B.C.), Dyn. II (2035-1731 B.C.), and Dyn. III (1731-1154 B.C.).
