# Archelaus Procures A Reconciliation Between Alexander and Herod

## Overview of the Conflict
A significant familial and political disturbance occurred between Herod and his son, Alexander. The conflict reached a point where Herod bound Alexander and held him in a state of great distress. This tension was exacerbated by calumnies laid upon the young man, which led Herod to a temper of harshness.

## The Intervention of Archelaus
Archelaus, the King of Cappadocia and father-in-law to Alexander, traveled to Jerusalem specifically to compose the differences between Herod and his son. Archelaus acted as a friend to Herod, grieving with him over the disturbance. 

Upon arriving, Archelaus observed Herod's volatile temper and determined that it would be unseasonable to reprove the king or suggest that he had acted rashly, as such an approach would likely irritate Herod further and lead to a dispute. Instead, Archelaus employed a strategic approach to correct the misfortunes. He pretended to be angry with Alexander, asserting that Herod had actually been too mild and had not acted rashly at all. Archelaus went so far as to suggest that he would dissolve the marriage between his daughter and Alexander, stating that he could not in justice spare his own daughter if she were conscious of any information she had failed to inform Herod of.

## Shift in Herod's Temper
This unexpected alignment with Herod's position caused the king to abate his harshness. Feeling that his previous actions had been justified, Herod gradually returned to an affectionate paternal disposition. As Archelaus continued to join in the accusations against the young man, Herod was dissolved into tears and sorrow. Consequently, Herod decided that he would not dissolve Alexander's marriage and became less angry regarding his son's offenses.

Once Herod reached a more moderate temper, Archelaus shifted the blame away from Alexander. He argued that the young man was unacquainted with malice and had been corrupted by his friends. Specifically, Archelaus suggested that there was more reason to suspect Pheroras, Alexander's brother, than the son.

## Reconciliation with Pheroras
The shift in Herod's inclinations left Pheroras in a precarious position, as he had no one to reconcile him with the king. Pheroras attempted to seek safety through impudence, eventually having recourse to Archelaus. Archelaus informed Pheroras that he could not secure an excuse for him because he was caught in many crimes that demonstrated he had plotted against the king and caused Alexander's misfortunes. Archelaus told Pheroras that the only way to receive assistance was to abandon his cunning knavery and denials, confess the charges, and implore pardon from his brother.

## Aftermath and Diplomatic Relations
Following these events, Herod returned from Rome and appeared to have forgiven his sons, though he maintained some suspicions. The reconciliation was further solidified during Herod's voyage by Cilicia. At Eleusa, Archelaus treated Herod and his party in an obliging manner, expressing pleasure at the reconciliation of Herod and his son-in-law. Archelaus had previously written to friends in Rome to request assistance for Alexander during his trial. As a gesture of friendship, Archelaus conducted Herod to Zephyrium and provided presents valued at thirty talents.

Upon the conclusion of these reconciliations, the parties engaged in feastings and agreeable entertainments. As Archelaus departed, Herod presented him with seventy talents, a golden throne set with precious stones, eunuchs, and a concubine named Pannychis. Herod and his nobility conducted Archelaus on his way as far as Antioch.

## Other Family Relations
The archive notes a separate domestic history involving Glaphyra, the daughter of King Archelaus. Glaphyra was first married as a virgin to Alexander, the son of Herod and brother of Archelaus. After Alexander was slain by his father, Glaphyra married Juba, the King of Lybia. Following Juba's death, while living in widowhood in Cappadocia with her father, Archelaus divorced his former wife, Mariamne, and married Glaphyra due to his great affection for her. Before her death, Glaphyra reported a dream in which the deceased Alexander complained that she had forgotten their affection and their children by marrying his brother Archelaus, though he ultimately promised to set her free from reproach and make her his again.
