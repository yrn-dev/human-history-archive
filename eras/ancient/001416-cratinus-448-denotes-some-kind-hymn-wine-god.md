# Dithyrambs and Early Greek Hymns

The dithyramb is a form of lyric poetry and choral song that originated in the cult of Dionysus, the god of wine. First mentioned by Archilochus (c. 670 B.C.), the form was later given a finished choral structure by Arion of Lesbos (c. 600 B.C.). Arion's dithyrambs were produced at Corinth, and his chorus, known as the *tragikos choros*, consisted of members who personated satyrs.

## Evolution and Scope
Originally, the dithyramb focused on the birth of Dionysus, but it eventually expanded to cover all of the god's fortunes. By the close of the 6th century B.C., the scope of the dithyramb widened further, allowing for the celebration of any hero or god. Examples of this expanded use include Simonides, who wrote dithyrambs on Tithonus and Memnon, and Pindar, who wrote on Heracles and Orion. 

Because of this evolution, Alexandrian scholars adopted a broad definition of the term *dithurambos*, using it to denote any lyric poem that dealt with a mythical narrative. Under this wide classification, the Alexandrians included Ode xvii of Bacchylides—which relates the voyage of Theseus to Crete—despite it being a *paian* for the Delian Apollo.

## Relation to Drama
The development of Greek drama is closely linked to the festivals of Dionysus, held from the beginning of winter to the beginning of spring. These festivals involved rustic worshippers gathering around an altar to sing hymns detailing the sufferings or victories of the god. These hymns served as the foundation for both comedy and tragedy:
*   **Tragedy:** Derived from "the goat-song" (*tragos*), as a goat was sacrificed to Dionysus before the hymn was sung.
*   **Comedy:** Known as "the village-song," representing the same hymn used as an occasion for rustic jest.

The transition to drama occurred when the leader of the chorus began reciting adventures as the god himself or a messenger from the god. The introduction of dialogue between the leader (*koryphaios*) and a member of the chorus called "the answerer" (*hypokrites*) is attributed to Thespis of Attica (c. 536 B.C.). While the dithyramb received its artistic form from the Dorians, the dialogue element was introduced in Attica.

## Other Hymnic and Choral Forms
Beyond the dithyramb, ancient Greek literature contains various other religious and choral compositions:
*   **Hymnoi:** This category includes *apopemptikoi*, which were hymns of pious farewell used to speed a god on his way during seasonal migrations.
*   **Paianes:** Represented by fragments such as those concerning the blessings of peace.
*   **Prosodia:** Choral odes performed during processions to temples.
*   **Huporchemata:** Lively dance-songs for religious festivals. Pratinas of Phlius wrote these primarily in honor of Apollo.
*   **Erotika:** A class of drinking-songs akin to *skolia*, some of which featured humorous verses regarding the power of wine.
*   **Homeric Hymns:** A collection of 33 pieces in hexameter verse, likely formed in Attica for rhapsodists. Rather than formal worship hymns, these were *prooimia* (prefatory addresses) used to introduce epic recitations.

Menander the Rhetor provided a detailed classification of hymns into nine distinct classes, including *kletic* hymns (used to invoke gods from various places), *mythikoi*, and *genealogikoi*.

## Cratinus and Old Comedy
Cratinus (c. 450–422 B.C.) was a bold and broad satirist of "Old Comedy," a form that emerged following the establishment of democracy by Pericles. Cratinus, along with Crates, helped mold early rural farcical entertainments into the forms of Attic art. In the context of terminology, Cratinus (c. 448 B.C.) used the word *thriambos* to denote a type of hymn to the wine-god. 

Despite his boldness as a satirist, Cratinus is described as having conservative tendencies. He opposed the emergence of excessive license from the freedom of the art and proposed Cimon as an example of an honest and good citizen.
