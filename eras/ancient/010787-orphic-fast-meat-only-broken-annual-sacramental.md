# Orphic Fasting and the Abstention from Flesh

The Orphic fast from meat was a disciplined practice of vegetarianism observed by those initiated into the mysteries of Orpheus, known as *mystae*. This abstention from flesh was maintained throughout the practitioner's life. According to the confession of one initiated in these mysteries (as rendered by Dr. Gilbert Murray in Euripides' *Cretans*), the initiate sought to remain "clean" and "robed in pure white," specifically exiling from their lips any "touch of all meat where life hath been."

## Purpose and Philosophy of Abstention
The practice of avoiding a flesh diet was driven by several spiritual and metaphysical concerns. One primary motivation was the fear of imbibing the irrational soul of animals, which was believed to reside especially in the blood. Practitioners feared that consuming such souls would reinforce the lower instincts and appetites of the human being. 

Porphyry provided further arguments for this discipline, suggesting that the souls of humans transmigrated into animals. Consequently, eating meat was viewed as a form of cannibalism, as one might consume their own kind. Porphyry argued that a "lover of wisdom" and priest of the universal God would abstain from eating animals to avoid the risk of taking inferior souls and polluting demons into themselves. For those intent on "casting aside the body and living through Reason with Truth," this discipline was seen as a way to enter the "Olympic contest of the soul" naked and without the "tunic of the flesh."

## The Sacramental Exception
While the Orphic fast was generally lifelong, it was broken by an annual sacramental banquet. Originally, this banquet may have consisted of human flesh, but it later transitioned to the use of raw bovine flesh. In a broader context of ancient rites, fasting often served as a mode of preparation for the sacramental eating of a sacred animal, a process sometimes assisted by the use of aperients and purgatives.

## Parallel Practices of Abstention
The scruples against eating flesh were mirrored in other ancient and religious traditions:
*   **Pythagoreans:** Like the Orphic *mystae*, Pythagoreans abstained from flesh throughout their lives.
*   **Therapeutae and Early Christians:** The Therapeutae ensured their sacred meals were free from blood-containing meats. Similarly, early Christians and some Jewish traditions maintained rules against eating things that had been strangled, motivated by the fear of absorbing the animal's psychic qualities.
*   **Manichaean Sects:** These groups believed that fish were free from the taint that polluted animals generated through copulation, as fish were born in and of the waters without sexual connection. Consequently, fish could be safely eaten, a belief that formed the origin of the Catholic rule of fasting.
*   **Greek Rites:** Abstaining from flesh was essential in old Greek rites intended to avert *Keres* or djinns—ill-regulated ghosts that returned to earth to molest the living.

## General Functions of Fasting
Beyond the specific Orphic tradition, the archive records several widespread reasons for fasting across various cultures:
*   **Ecstatic Revelations:** Fasting was used to induce morbid excitement and imaginative ecstasies to converse with gods or receive supernatural revelations. Examples include King Saul, who fasted before the witch of Endor revealed the ghost of Samuel, and North American Indians, whose visions during fasting were examined by elders to determine fitness for chieftaincy.
*   **Spiritual Power:** In the Semitic East, a fast of forty days and nights in the wilderness was believed to grant power over Djinns. Similarly, Indian magicians fasted for twelve days before performing cures or producing rain, and Indian *yogis* fasted to see the gods of their Pantheon.
*   **Exorcism:** In early Christian traditions, such as those mentioned in the *Didache* and by Justin, fasting was used to expel evil spirits, based on the belief that demons preferred a well-fed body over an emaciated one.
