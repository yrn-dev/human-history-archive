# Bashan

Bashan is a region located east of the Jordan River toward its source. While its boundaries are not precisely defined, it is generally situated north of the territory of Gilead.

## Geography and Physical Characteristics
The physical characteristics of Bashan are noteworthy, specifically its volcanic origin. The Jebel ed-Druz, or Hauran mountain, consists of a group of extinct volcanoes. This volcanic origin has resulted in friable soil that is extraordinarily fertile; it is reported to yield barley a hundred-fold and wheat eighty-fold. 

The region is also distinguished by its mountains and its oaks, the latter of which were once famous and are still found in certain places. These oaks were used to manufacture oars and are frequently mentioned alongside the cedars of Lebanon.

## Historical Provinces and Boundaries
According to Josephus, Bashan was divided into four provinces:
*   **Gaulanitis:** Extending from the Haj Road to the Jordan lakes, this province is represented by the modern Jaul[=a]n. It was further divided into two sections known as Sogana and Gamalitis. The city of refuge, Golan, was located here, though its site remains undiscovered.
*   **Trachonitis:** This area adjoined the territory of Damascus, Auranitis, and Batanaea. It corresponds to the modern district of the Lej[=a], where inscriptions identify its former name as Trach[=o]n.
*   **Auranitis:** Identified as the Hauran of modern Arabs and the prophet Ezekiel, this region lies north of Gilead and south of the Jaul[=a]n. Some sources restrict the name to the narrow strip on the west and the plain south of the Lej[=a].
*   **Batanaea:** Located east of the Hauran plain and the Lej[=a], this province includes the Jebel ed-Druz. It is still remembered in the name *'Ard el-Bathaniyeh*.

Other landmarks associated with the region include Mt. Hermon, which serves as a northern limit, and the city of Salecah.

## Biblical and Ancient History
The name Bashan first appears in Hebrew history during the wanderings of the Israelites. After the defeat of Sihon, king of the Amorites, the Israelites encountered Og, the king of Bashan, at Edrei. Og was defeated and slain; he is described as being of gigantic stature, a characteristic attributed to his descent from the aboriginal Rephaim, a race known for their lofty stature. The Rephaim were established in Ashteroth-Karnaim (likely the same as Ashtaroth), an important city of Bashan.

Following the conquest, the territory was allotted to the eastern division of the tribe of Manasseh. During the reign of Solomon, the region of Argob in Bashan—which contained sixty great cities featuring walls and brazen bars—was assigned to Ben-Geber, one of Solomon's lieutenants. The last historical event regarding Bashan in the Old Testament occurs during the time of Jehu, when the country was taken from Israel by Hazael, the king of Syria.

## Natural History and Economy
In prophetic and poetical texts, Bashan is noted for its natural products and wildlife. It is described as having been inhabited by lions and is famous for its cattle, which were proverbial for their ferocity. The "kine of Bashan" are used as a reference to the wealthy women of Samaria who oppressed the poor.

## Later History
In later periods, Bashan became known as a refuge for robbers and outlaws. Strabo mentions a great cave in Trachonitis capable of holding 4,000 robbers, a site that Arab tradition identifies as the home of Job. The region is also famous as the center of the Ghassanid dynasty. 

The Hauran serves as one of the primary habitations for the sect of the Druses. To prevent the area from remaining a sanctuary for fugitives fleeing government requirements—such as taxation and conscription—or justice, the local sub-governor partially destroyed the great subterranean "city" at Ed-Dera'a.
