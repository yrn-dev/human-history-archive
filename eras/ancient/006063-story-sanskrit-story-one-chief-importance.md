# The Mahāvastu and Sanskrit Buddhist Narratives

The *Mahāvastu*, or "Sublime Story," is a Sanskrit work of chief importance to Buddhists. It details the narrative of how the Buddha attained the *Sambodhi* (the "higher Wisdom") of *Nirvāna* and achieved victory over ignorance under the Bo Tree. The text begins by recounting the Buddha's previous births, during which he accumulated the qualities necessary for his eventual enlightenment.

## Sectarian Origin and Composition
The *Mahāvastu* served as a standard work for a specific school or sect known as the Mahā-sanghikas. Because of this, the text preserves a theory of the Buddha that differs from the views held by followers of the canon. These Mahā-sanghika perspectives developed in subsequent centuries into the modern form of Buddhism in India, known as Mahāyāna. 

Regarding its composition, the *Mahāvastu* was written in the valley of the Ganges rather than in the north or in Nepal. The work is composed using a combination of prose and verse.

## Related Sanskrit Works
Other Sanskrit texts address the same subject as the *Mahāvastu*. These include the *Lalita Vistara* and the *Buddha Carita*, both of which are epics or poems. The *Lalita Vistara* is noted for its influence on other narratives; for example, the phraseology used in the history of Josaphat almost literally renders the Sanskrit found in the *Lalita Vistara*. 

Additionally, all later Buddhist accounts, whether written in Sanskrit or Pali, repeat the story of the Buddha's birth. According to these accounts, the mother of the Buddha was traveling from Kapilavastu (the capital of the Sakiyas) to her mother's home in Devadaha (the capital of the Koliyas) to be confined. She gave birth to her son in a grove or garden called Lumbini, located near Devadaha, after her pains began during the journey.

## Broader Context of Sanskrit Literature
Sanskrit is one of the chief original literatures of Asia, and its extensive body of work is primarily theological and poetical, with history being conspicuously absent. While Sanskrit ceased to be a popular language by 300 B.C., it remained the sacred, learned, and court form of speech for the Brahmans. 

Beyond the life of the Buddha, Sanskrit literature includes a wide array of other stories and genres:
*   **Collections of Tales:** The *Katha-sarit-sagara* ("The Ocean of the Streams of Story") is a large collection of Sanskrit stories compiled by Soma Deva in the 12th century. Other early fables include the *Pancha Tantra* (Five Sections) and the *Hitopadesa*. The *Pancha Tantra* served as the basis for the *Hitopadesa* and was later translated into Pahlavi and Arabic (as the "Book of Kalilah and Dimna"), eventually reaching Europe.
*   **Epics:** The *Mahabharata* contains various episodes, such as the story of *Nala and Damayanti*.
*   **Drama:** Northern Indian plays were largely written in Sanskrit. In these dramas, the principal personages and heroes speak Sanskrit, while female and inferior characters use varieties of Prakrit languages.
