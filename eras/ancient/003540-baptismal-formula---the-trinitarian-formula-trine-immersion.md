# The Baptismal Formula and Trine Immersion

The baptismal formula and the practice of trine immersion are central elements of the Christian rite of baptism. Historically, these two features were not uniformly applied from the beginning, nor did they always occur together.

## Evolution of the Baptismal Formula
In the apostolic age, the normal formula used was baptism "into Christ Jesus," "of Jesus Christ," "of the Lord Jesus Christ," or "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ." Paul interpreted these formulas as being equivalent to baptism "into the death of Christ Jesus," symbolizing the believer being raised from death into everlasting life.

Over time, different formulas emerged and were debated:
*   **The Trinitarian Formula:** This formula—"I baptize thee in the name of Father and Son and Holy Spirit"—eventually became the accepted standard of all Christendom following theological disputes between Arians (who viewed Jesus as divine but inferior to the Father), Sabellians (who viewed Jesus as an aspect of the Father), and Trinitarians (who taught God was both one and three). The Athanasian Creed provides the most complete expression of this formula. The *Teaching of the Apostles* prescribes this trinitarian form, though it also mentions baptism into the name of the Lord.
*   **The Formula of Christ Alone:** Baptism in the name of Christ remained widespread into the 3rd century. Pope Stephen declared such baptisms valid in opposition to Cyprian of Carthage. Later, Pope Nicholas (858-867) allowed baptism to be valid *tantum in nomine Christi* (only in the name of Christ), as seen in the Acts. Other figures, including Hilary, the African monk Ursinus, and the synod of Nemours (A.D. 1284), also asserted the validity of baptism into the name of Christ alone.
*   **Regional Variations:** In the East, the formula "so-and-so, the servant of God, is baptized" was used, with the Greeks adding "Amen" after each person and concluding with "Now and ever and to aeons of aeons, amen." Conversely, Basil condemned baptism into the Lord alone as insufficient, and Armenian fathers often specified that baptism "into the death of Christ" was the only essential element.

## Trine Immersion
Trine immersion refers to the practice of dipping the candidate in water three times. Tertullian provides an early explanation, stating that candidates are dipped thrice for the several names and persons. Jerome added that this was done so the "one sacrament of the Trinity may be shown forth."

Alternative interpretations of the three-fold immersion exist, particularly among the fathers of the East and West, including Athanasius, Cyril of Jerusalem, Gregory of Nyssa, Leo of Rome, and Theophylactus. These figures regarded trine immersion as symbolic of the three days Christ spent entombed. This interpretation is enjoined in the Armenian baptismal rubric and an epistle from Macarius of Jerusalem (c. 330), where it is associated with the idea of baptism into the death of Christ.

Regarding the origin of the practice, Basil confessed his ignorance of how it began, suggesting that the rival explanations were aetiological inventions to justify an existing custom. The archive notes that the church may have adopted this from a pagan medium, as threefold lustrations were common in Greek and Latin antiquity. Examples include writings by Virgil, Ovid, Horace, and Persius; a scholiast on Horace noted that people plunged themselves thrice when expiating sins.

## Ritual Context and the Disciplina Arcani
The baptismal formula was part of a broader ritual process. By approximately the year 300, candidates entered the catechumenate, underwent scrutiny, and were repeatedly exorcized to remove the taint of demon worship. They learned the Creed and the Lord's Prayer by heart—a process known in the West as *traditio* and *redditio* of the symbol.

This led to the *Disciplina arcani*, an obligation to keep the formula of the threefold name, the Creed, and the Lord's Prayer secret from the unbaptized and pagans. This practice mirrored pagan mysteries and the tendency in primitive religions to keep the name of a deity secret. While some 4th and 5th-century Fathers emphasized this atmosphere of mystery, earlier apologists like Tertullian and Justin Martyr in the 2nd century did not conceal these elements from pagan emperors.
