# Dissertation on the Fables of Aesop

The *Dissertation on the Fables of Aesop* is a critical work authored by Richard Bentley. This study is noted for its role in transforming the scholarly understanding of Babrius from a mere name into a recognized figure of literary importance.

## The Research of Richard Bentley
In his *Dissertation on the Fables of Aesop*, Richard Bentley conducted a careful examination of prose Aesopian fables. These fables had been preserved in various collections since the time of Maximus Planudes. Through his analysis, Bentley discovered traces of versification within these prose texts. He was subsequently able to extract a number of verses, which he assigned to the author Babrius. 

Bentley's research was later followed up by Tyrwhitt in the 1776 work *De Babrio*. For a period following these discoveries, scholars focused their efforts on attempting to reconstruct the metrical originals of the prose fables.

## Babrius and the Aesopian Tradition
Babrius was an author who wrote a collection of fables in Greek. While practically nothing is known of him, it is supposed that he was a Roman, possibly named Valerius, who lived in the East—likely Syria—where the fables appear to have first gained popularity. 

The dating of Babrius's work has been a subject of speculation due to an address in his writing to "a son of King Alexander." This has led to assigned dates ranging from the 3rd century B.C. to the 3rd century A.D. One theory suggests the "son of Alexander" refers to Alexander Severus (A.D. 222-235), a ruler known for keeping various literary men at his court. Another theory identifies this figure as a person named Branchus, suggesting Babrius may have served as his tutor, though it is probable that Branchus is a fictitious name. 

Evidence from his language and style, as well as the fact that there is no mention of him in ancient writers before the beginning of the 3rd century A.D., suggests he belonged to that period.

## The Discovery of the Babrius Manuscript
In 1842, a Greek man named M. Minas—who also discovered the *Philosophoumena* of Hippolytus—found a manuscript of Babrius at the convent of St Laura on Mount Athos. This manuscript, now held in the British Museum, contains 123 fables out of a supposed original total of 160. The fables in the manuscript are arranged alphabetically but break off at the letter O.

The works are written in choliambic verse (also known as "limping" or imperfect iambic verse), which features a spondee as the last foot. This specific metre was originally used for satire. The style of Babrius is described as elegant, correct, and terse, with pointed expression and fully constructed stories.

## Other Contexts of Aesopian Fables
The fables of Aesop appear in various other historical and literary contexts within the archive:
* **Educational Materials:** In the 4th century A.D., the Greek grammarian Dositheus Magister produced a Greek translation of a Latin grammar at Rome. An appendix to this work included fables of Aesop alongside other materials such as extracts from Hyginus and a history of the Trojan War.
* **Illustrations:** The English artist and illustrator Randolph Caldecott (1846-1886) produced a work titled *Some Aesop's Fables with Modern Instances, &c.* in 1883.
* **Comparative Literature:** The *Fables of Luqman*, known to have existed in the 13th century and edited by Erpenius in 1615, have been studied in relation to similar literature, including J. Jacobs's edition of Caxton's *Fables of Aesop*.

## Historical Figures
Aesop is identified as the writer of the fables. Historical accounts place him at Sardis, where he enjoyed the favor of Croesus. Aesop is recorded as having advised Solon regarding the proper way to speak to kings, suggesting that one should either say very little or say what the king wishes to hear. This contrasted with Solon's view that one should say what is best for the king to hear.
