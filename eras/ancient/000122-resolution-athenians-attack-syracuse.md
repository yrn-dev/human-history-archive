# The Athenian Expedition Against Syracuse

The Athenian military campaign against Syracuse was a major conflict characterized by initial optimism, strategic errors, and a protracted siege. The operation was marked by the tension between Athenian leadership and the evolving defenses of the Syracusans, eventually leading to a catastrophic failure for the Athenian forces.

## Origins and Preparations
The expedition was preceded by general enthusiasm and sanguine hopes in Athens, supported by an abundance in the Athenian treasury. The resulting armament was a display of both wealth and force. However, the departure was marred by the mutilation of the Hermæ in Athens, an act of conspiracy that caused violent religious alarm and excitement. Political enemies of Alkibiadês used this event to attempt to ruin him, accusing him of profaning and divulging the Eleusinian mysteries. Despite these internal tensions, the armament departed from Peiræus in a splendid spectacle, mustered at Korkyra, and proceeded to Rhegium.

Upon arrival at Rhegium, the Athenians were discouraged to learn the truth regarding the poverty of Egesta. The Athenian generals—Nikias, Alkibiadês, and Lamachus—debated their plan of action. While Lamachus showed superior discernment, the plan proposed by Alkibiadês was preferred.

## The Siege of Syracuse
Upon arriving in Sicily, Nikias moved in the early spring. At the time, Syracuse consisted of an Inner and Outer City, with the upper ground of Epipolæ located outside the outer city walls. The Athenians successfully surprised the summit of Epipolæ, a victory essential for the future prosecution of the siege. 

The Athenians established a central work on Epipolæ known as "The Circle." The Syracusans responded by building a counter-wall south of the Circle, which the Athenians subsequently stormed and destroyed. Nikias then occupied the southern cliff to continue the blockade. A second Syracusan counter-work, extending across the marsh to the river Anapus, was attacked and taken by Lamachus; however, Lamachus died during the ensuing general battle. Despite this loss, the Athenians were victorious, and their fleet entered the Great Harbor. As the southern portion of the blockade wall across the marsh neared completion, the Syracusans ceased offering obstruction, leading to a state of despondency in the city and triumphant prospects for the Athenians.

## The Intervention of Gylippus
The Athenian momentum stalled due to the conduct of Nikias, whose operations became characterized by comparative languor and confidence. This provided an opportunity for Gylippus to intervene. Despite discouraging reports, Gylippus landed at Himera and levied an army to march across Sicily to Syracuse. Nikias, in an egregious mistake, despised Gylippus and allowed him to advance unobstructed.

Gylippus's arrival shifted the tide of the conflict. He determined to attack the Athenians at sea. Although the Athenians were initially victorious in a naval combat in the harbor of Syracuse, Gylippus surprised and captured Plemmyrium. This capture increased the confidence of the Syracusans and encouraged them to seek reinforcements from other Sicilian towns.

## Naval Conflict and the Second Armament
The Athenians faced significant disadvantages in the Great Harbor, as the narrow space rendered their naval tactics impossible. Conversely, the Syracusans improved their ships to better suit the confined area. Following a complete defeat of the Athenian fleet, a second armament under Demosthenês arrived from Korkyra. His entry into the Great Harbor initially revived Athenian courage.

Demosthenês attempted a decisive nocturnal march to surprise Epipolæ and turn the Syracusan line of defense. While he achieved partial success at first, the operation ended in a ruinous defeat with great Athenian losses during the flight.

## The Final Retreat
Following the failure at Epipolæ, the Athenian armament suffered from weariness and the effects of fever. Demosthenês insisted on relinquishing the enterprise and returning home, arguing that Athens needed the forces to defend against the Peloponnesian garrison at Dekeleia. Nikias initially opposed this removal from the Great Harbor, but eventually consented to retreat.

The planned retreat was privately circulated but was postponed following an eclipse of the moon, which was viewed as a sign.

## Peloponnesian Involvement
The conflict in Sicily drew the attention of Sparta. In November 414 B.C., envoys from both Nikias and Gylippus arrived at Sparta requesting support. The Lacedæmonians, recognizing that the Athenian resolution to send a second army made Athens more vulnerable at home, resolved to support Syracuse. Sparta determined that a Peloponnesian allied force would be sent to Syracuse in the spring and that Attica would be invaded, including the fortification of Dekeleia.
