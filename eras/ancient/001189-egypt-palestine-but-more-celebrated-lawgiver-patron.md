# Egypt and Palestine

Egypt and Palestine have historically been linked through trade, conflict, and cultural exchange, often serving as strategic focal points for various empires and civilizations.

## Ancient Relations and Conflict
Early trade existed between Egypt, Palestine, and Phenicia prior to any acquaintance with the Greeks. Herodotus noted that Egypt imported large quantities of wine from Phenicia and Greece, using the earthen vessels from these shipments to transport water during desert journeys.

Palestine frequently became a zone of discord due to its position as the only country separating Egypt from the Assyrian Empire. This led to long-term conflict over boundaries, a pattern of contention that continued later between the Seleucidæ and the Ptolemies, with the territory being won alternately by whichever power was stronger. During the reign of Psammetichus, the Egyptian ruler entered Palestine with an army to secure his frontiers against the increasing power of the Assyrian king.

## Greek and Roman Influence
Egypt came under the influence of Greek civilization earlier than Palestine and Syria. In the latter two regions, Greek culture and literature reached their peak between the 3rd and 8th centuries of the Christian era. This period saw the rise of influential centers such as Gaza, renowned for its school of rhetoric, and Berytus, known for its academy of law. Syria and Palestine were also the birthplace of sacred poetry, considered the most aesthetically valuable class of Byzantine literature.

In the Roman era, the region saw the development of cities such as Caesarea Philippi. Located 95 miles north of Jerusalem and 35 miles southwest of Damascus, it was a place of great sanctity. Originally associated with a local *numen* and later the worship of Pan, the town was re-founded by Philip the Tetrarch, who named it Caesarea in honor of Tiberius and added Philippi to immortalize himself. The city later served as a site for gladiatorial shows hosted by Titus to celebrate the capture of Jerusalem.

## Religious and Monastic History
Palestine and Egypt were significant centers for early Christian monasticism. In Egypt, distinctions were made between Coenobites and Hermits. In the wilderness of Jordan, Gerasimus founded a monastery accompanied by a Laura consisting of seventy cells. The monks and hermits of Palestine were celebrated in the writings of Evagrius, while the lives and miracles of thirty Anachorets were collected by Theodoret.

The regions also experienced periods of persecution. Eusebius recorded martyrdoms in Palestine and in the province of Thebais in Egypt. In Thebais, he claimed that ten to one hundred persons frequently suffered martyrdom in a single day, though some historians have noted his style in describing Egypt as obscure and intricate.

## Medieval and Early Modern Transitions
In 1260, the defeat and death of the general Kitboga in Palestine prevented the potential fall of Egypt. At this time, Egypt was a Turkish sultanate dominated by the Mamelukes, a body of soldiers recruited from trained boy slaves. This system allowed capable Sultans to maintain power, while weak or evil ones were replaced. Egypt remained an independent power under this system until 1517, when it was conquered by the Ottoman Turks.

In the Crusader period, the city of Caesarea Philippi was captured in 1130 and subsequently lost to the Moslems in 1165. The summit of a conical hill above the village of Banias still holds the Crusaders' castle of Subeibeh, described as one of the finest in Palestine.

## Modern Military History
During the Great War, the Turkish Government and Germany aimed to conquer Egypt, viewing it as the key to Britain's Eastern possessions and a base for operations in Mesopotamia and Gallipoli. An initial Turkish invasion was defeated on the banks of the Suez Canal in early 1915.

British military strategy shifted to the realization that Egypt could only be properly defended on the Palestine frontier, beyond the Sinai Desert. To carry the war into Palestine, the British laid water pipes and railways across the desert. After several desert battles, the British army crossed the Palestine frontier in the spring of 1917. The objective evolved from a defensive posture to an offensive one, intended to pin down large Turkish forces and force Turkey to request aid from Germany, thereby disrupting the plans of the German General Staff.

## Scientific Exploration
The regions have also been subjects of biological research. Following the work of Linnaeus and Artedi, F. Hasselquist conducted collections in both Egypt and Palestine. Additionally, Forskål examined and described the fishes of the Red Sea.
