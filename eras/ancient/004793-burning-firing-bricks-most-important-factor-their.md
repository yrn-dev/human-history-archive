# The Production and Firing of Bricks

The production of bricks relies most critically on the process of burning or firing. The strength and durability of the final product depend largely on the degree and character of the firing process, as heat triggers chemical re-combinations and decompositions that fundamentally alter the physical properties of dry clay.

## The Firing Process and Temperature
To produce a hard, strong brick that maintains its shape, resists porosity, and withstands frost, the firing atmosphere for ordinary bricks should be oxidizing. The finishing temperature typically ranges from 900° C to 1250° C, with 1050° C being the usual temperature for ordinary bricks. However, lime-clays require higher temperatures—usually between 1150° C and 1200° C—to ensure the lime enters into a chemical combination with other substances.

The chemical composition of the clay significantly influences the resulting brick. Oxide of iron, present in amounts from 2% to 10%, determines the hardness, durability, and color of the burnt brick, which can range from light buff to chocolate. For example, clay containing 5% to 8% oxide of iron typically produces a red brick. If the brick is fired too hard or contains 3% to 4% alkalis, the color becomes darker or more purple, as the clay approaches its fusion point. Conversely, an excess of alumina tends to make the color brighter and lighter.

## Firing Methods and Kilns
There are two primary methods of firing: the use of permanent kilns and the older method of "clamps."

### Clamps
Clamps are used primarily in smaller brickfields. Unfired bricks are arranged in a rectangular stack of closely placed rows or walls. Fine coal is spread in horizontal layers between the bricks, and fires are lit in "fire-mouths" at the bottom. The stack burns until the fuel is consumed and then cools. Once taken down, the bricks are sorted; under-fired bricks are saved to be refired in the next clamp. Some clamps are temporary kilns with clay-plastered burnt brick exteriors. This method is considered slow, uneconomical, and irregular.

### Permanent Kilns
Permanent kilns are categorized into two main types:
*   **Intermittent Kilns:** These are usually vertical cylinders with domed tops. In older "up-draught" versions, combustion products move from the fire-mouth upward and out the top. Modern "down-draught" kilns introduce combustion products near the crown and draw them downward through the bricks via flues and a chimney. Down-draught kilns are more fuel-efficient and produce a higher percentage of well-fired bricks.
*   **Continuous Kilns:** Developed from the idea of connecting multiple down-draught kilns to a single tall chimney, the earliest form was invented by Friedrich Hoffman. These consist of firing chambers in series where combustion products from one kiln pass through several others before exiting.

## Materials and Clay Types
Brick clays are generally divided into two classes. Class (1) consists of ordinary brick clays, which are widely distributed and contain hydrated aluminium silicates, free silica (sand), and undecomposed felspar. Class (2) consists of lime-clays or "marls," which contain a high percentage of limestone or chalk (usually 15% to 30% calcium carbonate). Marls burn to a distinctive yellow color.

The preparation of marls is intensive. In London, an artificial "malm" is created by mixing ordinary brick-clay with ground chalk in water to a cream-like consistency. This pulp is run through a sieve onto a drying bed, mixed with fine ashes, and tempered with water. In other regions, marls may be squeezed through iron gratings to remove pebbles or passed through a grinding-mill with iron rollers. Removing limestone pebbles is vital because they convert to quicklime during firing, which can shatter the brick when exposed to weather.

## Historical Development
The Greeks produced three kinds of bricks, though they were not used extensively; the walls of Athens near Mount Hymettus are among the most important ancient Greek brick structures. The Romans mastered the art, likely acquiring knowledge from Egypt and Greece. Around the beginning of the Christian era, the Romans revived brick manufacture, introducing kiln-burning and careful clay selection, spreading these methods across western Europe, including Britain and Germany.

In England, the art of brickmaking was largely lost after the Roman withdrawal, with few burnt bricks produced until the 13th century. Early post-revival buildings include Little Wenham Hall (c. A.D. 1210), but general use did not return until the 15th century. During the reign of Henry VIII, the craft reached a high level of perfection, possibly via Flemish workmen, as seen in Hampton Court Palace and St James's Palace. Following the Great Fire of London in 1666, bricks replaced timber frameworks in city rebuilding. In 1625, the standard brick size was regulated by statute as 9 x 4½ x 3 inches. A tax on bricks existed from 1784 until 1850.

## Classification of Finished Bricks
Bricks are categorized by quality and use:
*   **Stocks:** Hard, sound, well-burnt bricks for ordinary use.
*   **Hard Stocks:** Over-burnt bricks used for footings.
*   **Shippers:** Hard-burnt bricks of imperfect shape, often used as ship ballast.
*   **Rubbers/Cutters:** Sandy bricks suitable for wire sawing and rubbing.
*   **Grizzles:** Under-burnt bricks of fair shape for inferior work.
*   **Place-bricks:** Defective, under-burnt bricks for temporary work.
*   **Chuffs:** Badly burnt, cracked bricks of defective shape.
*   **Burrs:** Vitrified lumps used for garden work or rough walling.
*   **Pressed bricks:** Moulded under hydraulic pressure for facing work, often featuring a "frog" (hollow) to reduce weight and key the mortar.
