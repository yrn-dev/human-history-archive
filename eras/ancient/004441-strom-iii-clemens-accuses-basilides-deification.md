# Basilides

Basilides was a prominent exponent of Gnosticism who lived in Alexandria, likely during the first few decades of the 2nd century. While Eusebius, in his *Chronicle*, dates his first appearance to A.D. 133, other evidence suggests his activity may have begun earlier; Agrippa Castor, who lived under the reign of Hadrian (117-138), wrote a polemic against him.

## Literary Works and Sources
Basilides authored an exegetical work consisting of twenty-four books based on a gospel of his own, though the specific identity of this gospel remains unknown. Other related texts include the *Ethika*, *Exegetika* on the prophet Parchor, and writings by his son, Isidorus, titled *Peri prosphuous psuches*. Fragments of these works have been collected and commented upon by Hilgenfeld, including a significant portion of the 13th book of the *Exegetika* found in the *Acta Archelai et Manetis*.

Knowledge of Basilides' system is derived from several early church sources. Irenaeus provided a sketch of his school of thought, which may have been based on Justin's *Syntagma*. This account is closely related to the *Syntagma* of Hippolytus, preserved in the works of Philaster, Epiphanius, and Pseudo-Tertullian. Additional notices are found in the *Stromateis* of Clemens Alexandrinus. A different account with a monistic tendency appears in the *Philosophumena* of Hippolytus, though recent scholarship tends to favor the accounts of Irenaeus and the *Syntagma* over the *Philosophumena*.

## Cosmological System
The system of Basilides involves a supreme God, referred to as the *innatus pater*. From this supreme God, 365 heavens and their corresponding spirits emanated. This number corresponds to the astronomical conception of the heavens' 365 daily aspects. Consequently, the supreme God is called Abraxas (or Abrasax), a name with a numerical value of 365. This naming convention is noted to be similar to the numerical value associated with the Persian god Mithras.

The *Pater innatus* is surrounded by a series of Hypostases. According to Irenaeus, these include *Nous*, *Logos*, *Phronesis*, *Sophia*, and *Dunamis*; Clemens Alexandrinus suggests that *Dikaiosune* and *Eirene* may also be included. Furthermore, the world originates from seven powers, which are viewed as the lowest emanations of the supreme God. These seven world-creating angels are considered an oriental speculation, likely derived from the planetary deities of later Babylonian religion, which were later degraded to daemonic forces under the influence of Iranian monotheism.

## Soteriology and the Saviour
Salvation in the system of Basilides is introduced to address the extreme confusion within the world. To remedy this, the supreme God sends his *Nous* into the world, who is also called Christ. Clemens Alexandrinus refers to this Saviour as *diakonos* or *pneuma diakonoumenon*. 

The relationship between this Saviour and the historical Jesus of Nazareth is a point of complexity. Basilides recognized an earthly Jesus but denied the principle of his sinlessness. Irenaeus reports that the Saviour appeared only as a phantasm, a view that aligns Basilides with the Docetæ—a group of Gnostics who taught that Christ possessed only a "seeming" body rather than a material one. Conversely, the *Excerpta ex Theodoto* suggests that the *Diakonos* descended upon Jesus in the form of a dove during his baptism. Because of this belief, followers of Basilides observed the day of the baptism of Jesus, known as the day of the *epiphaneia*, as a high festival.

The process of redemption is described by Clemens as the "great separation of the sexes," which marks the restoration and fulfillment of all things. In this framework, salvation consists of separating that which was combined for evil.
