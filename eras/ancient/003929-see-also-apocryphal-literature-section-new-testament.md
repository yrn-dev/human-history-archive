# Apocryphal Literature of the New Testament

The literature surrounding the New Testament includes a broad fringe of writings that approximate the canonical texts in character. This body of work consists of various categories, including the writings of the Sub-Apostolic Fathers, apocryphal gospels, acts, and epistles.

## The Apostolic and Sub-Apostolic Fathers
The writings most closely aligned with the New Testament are those of the so-called Apostolic Fathers, though they are more accurately described as Sub-Apostolic. These texts were generally produced by leading figures in the Church who continued the traditions of the apostles. Notable examples include:
*   The *Didache*
*   The letters of Ignatius
*   The single letter of Polycarp
*   The *Shepherd* of Hermas
*   Clement of Rome's letter to the Corinthians
*   The homily known as the Second Epistle of Clement

While most of these authors were persons of note, some writings, such as the *Epistle of Barnabas* and 2 Clement, are considered more eccentric.

## Apocryphal Gospels and Acts
Outside the group of the Sub-Apostolic Fathers are the Apocryphal Gospels and Acts. The archive identifies several such works:
*   **Gospels:** The Gospel according to the Hebrews, the Gospel according to the Egyptians, the Gospel of Peter, the Gospel of Truth, the Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel of Nicodemus, the Protevangelium of James, and the Ebionite Gospel (also known as the Gospel of the Twelve). Additionally, a recently recovered work known as the *Logia* is included in this category.
*   **Acts and Apocalypses:** The Acts of Paul, Peter, John, Andrew, and Thomas; the Acts of Pilate; the Preaching of Peter; and the Apocalypse of Peter.

Certain works have been attributed to Mark beyond his Gospel, including the apocryphal *Acta Barnabae*. Other New Testament books, such as the Apocalypse, the Epistle of Jude, and the Epistle to the Hebrews, have also been attributed to him by various scholars.

## Apocryphal Epistles
Certain epistles existed outside the formal canon but held significance in early Christian communities. 

### The Epistle of Barnabas
The *Epistle of Barnabas* is an apocryphal book of the New Testament that once enjoyed quasi-canonical authority, particularly in Alexandria. It appears as an appendix to the New Testament in the 4th-century *Codex Sinaiticus*, where it is followed by the *Shepherd of Hermas*. In an 11th-century manuscript containing the *Didache*, it is followed by two writings that serve as an appendix in the *Codex Alexandrinus*. 

While Clement ascribed the work to "the prophet" or "the apostle" Barnabas (referring to Paul's fellow-apostle), and Origen cited it in the spirit of scripture, internal evidence suggests this ascription is impossible. The text itself makes no claim to such authorship.

### The Corinthian Correspondence
There exists an apocryphal correspondence between Paul and the church at Corinth, comprising a letter from the church and a reply from Paul. This correspondence relates to the condemnation of specific Gnostic views. In the 4th century, it held canonical authority within the Syrian church. The text is preserved in Latin and Armenian manuscripts and was originally part of the *Acts of Paul*, a 2nd-century work.

## Textual Criticism and Historical Context
The study of these texts often involves textual criticism, the process of reconstructing an original text from manuscripts, versions, and quotations in early writers. The goal is to remove corruptions through the grouping of authorities and the reconstruction of archetypes.

The development of the New Testament literature was followed by a period of controversial or philosophical works written by figures such as Quadratus, Aristides, Castor, and Agrippa. By the middle of the 2nd century, the appearance of Justin Martyr marked the end of the twilight period succeeding the apostolic age and the beginning of the main course of ecclesiastical history.
