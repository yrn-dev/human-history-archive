# The Nineveh Hypothesis: Shifting the Geography of the Hanging Gardens

While traditional narratives attribute the Hanging Gardens to King Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon, the archaeological record presents a frustrating silence. Despite extensive excavations of the Babylonian site, no definitive structural evidence of a tiered botanical paradise has ever been found. This discrepancy has led modern historians to pivot their focus: what if the "Hanging Gardens" were not in Babylon at all, but were instead located hundreds of miles north in Nineveh?

This perspective shifts the gardens from a romanticized Babylonian myth to a tangible Assyrian achievement, suggesting that the ancient world's most famous garden was a victim of historical misidentification.

## The Case for Nineveh
The theory, championed most notably by Dr. Stephanie Dalley of Oxford University, posits that the gardens were actually constructed by the Assyrian King Sennacherib around 700 BCE. Unlike the vague accounts provided by Greek historians, Assyrian records are meticulously detailed. Sennacherib’s own annals describe the construction of an unprecedented palace complex at Nineveh, featuring a vast botanical garden that mirrored the mountains of Assyria.

The evidence is not merely textual but engineering-based. To sustain such a lush environment in the arid climate of Upper Mesopotamia, Sennacherib engineered a sophisticated system of canals and aqueducts. The discovery of a massive stone aqueduct at Jerwan, which transported water from the mountains to Nineveh, provides the physical proof of the hydraulic capability required to maintain "hanging" terraces—a feat far more documented in Nineveh than in Babylon.

## The Source of the Confusion
If the gardens existed in Nineveh, why does history credit Babylon? Historians suggest a combination of linguistic drift and cultural conflation. To the later Greeks, who wrote the primary accounts of the gardens, "Babylonia" was often used as a general term for the entire region of Mesopotamia. Furthermore, the architectural styles of the Neo-Assyrian and Neo-Babylonian empires were closely linked, making it easy for later chroniclers to attribute the wonders of the north to the more famous city of the south.

While some scholars still argue that the gardens were a poetic invention—a "literary trope" used by Greeks to describe the exoticism of the East—the Nineveh hypothesis provides a grounded, archaeological alternative to the myth.

## Key Facts
* **Traditional Attribution:** King Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon (c. 605–562 BCE).
* **Alternative Attribution:** King Sennacherib of Assyria (c. 705–681 BCE).
* **Engineering:** Required advanced irrigation, likely using Archimedes-style screws or heavy-duty aqueducts.
* **Location Debate:** The conflict centers on whether the gardens were in Babylon (Southern Iraq) or Nineveh (Northern Iraq).
* **Primary Sources:** Greek accounts (Strabo, Diodorus Siculus) vs. Assyrian royal inscriptions.

## Did You Know?
* **The Missing Record:** There is not a single mention of "Hanging Gardens" in the native Babylonian texts of Nebuchadnezzar’s reign.
* **Botanical Diversity:** Sennacherib claimed to have brought exotic plants from all over the known world to his gardens.
* **The Jerwan Aqueduct:** This ancient structure is one of the oldest known aqueducts in the world, predating Roman engineering.
