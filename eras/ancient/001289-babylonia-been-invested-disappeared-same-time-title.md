# Babylonia

Babylonia was an ancient civilization located on a low alluvial plain known as Edin. This region, characterized by flat and fertile alluvium, began after passing Pylæ—a gate or pass marking the transition from the undulating desert country to the north. The geography of the region included the "bank" or *kisad* on the western bank of the river, and the coast-land known as *Gu-abba* (the "bank of the sea"). Historically, the region was divided between the lowlands of Kengi (or Kengi Sumer) and the highlands of northern Babylonia, originally called *Urra* but later known as Akkad.

## Early History and Semitic Influence
The earliest records of the region are Sumerian and appear to date before 4000 B.C. Following this period, Babylonia was invaded by Semites, who became the ruling race. These newcomers borrowed and adapted the cuneiform system of writing, and the resulting Semitic Babylonian language was profoundly influenced by the older language of the country. Conversely, Sumerian borrowed from Semitic Babylonian.

The first Semitic empire was established in the north at Akkad. Semitic settlers spread from Susa to Sippara, while "West Semitic" tribes occupied Khana to the east of the Tigris. The city of Babylon emerged as a metropolis around 2285 B.C. under Khammurabi.

## Governance and the Concept of Kingship
In the early states of Babylonia, the *patesi* (high-priest of the god) preceded the king. The state grew around a sanctuary where the god was the nominal ruler and the human *patesi* served as a viceregent. Over time, many high-priests assumed the title and functions of king, claiming supreme secular power while remaining priests.

When Semitic princes unified Babylonia, the earthly king became an incarnate god. This divine status remained until the Kassite conquest. At Babylon, a tradition survived where the god Bel-Merodach adopted the king before his right to rule was officially allowed.

## The First Dynasty and the Code of Laws
The "First Dynasty of Babylon," as termed by native historians, produced one of its most significant works under the order of Khammurabi: a code of laws. This compilation followed the settlement of his kingdom and the expulsion of the Elamites. Fragments of this Code, recovered from the library of Assur-bani-pal at Nineveh, show it was studied and recopied for over fifteen hundred years. The Code influenced later Mahommedan law in Mesopotamia and remained largely in force through Greek, Persian, and Parthian conquests.

The last king of this dynasty was Samsu-ditana, son of Ammi-zadok. He was succeeded by a dynasty of 11 Sumerian kings who reportedly reigned for 368 years, though this number is considered exaggerated.

## The Kassite Period and Later Transitions
The Sumerian kings were overthrown by the Kassites (or Kossaeans) from the mountains of Elam. The Kassite dynasty was founded around 1780 B.C. by Kandis, Gandis, or Gaddas, and lasted for 576-3/4 years. 

Under Kassite dominion, Babylonia experienced significant changes:
* **Loss of Empire:** Babylonia lost its empire over western Asia; Palestine and Syria became independent.
* **Rise of Assyria:** The high-priests of Assur established themselves as kings of Assyria.
* **Loss of Divine Status:** The divine attributes previously invested in the Semitic kings disappeared; Kassite sovereigns were never given the title of "god."

Despite these losses, Babylon remained the holy city of western Asia and the capital of the kingdom. Northern Babylonia became known as Kar-Duniyas ("the wall of the god Duniyas").

## Relation to Assyria and Persia
Assyria began as an offshoot of Babylonia to the north-west. While sharing the same language, Assyrians maintained a distinct individuality in religion and art. By the 9th and 8th centuries B.C., Assyria became the chief power and the suzerain of Babylon. However, Assyria disappeared following the destruction of Nineveh during the advance of Medo-Persian power in 606 B.C. Babylon survived longer, eventually being taken by Cyrus in 555 B.C. Even after the Persian conquest, Babylon continued to influence religion and culture for many centuries.

## Religion and Culture
In Babylonia, the deity Baal became a definite individual deity identified with the planet Jupiter. Through the influence of the Aramaeans, who used the spelling Bel, this deity eventually became known to the Greeks as Belos and was identified with Zeus.
