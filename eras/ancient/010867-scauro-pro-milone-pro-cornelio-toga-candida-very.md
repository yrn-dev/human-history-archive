# Asconius Pedianus and the Commentaries on Cicero

## Overview of Asconius Pedianus
Quintus Asconius Pedianus was a Roman historian and grammarian who lived from approximately 9 B.C. to A.D. 76 (or A.D. 3-88). Likely a native of Patavium (Padua), he spent his later years residing in Rome. Asconius died at the age of eighty-five, having been blind for the twelve years preceding his death.

## Historical Commentaries on Cicero
During the reigns of Nero and Claudius, Asconius compiled historical commentaries on the speeches of Cicero for his sons. To create these works, he drew from a wide array of sources, including:
*   Shorthand reports or "skeletons" (*commentarii*) of speeches by Cicero that remained unpublished.
*   The life of Cicero written by Tiro.
*   Letters and speeches from contemporaries of Cicero.
*   The *Acta Publica* (Gazette).
*   Various historical writers, such as Antias, Atticus, Varro, Tuditanus, and Fenestella; Asconius frequently criticized Fenestella, who was a contemporary of Livy.

Of these commentaries, only five have been preserved, though they exist in a very mutilated condition. These include the commentaries on the speeches *pro Scauro*, *pro Milone*, *pro Cornelio*, *in toga Candida*, and *in Pisonem*. These notes are written in good Latin and focus primarily on antiquarian, historical, and legal matters.

## Dating and Authenticity
The dating of Asconius's work is supported by a note in the speech *pro Scauro*. In this note, Asconius refers to Longus Caecina (who died in A.D. 57) as still living, while his phrasing suggests that Claudius (who died in A.D. 54) was no longer alive. Consequently, this specific statement must have been written between A.D. 54 and A.D. 57.

While his historical commentaries are valued, a separate commentary on Cicero's Verrine orations is universally regarded as spurious due to its grammatical character and inferior Latinity.

## Preservation of the Texts
The works of Asconius were rediscovered in 1416 by Poggio, who found them in a manuscript at St Gallen. Although the original manuscript was lost, three transcripts were produced:
*   **Poggio's transcript:** Currently located in Madrid (Matritensis x. 81).
*   **Zomini's (Sozomenus of Pistoia) transcript:** Located in the Forteguerri library at Pistoia (No. 37).
*   **Bartolommeo da Montpulciano's transcript:** A copy exists in Florence (Laur. liv. 5).

Subsequent manuscripts of Asconius's work are derived from the copy made by Poggio.

## Account of the Trial of Scaurus
In his commentary on the speech *pro Scauro*, Asconius provides a detailed account of the supplications made to the *judices* (judges) in Rome as a sentence was about to be pronounced upon Scaurus, who was defended by Cicero. 

Asconius records that nine consulares praised Scaurus, many of whom did so via *tabellas* because they were absent, including Pompeius. Additionally, Scaurus's brother, Faustus Cornelius (son of Sylla), delivered a humble and tearful laudation that moved the listeners as much as Scaurus's own words had. 

When the sentences were being delivered, those pleading for Scaurus divided into two groups to kneel at the judges' knees:
*   **First group:** Scaurus himself, P. Lentulus, Paulus, M. Glabrio (his sister's son), C. Memmius, and L. Æmilius Buca.
*   **Second group:** T. Annius Milo, Sylla Faustus (Scaurus's brother), C. Cato, T. Peducæus, and M. Octavius Lænas.

## Other Attributed Works
Beyond the commentaries on Cicero, other works have been attributed to Asconius, including a treatise on health and long life (possibly a symposium imitating Plato), a defense of Virgil against detractors, and a life of Sallust.
