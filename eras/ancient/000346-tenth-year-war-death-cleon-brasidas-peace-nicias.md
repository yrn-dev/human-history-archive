# Tenth Year of the War—Death of Cleon and Brasidas—Peace of Nicias

The tenth year of the Peloponnesian War was characterized by the transition from intense conflict in Thrace to a period of diplomatic negotiation, culminating in the Peace of Nicias. This shift was precipitated by the deaths of the two primary opponents of peace: the Athenian leader Cleon and the Lacedaemonian general Brasidas.

## Conflict in Thrace and the Fall of Torone
Following a one-year truce that lasted until the Pythian games, the Athenians expelled the Delians from Delos, believing the island had been polluted by an old offense. The Delians subsequently settled at Atramyttium in Asia, granted to them by Pharnaces.

In Thrace, hostilities continued despite the general armistice. Cleon persuaded the Athenians to allow him to sail toward Thrace with thirty ships, twelve hundred heavy infantry, three hundred horse, a large force of allies, and additional troops including Lemnians, Imbrians, targeteers from Aenus, and four hundred archers. Cleon first visited the besieged city of Scione before advancing on Torone. 

At Torone, Cleon attacked a fortification Brasidas had constructed to incorporate a suburb into the city. The Lacedaemonian commander, Pasitelidas, attempted to repel the assault but retreated into the town when Athenian ships entered the harbor. The Athenians captured the city, killing some Peloponnesians and Toronaeans and taking the rest, including Pasitelidas, prisoner. Cleon enslaved the wives and children of the Toronaeans and sent seven hundred men—including Peloponnesians and Chalcidians—to Athens.

## The Campaign for Amphipolis
After his success at Torone, Cleon established a base at Eion. He launched an unsuccessful assault on the Andrian colony of Stagirus but successfully took Galepsus, a colony of Thasos, by storm. Cleon then awaited reinforcements from the alliance, specifically an army from Perdiccas and Thracian mercenaries from Polles, king of the Odomantians.

Brasidas monitored Cleon's movements from Cerdylium, a high-ground position in the Argilian country that provided a comprehensive view of the surrounding area. Brasidas assembled a significant force, including fifteen hundred Thracian mercenaries, the Edonians (horse and targeteers), one thousand Myrcinian and Chalcidian targeteers, and approximately two thousand heavy infantry and three hundred Hellenic horse. While fifteen hundred of these troops remained with Brasidas at Cerdylium, the remainder were stationed in Amphipolis under the command of Clearidas, son of Cleonymus.

## The Death of Cleon and Brasidas
The Athenian defeat at Amphipolis resulted in the deaths of both Cleon and Brasidas. These two men had been the principal obstacles to peace in their respective cities. Brasidas had opposed peace due to the honor and success he gained through warfare. Cleon opposed it because he believed that a restoration of tranquility would make his slanders less credited and his crimes more susceptible to detection.

Prior to the final expedition, Cleon had faced criticism from the Athenians for his hesitation to sail. In response, Nicias—the general at the time—offered to resign his command against Pylos to allow Cleon to take whatever force he wished. Although Cleon initially hesitated, the Athenian multitude pressured him to accept the command. Cleon eventually undertook the expedition, boasting that he would either kill the Lacedaemonians or bring them back alive within twenty days.

## The Peace of Nicias
With the deaths of Cleon and Brasidas, the path to peace was opened by two foremost candidates for power: Nicias, son of Niceratus, and Pleistoanax, son of Pausanias, king of Lacedaemon.

Nicias sought peace to secure his existing good fortune and reputation as a successful statesman. He believed that peace was the only way to avoid danger and minimize the influence of fortune on his future. Pleistoanax was motivated by a desire to silence his political enemies. He had been banished to Lycaeum on suspicion of being bribed to retreat from Attica and had only been restored after nineteen years of exile. His enemies accused him and his brother Aristocles of bribing the prophetess of Delphi to secure his return. Pleistoanax believed that in a state of peace, no disasters could occur that his enemies could use to scandalize his high station.

## Earlier Tensions and the Scione Dispute
The road to the Peace of Nicias had been complicated by disputes over the city of Scione. Following a truce sworn in March 423 B.C., Scione revolted from Athens to Brasidas two days after the convention. While the Lacedaemonians claimed the revolt happened before the truce, the Athenians maintained it occurred after. This dispute led the Athenians to decree that the Scionaeans be reduced and put to death, further delaying a definitive treaty.

Additionally, the Lacedaemonians had begun placing their own young men in command of towns to prevent local officials from taking control, such as placing Clearidas in Amphipolis and Pasitelidas in Torone.
