# Brickmaking in Antiquity and Later Periods

## Ancient Greek and Roman Brickwork
Pliny mentions that the Greeks produced three different kinds of bricks. However, there is no evidence to suggest these were used on a large scale. The most significant brick structures in ancient Greece were likely the walls of Athens on the side facing Mount Hymettus.

The Romans eventually became masters of the art of brickmaking. While they likely acquired much of this knowledge in the East during their occupation of Greece and Egypt, they revived and expanded the manufacture of bricks around the beginning of the Christian era. The Romans introduced the method of burning bricks in kilns and exercised great care in the preparation and selection of their clay. These methods and knowledge were spread throughout western Europe, with abundant evidence of extensive brick production in Britain and Germany.

## Floating Bricks
A specific variety known as "floating bricks" was first mentioned by the Greek geographer Strabo and later by Pliny. These bricks were produced at Pitane in the Troad. The secret of how to manufacture them was lost for many centuries until 1791, when an Italian named Fabroni rediscovered the process. Fabroni produced them using diatomaceous earth (fossil meal) found in Tuscany. Floating bricks are characterized by being very light and fairly strong. Because they are poor conductors of heat, they have been used for constructing powder-magazines on ships.

## General Brickmaking Processes
Bricks made of tempered clay can be produced by machine or by hand. Machines may be powered mechanically or by hand. Bricks made from semi-plastic clay—ground shale or clay damp enough to adhere under pressure—are generally machine-made.

The hand-making process is consistent worldwide with minor variations. Tempered clay is pressed into a four-sided metal or wooden mould of a specific size and shape, allowing for shrinkage during firing and drying. To prevent the clay from sticking, the moulder dips the mould in water or a mixture of water and sand. The clay is dashed into the mould, pressed into the corners by hand, and the top is leveled using a strip of wood called a "strike." The brick is then turned out onto a board for drying. If a "frog" (a hollow) is required in the bottom of the brick, the mould is placed on a stock-board featuring an elevated tongue of wood in the center.

## Bonding and Construction Techniques
Various bonds are used when laying bricks to ensure strength and appearance:
*   **Flemish and English Bonds:** A "single Flemish bond" is a compromise where bricks are laid as Flemish bond on the face for appearance and English bond for the backing to combine the benefits of both and avoid using bats in the interior.
*   **Stretching Bond:** This is used only for walls that are half a brick thick, with all bricks laid as stretchers and a half brick used in alternate courses to start the bond.
*   **Heading Bond:** Used for footings, projecting corbels, mouldings, and work with sharp curves on a plan. In this bond, bricks are laid as headers, meaning their length is across the thickness of the wall and their ends face forward.
*   **Herring Bone (Diagonal Bond):** In walls three bricks thick or more, this bond is used in the interior to save labor without sacrificing strength, while the outer faces remain in English or Flemish bond. This method is not recommended for walls thinner than 27 inches.

## Brickwork in Religious Architecture
In the modified Byzantine style, Orthodox Greek churches are generally built of brick and feature very narrow windows. These structures are typically surmounted by two or three towers. In Bucharest, the metropolitan church (the cathedral), built between 1656 and 1665, is shaped like a Greek cross and culminates in three brick towers.
