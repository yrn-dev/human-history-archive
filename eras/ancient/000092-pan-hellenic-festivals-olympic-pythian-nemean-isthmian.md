# PAN-HELLENIC FESTIVALS — OLYMPIC, PYTHIAN, NEMEAN, AND ISTHMIAN

The Pan-Hellenic festivals were great religious celebrations in ancient Greece that combined religious solemnities with recreative amusement and hearty sympathies. While these festivals eventually acquired a Pan-Hellenic character—meaning they were frequented by crowds from every part of Greece—they began as purely local events that attracted visitors only from narrow neighborhoods. These institutions served as a bond of union for the Greeks, fostering religious, intellectual, and social cohesion.

## The Olympic Games
The Olympic games were the oldest and most conspicuous of the four major festivals, and the Olympic prize was considered the highest of all. They provided historical computers with the oldest backward record of continuous time, beginning in 776 B.C. In that year, the Eleians inscribed the name of their countryman, Korœbus, as the victor in the running competition. This practice of inscribing the winner's name continued every fifth recurring year (the Olympic cycle).

Despite their eventual celebrity, the games remained a local festival for a significant period; during the first twelve Olympiads, the prizes were uniformly won by competitors from Elis or its immediate vicinity. The Olympic games were the first of the festivals to transition from a local to a Pan-Hellenic character.

## The Pythian Games
The Pythian games were celebrated near Delphi and were under the superintendence of the Amphiktyons or a chosen acting magistrate. In its earliest, humble form, the festival consisted of a competition among bards singing hymns in praise of Apollo. While this early form was of immemorial antiquity, the festival only achieved Pan-Hellenic notoriety, expanded its competitions, and began a continuous record of conquerors after the First Sacred War in 595 B.C.

The First Sacred War resulted in the destruction of Kirrha, the seaport of the Phocian town of Krissa, following the insolence of the Kirrhæans. The Amphiktyons founded the Pythian games following this conflict. The first Pythian contest is dated to 585 B.C. (the third year of the 48th Olympiad). Like the Olympic games, the Pythian games occurred every four years, an interval the Greeks called a *Pentaetêris*.

## The Nemean and Isthmian Games
The Nemean and Isthmian games became notorious and frequented later than the Olympic and Pythian festivals. Unlike the four-year cycle of the others, these two festivals recurred every two years. The Nemean games took place in the second and fourth years of each Olympiad, while the Isthmian games occurred in the first and third.

**The Nemean Games** were celebrated in honor of the Nemean Zeus in the valley of Nemea, located between Kleônæ and Phlius. Originally administered by the Kleônæans, the honor of administration was taken over by the Argeians after 460 B.C. The first historical Nemean festival is placed in Olympiad 52 or 53 (572–568 B.C.). Like the Olympic games, the Nemean games employed *Hellanodikæ* to maintain order, superintend the events, and distribute prizes.

**The Isthmian Games** were celebrated by the Corinthians at their isthmus in honor of Poseidôn. Historical information regarding these games dates to at least 594 B.C., as the Athenian legislator Solôn provided rewards for citizens who won prizes there. Legends regarding the foundation of the Isthmian games often link them to Theseus, who is said to have established them as funeral games for Skeirôn or after a victory over Sinis. Because of these legends, Athenians identified the festival with their own state's antiquities.

## Social and Political Impact
The festivals played a critical role in the Greek mind and the development of inter-city relations. Most Greek cities encouraged their citizens to visit these festivals, with the notable exception of Sparta. While Sparta was peculiarly connected to the Olympic games from an early period, there is little evidence that they interested themselves in the other three national festivals.

The prestige of these games was reflected in civic rewards. Solôn, for example, proclaimed a reward of five hundred drachms for an Olympic prize, viewing it as an ornament to the city of the victor due to its Pan-Hellenic rank. In contrast, he offered a lower sum of one hundred drachms for an Isthmian prize, which he characterized as partial and confined to the neighborhood.
