# DEUKALION, HELLEN, AND SONS OF HELLEN

## Deukaliôn and the General Deluge
Deukaliôn is a central figure in Grecian mythical narrative, primarily recognized as the individual specially saved during a general deluge. According to the Hesiodic Catalogue of Women, Deukaliôn was the son of Promêtheus and Pandôra. His wife, Pyrrha, is described in the same source as the daughter of Epimêtheus.

The location of Deukaliôn's activities is subject to varying accounts. Aristotle asserted that the deluge took place chiefly in the most ancient district called Hellas, located near the river Achelôus and Dôdôna, an area then inhabited by the Selli (or Helli) and people then called Græci. However, the general feeling among the Greeks connected Deukaliôn and his descendants primarily with Achaia Phthiôtis, situated between Mount Œta and Mount Othrys. Deukaliôn's permanent seat was in Phthiôtis, and he is recorded as ruling in that portion of Thessaly.

Regarding his arrival in Thessaly, one legendary account suggests the deluge transferred him there. An alternative, more historicized version claims he led a body of Leleges and Kurêtes into Thessaly, where he expelled the prior Pelasgian occupants. The lineage of the Deukalidæ continued to reign in Phthia until the time of Dikæarchus.

## Hellên and the Origin of the Hellenes
Deukaliôn and Pyrrha had two sons, Hellên and Amphiktyôn, and a daughter, Prôtogeneia. While the more current story identifies Hellên as the son of Deukaliôn, other statements maintain that Hellên was actually the son of Zeus.

Hellên is regarded as the great eponym of the Hellenic race. He gave the name "Hellenes" to those who had previously been called Greeks. According to the statement of Apollodôrus, Hellên partitioned his territory among his three children, who were born to him by a nymph. These sons—Dôrus, Xuthus, and Æolus—became the eponyms for the major divisions of the Greek people.

## The Sons of Hellên and Territorial Division
The three sons of Hellên divided the land and gave their names to the inhabitants of their respective regions:

*   **Æolus:** He reigned in Thessaly, and his descendants were known as the Æolians.
*   **Dôrus:** He occupied the country on the northern side of the Corinthian Gulf, opposite the Peloponnêsus. His descendants were the Dôrians.
*   **Xuthus:** He received the Peloponnêsus. Xuthus and his wife Creüsa had two sons, Achæus and Iôn. These descendants became the Achæans and the Iônians.

The genealogy that establishes Æolus, Xuthus, and Dôrus as the sons of Hellên is considered as old as the Hesiodic Catalogue. This genealogical structure is noted to harmonize with the historical establishments of the Dôrians and implies a large extent for the region of Dôris.

## Related Lineages and Discrepancies
The archive notes several genealogical diversities regarding these figures. For instance, while Apollodôrus and Pherekydês call Prôtogeneia the daughter of Deukaliôn, Pindar designates her as the daughter of Opus. To resolve this, some suggested that Deukaliôn was "dionymous," meaning he possessed two names, one of which was Opus.

Prôtogeneia's son by Zeus was Aëthlius. Aëthlius married Calycê, a daughter of Æolus, and led a colony from Thessaly to settle in the territory of Elis. Their son, Endymiôn, is the subject of various legends; some claim Zeus granted him the ability to determine the hour of his own death, while others say the goddess Sêlêne became enamored of his beauty. Endymiôn's sons—Epeios, Ætôlus, and Pæôn—competed in a race at Olympia, where Epeios emerged victorious and became the successor to the kingdom, giving his name to the Epeians.

Regarding Achæus, different traditions exist. While some view him as the son of Xuthus, Dionysius of Halicarnassus identifies him as a son of Poseidôn and Larissa. In that version, Achæus, Phthius, and Pelasgus migrated from the Peloponnêsus to Thessaly and distributed the territory among themselves before being driven out by Deukaliôn. Other accounts by Pausanias suggest Achæus returned to Thessaly to occupy the land his father was entitled to, and that his descendants, Archander and Architelês, later brought the name "Achæans" to Argos and Sparta after marrying daughters of Danaus.
