# Early Tendencies and Practices of the Christian Church

## Asceticism and Social Integration
From its earliest age, the Christian Church experienced a leaning toward excessive asceticism. This tendency was particularly strong among early enthusiasts who believed in a speedy advent of the millennium. These individuals condemned the permanent institutions of society—including the ownership of property, the assumption of public office, and engagement in commerce and war—viewing them as impediments to the life of grace and alien to the kingdom of God.

Securing the rights of the baptized to participate in these societal norms, including the right to be married, required a severe struggle led by Paul and the Catholic teachers who followed him. Similar rigorous asceticism was found among the disciples of Marcion. Between 150 and 250, the Marcionite churches flourished, maintaining a rejection of the Old Testament and the recognition of a "new God." While they lived in close communities ruled by presbyters and bishops, their original constitution was loose to avoid the appearance of "legality."

## Rituals and the Use of Incense
The use of incense in the Christian Church has been a subject of historical debate. While some conclusions suggest it was unknown before the 5th century, evidence indicates earlier usage. In the first Christian services held in the catacombs of Rome, incense was used at least as a sanitary fumigation. Tertullian noted that the Sabaeans testified that costly merchandise was lavished on Christian burials in the form of aromatics.

The ceremonial use of incense is supported by biblical and early references: the Persian Magi offered frankincense and myrrh to the infant Jesus, and the Book of Revelation describes the offering of incense with the prayers of saints before the throne of God. Additionally, a passage by Ambrose of Milan may testify to the practice of "incensing the altars" by the 4th century. However, the first express mention of Christian priests censing the altar appears in works attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite, first quoted in the 6th century.

## Prayer and Liturgical Posture
The posture used in solemn prayer in the early church involved stretching the hands and eyes toward heaven. This practice is linked to the account of Moses, whose Israelites prevailed while his hands were lifted toward heaven. This posture continued in the Christian church until a later practice was introduced under a "corrupt state of the church," wherein clergy began reading prayers from a book rather than learning them by heart, a change that was inconsistent with the elevated posture. Despite this shift, the use of divine forms of thanksgiving, praise, and prayer remained a constant practice for Christians, Jews, and patriarchs across past ages.

## Early Administration and Architecture
In the early church, the sacrament of baptism was administered in a separate hall or chapel known as the baptistery (or *photisterion* in the Greek Church). These were commonly circular buildings, though some had twelve or eight sides. They featured an ante-room for the instruction of catechumens and a confession of faith, and an inner apartment containing a baptismal font (*piscina* or *kolumbethra*). 

In these fonts, which were initially stone, candidates were immersed thrice. The fonts were often adorned with a suspended gold or silver dove and wall pictures depicting the life of John the Baptist. Because the bishop typically baptized all catechumens in his diocese only thrice a year, these buildings were often large enough to host synods and councils. Baptisteries are generally found attached to cathedrals rather than parish churches. There is little trace of these structures before the 4th century, when Constantine made Christianity the state religion; by the 6th century, fonts were moved into the porch or the main body of the church.

## Early Writings and Theological Challenges
The first Christian writings were not the products of theologians seeking to create a sacred volume, but arose from practical needs. St. Paul's two epistles to the Thessalonians, the oldest possessed Christian documents, served as substitutes for his personal presence to provide advice to his converts and calm excited expectations.

The early church also struggled with the relationship between Christianity and Judaism, specifically the relation between the Old and New forms of a Covenant. This problem was a central theme in the earliest writings of the Apostolic Fathers.

## Relation to the Roman State
During the reign of Aurelius, Christianity faced systematic persecution. Aurelius, a Salian priest from age eight, identified the ritual of the Roman religion with the imperial idea and viewed the prevalence of Christianity as incompatible with Roman prosperity. While previous emperors like Trajan, Hadrian, and Antoninus did not pursue Christians, Aurelius's administration regarded Christianity as sacrilege. Some historians argue this was due to a rescript issued around A.D. 176, while others suggest it resulted from *mandata* (instructions) given to provincial governors.
