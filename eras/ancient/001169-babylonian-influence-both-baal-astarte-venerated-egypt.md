# Babylonian Influence and the Cults of Baal and Astarte

## Babylonian Influence on the Deity Baal
In Babylonia, Baal developed from a very early period into a definite individual deity. This specific development is regarded as a mark of superior culture and may have been spread through Babylonian influence. Within the Babylonian system, Baal was identified with the planet Jupiter. This influence extended to the Aramaeans, who borrowed the Babylonian spelling "Bel." Through this linguistic transmission, the deity ultimately became known to the Greeks as Belos, who was then identified with Zeus.

## The Cult of Baal in Egypt and Other Regions
The veneration of Baal was not limited to Babylonia. During the XIXth Dynasty, Baal was venerated in Egypt at both Memphis and Thebes. Similarly, a specific Baal of the heavens was known among the Hittites during the time of Rameses II. By the beginning of the 7th century, Baal served as the title for one of the gods of Phoenicia.

In the broader Semitic world, Baal was often associated with specific geography, leading to frequent compounds such as the Baal of Tyre or the Baal of Lebanon. Other specialized forms included a Baal of healing in Cyprus and the [Greek: Balmarkos] near Beirut, who apparently presided over dancing. In many regions, the Baal was viewed as the "Lord" of that specific area or as the husband of the land and its worshippers.

## Nature and Attributes of Baal
Baal is characterized as the head of worshipping groups and the source of all gifts of nature. As a god of fertility, he is the patron of all growth, and the produce of the soil is considered his; consequently, adherents brought him tributes of first-fruits. This system of worship, known as baalism, originated from observing the fertilizing effects of streams and rains on reproductive soil.

Because of primitive notions of analogy and "sympathetic magic"—the belief that nature's powers could be aided or controlled—the cult of the baals was characterized by licentiousness and gross sensuality. Practices denounced by Israelite prophets included the burning of incense, human sacrifice, the preparation of sacred mystic cakes, and violent, ecstatic exercises. Some cults, such as that of Baal Peor, exemplified Dionysiac orgies, with licentiousness practiced "under every green tree" and on mountain summits to secure crop abundance.

## Astarte: The Female Principle
Astarte is a Semitic goddess, appearing in the Bible as Ashtoreth. She represents the great female principle and serves as the counterpart to the Baal of the Phoenicians and Canaanites, as well as the Dagon of the Philistines. Her worship was widespread, with temples at Tyre and Sidon; from Tyre, her worship was later transplanted to Carthage. In Jerusalem, Solomon built a high-place for her that remained until the days of King Josiah. The extent of her cult among the Israelites is evidenced by biblical references and archaeological representations found on Palestinian soil.

Astarte is associated with various forms and attributes:
* **Nature and Fertility:** As a great nature-goddess, she is linked to reproduction and fertility.
* **Animal Associations:** She has been associated with the doves of Ascalon and the gazelle or antelope. Some theories suggest she was originally a sheep-goddess, noting the term "Astartes of the flocks" and the discovery of a model of Astarte with ram's horns at Gezer.
* **Celestial Associations:** While her star was the planet Venus, some suggest she was a lunar goddess, possibly due to the influence of the Egyptian deities Hathor and Isis.
* **Regional Variations:** In Gabal (Byblus), she was known as the *ba'alath*. In Moab, she was part of a compound deity, Ashtar-Chemosh. The Babylonian and Assyrian prototype, Ishtar, similarly lacks the feminine termination.

## Punic and Carthaginian Developments
In Carthage, the religion was Phoenician in origin. Astarte was part of a trinity of great gods, alongside Eshmun and Baal-Ammon (or Moloch). In Carthage, she was known as Tanit, the virgin goddess of the moon and heavens. Tanit was viewed as the manifestation of Baal (*Tanti-Pene-Baal*), and the two were regarded as associate deities. The symbol of Tanit was the crescent moon, and her temple housed a venerated veil known as the city's palladium.

Over time, Hellenic elements entered the Carthaginian religion. This resulted in Tanit (the Punic Astarte) being approximated to Demeter, leading to Punic coins depicting her crowned with ears of corn.

## Rituals and Representations
The cults of Baal and Astarte often utilized specific physical symbols. Phoenician colonists sometimes carried the cult of their ancestral Baal or Astarte using conical stones or pillars. For example, at Paphos, the original home of Astarte, the goddess was held to be immanent within a cone of stone. While these shapeless cones were the focus of public pilgrimage and adoration, small portrait statues were also produced for private worship.
