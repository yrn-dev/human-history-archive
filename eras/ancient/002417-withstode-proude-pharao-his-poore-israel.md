# Withstode proude Pharao for his poore Israel

The phrase "Withstode proude Pharao for his poore Israel" appears in the context of a play titled *Kynge Johan*. In this work, King John is depicted as a "noble Kynge" and a "faythfull Moses" who stood against a proud Pharaoh on behalf of his "poore Israel." The play utilizes allegorical characters, such as *Ynglonde vidua*, who represents the nation, and Sedwyson, who provides a jocular element. The manuscript of the play was discovered between 1831 and 1838 among corporation papers in Ipswich, a location where the play was likely performed due to references to King John's charitable foundations in that town and its surrounding area. While the manuscript is described as two plays, there is no obvious division beyond the end of the first act. The first part was corrected by Bale, and the latter half is in his handwriting, though his name does not appear in the text; however, Bale lists a play titled *De Joanne Anglorum Rege* in his own works. In the play, King John is also referred to as a Lollard and accused of "heretycall langage" before being poisoned by a monk of Swinestead.

## The Children of Israel in Sacred Texts

The archive's sacred texts detail several covenants made between Allah and the Children of Israel. In one covenant, Allah appointed twelve captains (or chieftains) among them and promised to remit their sins and admit them into gardens with flowing rivers if they established regular prayers, practiced regular charity (also referred to as the poor-due or poor-rate), believed in His messengers, supported them, and offered a "beautiful loan" or "goodly gift" to Allah. Those who disbelieved after this covenant were described as having wandered from the path of rectitude or the right way.

Another covenant required the Children of Israel to worship none but Allah and to treat parents, kindred, orphans, and those in need with kindness. They were commanded to speak fair and kindly to mankind, be steadfast in prayer, and practice regular charity. The texts note that, except for a few, the people turned back and backslid from these commands. Additionally, a covenant was ratified stating that they should not shed blood among themselves nor turn their own people out of their homes.

Regarding dietary laws, the texts state that all food was lawful to the Children of Israel, except for that which Israel had forbidden to himself before the revelation of the Law (also referred to as the Torah or Taurat).

## Historical and Tribal Context

The Children of Israel are also discussed in the context of their settlement and slavery in Egypt. Egyptian records mention the settlement of Semitic peoples in the land of Goshen by Pharaoh Rameses II, noting they were drawn to Egypt due to a want of food. However, the archive notes that there are no Egyptian records regarding the life of Moses, the plagues of Egypt, or a Pharaoh drowning in the Red Sea. Some elements of the Moses narrative, such as being concealed in an ark of bulrushes, share similarities with a Sumerian inscription regarding Sargon I, who was placed in a basket of reeds by his poor mother and abandoned to the river.

Within the tribal structure of Israel, Ephraim was a tribe named after the younger son of Joseph. Joseph's benediction exalted Ephraim over his elder brother, Manasseh; these two divisions were frequently known as the "house of Joseph." Ephraim is described as having a proud and ambitious character and likely exceeded Manasseh in numerical strength, eventually becoming a synonym for the northern kingdom of Israel. The tribe's strength was centered on several famous sites:
*   **Shechem**: A capital and the location of the tribal ancestor's tomb.
*   **Shiloh**: The home of the ark during one period.
*   **Timnath-Serah (or Heres)**: The burial-place of Joshua.
*   **Samaria**: A site whose name eventually extended to the entire district.

## Vision of Life and Afterlife

The sacred texts present a vision of the afterlife based on faith and conduct. Those who believe and perform good works are described as the rightful owners and dwellers of the Garden, where they shall abide. Conversely, those who disbelieve and die as unbelievers will face a painful chastisement; the texts state that even if the earth were full of gold, it would not be accepted as a ransom for them, and they would have no helpers.

The texts further emphasize that righteousness and piety cannot be attained unless an individual gives freely and benevolently from that which they love, a fact of which Allah is aware.
