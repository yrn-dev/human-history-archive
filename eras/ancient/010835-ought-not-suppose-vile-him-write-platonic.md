# Plato and Platonic Thought

The philosophy and theories associated with Plato have exerted a significant influence on Western intellectual history, ranging from the dramatic arts of ancient Greece to the philosophical syntheses of the Alexandrian school and the academic discussions of the medieval period.

## Platonic Theory and the Dramatic Arts
In the realm of Greek drama, there existed a distinct separation between tragedy and comedy, which never actually coalesced. While tragic poets sometimes composed satyr-dramas—works that shared the form and theme of tragedies but utilized different choric dances performed by satyrs—they did not write comedies. Conversely, comic poets wrote neither tragedies nor satyr-dramas. 

Within this context, the Platonic Socrates proposed a theory that a single individual ought to be the best comic poet and the best tragic poet simultaneously. However, this specific theory was never exemplified in actual Greek practice. Later writers developed the "tragi-comedy" or "hilaro-tragedy," which maintained the form of tragedy but included a happy ending and comic elements in its characters.

## Plato and the Sophists
Plato is frequently discussed in relation to the sophists, who were generally defined as philosophers, lettered men, and teachers. Lysias referred to both Plato and Æschinês (a disciple of Sokratês) as sophists. Isokratês also appears to include Plato among the sophists he criticized, specifically noting the contrast between *epistēmē* (knowledge) and *dóxa* (opinion), a distinction that is prominently featured throughout Platonic writings.

## The Socratic Method and Knowledge
Platonic dialogues, such as the *Apology*, describe Sokratês' efforts to test supposed knowledge by questioning statesmen, poets, and artisans. Sokratês found satisfaction only in the answers provided by artisans and craftsmen regarding their specific trades. However, he noted a recurring flaw: because these individuals possessed specialized knowledge in their own professions, they mistakenly believed they possessed knowledge in other areas as well.

## Influence on the Alexandrian School and Oriental Philosophy
In the city of Alexandria, the philosophy of Plato was one of several sources contributing to a fusion of Greek, Egyptian, and Jewish thought. The Greeks introduced a version of Platonism that had already undergone significant changes. 

A primary point of divergence between Platonic thought and the views of the Jews and Orientals concerned the nature of matter. While Plato supposed that matter was eternal, the Jews and Orientals viewed matter as a creation of God, who alone was eternal. Furthermore, the concept of the *logos* differed between these traditions. While Philo personified the *logos* as the ideal archetype of the world, the archive's sources indicate that Plato never did so. Other doctrines found in the later prophets, such as Ezekiel, and in books like the Wisdom of Solomon and Ecclesiasticus—including the history of good and bad angels, paradise, hell, and the representation of God as light and evil as darkness—are attributed to Oriental philosophy rather than Platonism.

## Plato in Medieval Western Europe
Until the fifteenth century, very little Greek philosophical literature was read in Western Europe, with the exception of certain translations of Aristotle's logic. During this time, the "creative Plato" was almost entirely unknown, in contrast to the "scientific Aristotle." While some neo-Platonic writers were known, neo-Platonism maintained a relationship to Plato similar to that of Christian Science to Christ.
