# Takht-i-Rustam

The Takht-i-Rustam is an ancient construction located within the ruins of Balkh, a city in Afghanistan. Balkh, also known as Zainaspa or ancient Bactra, is situated approximately 46 miles south of the Oxus and 100 miles east of Andkhui. The city currently exists largely as a mass of debris and ruins, though it contains a small bazaar, about 500 houses of Afghan settlers, and a colony of Jews.

## Description and Construction
The Takht-i-Rustam is characterized by a wedge-shaped plan with sides that are uneven. According to archival records, the structure is apparently constructed from pise mud, which consists of mud puddled and mixed with straw. 

The Takht-i-Rustam is located in an area where earlier Buddhist constructions have proven to be more durable than the later Mahommedan buildings. In the vicinity of the Takht-i-Rustam, there are the remains of many other topes, or stupas. It is suggested that these ruins may be the site of the Nan Vihara, as described by the Chinese traveler Hsuan Tsang.

## Related Structures in Balkh
The Takht-i-Rustam is situated among other notable ruins in Balkh. One such structure is the Top-i-Rustam, a circular building approximately 50 feet high, measuring 50 yards in diameter at its base and 30 yards at the top. The Top-i-Rustam features four circular vaults in its interior and four external passages that likely lead to these vaults. Its base is built from sun-dried bricks, each approximately 2 feet square and 4 to 5 inches thick.

Other remnants in the city include the Masjid Sabz, featuring a green-tiled dome and identified as the tomb of Abul Narsi Parsar (a Khwaja), and the arched entrance of a traditionally recent Madrasa. The city's outer walls, which are in a state of utter disrepair, have a perimeter of roughly 6-1/2 to 7 miles. On the south-eastern borders, these walls are set upon a rampart or mound that indicates a Mongol origin. To the north-east, a moated and walled fort and citadel stand on a barren mound, though only a few pillars remain.

## Historical Context of Rustam
While the Takht-i-Rustam is a physical ruin in Balkh, the name Rustam is associated with a general of ability who led the Persian forces against the Arabs at the Battle of Kadessia in 637. During this conflict, Rustam commanded a composite host that included thirty-three war elephants. He observed the battle from a golden throne situated on a raised platform behind the Persian ranks. 

The battle lasted three days. On the third day, a wounded elephant became uncontrollable, causing panic among the other elephants and creating confusion within the Persian array. This allowed the Arabs to charge and eventually shatter the Persian army. Rustam was killed in the conflict, and his golden throne and platform were broken down.

Additionally, historical records from the Mamasenni Lúrs, a tribe that occupied Shúlistán (the country between Khuzistán and Shíráz), indicate a claim of direct descent from Rustam.

## Distinctions from Other "Takht" Sites
The Takht-i-Rustam in Balkh is distinct from other similarly named sites in the region:
*   **Takht-i-Suliman (Solomon's Throne):** A high tableland in northern Baluchistan, approximately 8,000 feet above sea-level, known for a celebrated shrine (zirat) located at 11,070 feet.
*   **Takht-i-Bostan:** A site featuring a great arch with a 6th-century A.D. carving of a Persian bag-pipe from the reign of Chosroes II.
