# SICILIAN AFFAIRS AFTER THE DEATH OF THE ELDER DIONYSIUS

## The Legacy of Dionysius the Elder
At the time of his death in 367 B.C., Dionysius the Elder claimed to have secured his dominion with "chains of adamant." This stability was based upon several pillars: a large, well-paid, and well-trained body of mercenaries; immense military stores and magazines of arms; four hundred ships of war; and impregnable fortifications located on the islet of Ortygia. Additionally, he relied on the established intimidation of the Syracusan people. While these assets provided security, they required a leader of Dionysius's capabilities to maintain them.

## Succession and Family Dynamics
The elder Dionysius left behind a complex family structure resulting from two simultaneous marriages. With his Lokrian wife, Doris, he had three sons, the eldest being Dionysius (the Younger), who was approximately twenty-five years old at the time of his father's death. With his Syracusan wife, Aristomachê (daughter of Hipparinus), he had two sons, Nysæus and Hipparinus, and two daughters, Aretê and Sophrosynê. 

The elder Dionysius had previously attributed Aristomachê's long period of childlessness to incantations performed by the mother of Doris, leading him to execute the suspected sorceress.

## The Role of Dion
Dion, born around 408 B.C., was a central figure in the Syracusan political landscape. He was the son of Hipparinus—who had helped the elder Dionysius in his original usurpation to recover his own ruined fortune—and the brother of Aristomachê. Through these connections, Dion was the brother-in-law of the elder Dionysius and the uncle of both Sophrosynê and Aretê. Dion further solidified these ties by marrying Aretê, making him both the son-in-law and brother-in-law of the elder Dionysius.

Dion was one of the wealthiest men in Syracuse, with property estimated at over one hundred talents. He had participated in the wars that expanded the dominion over the Italian and Sicilian Greeks and lived a life of luxury. However, his trajectory changed through his association with the Pythagoreans—a brotherhood of philosophers known for mysticism and recluse study—and his subsequent relationship with the philosopher Plato.

## Political Philosophy and Ambition
Under the influence of Plato and the Academy, Dion developed a profound abhorrence for government by will. He aspired to replace despotism with a government of laws and qualified freedom. Despite this, Dion's education was theoretical rather than practical; he had spent his life within a despotic family and had not learned the needs of a community of freemen. 

Consequently, Dion held the contradictory belief that while despotism was negative, a thoroughly popular government was also undesirable. He believed that once he removed the despot, it was his role to determine the laws and the amount of liberty to be granted to the community, effectively positioning himself as a despotic lawgiver rather than a democratic leader.

## Regional Conflicts and Treaties
The geopolitical landscape of Sicily remained contested. In one instance, Mamerkus assembled a force near Katana, supported by Carthaginian allies under Giskon, but was defeated by Timoleon near the river Abolus. This defeat led the Carthaginians to seek peace.

The resulting peace treaty established the river Halykus (or Lykus) as the boundary. All territory west of the Halykus was recognized as Carthaginian, though Greeks in that region were permitted to emigrate to Syracuse with their property. All territory east of the Halykus was designated as "free Greek," distributed among free cities exempt from despots. The Carthaginians also pledged not to ally with any Grecian despot in Sicily. This stood in contrast to the first treaty made by the elder Dionysius, which had stipulated that the Syracusans remain subject to him.
