# Babylonian City Laws and the Treatment of Aliens

## Legal Framework and City Rights
The legal history of Babylonia is characterized by a transition from early tribal customs to a structured system of city laws. When Semitic tribes first settled in Babylonian cities, their tribal customs were integrated into the laws of those cities. In the early history of the region, various cities struggled for supremacy; while a dominant metropolis would demand military support and tribute from subject cities, it generally left local customs and cults unaffected. Consequently, city rights and usages were respected by both conquerors and kings.

The development of Babylonian law began with the Sumerians, the race that first developed the region's civilization. They invented pictorial hieroglyphs (which later became cuneiform) and established the foundations of the chief cities. Sumerian remained the language of law and religion long after Semites became the ruling race. Eventually, Khammurabi utilized his genius to establish Babylon as his metropolis and unified his vast empire through a uniform system of law known as the Code of Khammurabi. This Code served as the backbone for subsequent legal reconstructions. Fragments recovered from the library of Assur-bani-pal at Nineveh indicate the Code was divided into chapters titled *Ninu ilu [s.]irum* and was recopied for over fifteen hundred years. Its influence persisted through the conquests of the Greeks, Persians, and Parthians, eventually affecting later Mahommedan and Syro-Roman law in Mesopotamia.

## Rights of Aliens and Residents
Babylonia was composed of many different races from its early stages, and there was incessant intercommunication between its cities. Because every city hosted a large number of resident aliens, there was a strong tendency for customs to be assimilated.

As late as the accession of Samas-sum-yukin and Assur-bani-pal, the Babylonians appealed to their city laws to protect specific rights regarding foreigners and residents. These laws stipulated that groups of aliens, up to twenty people at a time, were permitted to enter the city freely. Furthermore, the laws provided protections for foreign women who had married Babylonian husbands, decreeing that they could not be enslaved. The strict adherence to legal process was such that the city laws protected even animals; for example, a dog that entered the city could not be put to death without being tried.

## Social and Political Conflict
Despite these legal protections for aliens, the archive records instances of severe social friction. In one period, the Babylonians and the Israelites were frequently at variance due to the contrariety of their laws. This legal divergence led to mutual assaults whenever one party grew bolder than the other. Following the ruin of the party of Anileus, the Babylonians attacked the Israelites. Unable to fight back or endure living with the Babylonians, the Israelites fled to Seleucia, a principal city built by Seleucus Nicator. Seleucia was inhabited by Grecians, Macedonians, and Syrians, and the Israelites lived there for five years before a pestilence in Babylon caused further migrations and subsequent calamities.

## Religious and Political Integration
The legal and political structure of Babylonia was mirrored in its religious organization. In the post-Khammurabic period, the pantheon was organized similarly to a secular royal court. Marduk became the chief deity, with the gods of minor local cults and chief centers grouped around him as loyal attendants. This structure increased the reflected glory of Marduk.

This religious predominance ensured that Marduk remained recognized even by Assyrian rulers, who often conceded a form of independence to Babylonia even while exercising absolute political control. Assyrian kings frequently appointed their brothers or sons as governors of Babylonia. To exercise authority in the Euphrates valley, both Babylonian and Assyrian rulers recognized the ceremony of "taking the hand of Bel-Marduk" as an essential preliminary to installation. Marduk and the Assyrian god Assur only became rivals when Babylonia caused trouble for the Assyrians, such as during the time of Sennacherib in 689 B.C.
