# Tell-el-Amarna

Tell-el-Amarna is the site of the record-office of the Egyptian king Amenophis IV (also known as Akhenaton). The site is primarily noted for the discovery of a mass of letters comprising royal correspondence between Egyptian monarchs and various other rulers, including those of the Hittites and Babylonians. These documents have provided significant insight into the political and social affairs of the era.

## The Amarna Correspondence and Diplomacy
The letters found at Tell-el-Amarna illuminate the international relations of the Egyptian eighteenth dynasty. Among the correspondence is a letter from Tushratta, the King of Mitani, who had conquered Assyria. In this communication, Tushratta mentions having taken the statue of the goddess Ishtar and sending it to Egypt, partly to acknowledge the overlordship of Amenophis and partly due to a fear of the goddess's anger.

The tablets also record the relationship between Egypt and Assyria. Assur-yuballidh of Assyria sent letters to Amenophis IV; he had previously married his daughter to the Kassite king of Babylonia. Following the murder of the Kassite king by a faction at court, Assur-yuballidh marched into Babylonia to avenge his son-in-law, installing Burna-buryas of the royal line as king. Burna-buryas subsequently maintained a correspondence with Amenophis IV during his 22-year reign.

## Regional Geography and Administration
The Tell el-Amarna tablets provide geographical and administrative data regarding the Egyptian suzerainty over Palestine. The city of Damascus, referred to in the correspondence as Dimashka, served as the capital of the small province of Ubi. Additionally, the city of Ascalon—a well-fortified town and center for the worship of the fish-goddess Derketo—is mentioned several times in the correspondence.

The tablets also contain references to Sankhar, which is believed to be the equivalent of the biblical Shinar, representing northern Babylonia. Furthermore, the letters mention the Khabiri as foes who threatened to invade Palestine and challenge Egyptian supremacy. While some have attempted to identify the Khabiri with the Hebrews, scholars generally maintain that the Khabiri of the Tell el-Amarna letters are not the Hebrews who invaded Canaan under Joshua.

## The Legend of Adapa
Beyond political records, a portion of the legend of Adapa, the first man, was discovered within the record-office at Tell-el-Amarna. This legend serves to explain the origin of death. According to the text, Adapa was fishing when he broke the wings of the south wind. As a result of this action, he was summoned to appear before the tribunal of Anu in heaven.

Before his ascent, Adapa received counsel from Ea, who advised him not to eat or drink while in heaven. Adapa followed this advice and refused the food offered to him. Because he declined this sustenance, he lost the opportunity to secure immortality for himself and his descendants.
