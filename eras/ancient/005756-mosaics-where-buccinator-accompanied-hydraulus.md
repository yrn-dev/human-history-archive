# Mosaics

Mosaic work is a method of creating designs by assembling small pieces of materials—such as pottery, stone, glass, or marble—which are laid as wall linings or paving. To create a mosaic, a firm bed of concrete is required, and the materials are fixed into a float of cement approximately three-quarters of an inch or half an inch thick.

## Technical Methods and Materials
In Roman mosaics, cubes of various colored marble are pressed into the cement float. A more economical version, known as "terazzo mosaic," is produced by strewing irregularly shaped marble chips over the floated surface, pressing them in with a plasterer's hand float, and rolling the surface with an iron roller. In both the Roman and terazzo methods, any specific designs or patterns are worked into position first, with the ground-work filled in later.

While a distinction is often made between classical mosaics (generally marble) and Christian mosaics (mostly gilt and colored glass), glass mosaics were already utilized during the Augustan age. The use of gilt tesserae dates back to the 1st or 2nd century. This technique likely originated in Egypt, a major center for glass-working in antiquity, which is also the probable source of the gilt glass vessels found in catacombs.

## Classical and Secular Examples
Mosaics have been used to depict a wide variety of secular and historical subjects. In a Roman villa at Nennig, as documented in J.N. von Wilmowsky's *Eine römische Villa zu Nennig*, mosaics depict a buccinator (a player of the buccina) being accompanied on the hydraulus. 

Mosaics have also served cartographic purposes. The oldest original map in existence is a 6th-century picture-map in mosaic discovered in 1896 on the floor of the church of Madaba, located to the east of the Dead Sea, depicting Palestine.

## Christian and Byzantine Mosaics
Christian mosaics emerged in Rome, with some of the earliest typical examples found at Santa Costanza in the 4th century. Other works in that church utilize marble, following classical traditions. By approximately A.D. 400, the great apse-mosaic of S. Pudenziana was created in Rome. Other significant early works include the 5th-century incrustations in the mausoleum of Galla Placidia in Ravenna and the dome mosaics of the church of St George in Salonica.

By the 6th century, magnificent examples were produced at the basilica at Parenzo, St Catherines in Sinai, and St Sophia in Constantinople. In Cyprus, the small church of Keti contains a Byzantine mosaic in the conch of a tiny apse, depicting the Virgin with the Holy Child between the angels Michael and Gabriel, who hold disks marked with the sign [CHI].

## Architectural Integration and Schemes
In early church architecture, mosaic pictures in panels were commonly used to decorate the space between the colonnade and the windows (the later triforium-space). Mosaics also decorated the face and soffit of the wide arches dividing the nave from the transept. These often featured a bust of Christ or the holy lamb upon the altar, flanked by the twenty-four elders, the seven candlesticks, and evangelistic symbols.

Complex mosaic schemes were developed for church domes. At St Sophia in Constantinople, the Pantocrator occupies the center of the dome, surrounded by four colossal cherubim at the corners, with prophets, saints, doctors, and angels on the walls below. The church of the Holy Apostles featured the Ascension in its central dome. In Venice, St Mark's repeats the Ascension composition on its central dome, while its eastern dome shows Christ surrounded by prophets and its western dome depicts the Descent of the Holy Spirit upon the Apostles.

## The Cosmati School and Later Developments
The Cosmati school produced mosaic pictures and sculpture of high merit, including recumbent effigies in the tombs of S. Maria Maggiore and Ara Coeli. Their work is characterized by a Gothic main line, particularly in altar-canopies with pierced geometrical tracery. Notable examples include the marble altar in S. Cesareo and the cloisters of S. Paolo fuori le Mura (built c. 1285 by Giovanni, the youngest Cosmati). These cloisters, along with those of the Lateran, feature slender marble columns inlaid with brilliant and delicate bands of glass mosaic. This school's influence extended to England, as seen in the shrine of the Confessor at Westminster (c. 1268).

Mosaic traditions spread from art centers to Paris, Aachen, Kiev, and as far east as Sanâ, the capital of Yemen. This tradition continued for a thousand years, interrupted only by the iconoclastic dispute. A prominent late example is the 14th-century "mosaic-church" (the Convent of the Saviour) at Constantinople.

## Other Religious Applications
During the period of the iconoclastic emperors, religious art was restricted to geometrical designs, flourishes, and spirals, while secular art remained permitted. Similarly, early Mahommedans utilized mosaics in mosques, analogous to those in Ravenna, to depict the prophets and scenes from the life of Mahomet.
