# Bubastis

Bubastis (also Bubastos) is the Graecized name for both an Egyptian goddess and her associated city. The city, known in Egyptian as *P-ubasti* ("place of Ubasti"), served as the capital of the 19th nome of Lower Egypt. The goddess, Ubasti, derived her name from the city (*Bast*), and the city subsequently derived its name from the goddess. The Greeks, confusing the two, applied the name Bubastis to both.

## Geography and Archaeology
In the modern era, the site of Bubastis is represented by Tell Basta, a great mound of ruins located near Zagazig. The site includes a large temple described by Herodotus, which is currently strewn with blocks of granite. While only monuments made of hard stone have survived, the discoveries at Bubastis are considered more important than those at any other site in the Delta, with the exception of Tanis. These monuments cover a wide chronological range, beginning with Khufu (Cheops) and continuing through to the thirtieth dynasty.

## The Goddess Ubasti
Ubasti was one of several feline goddesses and was typically depicted with the head of a lioness. Her character is described as essentially mild and playful, distinguishing her from other feline goddesses such as Sokhmi. 

Following the New Kingdom, there was a significant increase in the reverence for sacred animals, and the domestic cat became specifically associated with Bubastis. This was likely due to the intractability and scarcity of other feline species. Consequently, while her general and hieratic form remained lioness-headed, a popular form—often rendered in bronze—depicted her as a woman with a cat's head. In this form, she is often shown holding a basket on her left arm and a lion aegis (a semicircular pectoral topped with a lioness's head) in her right hand.

The Greeks identified Ubasti with their goddess Artemis. They also confused her with the leonine goddess Tafne, sister of Shöou (Apollo), as the two were almost indistinguishable in form. Ubasti's son, Iphthimis (Nfr-tm), pronounced Eftem, has a name that may mean "All-good," which suggests a reason for his identification with Prometheus.

## Religious Practices and Festivals
The worship of Ubasti received a great stimulus from the twenty-second dynasty (the Bubastite dynasty) and the general rise in the importance of Lower Egypt in later periods. Her name appears on monuments dating back to the third dynasty.

The city featured a cat cemetery on its west side, consisting of numerous large brick chambers filled with decayed and burnt mummies. Many of these mummies were placed in cat-shaped cases made of bronze or wood.

Herodotus describes the festival of Bubastis as a riotous event attended by thousands of people from across Egypt. Evidence from the tablet of Canopus indicates there were two such festivals: a great festival and a lesser one. It is possible the lesser festival took place in Memphis, specifically in the quarter called Ankhto, which housed a temple to the goddess.

## Historical and Political Context
The city of Bubastis is noted in the accounts of Herodotus for its physical elevation. During the reign of the Ethiopian king Sabacos, who ruled Egypt for fifty years, Egyptians who committed transgressions were sentenced to perform work throwing up embankments before their home cities. This process made many cities higher, but the earth was piled up especially in the town of Bubastis.

In later history, the nome of Bubastis became a site of political tension during the reign of Psammetichus. A new military settlement of foreigners was established within the nome, which was otherwise occupied by the Egyptian military caste. The introduction of these foreign mercenaries was viewed as an abomination by the conservative Egyptian society. Psammetichus further marginalized the native soldiers by assigning them the least honorable positions in the line of battle when fighting alongside mercenaries. This discontent eventually led to a mass emigration of 240,000 native soldiers into Ethiopia, where they established a settlement the Greeks called the Automoli.
