# Thracians and Greek Colonies in Thrace

The region of Thrace was inhabited by numerous distinct tribes who, despite their numbers, shared a similar character. These peoples were noted for their military efficiency, rapacity, and cruelty. Their culture and worship were described as Asiatic, and they were referred to by some as "butter-eating Thracians." In terms of social customs, some sources indicate the practice of tattooing; while Plutarch suggests this was a punishment specifically for women for the slaying of Orpheus, Cicero refers to the practice more generally.

## Thracian Tribes and Territory
The territory of Thrace was vast and composed of many different tribes. Among those located in the tract between the Strymôn and Hebrus rivers, extending to the sea-coast, were the Edonians, Satræ, Dersæans, Sapæans, Bistones, Kikones, and Pætians. The Edonians specifically occupied the valuable territory near the mouth of the Strymôn, including a settlement known as Ennea Hodoi (or "Nine Ways").

## Early Greek Colonization
Greek settlement in Thrace began with the Chalkidic colonies. Methônê is cited as the earliest, established around 720 B.C. Other settlements were formed on the Chalkidic peninsula and its three projecting headlands: Pallênê (the westernmost), Sithonia (the middle), and the headland of Athos. In the headland of Athos, colonies such as Stageira and Akanthus were founded; both are said to have been established in 654 B.C. These two, along with Sanê and Argilus, were colonies from Andros, which had been colonized from Eretria.

Further east, Greek settlements were established on the island of Thasus and the Thracian Chersonesus, including Byzantium, Selymoria, and Perinthus. Other settlements were placed on the Euxine south of the Danube, as well as on the islands of Imbros and Lemnos.

## Coastal Settlements and the Euxine
Along the southern coast of Thrace, the Ionic colony of Abdêra was established from Teôs in Ionia, though an earlier unsuccessful attempt by the Klazomenians occurred in 651 B.C. Other settlements include the Chian colony of Marôneia and the Lesbian settlement of Ænus at the mouth of the Hebrus.

On the Euxine, the Milesians planted a prosperous colony at Sinôpê before 600 B.C. Sinôpê subsequently served as the mother-city for the colonies of Kerasus, Kotyôra, and Trapezus (Trebizond), each of which paid annual tribute and received a governor, or harmost, from Sinôpê. Additionally, the Megarians established the colony of Herakleia in the territory of the Mariandyni.

## The Athenian Colony of Amphipolis
The settlement of Amphipolis, located on the eastern bank of the river Strymon, was established in 437 B.C. under the conduct of the Athenian Agnon. The site was highly valuable due to its proximity to a bridge over the Strymon and its access to gold, silver, and ship-timber mines. 

The establishment of Amphipolis followed several failed attempts. Histiæus the Milesian and his brother Aristagoras attempted a settlement around 497-496 B.C., followed by an Athenian attempt around 465 B.C. under Leagrus. In the latter case, despite sending ten thousand men, the Athenians were defeated and expelled by native Thracian tribes. Eventually, Athenian citizens (kleruchs) and others enriched themselves through mining in the region. The historian Thucydidês is noted as having acquired large property in these mines through marriage and parentage involving Thracians and Athenian colonists.

## Islands and Peræa
The islands of Samothrace and Thasus each possessed a "Peræa," a strip of the mainland used for defense and cultivation to support the islands. Thasus, described by the poet Archilochus in the seventh century B.C. as "an ass’s backbone, overspread with wild wood" due to its barren, mountainous nature, was originally occupied by Phoenicians who worked its gold mines. In the seventh century B.C., Parian colonists, including Archilochus, settled on the island and engaged in warfare against the Thracian Saian tribe.

## Thracian Princes and Greek Influence
Greek individuals often formed strategic alliances with Thracian royalty. The Athenian Iphikrates served the kings of Thrace, first aiding Seuthes near the Propontis and later marrying the daughter of Kotys. As part of his dowry, Iphikrates received a seaport village called Drys near the mouth of the Hebrus, where he established a fortified post and a dependent Greek colony. Other eminent Athenians, such as Miltiades and Alkibiades, followed similar patterns of establishing themselves in Thrace.

## Cultural Exchange
The lyre, an ancient stringed instrument used in Greek recitations, is believed to have been introduced to Greece through Lydia or Thrace, as its origin is sought in Asia. While the instrument's technical improvers were from the Ionian or Aeolian colonies, the mythic masters of the lyre—Thamyris, Musaeus, and Orpheus—were Thracians.
