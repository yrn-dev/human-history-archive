# Francesco Bianchini

Francesco Bianchini was a scholar and critic whose works contributed to the study of ancient musical instruments, Roman history, and ecclesiastical records.

## Contributions to Musicology
Bianchini authored the work *de tribus generibus instr. mus. veterum*, published in Rome in 1742. This text includes plates (specifically pl. ii., Nos. 12 and 13) and descriptions (p. 11) regarding ancient musical instruments. His depictions of these instruments have been compared to other historical sources, such as the work of Montfaucon (*Suppl. de l'antiq. expliquee*, vol. iii. pl. lxxiii., Nos. 1 and 2, and explanation p. 189). 

Further analysis of these instruments appears in the *Vitae Neronis* by Suetonius (ed. Charles Patin, cap. 41, p. 304), which figures a contorniate. The musical instrument depicted in Suetonius is noted to differ essentially from the versions presented by both Bianchini and Montfaucon.

## Historical and Archaeological Research
Bianchini conducted research into the social structures and domestic life of ancient Rome. In his work *Camera Sepolchrale*, he documented the discovery of six thousand urns belonging to the freedmen and slaves of Livia and Augustus. 

Through this research, Bianchini highlighted the extreme specialization of office within the imperial household. His findings revealed that specific slaves were appointed to highly minute tasks, such as the care of the empress's lap-dog or the weighing of wool spun by the maids of the empress. These details serve as evidence of the general wealth present in the city during that era.

## Critical Analysis of Ecclesiastical Records
Bianchini is recognized as one of the most learned critics regarding the *Liber Pontificalis*. He, along with other scholars including Muratori, Ciampini, Lucas Holstenius, and Schelestrate, reached a consensus on the origins of this text. 

Bianchini and his contemporaries agreed that the *Liber Pontificalis* was composed and continued by notaries and apostolic librarians during the ninth and eighth centuries. They further identified that the smallest and final portion of the work was written by Anastasius. While the critics noted that the narrative of the *Liber Pontificalis* is partial, its details are trifling, and its style is barbarous, they maintained that it remains an authentic and curious record of the periods it describes.
