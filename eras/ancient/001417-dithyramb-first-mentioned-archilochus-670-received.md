# Dithyramb

The dithyramb was a form of Greek lyric poetry and choral performance. First mentioned by the poet Archilochus (c. 670 B.C.), it evolved from a spontaneous religious expression into a highly structured art form that eventually contributed to the origin of tragedy.

## Origins and Primitive Form
In its primitive state, the dithyrambus was a round choric dance and song performed in honor of Dionysus. This practice was common in various locations, including Thebes and Naxos, and was typically performed during Dionysiac festivals. These early dithyrambs were characterized as spontaneous effusions of drunken hilarity and revelry. Archilochus is noted as having often taken the chief part in these performances, described as being "with the thunder of wine full upon his mind." This exciting, unrestrained character stood in contrast to the stately and solemn pæan addressed to Apollo and bore similarities to the worship of the Great Mother in Asia.

## Evolution and Refinement
The transition of the dithyramb from "spontaneous nature into the garden of art" was led by Arion of Lesbos (c. 600 B.C.). Born at Methymna, Arion was a composer, singer, and harper who was favored by Periander at Corinth. It was in Corinth that Arion first taught, denominated, and composed the dithyramb in a finished choral form.

Arion transformed the dithyramb into an elaborate composition. While the primitive version was a popular chorus, Arion utilized a body of fifty trained men who were strictly disciplined and sober. Despite this professionalization, the performance maintained an imitation of primitive license through its rhythms, movements, and the use of a chorus (the *tragikos choros*) that personated satyrs.

## Expansion of Subject and Scope
Originally, the dithyramb focused specifically on the birth of Dionysus, but it eventually expanded to cover all the fortunes of the god. By the close of the 6th century B.C., the scope of the genre widened further to celebrate any god or hero. Examples of this expansion include:
*   **Simonides**, who wrote dithyrambs regarding Tithonus and Memnon.
*   **Pindar**, who composed dithyrambs on Heracles and Orion.

Because of this broadening, Alexandrian scholars adopted a wide definition of the term, using *dithurambos* to denote any lyric poem focused on a mythical narrative. Under this broad classification, the Alexandrians included Ode xvii of Bacchylides—which relates the voyage of Theseus to Crete—among his dithyrambs, even though the work was clearly a pæan for the Delian Apollo.

## Characteristics and Later Influence
The dithyrambs of Bacchylides exemplify the principal features of the developed genre, which centered on epic narrative. These works were often animated by short speeches in the epic style and adorned with picturesque details. The six dithyrambs of Bacchylides are arranged approximately alphabetically: *Antenoridai*, *Herakles*, *Eitheoi e Theseus*, *Theseus*, *Io*, and *Idas*.

The influence of the dithyramb extended into other forms of Greek literature. Aristotle noted that tragedy originated from the leaders of the dithyramb. Additionally, the comic writer Kratinus (c. 448 B.C.) is described as having an abrupt and dithyrambic style that resembled that of Æschylus.

## Etymology
The derivation of the word *di-thurambos* remains uncertain. One possibility is that *di* is a root found in *dios*. The term *thurambos* may be another form of *thriambos*, a word used by Kratinus to describe a type of hymn dedicated to the wine-god.
