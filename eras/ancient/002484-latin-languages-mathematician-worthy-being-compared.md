# Latin Languages and Robert Balfour

## The Latin Language and the Indo-European Family
Latin is a member of the Indo-European (or Aryan) family of languages, a group that extends across nearly all of Europe and into India. This family is characterized by shared fundamental roots and grammatical ideas. For example, the Latin word for father, *pater*, and mother, *mater*, are cognate with the English *father* and *mother*, the Greek *pater* and *meter*, and the Sanscrit *pitar* and *matar*. These languages are viewed as variations of a single entity, and the variations between them follow specific phonetic laws, such as Grimm’s Law, where the letter *f* in Germanic languages corresponds to *p* in Latin.

Comparative philology indicates that Latin is a "sister" to the Greek language. In some respects, Latin departs less from the supposed mother-language and common type than Greek does. However, Latin also contains non-Grecian elements and classes of words. These suggest a confluence of different peoples with distinct tongues. This non-Grecian element is also found, and more largely developed, in the remains of the Oscan language. Furthermore, an affinity exists between the Latins, the Sikels, and the Œnotrians—a connection sometimes referred to as "Pelasgic"—which existed prior to the establishment of Greek colonies in Italy and Sicily.

## Academic Study and Philology of Latin
The study of Latin has been central to the field of comparative grammar. Scholars such as Bopp worked to describe the original grammatical structure of languages and trace their phonetic laws by comparing Latin with others, including Sanskrit, Greek, Gothic, and Old-Slavonic. Bopp published a Latin edition of his *Ausfuhrliches Lehrgebaude der Sanskrita-Sprache* in 1832 and compiled a Sanskrit and Latin glossary.

In the United States, Francis Andrew March, a professor at Lafayette College, introduced scientific studies of English and published *A Comparative Grammar of the Anglo-Saxon Language* in 1870, illustrating Anglo-Saxon forms through those of Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, and others. March also edited the "Douglass Series of Christian Greek and Latin Classics" and contributed *Latin Hymns* in 1874.

Other scholars, such as Thomas Hewitt Key, influenced the teaching of classical languages. Key, a professor of Latin at the university of London, introduced the "crude-form" system (the uninflected stem of words) into classical language instruction, a method he detailed in his *Latin Grammar* (1846).

## Latin in Science and History
Latin served as a primary language for scholarly and scientific communication for centuries. Many significant mathematical and scientific works were written in Latin, including the works of the mathematician Barrow, such as his *Optical Lectures* and his explanations of the works of Archimedes, Apollonius, and Theodosius. 

The language also appears in the translation of ancient texts. The works of the Greek mathematician Autolycus of Pitane, who flourished in the second half of the 4th century B.C., have been preserved in several Latin versions, including an edition by F. Hultsch published in 1885.

Historically, the use of Latin was a marker of Roman identity and administration. During the era of the Roman Empire, the inability to master Latin was notable; for instance, the sister of the Carthaginian Emperor Septimius Severus never learned the language and instead conducted her Roman household in Punic.

## Robert Balfour
Robert Balfour (also known as Balforeus), a Scottish philosopher who lived from approximately 1550 to 1625, was a scholar of significant renown. Educated at the university of Paris and St Andrews, he served as the principal of Guienne College at Bordeaux for many years. He is recognized as one of the scholars who helped spread the fame of the *praefervidum ingenium Scotorum* (the fervent genius of the Scots) across Europe.

Balfour's primary work was the *Commentarii in Organum Logicum Aristotelis*, published in Bordeaux in 1618. He was also responsible for the Latin translation and notes of *Cleomedis meteora*, which remained relevant enough to be reprinted at Leiden as late as 1820.

Balfour was highly esteemed by his peers. His contemporary, Dempster, described him as the "phoenix of his age." Dempster specifically characterized Balfour as a philosopher who possessed profound skill in the Latin and Greek languages and described him as a mathematician worthy of being compared with the ancients.
