# Takht-i-Bostan and the Reign of Chosroes II

## The Arch at Takht-i-Bostan
The great arch at Takht-i-Bostan contains a rock carving that serves as significant historical evidence for the evolution of musical instruments. Specifically, the arch features an illustration of a Persian bag-pipe dating from the 6th century A.D., during the reign of Chosroes II. While the representation is described as very crude—potentially due to the skill of the draughtsman who drew the sculptures for record—it provides essential proof that the bag-pipe had survived across the centuries that elapsed between the carving at Takht-i-Bostan and the earlier moulding of a figurine found in the *tell* at Susa.

## Musical Culture under Chosroes II
The reign of Chosroes II was noted for maintaining a high standard of musical culture. During this period (A.D. 590–628), various instruments were utilized in Persian society. Records of a guest feast indicate the presence of singers holding the *barbiton* (or *barbut*), *lyre* (*chang*), *lute* (*rubab*), *flute* (*nei*), and *deff* (a hand-pauke). 

The *barbat* was a bass instrument and a variety of the *rebab*, differing primarily in its size and number of strings. The term *barbud*, applied to the barbiton, is said to be derived from a famous musician who lived during the reign of Chosroes II and was an expert in playing the instrument.

## The Reign of Chosroes II
Chosroes II ascended to the throne in 590 A.D. His rise to power followed a period of instability under his father, Hormizd IV, who had been deposed and blinded by magnates during a general insurrection triggered by the rebellion of the general Bahram Chobin. Hormizd IV was killed by partisans of his son, Chosroes II, in 590.

During his reign, Chosroes II expanded Persian influence significantly. By 619 A.D., he held Damascus, Jerusalem, and Egypt, and maintained armies on the Hellespont. However, his military fortunes shifted in 627 A.D. with a great Persian defeat at Nineveh at the hands of Heraclius. Chosroes II was eventually succeeded in 628 A.D. by his son, Kavadh II, who murdered him.

## Comparison with Chosroes I (Nushirvan)
The archive distinguishes Chosroes II from his predecessor, Chosroes I, also known as Nushirvan. Chosroes I began his reign in 531 A.D. and died in March 579 A.D. His reign was characterized by prosperous years and wars with the Romans, including the Colchian or Lazic War. 

While Chosroes I was viewed by some as a conqueror driven by ambition and prudence, his domestic administration was marked by severity. He secured his position by eliminating his two elder brothers and their families, as well as executing the general Mebodes. Despite these actions, he was applauded by the people, particularly in the East, for his execution of laws and punishment of crimes.
