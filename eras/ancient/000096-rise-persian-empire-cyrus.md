# Rise of the Persian Empire: Cyrus

## Origins and Early History
The early history of Cyrus, the founder of the Persian Empire, is characterized by uncertainty and conflicting accounts. Historical sources, including the narratives of Herodotus and Ktêsias, provide varying details. Herodotus describes Cyrus as the son of Kambysês and Mandanê, and the grandson of Astyagês. According to this account, Astyagês, troubled by a dream, ordered the newborn Cyrus to be exposed. Harpagus, the official charged with the task, instead gave the child to a royal herdsman. The infant was reportedly suckled by a bitch in the mountains and raised as the herdsman's child. During his youth, Cyrus demonstrated physical and mental superiority, even being chosen as "king" in play by village boys.

Cyrus eventually transitioned from being a subject of the Median king Astyagês to becoming his dethroner. In 550 B.C., Cyrus succeeded Cyaxares the Mede, ending internal struggles between the Medes and Persians. At this time, the native Persians occupied the territory of Iran, located between the Tigris and the Indus.

## Conflict with Lydia
The rise of Persian power was noted by Crœsus, the king of Lydia, who sought to curtail the growth of the Persians. Before attacking, Crœsus consulted the oracle at Delphi; despite the oracle's triumphant reply and the munificence Crœsus showed toward it, the resulting campaign ended in disaster. After soliciting an alliance with Sparta, Crœsus crossed the Halys river to attack the Persians.

Cyrus responded with a rapid march to Sardis. Following the siege and capture of the city, Crœsus became a prisoner of Cyrus. This conquest left the Asiatic Greeks in a precarious state. Although they applied to Sparta for assistance, their requests were in vain. After Cyrus departed Sardis, a Lydian revolt occurred but was suppressed.

## Expansion and Conquest of Ionia
Following the fall of Lydia, the Persians moved to conquer Ionia. The campaign began under the general Mazarês, who attacked alongside the Lydian Paktyas. Mazarês was later succeeded by Harpagus, who completed the Persian conquest of Ionia. 

The city of Phôkæa faced a specific fate during this period; while a general emigration was vowed by all, only half of the Phôkæans executed the plan. These emigrants first established a colony at Alalia and later at Elea. A proposal by Bias for a Pan-Ionic emigration was not adopted. Ultimately, the Persians achieved the entire conquest of Asia Minor.

## The Fall of Babylon and Empire Foundation
The Persian expansion continued toward the Chaldean Empire, which had its capital at Babylon. This empire had lasted under Nebuchadnezzar II and his successors until 539 B.C. In that year, Cyrus attacked and took Babylon, formally founding the Persian Empire. This victory united the Babylonian and Median empires under Persian rule. By the time of his reign, Cyrus's empire reached from the boundaries of Lydia to Persia and potentially as far as India.

## Succession and Legacy
Cyrus was succeeded by his son, Cambyses, who subjugated Egypt. Following the death of Cambyses, the empire saw a period of instability. A "false Smerdis," a Mede and Magian, attempted to restore Median supremacy over the Persians, recalling the era of Astyagês. This period of confusion included revolts by the Medes and the Babylonians.

Stability was eventually restored by Darius I (the son of Hystaspes), a chief councillor of Cyrus. Unlike Cyrus, who is described as being half Mede and half Persian, Darius was a pure Persian. Darius suppressed the rebellions of the Medes, the Babylonians, and the refractory satrap Orœtês (whom Cyrus had appointed satrap of Lydia and Ionia). Darius further extended the empire's reach, adding Samos to the conquest of Ionia and ruling from the Hellespont to the Indus. To solidify his legitimacy, Darius married the daughters of Cyrus, Atossa and Artystonê, as well as Parmys, the daughter of Smerdis.
