# Hebrew and the Scholarship of Fritzsche, Hilgenfeld, Reuss, Gifford, Schuerer, and Toy

The study of the original languages and authorship of ancient texts, specifically the book of Baruch, has been a subject of significant debate among various scholars. Among these, Fritzsche, Hilgenfeld, Reuss, Gifford, Schuerer, and Toy have contributed specific theories regarding the linguistic origins, dating, and integrity of the work.

## Linguistic Origins of Baruch
There is a lack of consensus among scholars regarding whether the book of Baruch was originally written in Hebrew or Greek. While some scholars, including Ewald, Koenig, Davidson, Rothstein, and Kneucker, believe the entire book was originally written in Hebrew, others propose a mixed origin. 

Fritzsche, Hilgenfeld, Reuss, Gifford, Schuerer, and Toy advocate the theory that only the section spanning chapters i. through iii. 8 was originally written in Hebrew, while the remainder of the book was originally composed in Greek. This position differs from that of Marshall, who argues for a Hebrew original for i.-iii. 8 and an Aramaic original for iii. 9-iv. 4, and from Bertholdt, Havernick, and Noeldeke, who view the Greek text as the primitive version.

Evidence supporting a Hebrew original for the first three chapters includes the presence of Hebraisms. Examples include the Greek *hou ... ep' autoi* (ii. 26), which corresponds to the Hebrew *'SHR ... `LYW*, and *hou ... ekei* (ii. 4, 13, 29; iii. 8), which corresponds to *'SHR ... SHM*. Additionally, certain "meaningless expressions" in the Greek are interpreted as mistranslations of Hebrew. For instance, in i. 9, the Greek *desmotes* ("prisoner") is used where the Hebrew *MASGEIR* ("locksmith") appeared. Similar mistranslations are found in the book of Jeremiah, leading some scholars to conclude that the same translator was responsible for the LXX of both Jeremiah and Baruch i.-iii. 8.

## Dating and Chronology
The dates for the constituents of the book are uncertain, and the scholars in question offer diverging views:

*   **Fritzsche and Toy:** These scholars date the text to the time of the Maccabees. Fritzsche specifically suggests the early decades of the 2nd century B.C. This theory is supported by the idea that during the Maccabean dynasty, it was believed the Twelve tribes were in Palestine, a belief that arose as the Maccabees extended their dominion over Galilee, Samaria, and eastwards beyond the Jordan.
*   **Reuss:** He assigns the text to a period some decades after 320 B.C. (the date of the conquest of Jerusalem by Ptolemy I).
*   **Gifford:** Following Ewald, Gifford assigns the period of i.-iii. 8 to after 320 B.C.
*   **Schuerer:** He assumes the work was written after A.D. 70.

## Integrity and Authorship
The integrity of the book is questioned by most scholars, who believe it was derived from a plurality of authors. Fritzsche, Hilgenfeld, Reuss, and others (including Hitzig and Kneucker) agree that the sections i.-iii. 8 and iii. 9-v. 9 were written by distinct authors. 

Further complexities arise in the relation between the Apocalypse of Baruch and 4 Ezra. While some assumed identity of authorship, others argued over which work came first. Schuerer, along with Ryssel, Wellhausen, Deane, Kabisch, De Faye, Thomson, and Bissell, maintained the priority of Baruch over 4 Ezra. Conversely, Hilgenfeld and others, such as Langen and Gunkel, argued for the priority of 4 Ezra.

## Contributions to Textual Criticism
Several of these scholars have produced significant editions or historical analyses:
*   **Fritzsche:** He produced an edition of the Greek text in *Libri Apocryphi Veteris Testamenti Graece* (1871) and reproduced a Latin translation of the Apocalypse of Baruch.
*   **Gifford:** He provided an edition of the book in the *Speaker's Apoc.* ii.
*   **Schuerer:** He authored *Geschichte des juedischen Volkes* (History of Jewish People), which is used as a reference for the history of the Israelite people and the dating of various texts. In his work, Schuerer also argues that Theodotion preceded Aquila, suggesting Theodotion's work was the first stage in creating a Greek version that corresponded more closely with the Hebrew.

Regarding other biblical texts, Reuss is noted as one of the scholars who defended the genuineness of the epistle to the Ephesians against the objections of the Tubingen school and others like Hilgenfeld.
