# FROM THE DEATH OF PELOPIDAS TO THE BATTLE OF MANTINEA

## The Career and Death of Pelopidas
Pelopidas served as Bœotarch of Thebes without interruption from the time of the Theban revolution until his death. According to Diodorus, he was re-appointed annually, a fact supported by Plutarch, who states that Pelopidas was in the thirteenth year of his appointment at the time of his decease. 

The timing of Pelopidas's final expedition into Thessaly is a subject of chronological debate. Some sources, including Sievers and Dr. Thirlwall, suggest the expedition began in June 364 B.C. based on an eclipse mentioned by Dodwell. However, other evidence suggests the eclipse of July 13, 364 B.C., mentioned by Calvisius, is a more accurate marker as it falls within the Olympic year indicated by Diodorus. Another perspective places the march into Thessaly in 363 B.C. This timeline suggests Pelopidas died while Epaminondas was absent on a maritime expedition, which would explain why the second Theban army sent to avenge Pelopidas was led by other generals rather than Epaminondas.

## The Maritime Project of Epaminondas
Diodorus places the maritime project of Epaminondas—including its recommendation, the equipping of the fleet, and the actual expedition—in the first year of Olympiad 104, during the archonship of Timokrates in Athens (Midsummer 364 to Midsummer 363 B.C.). It is probable that the year 364 B.C. was spent on naval equipment, with the actual transmarine cruise taking place in the summer of 363 B.C. This maritime activity was eventually hindered by the battle of Mantinea.

## Political and Military Developments in the Peloponnesus
Following the battle of Leuktra, the Peloponnesus underwent significant changes. Epaminondas broke through the Spartan allied army's line at Mount Oneium to march into the Peloponnesus. While Sikyon joined the Thebans, Phlius remained faithful to Sparta. Despite reinforcements arriving from Syracuse to aid Sparta, Epaminondas was noted for his mildness and forbearance. In contrast, the Arcadians, led and animated by the influential Lykomedes, acted with insolence. Tensions also rose as Elis attempted to recover supremacy over Triphylian towns that had joined the Arcadian union.

The reconstitution of Messênê became a central point of discord, marked by the proclamation of a Messenian victor at Olympia. Later, during his third expedition into the Peloponnesus, Epaminondas managed the Achæan cities, though the Thebans eventually reversed his policies following complaints from the Arcadians and others. This political shift resulted in Epaminondas not being re-elected as Bœotarch, although he continued to command in the Peloponnesus in 369 B.C.

## Conflict in Sikyon and Phlius
Sikyon experienced instability when Euphron established himself as a despot, characterized by sanguinary and rapacious conduct. The Phliasians, who remained steady in their adherence to Sparta, suffered under these conditions. The Athenian Chares provided assistance to Phlius, which included the surprise of the fort of Thyamia. Eventually, the Arcadians and Thebans expelled Euphron from Sikyon. After briefly surrendering the harbor to the Spartans, Euphron returned to Sikyon and later traveled to Thebes, where he was assassinated.

## The Battle of Mantinea
The events surrounding Mantinea involved both a cavalry engagement and a general battle. In the cavalry battle, the Athenians, under the general Hegesilaus (according to Ephorus) or Hegelochus (according to Diodorus), arrived unexpectedly to rescue Mantinea. While the Athenians boasted of success with their cavalry, Xenophon and others indicate that the real victory belonged to Epaminondas and the Thebans. The Lacedæmonians eventually confessed defeat by sending a herald to solicit a truce for the burial of the slain.

The subsequent general battle of Mantinea took place in June or July 362 B.C. and proved fatal to Epaminondas. Some traditions suggest that a man named Gryllus killed the Theban commander, though there is some confusion in the records as to whether the commander slain was indeed Epaminondas.

## Private Life of Epaminondas
Records regarding the private life of Epaminondas are sparse; he never married. He is noted for having devoted attachments to beautiful youths, a practice common among the countrymen of Pindar and often confirmed by oaths at the tomb of Iolaus to ensure military fidelity. Epaminondas was particularly devoted to Asopichus, who fought bravely at the battle of Leuktra, and Kaphisodorus, who died alongside Epaminondas at the battle of Mantinea and was buried in a grave adjacent to his.
