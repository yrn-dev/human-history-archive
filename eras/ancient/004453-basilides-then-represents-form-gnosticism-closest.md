# Basilides

Basilides was a prominent exponent of Gnosticism who lived in Alexandria, likely during the first decades of the 2nd century. While Eusebius's *Chronicle* dates his first appearance to A.D. 133, evidence suggests his activity may have begun earlier; Agrippa Castor, who lived under the reign of Hadrian (117-138), wrote a polemic against him. Basilides authored an exegetical work consisting of twenty-four books based on a gospel, though the specific gospel he used is unknown. Further writings are attributed to his son Isidorus, including *Peri prosphuous psuches*, *Ethika*, and *Exegetika* regarding the prophet Parchor.

## Theological Framework and Dualism
Basilides represents a form of Gnosticism closely related to the teachings of Satornil (Saturninus) and is characterized as being closest to Persian dualism in its final form. His system is viewed as a further development of Iranian dualism, which later influenced the religious system of Mani. 

A distinguishing feature of Basilides' doctrine, compared to most other Gnostic sects (with the possible exception of Jewish-Christian Gnosticism), is the complete absence of the idea of a fall within the godhead and the figure of the fallen female god, Sophia Achamoth. 

The system describes a supreme God, the *innatus pater*, from whom emanated 365 heavens and their spirits. This number corresponds to the astronomical conception of the heavens' 365 daily aspects. The supreme God is associated with the name Abraxas (or Abrasax), a name with a numerical value of 365. This numerical connection is also found in the name of the Persian god Mithras. Surrounding the *Pater innatus* is a series of Hypostases—ranging from five to seven—including *Nous*, *Logos*, *Phronesis*, *Sophia*, and *Dunamis*, with *Dikaiosune* and *Eirene* potentially added. These mirror the *Ameshas-spentas* that surround Ahura-Mazda.

## Cosmology and the World-Creators
In the system of Basilides, the world originates from seven powers, which are the lowest emanations of the supreme God. These world-creating angels are identified as the seven planetary deities of later Babylonian religion. The transition of these deities into daemonic or semi-daemonic forces in Gnostic systems suggests the influence of a stronger Iranian monotheistic religion that degraded Babylonian gods to daemons. The syncretism of Persian and Babylonian religions served as the foundation for Gnosticism.

## Christology and Redemption
Basilides' views on Christ and the nature of the material body align him with the Docetes, a group that taught that Christ possessed only a "seeming" body rather than a real material one. Irenaeus specifically reproached Basilides for this Docetic stance.

Regarding the identity of Jesus, Basilides recognized an earthly Jesus and denied the principle of his sinlessness. Some accounts suggest a "double system" where Jesus of Nazareth was a mortal man—the son of Joseph and Mary—who was selected as an instrument to restore the worship of the supreme Deity. In this view, the Christ (the first of the aeons) descended upon Jesus in the form of a dove at his baptism to direct his actions. This event, the *epiphaneia*, was celebrated as a high festival by the followers of Basilides. When Jesus was delivered to the Israelites, the immortal Christ forsook the earthly body and returned to the pleroma, leaving the mortal Jesus to suffer and die.

Alternatively, other accounts state that the supreme God sent his *Nous* (also called Christ or the *diakonos*) into the world to address the confusion of existence. In this version, the Saviour appeared only as a phantasm.

Redemption in the Basilidian system is linked to the restoration of all things and the "great separation of the sexes," where that which was combined for evil is separated once more.

## Magic and Mystic Names
Among the later followers of Basilides, magic became a determining factor. They maintained a "weighty secret" consisting of the names of the rulers of the various heavens. It was believed that anyone who knew these names could pass through the heavens after death to reach the supreme God. 

Consequently, redemption was conceived as the revelation of mystic names. Christ was believed to possess a mystic name, *Caulacau*, which granted him the power to descend through the heavens to earth and subsequently ascend to the Father. This magical tendency is further evidenced by the use of Abraxas gems.
