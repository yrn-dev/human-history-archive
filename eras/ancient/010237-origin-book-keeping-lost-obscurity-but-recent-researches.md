# The History and Evolution of Book-keeping

## Early Origins and Ancient Practices
While the exact origins of book-keeping are lost in obscurity, recent research indicates that methods of keeping accounts have existed since remotest times. In Babylonia, records dating as far back as 2600 B.C. have been discovered. These accounts were written using a stylus on small slabs of clay. These tablets frequently featured nail marks and impressions from cylinder seals; the use of nail marks suggests that a counterpart to the modern method of identifying criminals via fingerprints existed in Babylonia approximately 4,500 years ago.

In Egypt, records were commonly written on papyrus. Contemporary pictorial evidence depicts scribes keeping accounts of the quantities of grain that were removed from and brought into government store-houses. Because these forms of record-keeping existed long before the invention of bound books, the more general term "accounting" is considered preferable.

## Development of Accounting Systems
Early accounting records were primarily narratives of events. While these narratives could be complete in themselves, they failed to meet the requirements of an adequate book-keeping system. The development of such systems was impossible prior to the use of money. Even after the general employment of money, values were recorded using Roman figures, which did not allow for ready calculation.

In recent years, the trend in accounting has shifted back away from the bound book. Modern developments favor the use of card or loose sheets of paper that can be easily detached and rearranged according to convenience or circumstances.

## Tabular Book-keeping
To reduce clerical work, increase the speed of available results, and ensure greater certainty in handling data, modifications to book-keeping methods have been introduced. One such device is tabular book-keeping, which replaces the usual form of accounting with books ruled with numerous columns. This system can be applied to ledgers or books of first entry.

When applied to books of first entry, tabular book-keeping allows a single book to manage multiple classes of transactions. For example, a business divided into several departments can use separate columns for the sales of each department, simplifying the preparation of departmental trading accounts by allowing for the ready calculation of aggregate sales for each. When applied to ledgers, the system allows a single opening of a ledger to record a large number of personal accounts, provided the number of entries for each individual remains few.

## Education and Professional Study
The scientific study of book-keeping is primarily the domain of educational institutions, as there are no other notable organizations devoted to this study outside of professional accountants. Universities in the United States were the first to incorporate accounting into their curricula. In Great Britain, the subject has been treated seriously and upon adequate lines at the Victoria University of Manchester, the university of Birmingham, and the London School of Economics (university of London). More recently, Japan has begun a similar movement toward academic accounting.

In England, various bodies grant diplomas to successful candidates who pass examinations in book-keeping; these include Owens College, Manchester, the London Chamber of Commerce, and the Society of Arts. Additionally, most technical schools and polytechnics provide instruction in book-keeping, though these institutions generally regard the subject merely as a "craft."
