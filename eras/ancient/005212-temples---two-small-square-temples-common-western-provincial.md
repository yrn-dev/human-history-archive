# Temples

Temples have served various religious, political, and social functions across different civilizations, ranging from small provincial shrines to massive complexes that acted as centers of government and imperial worship.

## Classical Greek Temples
In ancient Greece, temples were often the center of religious partnerships known as Amphiktyony. In these arrangements, a group of towns entered into an exclusive partnership to celebrate periodic sacrifices to a god at a specific temple, which was considered common property. For example, seven cities—including Athens, Epidaurus, and Ægina—jointly maintained the sanctuary and temple of Poseidôn on the island of Kalauria. Similarly, in Triphylia on Cape Samikon, a religious partnership existed at the temple of the Samian Poseidôn, where the inhabitants of Makiston managed the superintendence and proclaimed the "Samian truce."

Specific architectural examples include the temple of Asclepius, which dates to approximately 460 B.C. This structure was raised on stages, approached by an eastern ramp, and featured eleven columns on its sides and six at its ends. It housed a gold and ivory statue by Thrasymedes of Paros. The temple's pediments featured sculpture, including a battle between Greeks and Amazons, and acroteria depicting Nereids on sea-horses. Nearby structures included a small temple of Artemis and the Tholos. The Tholos, designed by the architect Polyclitus, was a circular chamber with an inner Corinthian colonnade and an outer Doric colonnade. Known in building contracts as the Thymele, it may have represented a primitive pit of sacrifice. Its foundations consist of concentric walls that create a subterranean labyrinth of partitions and doors.

In Athens, stone Hermae—square figures common in temples and private doorways—were once the target of a mass mutilation event that was viewed as an ominous act of impiety and a potential conspiracy to upset the democracy.

## Roman and Provincial Temples
In early Rome during the fifth century B.C., the fortified town served as the center of government and religion, housing the temples of the gods. In the Roman province of Gaul, the town of Lugudunum featured the Temple of Roma and Augustus. This site was territorially distinct from the Roman *colonia* and served as a gathering place where delegates from 64 Gallic cantons in the provinces of Belgica, Lugudunensis, and Aquitania met every summer to celebrate the worship of the emperor.

In Silchester, archaeological evidence reveals several temples. Two small square temples of a common western-provincial type were located in the east of the town; the larger of these had a *cella* measuring 42 feet square, lined with Purbeck marble. A third temple was circular, situated between the south gate and the forum. A fourth, a small square shrine discovered in 1907, contained inscriptions relating to a *collegium* (gild) and confirmed the name Calleva.

## Buddhist Temples in Japan
In Japan, the establishment of provincial temples, known as *kokubun-ji*, was an official state effort. During the reign of Shomu, commands were issued for every province to erect a sixteen-foot image of Shaka with attendant *bosatsu* (Bodhisattva) and a seven-storey pagoda. These *kokubun-ji* eventually increased to two per province—one for nuns and one for priests. These temples were staffed by *kokushi* (Buddhist hierarchs) who offered prayers and expounded the Sutra, working alongside *kunihakase* (provincial pundits) who taught literature and law.

Later, during the Ashikaga period, the Zen sect was honored almost exclusively. Takauji built the Tenryu-ji temple in Kyoto and planned a group of provincial temples called Ankoku-ji to serve as a counterpoise to the power of the monasteries of Hiei-zan and Nara. The Hiei-zan monasteries were vast, comprising three thousand temples and seminaries and housing soldier-monks who frequently terrorized the palace and the city of Kyoto. In Nara, the establishment of the great temple Todai-ji in the eighth century led to the creation of affiliated *Kokubun-ji* throughout the provinces.

During the Muromachi period, priests of the "Five Great Temples" in Kyoto and their provincial branches preached Sung philosophy. This was influenced by Gen-e, a bonze of the Hiei-zan temple, who adopted the eclectic system of Buddhism and Confucianism from Sung and Yuan scholars, rejecting earlier Han and Tang commentaries.
