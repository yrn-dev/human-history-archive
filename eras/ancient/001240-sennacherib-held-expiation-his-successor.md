# Sennacherib and the Destruction of Babylon

Sennacherib was a monarch of the Assyrian Empire and the son of Sargon II. His reign was characterized by significant military expeditions and a violent conflict with the religious establishment of Babylon.

## Relationship with Babylon and the Priesthood
In the political landscape of the Assyrian and Babylonian empires, monarchs sought to legitimize their power by securing the support of the Babylonian priesthood. This was achieved through a ceremony known as "taking the hands" of Bel (or Bel-Merodach), which signified that the king had been adopted by the priesthood as the god's son and representative. While previous Assyrian kings, such as Tiglath-pileser III and Sargon II, sought this legitimacy, Sennacherib failed to secure the support of the priesthood and never underwent the ceremony.

This failure led to a violent quarrel between Sennacherib and the priesthood. Throughout his reign, Babylonia remained in a constant state of revolt. To suppress this opposition and strike at the power of the priesthood, Sennacherib destroyed the holy part of the city of Babylon. In 689 B.C. (cited as 691 B.C. in some records), he razed the city's palaces, temples, and walls to the ground. He further desecrated the city by throwing the resulting rubbish into the Arakhtu, the canal bordering the earlier Babylon to the south, and removing the statue of Bel-Marduk to Assyria.

## Military Campaigns and the Expedition to Egypt
Beyond his conflicts in Babylon, Sennacherib led Assyrian forces on several major expeditions. He invaded Judah in the fourteenth year of Hezekiah's reign (701 B.C.). He also led his hosts to the borders of Egypt. However, this campaign ended in disaster when his army was smitten by a pestilence. According to the Second Book of Kings, an angel of the Lord killed 185,000 Assyrians in a single night. Following this calamity, Sennacherib departed and returned to dwell at Nineveh.

## Death and Expiation
The complete destruction of Babylon shocked the religious conscience of western Asia. Sennacherib was eventually assassinated by one of his sons. This murder was held to be an expiation for his destruction of the holy city.

## Successors and the Restoration of Babylon
Sennacherib was succeeded by his son, Esarhaddon (who was not the son responsible for the assassination). Recognizing the need to make peace with the god Bel-Marduk, Esarhaddon hastened to rebuild the old city and restore the temple. He received his crown in Babylon and maintained the city as his residence for part of the year.

Upon Esarhaddon's death, Babylonia was left to his elder son, Samas-sum-yukin. However, Samas-sum-yukin eventually led a revolt against his brother, Assur-bani-pal of Assyria. This resulted in another Assyrian siege of Babylon, which ended when the city was starved into surrender. While Assur-bani-pal purified the city and performed a "service of reconciliation," he did not venture to "take the hands" of Bel.
