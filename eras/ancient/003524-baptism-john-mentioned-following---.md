# The Baptism of John

The baptism of John is a subject documented across various New Testament texts and early historical analyses. It is mentioned in the Gospel of Mark (i. 1-11), written approximately A.D. 60-70; the Gospel of Matthew (iii. 1-16) and the Gospel of Luke (iii. 1-22, vii. 29, 30), written between A.D. 80-90; the Acts of the Apostles (i. 22, x. 37, xiii. 24, xviii. 25, xix. 3, 4), also from A.D. 80-90; and the Gospel of John (i. 25-33, iii. 23, x. 40), written between A.D. 90-100.

## Nature and Significance
The baptism performed by John served different functions depending on the recipient. For soldiers and publicans who sought him out in the wilderness due to remorse, the rite acted as a purification from their past. In this capacity, it was identical to the bath of a proselyte. However, it differed from the proselyte's bath in that it did not impose circumcision; instead, it raised these individuals to be children unto Abraham, filled them with Messianic hope, and assured them of a place in the kingdom of God.

For those who were already Jews, the baptism of John did not carry the significance of a proselyte's baptism. Rather, it aligned with a practice used by Jews who sought to consecrate their lives through a stricter practice and study of the law. This is reflected in the writings of Epictetus, who noted that one only truly understands Judaism if they know "the baptized Jew."

## The Baptism of Christ
Historical analysis of the Baptism of Christ utilizes the synchronism of the first Passover mentioned after the event and the forty-sixth year of the building of Herod's Temple. Because Herod began the Temple in the eighteenth year of his reign (likely 20-19 B.C.), the Passover of the forty-sixth year is likely that of A.D. 27. Based on this reasoning, the Baptism of Christ is probably placed in A.D. 26-27. Given that the Nativity is placed at the latest in 7-6 B.C., Christ would have been approximately thirty-two years old at the time of his baptism, an estimate that aligns with the general account provided by St. Luke.

## Relation to Christian Baptism
The relationship between John's baptism and the subsequent Christian rite is a point of historical distinction. John baptized in the name of the coming Messiah without identifying that figure as Jesus of Nazareth. The apostolic age provided this identification, shifting the formula to "into Christ Jesus," "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ," or "of the Lord Jesus Christ." Paul described these formulas as being equivalent to "into the death of Christ Jesus," symbolizing the believer being raised from death into everlasting life.

The archive records instances where the two baptisms were distinguished in practice:
* **Apollos:** According to Acts xviii. 24, Apollos knew only the baptism of John. He became a full disciple after receiving instruction in prophetic *gnosis* from Priscilla and Aquila.
* **The Twelve Disciples:** In Acts xix. 1-7, twelve disciples who had been baptized into John's baptism—meaning into the name of the one who should follow John—had not heard of the Holy Spirit. At the instance of Paul, they were re-baptized into the name of the Lord Jesus. Following this, Paul laid hands on them, and the Holy Ghost came upon them, enabling them to prophecy and speak with tongues.

## Early Ritual Practices
While the baptism of John focused on the coming Messiah, early Christian baptism evolved into various forms. The *Teaching of the Apostles* (c. 90-120) instructs that baptism should be performed in living water in the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, though other water or warm water may be used if living or cold water is unavailable. 

Other early practices included the use of fonts, where neophytes entered naked. Candidates prepared through fasting, prayer, and vigils, and were required to renounce the devil and his angels. These rites were typically conferred by bishops, priests, or deacons during Easter and the season of Pentecost.
