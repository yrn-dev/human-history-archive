# The Tree Falls; Dost Thou See It?

The phrase "The tree falls; dost thou see it?" serves as a translation of a linguistic example used to illustrate the grammatical structure of the Bantu languages. This specific example demonstrates the concept of "concord," which involves the repetition of pronominal particles within a sentence.

## Linguistic Analysis of Bantu Concord
In the study of the original Bantu mother-tongue, the concord is explained through the reconstruction of sentences to show how pronominal particles are repeated. An example in Old Bantu is provided as: *Ngu-mu-ti ngu-no ngu-gwa ku-ngu-mbona*. When translated literally, this reads: "This tree this here this falls; thou this seest?"

This structure is preserved in modern dialects. For instance, in Kiguha, a dialect of North-West Tanganyika, the sentence is rendered as: *U-mu-ti gu-no gu-gwa u-gu-mona?* The literal translation for this version is: "It tree this here it falls; thou it seest?" In standard English, this is rendered as: "The tree falls; dost thou see it?"

## Concepts of Falling and Decline in Historical Texts
The theme of "falling" appears in various historical and narrative contexts within the archive, often referring to political collapse, military defeat, or moral failure.

### Political and Military Risks
In the records of the Lydian Croesus, the concept of "falling" is linked to the instability of human fortune. Croesus advised a king that for the affairs of men, there is a "revolving wheel" that prevents the same persons from always possessing good fortune. He expressed a desire to avert any "occasion of falling" that might approach the king's house. Croesus argued that if the king were to receive the Massagetai into his own land and be worsted, he would lose his entire realm, as the victors would likely march upon the provinces of the realm rather than retreat.

## Human Nature and Power
Historical accounts from Herodotus further explore the nature of human reaction and power.

### The Nature of Anger
Xerxes described the "passionate spirit of man" as dwelling within the ears. According to Xerxes, when the ears hear good things, the body is filled with delight; however, when they hear "opposite things," the spirit "swells up with anger." This anger was demonstrated when Xerxes ordered the eldest son of Pythios to be cut in two and placed on opposite sides of the road as a penalty for Pythios's perceived shamelessness.

### Power and Submission
The dynamics of power are also seen in the interactions between Dareios and Idanthyrsos, king of the Scythians.
