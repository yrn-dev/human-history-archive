# Destruction of Nineveh

The destruction of Nineveh marked the end of the Assyrian Empire, a military power that had dominated Mesopotamia and surrounding regions. While the city served as the capital of a vast empire, its fall in the early 7th century B.C. led to the disappearance of Assyria as a political entity and the rise of the Neo-Babylonian and Median powers.

## The Assyrian Empire and Nineveh
Assyria originated as an offshoot of Babylonia to the north-west and was colonized before the second millennium. Although the Assyrians used the same language as the Babylonians, they maintained a distinct individuality in their religion and art. By the 9th and 8th centuries B.C., the Assyrians became the primary power in their sphere and established suzerainty over their parent city, Babylon.

Under the reigns of Salmaneser and Sennacherib, the Assyrians of Nineveh were powerful conquerors in Phoenicia, Syria, and Judaea. During this era, the empire employed a political method of shifting entire populations *en masse* to unfamiliar regions to ensure obedience to the supreme power. For example, Salmaneser took the Israelites from their own country and moved them into Persis and Media, while bringing the Cuthæans from those regions into the lands of the Israelites. 

The empire reached significant heights under leaders such as Tiglath Pileser III and the usurper Sargon II. Sargon II adopted his name to flatter the Babylonians by referencing Sargon I, the founder of the Akkadian Empire. Later, Sennacherib's grandson, Assurbanipal (known to the Greeks as Sardanapalus), succeeded in holding lower Egypt for a period.

## The Decline of Power
The stability of the Assyrian Empire began to wane rapidly after the era of Sargon II, lasting only about 150 years beyond his reign. The empire faced increasing pressure from various groups, including the Cimmerians, Persians, Medes, and Scythians. While Assyrian monarchs initially played these groups against one another—even marrying Assyrian princesses, such as a daughter of Esarhaddon, to Scythian chiefs—the internal and external pressures eventually became insurmountable.

Egypt eventually threw off Assyrian rule under Pharaoh Psammetichus I, and under Necho II, Egypt attempted a war of conquest in Syria. This left Assyria struggling against closer enemies with little ability to resist.

## The Fall of the City
The final destruction of Nineveh is dated to 606 B.C. by several sources, including the testimonies of Scripture and Herodotus, as well as the records of the archive's chronologies. The city was taken by a coalition of forces. These included the Chaldeans (or Babylonians), a Semitic people from south-east Mesopotamia, and the Medes and Persians, Aryan peoples from the north.

There are differing historical accounts regarding the exact nature of the conquest:
* **The Combined Effort:** Some sources, including Josephus and the views of Ktêsias, state that the empire was destroyed by the Medes and Babylonians united.
* **The Median Conquest:** Herodotus suggests a series of successive revolts of dependent territories, beginning with the Medes. In this account, Kyaxarês the Mede took Nineveh around 600 B.C. without mention of the Babylonians. According to this view, the queen of Babylon, Nitokris, feared the Medes specifically because they had taken Nineveh.
* **The Role of Kyaxarês:** Other records specify that after the expulsion of the Scythians from Asia, Kyaxarês besieged and took the city, reducing all Assyrians under his dominion except those in the kingdom of Babylon. This occurred toward the end of his reign, before he bequeathed the empire to his son Astyagês in 595 B.C.

## Aftermath and Legacy
The fall of Nineveh in 606 B.C. effectively ended the Assyrian period of Babylonian-Assyrian religion. Because Assyria was essentially a military power, it disappeared entirely with the destruction of its capital. 

The vacuum left by the Assyrians allowed for the establishment of the Second Babylonian Empire under Chaldean rule, releasing Babylon from the Assyrian yoke. This neo-Babylonian period began with Nabopolassar (625-604 B.C.), the first independent ruler of this new era of prosperity. While Assyria vanished, Babylon continued to influence religion and culture for many centuries, persisting until the conquest by Cyrus in 539 B.C.
