# Avignon

Avignon (historically *Avenio*) is a city in south-eastern France and the capital of the department of Vaucluse. It is situated on the left bank of the Rhône river, a few miles above where the river meets the Durance, and is located 143 miles south of Lyons. As of 1906, the city had a population of 35,356.

## Early History and Governance
Originally, Avignon was an important town of the Cavares, a Gallic tribe. During the Roman period, it became one of the leading cities of Gallia Narbonensis. In the following centuries, the town was severely harassed by the Saracens and during various barbarian invasions. Its political administration shifted frequently, as it was attached successively to the domains of the counts of Toulouse, Provence, and Forcalquier, as well as to the kingdoms of Arles and Burgundy.

By the end of the 12th century, Avignon had become a republic. However, in 1226, Louis VIII dismantled the city and took it as punishment for its support of the Albigenses. In 1251, the city was forced to submit to the counts of Provence and Toulouse.

## The Papal Seat
In 1309, Clement V chose Avignon as his residence, and the city served as the papal seat until 1377. During this era, the popes constructed the city's ramparts, which remain as some of the finest examples of medieval fortification. These walls feature machicolated battlements, several gateways (three of which date from the 14th century), and thirty-nine massive towers.

In 1348, the city was sold to Clement VI by Joanna, the countess of Provence. After Gregory XI migrated back to Rome, Avignon became the residence of two antipopes: Benedict XIII and Clement VII. Benedict XIII was eventually expelled from the city in 1408. The popes continued to possess and govern the town via legates until 1791, when the National Assembly annexed it. This annexation was marked by bloodshed resulting from conflicts between Republicans and adherents of the Papacy. Later, in 1815, the town was the site of the assassination of Marshal Brune by royalist adherents.

## Geography and Architecture
The city occupies a large, oval-shaped area characterized by gardens and parks. A suspension bridge connects Avignon to Villeneuve-les-Avignon. Further up the river is the Pont Saint-Benezet, a ruined 12th-century bridge of which only four of the original eighteen piles remain; one pile supports the small Romanesque chapel of Saint-Benezet.

The city's layout features narrow, often badly paved streets, with public life centered on the Cours de la Republique and the Place de l'Hotel de Ville. At the northern end of the town, the Rocher des Doms is a precipitous rock forming a plateau. This summit houses a public garden, the Palace of the Popes, and the cathedral of Notre-Dame des Doms. The cathedral is a Romanesque building primarily from the 12th century, featuring a gilded statue of the Virgin on its western tower and the 14th-century Gothic mausoleum of Pope John XXII. The adjacent Palace of the Popes is a sombre complex of buildings covering more than 1 1/4 acres.

## Economy and Infrastructure
Avignon is located within a fertile district and maintains a large trade in its products. The city's industrial capabilities include leather-works, oil-works, distilleries, and flour-mills. It is known for producing chemicals, liquorice, soap, and fabrics such as sarsanet.

The city's administrative and educational infrastructure includes a lycee, a training college, a chamber of commerce, and a branch of the Bank of France. It also houses tribunals of commerce and first instance, as well as a council of trade-arbitrators. The city is the seat of an archbishop; the bishopric was founded in the 3rd century and was elevated to an archbishopric in 1475.

Climatically, Avignon is subject to violent winds, most notably the *mistral*. This is reflected in a popular proverb: *Avenio ventosa, sine vento venenosa, cum vento fastidiosa* (windy Avignon, pest-ridden when there is no wind, wind-pestered when there is).
