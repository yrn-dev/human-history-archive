# Aristobulus and the Roman Intervention

## Conflict and the Rise of Aristobulus
The political instability in Judea was marked by a rivalry between two brothers: Hyrcanus II, the elder son of Salome Alexandra, and the energetic Aristobulus, the younger son. While Hyrcanus had been appointed high priest, Aristobulus chafed at his exclusion from office. Taking advantage of his mother's illness, Aristobulus and his domestics seized approximately twenty strongholds. Using the funds discovered in these fortresses, he recruited mercenary soldiers and declared himself king. Following the death of Salome Alexandra, Hyrcanus agreed to retire in favor of his brother.

## Confrontation with Pompey
The dispute between the brothers eventually led to the interference of the Romans. Pompey, acting as arbitrator, initially treated Aristobulus civilly to prevent a national revolt. However, Aristobulus ignored Pompey's orders to remain quiet and marched into Judea. 

When Pompey pitched his camp at Jericho, Aristobulus repented and entered Jerusalem, promising Pompey money and requesting a peaceful resolution. Pompey forgave him and sent soldiers under Gabinius to receive the city and the funds. However, Aristobulus's soldiers blocked the agreement, excluding Gabinius from the city and withholding the money. In response, Pompey imprisoned Aristobulus and besieged Jerusalem, eventually taking the city by force.

Prior to the siege, Pompey had conducted an expedition against Aristobulus, who had fled to a magnificent fortress on a mountain called Alexandrium. Though Aristobulus initially wished to fight, he complied with Pompey's command to come down after being urged by friends who feared the irresistible power of the Romans. Aristobulus attempted to plead the justice of his cause to maintain his government, but Pompey forced him to deliver up his fortified places by writing to their governors.

## Imprisonment and Release by Caesar
Following the Roman victory, Aristobulus was placed in bonds. Later, after Caesar took control of Rome and the empire—following the flight of Pompey and the senate beyond the Ionian Sea—Caesar released Aristobulus from his bonds. Recognizing him as a potent man in his country, Caesar resolved to send him into Syria to set matters right, delivering two legions to him for this purpose.

## Death of Aristobulus and Alexander
Despite the power granted to him by Caesar, Aristobulus was unable to achieve his goals. He was intercepted and destroyed by poison administered by those of Pompey's party. Members of Caesar's party buried him, but his body remained embalmed in honey for a significant period. Eventually, Antony sent the body to Judea to be interred in the royal sepulcher.

The fate of Aristobulus's son, Alexander, was similarly grim. Pompey sent a command to Scipio to slay Alexander, based on accusations of offenses the young man had committed against the Romans. Scipio executed this order at Antioch by beheading him.

## Aftermath and the Fate of the Family
Following the deaths of Aristobulus and Alexander, Ptolemy, the son of Menneus and ruler of Chalcis under Mount Libanus, provided refuge for the remaining siblings. Ptolemy sent his son, Philippion, to Ascalon to bring Aristobulus's wife's children—Antigonus and his sisters—to him. 

Philippion fell in love with one of the daughters, Alexandra, and married her. However, Ptolemy later slew his own son and married Alexandra himself. Following this marriage, Ptolemy took greater care of Alexandra's brother and sister. Antigonus, the sole surviving son of Aristobulus, was later set up as king in 40 B.C. by the Parthians.
