# Building Construction

Building construction is the branch of the science of architecture that focuses on the practical execution of the works required to produce any structure. The successful planning of a building depends on several key conditions: ease of access, good light, good service, a pleasing environment and approaches, and minimum cost achieved through true economy. In the specific case of office buildings, the ability to easily rearrange the space to suit tenants is also necessary.

Architects are expected to be practically acquainted with all modes of operation across the various trades and arts used in building. This includes the ability to minutely estimate the absolute cost of a proposed structure, which requires the skill of measuring work and ascertaining the quantities to be completed. While a quantity surveyor typically handles measuring at an advanced stage, the architect usually "cubes" a building at a price per foot cube.

## Regulatory Frameworks and Planning
The construction and planning of buildings are governed by various authorities and rules. In England, the board of education governs the planning of public schools, while churches are governed by societies that assist in financing, with the Incorporated Church Building Society exercising the strongest control. Factories in both France and England must be planned to meet separate acts.

In London, dwelling-houses are subject to numerous building acts that regulate materials, methods of employment, wall thickness, drainage, roof inclination, space at the rear, and means of escape from fire; these laws specifically forbid timber framed buildings. In contrast, timber buildings are allowed in parts of New York. For combined premises used as a shop and dwelling-house in London, if the area exceeds 10 squares or 1,000 square feet, the stairs and a large portion of the building must be constructed from fire-resisting materials. Similarly, certain London flats require fire-resisting corridors and stairs. In London, Paris, and New York, public buildings and theatres must conform to regulations regarding construction, seating, exits, and the stage (including the flies and scenery dock).

Fire insurance companies also influence construction by laying down requirements based on the building's size and trade, which in turn determines the premium rate. Some districts in England, such as Letchworth, Herts, allow for the use of sound materials untrammelled by by-laws because model by-laws are not in force there.

## Materials and Methods

### Brickwork
Brickmaking was introduced to Britain nearly 2,000 years ago but was likely lost after the Romans withdrew. Until the 13th century, burnt bricks were rarely made in England, and those used were generally salvaged from Roman remains at sites like St Albans Abbey and Colchester. Little Wenham Hall in Suffolk, built around A.D. 1210, is one of the earliest brick buildings after the revival of the art. Bricks became common for important edifices in the 15th century and reached a state of great perfection during the reign of Henry VIII, likely due to workmen from Flanders, as seen in Hampton Court Palace and the older parts of St James's Palace.

Until the Great Fire of London in 1666, smaller buildings and shops were typically timber frameworks filled with plaster and lath. Following the fire, bricks were used extensively. From the end of the 17th century, bricks became the exclusive material for ordinary buildings, except in areas where good brick-clay was unavailable and building stone was plentiful. In 1625, a statute regulated brick sizes to a standard of 9 x 4½ x 3 inches. A tax on bricks, averaging 4s. 7d. per thousand for ordinary bricks, was levied in 1784 and repealed in 1850.

### Carpentry and Framing
Frame buildings feature sides covered with siding fastened to a sheathing of rough boards nailed to studs. Siding options include shingles, wooden tiles, matched boards placed diagonally, or clapboards (also called weather boards). Clapboards are thin boards, thicker at one edge, arranged horizontally so the thick edge overlaps the thin edge of the board below.

### Mosaic and Specialized Construction
Mosaic work consists of small pieces of glass, pottery, stone, or marble laid in ornamental patterns as wall lining or paving. This requires a firm concrete bed with a cement float approximately half to three-quarters of an inch thick. Roman mosaics use various colored marble cubes pressed into the float. "Terazzo mosaic" is a less costly version created by strewing irregular marble chips over the surface, pressing them in with a plasterer's hand float, and rolling them with an iron roller.

Other specialized methods include the use of steel dovetailed sheets fixed between small steel stanchions and plastered in cement. This fire- and burglar-proof method was used for the Newgate prison cells in London (with walls only 3 to 4 inches thick), the British pavilion at the 1900 Paris Exhibition, and buildings in India (Delhi durbar), Venezuela, and South Africa.

## International Comparisons: London and New York
The New York building code provides more detailed prescriptions for material strength and proportion than the London Building Act. New York regulations specify that bricks must be hard, well-burned, and good, and that mortar sand must be clean, sharp, grit sand free from dirt. New York laws also define the strengths of cast-iron and steel, and provide tests for new materials.

New York requirements include the use of sheath-piling for excavations to prevent earth from caving in and the filing of statements regarding soil character at the level of footings. New York's code also specifically addresses wind pressure, calculations for the strength of materials, and floor loads. In contrast, English acts do not define the quality of steel or timber. Iron and steel construction is noted as being in its infancy in England compared to America.

Regarding fire safety, New York code requires all tenement houses to have an iron escape ladder. London introduced a similar measure in 1907, requiring all existing buildings over 50 feet in height to have means of escape to and from the roof, and all existing projecting one-storey shops to have fire-resisting roofs.
