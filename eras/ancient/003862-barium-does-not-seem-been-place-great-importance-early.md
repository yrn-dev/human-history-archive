# Barium

The term Barium refers to both a historical location in Italy and a metallic chemical element.

## Barium (City)
Barium was not considered a place of great importance during early antiquity, with only bronze coins struck by the city having been discovered. During the Roman period, the city served as a junction for the Via Traiana and the coast road, and it maintained a branch road leading to Tarentum. Its harbour, which was mentioned as early as 181 B.C., was likely the primary harbour for the district in ancient times and served as a center for fishery.

The city's significance increased in 852 when it became a seat of Saracen power, and later in 885 when it became the residence of the Byzantine governor. In 1071, Barium was captured by Robert Guiscard. The city was the site where Peter the Hermit preached the first crusade in 1095. Barium has suffered destruction several times, including in 1156 when it was razed to the ground. By the 14th century, it had become an independent duchy. In 1558, Bona Sforza left the city to Philip II of Spain and Naples.

## Barium (Chemical Element)
Barium is a metallic chemical element belonging to the alkaline earth group, represented by the symbol Ba. Its name is derived from the Greek word *barus*, meaning "heavy," due to its presence in witherite and barytes (also known as heavy spar). 

### Discovery and Isolation
V. Casciorolus, a shoemaker from Bologna, first investigated heavy spar in 1602, noting that it became phosphorescent after ignition with combustible substances; consequently, it was often called Bolognian phosphorus. In 1774, K. W. Scheele discovered a new substance while examining pyrolusite, finding that treatment with sulphuric acid produced an insoluble salt identical to that found in heavy spar.

The metal is difficult to isolate. Sir H. Davy attempted to electrolyse baryta without success, but later obtained a barium amalgam by using barium chloride with mercury, distilling off the mercury to leave a silver-white residue. In 1854, R. Bunsen obtained a barium amalgam by electrolysing a thick paste of barium chloride and dilute hydrochloric acid at 100°C. In 1901, A. N. Guntz used a mercury cathode to electrolyse a saturated solution of barium chloride, producing a 3% barium amalgam which, when heated electrically in a porcelain tube to about 1000°C, yielded pure barium.

### Physical and Chemical Properties
Freshly cut barium possesses a silver-white lustre and is slightly harder than lead. It is extremely easy to oxidize when exposed, attacks both alcohol and water, and is soluble in liquid ammonia. Its atomic weight has been determined by various researchers: C. Marignac found a mean value of 136.84, while T. W. Richards obtained a value of 137.44 in 1893.

Barium and its salts can be detected by the yellowish-green color they produce when heated in a Bunsen flame after being moistened with hydrochloric acid, or by the two characteristic green lines seen in their spectra. In solution, barium salts can be distinguished from calcium salts by the immediate precipitate they form when calcium sulphate is added, or by the yellow precipitate of barium chromate formed upon the addition of potassium chromate.

### Compounds and Applications
Barium occurs naturally primarily as witherite ($\text{BaCO}_3$) and barytes ($\text{BaSO}_4$), and to a lesser extent in baryto-calcite, baryto-celestine, and complex silicates.

*   **Barium Sulphate ($\text{BaSO}_4}$):** The most abundant natural barium compound, it is practically insoluble in water and only slightly soluble in dilute acids. It is used as a pigment known as *blanc fixe* or "permanent white."
*   **Barium Nitrate ($\text{Ba(NO}_3)_2$):** This compound crystallizes in octahedra and melts at 597°C. It is used extensively in pyrotechny to create green fire.
*   **Barium Chloride ($\text{BaCl}_2 \cdot 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$):** A strong poison with a bitter taste, it is readily soluble in water but almost insoluble in absolute alcohol and concentrated hydrochloric acid.
*   **Barium Dioxide ($\text{BaO}_2$):** A grey powder used as an intermediate product in the Brin process for manufacturing oxygen.
*   **Other Compounds:** Barium also forms a nitride ($\text{Ba}_3\text{N}_2$), an amide ($\text{Ba(NH}_2)_2$), a carbonate ($\text{BaCO}_3$), a bromide ($\text{BaBr}_2$), a bromate ($\text{Ba(BrO}_3)_2$), a chlorate ($\text{Ba(ClO}_3)_2$), an iodate ($\text{Ba(IO}_3)_2$), a carbide ($\text{BaC}_2$), and a sulphide ($\text{BaS}$).
