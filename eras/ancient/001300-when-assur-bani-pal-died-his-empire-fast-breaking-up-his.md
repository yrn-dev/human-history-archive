# The Reign and Decline of Assur-bani-pal

Assur-bani-pal, whose name means "Assur creates a son," was a grand monarque of Assyria and the son of Esar-haddon. He was publicly nominated as king of Assyria on the 12th of Iyyar prior to his father's death. Upon succeeding Esar-haddon in 668 B.C., he inherited an empire that included both Assyria and Babylonia. To satisfy Babylonian national sentiment, his twin-brother, Samas-sum-yukin, was appointed viceroy of Babylonia, providing the region with a semblance of independence.

## Military Campaigns and Imperial Height
The initial priority of Assur-bani-pal's reign was the completion of the Egyptian campaign. After Tirhakah, who had reoccupied Egypt, fled to Ethiopia, the Assyrian army ascended the Nile from Memphis to Thebes over forty days. Despite a general revolt headed by Thebes and the subsequent assault and capture of Memphis, Assur-bani-pal deployed fresh forces to the province. The Assyrian army once again ascended the Nile, plundering Thebes and destroying its temples; two obelisks were taken to Nineveh as trophies. Simultaneously, the Assyrians pressed the siege of insular Tyre, cutting off its water supply until the city surrendered.

At the height of his power, Assur-bani-pal expanded his influence across Western Asia. The Assyrians captured the capital of the land of the Manna, south-east of Ararat, reducing its king to vassalage. In Elam, a war resulted in the overthrow of the Elamite army and the leveling of the capital, Susa (or Shushan). The head of the Elamite king Teumman was sent to Nineveh, and a new king, Umman-igas, was appointed by the Assyrians. During this period, the kings of Tabal and Cilicia offered their daughters to Assur-bani-pal's harem, and embassies arrived from Ararat and Gyges of Lydia.

## Internal Conflict and the Babylonian Revolt
The arrangement between the two brothers failed to maintain stability. Samas-sum-yukin became more Babylonian than his subjects, reviving the Sumerian language as the official tongue and claiming succession to monarchs whose empire reached the Mediterranean. This led to a revolt that shook the foundations of the Assyrian empire. After several years of struggle—during which Egypt regained its independence in 660 B.C. with help from Lydian mercenaries—Babylon was starved into surrender. Assur-bani-pal put the rebel viceroy and his supporters to death. While he purified the city and performed a "service of reconciliation," he did not "take the hands" of Bel-Merodach, the ceremony required to legitimize power as the inheritor of the old Babylonian empire.

## Cultural Contributions and Character
Assur-bani-pal is described as a magnificent patron of art and literature, though he was also characterized as cruel, superstitious, luxurious, and indolent. He frequently entrusted army commands to others while appropriating their successes. His most significant contribution was the creation of the great library of Nineveh, located in a palace he built on the northern side of the mound of Kuyunjik. The palace was lined with sculptured slabs representing the peak of Assyrian art. Scribes were employed to copy older Babylonian tablets, although Assur-bani-pal's personal interests leaned toward astrology and omens.

## The Collapse of the Assyrian Empire
The extensive wars in Elam and Babylonia left Assyria maimed and exhausted, drained of both wealth and its fighting population. The devastated provinces could not supply the imperial exchequer, and the state struggled to garrison conquered populations. This exhaustion left Assyria unable to resist the Cimmerian and Scythian hordes. Assur-bani-pal regarded the death of the Cimmerian leader Dugdamme (whom he called "a limb of Satan") as a mark of divine favor, but other Scythian invaders continued to pour over western Asia.

When Assur-bani-pal died in approximately 626 B.C., the empire was already in decay. Under his successor, Assur-etil-ilani, Scythians penetrated as far as the borders of Egypt and burned Calah. Although the walls of Nineveh protected the remnants of the Assyrian army, the subsequent construction of a new palace among the ruins was small and architecturally poor, reflecting the low ebb of Assyrian resources.

The final stages of the empire saw disputed rule between Assur-etil-ilani and his vizier, Assur-sum-lisir. The last king of Assyria was likely Sin-sar-iskun (possibly the Sarakos of Berossus), the brother of Assur-etil-ilani. Sin-sar-iskun attempted to restore ruined temples and their priests, coupling the gods Merodach of Babylon with Assur of Nineveh. However, Babylonia remained restless. Nabopolassar, the successor to the viceroy Kandalanu, entered into a war with the last Assyrian king. Aided by the Scythian king of Ecbatana, Cyaxares, the Babylonians captured and destroyed Nineveh and the cities of northern Babylonia that had sided with Assyria, bringing the Assyrian empire to an end in 606 B.C.
