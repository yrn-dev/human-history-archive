# FROM THE COMMENCEMENT OF THE SACRED WAR TO THAT OF THE OLYNTHIAN WAR

## The Rise of Philip and Early Hostilities
During the Social War, Philip of Macedonia expanded his power in Thrace and Macedonia at the expense of Athens. He acquired Amphipolis—which he captured under false assurances—as well as Pydna and Potidæa, the latter two being taken directly from Athens. Philip further strengthened his position by enlisting Olynthus as both an ally and an enemy of the Athenians. This initiated a conflict, often referred to as the war about Amphipolis, which continued for twelve years without a formal peace.

Athens' initial resistance to these aggressions was ineffective and faint, partly due to various embarrassments. During this period, Philip conducted a series of hostile measures without an express declaration of war, depriving Athens of valuable maritime possessions. The loss of Potidæa was particularly significant, as it was the most important Thracian possession after Amphipolis. The relief sent by Athens to Potidæa was tardy; the relieving force consisted of a general and mercenary foreigners who, lacking pay, postponed their mission to plunder elsewhere for profit. Consequently, Potidæa fell to Philip and the Olynthians, and the impoverished Athenian settlers (Kleruchs) were expelled from their properties.

## The Sacred War and Shifting Alliances
While the Social War was still ongoing, a new and more formidable complication emerged known as the Sacred War. This conflict divided the Hellenic world and proved profitable only to Philip. 

Parallel to these events, sentiments at Olynthus began to change. Fearing Philip, the Olynthians sought a new alignment and sent an embassy to Athens in 352 B.C. to make peace with their only sure ally. By 350 B.C., a second embassy was sent to seek Athenian help.

## The Outbreak of the Olynthian War
The Olynthian war began around 350–349 B.C. Philip's unfriendly feelings toward Olynthus ripened into war, fueled in part by the fact that Philip's fugitive half-brothers had obtained shelter there. Philip employed intrigues, corruption, and the fomenting of internal discord to weaken the city. 

Philip launched attacks against the Olynthians and the Chalkidians, opening war against the Chalcidic towns of the Olynthian League in 349 B.C. In response, the Olynthians concluded an alliance with Athens.

## The Olynthiac Orations of Demosthenes
The Athenian response to the crisis was characterized by the speeches of Demosthenes, an opposition speaker who sought to spur the city to action.

*   **The Second Olynthiac:** This was the earliest of the speeches. Demosthenes argued that Philip's strength was overrated and advocated for a vigorous, joint aggressive war carried on by Athens and Olynthus.
*   **The First Olynthiac:** As Philip continued to press the Olynthian confederacy and bad news arrived from Thrace, Demosthenes delivered this more urgent oration. His tone shifted from the offensive to the defensive, warning that if the Olynthians and their confederate cities were ruined, nothing would stop Philip from marching into Attica. He also addressed the question of the Theôric (festival) Fund.
*   **The Third Olynthiac:** Spoken in 348 B.C., this speech dealt with practical details, insisting that citizens serve in person and that the festival fund be used for the war effort.

Despite these appeals, Athens largely followed its habit of inaction. While they accepted the alliance, they took no active steps to cooperate, leaving the Olynthians and Chalkidians to face Philip alone.

## The Fall of Olynthus and Eubœan Complications
Athens eventually sent some assistance, leading to partial success, but this created an exaggerated confidence in Athens, causing them to lose sight of the danger. Meanwhile, Philip continued his advance, mastering several Chalkidic cities. 

Simultaneously, Athens faced the revolt of Eubœa. Philip intrigued in Eubœa, and Plutarch of Eretria requested Athenian aid. Despite Demosthenes' dissuasion, aid was sent under Phokion. Although Plutarch proved treacherous, Phokion achieved a victory at Tamynæ. Following this, in March 349 B.C., Demosthenes was insulted by Meidias and reproached for his absence from the battle of Tamynæ; in response, he served in Eubœa as a hoplite and was named senator for 349-348 B.C.

The Olynthian conflict ended in disaster. Olynthus and the thirty-two towns of the confederacy were destroyed. The destruction was so complete that Demosthenes later remarked that one could walk over the sites without knowing such cities had ever existed. This victory ensured that Philip could no longer be stopped outside of Greece.
