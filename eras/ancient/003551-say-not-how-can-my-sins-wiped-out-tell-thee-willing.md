# Sins and Forgiveness in Sacred and Historical Texts

The concept of sin, its consequences, and the means of its removal are addressed across various historical and sacred records, ranging from the teachings of the Qur'an to the hagiographic accounts of Christian figures and the rites of the early church.

## Sins and Divine Forgiveness in the Qur'an

The Qur'an identifies several specific actions as sinful. Intoxicants (strong drink/wine) and games of chance (gambling) are described as containing "great sin." While these activities may offer some utility or profit for men, the text asserts that the sin associated with them is greater than any profit. Additionally, the act of undeservedly maligning or speaking evil of believing men and women is characterized as a "manifest sin" and the "guilt of slander."

The text presents a vision of a divine judge, Allah, who is described as the All-knowing Judge. It is taught that the Lord will gather all people together to judge between them with truth. For those who reject faith and disobey the messenger, the Day of Judgment is presented as a time of such distress that they will wish they were level with the ground, though they will be unable to hide any word or fact from Allah.

However, the Qur'an also emphasizes that Allah is "Oft-Forgiving" and "Merciful." Those who believe, emigrate to escape persecution, and strive in the way of Allah are said to have hope in His mercy. The text further notes that Allah "doth blot out sins and forgive again and again." In the context of ceremonial purity, believers are instructed not to approach prayers with a "mind befogged" or in a state of impurity; they are commanded to wash their whole body or, if water is unavailable, to use clean sand or earth to rub their faces and hands.

## Human Response to Suffering and Sin

The archive's sources describe a pattern where messengers were sent to many nations who were then visited with tribulation, adversity, and affliction. The purpose of this suffering was to encourage these people to learn humility. In some instances, however, the texts state that hearts became hardened and Satan made sinful acts appear alluring, preventing the people from learning humility even when suffering reached them.

In times of distress, the text suggests that humans tend to call upon Allah, and if it is His will, He removes the cause of the distress. Following this relief, people may forget the partners or false gods they had previously ascribed to Him.

## Asceticism and Penance in Christian Tradition

Other historical accounts illustrate the pursuit of a sinless life through extreme personal sacrifice. One account describes a one-eyed Christian cobbler who was regarded as a holy, just, and righteous man. This individual led a life of chastity and uprightness, fasted, attended Mass daily, and gave a portion of his earnings to God. 

The cobbler's loss of an eye was a result of his desire to avoid sin. After experiencing "sinful thoughts" while measuring a woman's foot, he recalled the Holy Evangel's teaching that if an eye offends, one should pluck it out and cast it away rather than sin. Consequently, he used his stitching awl to destroy his own eye to ensure his spiritual purity.

## Rites of Initiation and Grace

In the early church, the process of admission to the faith involved a period of instruction known as the catechumenate. Candidates for baptism were examined by the bishop and clergy, who verified with neighbors whether the candidates had led "exemplary lives" worthy of admission. Sponsors were often required to vouch for the candidate's sincerity of repentance and their past life.

The archive notes a theological discussion regarding the necessity of physical rites. Macarius of Jerusalem declared that the grace of the spirit is granted in response to prayers and entreaties, suggesting that a font is not strictly necessary, but rather the "wish and desire for grace." Conversely, Tertullian argued that such a desire is not always sufficient for the granting of the holy rite of baptism.
