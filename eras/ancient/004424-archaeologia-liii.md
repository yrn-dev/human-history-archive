# Early Christian Basilicas

The early Christian basilica was a religious structure characterized by specific architectural features designed to facilitate worship and ecclesiastical functions. While the term "basilica" was applied to various types of structures—including civil buildings and even meeting-places for pagan religious associations—the early Christian version developed distinct characteristics that separated it from the normal columned civil basilica.

## Architectural Layout and Features
A typical early Christian basilica began with a porch supported on pillars, which led into an open court known as an *atrium*. This court was surrounded by a colonnaded cloister. In the center of the atrium, a fountain or cistern, referred to as a *phiale* or *cantharus*, was provided for ablutions and drinking. 

Adjacent to the atrium, frequently positioned to the west, was the baptistery, which was usually octagonal in shape. Entry into the main church was gained through a long, narrow porch called the *narthex*. This area served as a boundary; those under ecclesiastical censure or penitents were forbidden from passing beyond the narthex.

Admission to the interior of the church was provided by three or more lofty doorways, the number of which corresponded to the number of aisles. These doorways were set in marble cases and featured doors made of bronze (often gilt) or rich wood, elaborately carved with scriptural subjects. To protect the interior from winter cold and summer heat, magnificent curtains embroidered with sacred scenes or figures were used to close the entrance.

## Interior Structure and the Apse
The interior of the early Christian basilica was dominated by a central nave that terminated in the semi-dome of an apse. This vaulted apse is a primary distinguishing feature from the normal civil basilica, as there is no known instance of a vaulted apse in a standard columned civil basilica, with the doubtful exception of the site at Silchester. Furthermore, early Christian basilicas in the west very seldom, if ever, featured galleries over the side aisles.

In certain regions, such as Syria and North Africa, the apse was flanked by two chambers known as the *diaconicon* and *prothesis*. These functioned as sacristies and were cut off from the rest of the church. While these features are almost universal in North African and Syrian churches, they are rare in Italy.

## Regional Examples and Survivals
Various examples of basilican churches exist across the East and West:

*   **The East:** Notable examples include the church of St Demetrius at Thessalonica, St Philip at Athens, Bosra in Arabia, Xanthus in Lycia, and the church of Dana located between Antioch and Bir. Additionally, Justinian built the monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai.
*   **North Africa:** The Roman province of North Africa contains abundant remains, with over 130 examples noted by S. Gsell.
*   **England:** England possesses more early Christian survivals than Germany or France. In 1892, excavations of the Roman city of Silchester revealed the remains of a small 4th-century basilica. Another early example is found at Brixworth in Northamptonshire, likely erected around A.D. 680 by Saxulphus, the abbot of Peterborough. The Brixworth church featured a nave divided from its aisles by quadrangular piers and arches of Roman brick, with clerestory windows and a short chancel ending in an apse. A circumscribing crypt, entered by steps from the chancel, ran outside the apse.
*   **Continental Europe:** Strictly early Christian basilicas are not currently found in Spain, France, or Germany. However, Germany has an unaltered basilica from the time of Charles the Great at Michelstadt in the Odenwald. In France, the "Basse Oeuvre" at Beauvais may date from Carolingian times. Later developments include the 11th-century basilica of St Mauritius near Hildesheim and a modern revival of the form in Munich.
