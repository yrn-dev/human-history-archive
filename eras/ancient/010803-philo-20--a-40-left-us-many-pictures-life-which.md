# Philo

Philo (20 B.C.–A.D. 40) was a writer and philosopher whose work provides detailed depictions of a life dedicated to the highest wisdom. His perspectives were influenced by a respectable form of cynicism that was deeply rooted among the Greek Jews of his era.

## Philosophical Views and Asceticism
In his tract *On Change of Names*, Philo describes the "good and wise" as individuals who, by their own free will, divested themselves of excessive wealth and rejected things dear to the flesh. He contrasts the "disciples of wisdom" with athletes; while athletes possess strong, lusty bodies to serve as a fortification for the soul, the disciples of wisdom are described as pale, wasted, and reduced to skeletons. This physical state is the result of sacrificing bodily strength to the faculties of the soul.

Philo's favorite ascetics were the Therapeutae, a group centered in Egypt. These individuals renounced all property and the temptations associated with it. Their commitment to this path involved the irrevocable abandonment of their fatherlands, their parents, wives, children, brothers, friends, and large groups of kinsmen.

## Intellectual Influences and Works
Philo's association with Platonism was famous to a proverb. According to the archive's records, the Therapeutae studied philosophy and gave preference to that of Plato. It has been ascertained that Philo's theological works were composed before the death of Christ, and most likely before his birth.

## Political Activity
Philo served as a principal ambassador for the Jews in a legation to Caius. While Josephus asserts that the ambassadors from Alexandria to Caius numbered only three for the Jews and three for the Gentiles, Philo states that the number of Jewish ambassadors was no fewer than five toward the end of his legation.

## Personal Connections
Philo had a brother named Alexander, who served as the alabarch, or governor of the Jews, at Alexandria.
