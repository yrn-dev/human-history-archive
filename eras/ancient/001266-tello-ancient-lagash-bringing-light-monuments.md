# Tello and Ancient Lagash

Tello is the site of the ancient city of Lagash, which played a significant role in early Babylonian history. Located eastward of the Euphrates and southward of Sippara and Kutha, it was situated probably eastward of the Shatt el-Hai, which is believed to be the ancient channel of the Tigris.

## Archaeological Excavations
Between 1877 and 1881, the French consul de Sarzec conducted excavations at Tello. His work brought to light monuments from the pre-Semitic age, including diorite statues of Gudea, which are currently housed in the Louvre. Inscriptions on these statues indicate that the diorite was brought from Magan, the Sinaitic peninsula. Subsequent excavations by de Sarzec in Tello and the surrounding area extended the known history of the city back to at least 4000 B.C. Among the discoveries was a collection of more than 30,000 tablets, which had been arranged on shelves during the time of Gudea (approximately 2700 B.C.).

## Early Rulers and Dynasties
The monuments found at Lagash provide some of the earliest approximately datable records of the region. Early texts mention a "king of Kengi" and Me-silim, the king of Kis, who had dealings with Lugal-suggur, the high-priest of Lagash, and the high-priest of a neighboring town provisionally transcribed as Gis-ukh.

Over time, the high-priests of Lagash assumed the title of kings, and a dynasty was established by Ur-Nin[=a]. In a building Ur-Nin[=a] attached to the temple of Nin[=a], archaeologists found onyx plates and heads of lions in onyx—described as "booty" dedicated to the goddess Bau—as well as terra-cotta bas-reliefs depicting the king and his sons.

## Expansion and Conflict
The prestige and power of Lagash expanded significantly under E-anna-du, the grandson of Ur-Nin[=a]. E-anna-du became the master of southern Babylonia, including the cities of Ur, Erech, and Larsa, as well as the "district of Sumer." His campaigns extended beyond Babylonia, overrunning part of Elam and capturing the city of Az on the Persian Gulf. During his reign, he rebuilt the town of Nin[=a], excavated reservoirs and canals, and repaired or erected palaces and temples in Lagash and other locations. He also annexed the kingdom of Kis, though it regained independence after his death.

E-anna-du made Gis-ukh a tributary state, levying a grain tax on each person to be paid into the treasuries of the god Ingurisa and the goddess Nin[=a]. This victory was commemorated by the "Stele of the Vultures," now in the Louvre. The stele depicts various war scenes:
* The king in a chariot holding a curved weapon made of three metal bars bound by rings.
* Kilted followers wearing helmets and carrying lances.
* Vultures feeding on the bodies of fallen enemies.
* The creation of a tumulus over those slain on the side of Lagash.

Following E-anna-du, his brother En-anna-tum I succeeded him. During this period, Gis-ukh regained its status as the dominant power; because En-anna-tum held only the title of high-priest, it is probable he acknowledged Ur-lumma of Gis-ukh as his suzerain. However, the prestige of Lagash was later restored by Entemena, the son and successor of En-anna-tum. Entemena subdued Gis-ukh and appointed a priest named Illi as its governor.

## Artistic and Religious Significance
The excavations at Lagash revealed a high level of artistic skill, particularly in goldsmithing. A silver tripod dedicated by Entemena to his god, now in the Louvre, features a globular part adorned with the eagle crest of Lagash and a neck encircled by a skillfully incised frieze of deer and ibexes being devoured by lions. Entemena also dedicated a calcite vase, which was discovered at Nippur.

In terms of religious history, the site of Shirgulla in Lagash was the chief seat of the deity Ninib. In the evolution of Babylonian religious literature, Ninib became identified as the sun-god of the morning and the springtime, associated with bringing new life and joy to the earth.
