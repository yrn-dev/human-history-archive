# The Life of the Buddha: The First Long Episode

The historical Buddha, known by the family name Gotama, was the son of Suddhodana, a chief of the Sakiyas, who were among the republican clans in India. According to Buddhist theory, a "Buddha" appears in the world periodically to preach the true doctrine; once this teaching is corrupted or lost over time, it is not restored until a new Buddha appears.

## Canonical Sources and Authorities
The early history of the Buddha is documented in several Canonical Pali sources, which reached their current form before the 4th century B.C. These records consist largely of episodes, some of which are categorized as "long episodes." 

The "First long episode" is primarily recorded in the *Majjhima Nikaya* (specifically at ii. 93), with various parts repeated in sections i. 163-175 and 240-249, as well as ii. 212. Additional accounts of this period are found in the *Vinaya* (vol. i. pp. 1-13). Other related early accounts include the *Digha Nikaya*, the *Anguttara Nikaya*, and the *Sutta Nipata*.

## The Going Forth and Attainment
The "First long episode" encompasses several critical stages of the Buddha's early spiritual journey. It begins with "the going forth," a transition that is also described in an old ballad found in the *Sutta Nipata* and a prose account in the *Digha Nikaya*. 

Following his departure, the episode details the Buddha's years of study and penance. This period of rigorous effort culminated in the attainment of Buddhahood and the reaching of Nirvana.

## Early Ministry
After achieving enlightenment, the Buddha began the process of sharing his insights. The First long episode concludes with the conversion of his first five converts. This event serves as the transition to the "Second long episode," which tracks the Buddha's activities from the conversation with these five converts through the end of the first year of his teaching.

## Historical Perspective
The biography of the Buddha has been subject to scholarly debate. In 1854, Professor Wilson of Oxford presented a paper to the Royal Asiatic Society of London arguing that the Buddha was an imaginary being and his life a myth. However, current academic consensus rejects this view, acknowledging that while miraculous tales surround his history, there is a sufficient basis of truth to construct an intelligent historical account.
