# The Bronze Age

The Bronze Age represents a distinct stage of human civilization characterized by the production of weapons, utensils, and implements that differ in size and design from those of the preceding Stone Age or succeeding eras.

## European Bronze Age
In Europe, the Bronze Age is marked by a consistency in the pattern and size of objects across different regions, which some argue suggests a foreign origin for the knowledge of bronze. Specifically, the patterns of arms are described as distinctly oriental. This theory is supported by the design of sword and dagger handles, which are narrow and short, suggesting they were not designed for the large-handed races of Europe.

The implements of this period include:
*   Swords, daggers, and arrow-heads.
*   Knives, gouges, hammers, and awls.

European swords from this era exhibit a handle in the shape of a cross and tracery believed to be an imitation of the Svastika, an ancient Aryan symbol. A leaf-shaped bronze sword, generally lacking a central ridge, has been found across Europe, extending from Lapland to the Mediterranean.

Beyond weaponry, the period is noted for high-workmanship items found in barrows (which served as family or tribal cemeteries), including:
*   Solid gold diadems, coronets, and amulets.
*   Elegant gold and bronze vases.

The Bronze Age also saw a shift in funerary practices; while burial was the rule in the Stone Age, cremation became the mode of disposal for the dead. Cinerary urns, typically 12 to 18 inches in height and decorated with projecting flanges or bands around the upper portion, were used to hold burnt bones. In Denmark, some single mounds have contained as many as seventy deposits of burnt bones. Ornamentation of the period generally consists of concentric circles, bosses, and spirals.

## Bronze Culture in Japan
Archaeological evidence indicates that a bronze culture intervened in southern Japan between the stone and iron phases. Bronze relics have been discovered in the soil of southern and western Japan, specifically in three provinces on the Inland Sea, five provinces of northwest Kyushu, one province facing China and Korea, and on the islands of Tsushima and Iki.

The hand-weapons of this culture include:
*   Swords and daggers (the latter resembling the Malay kris).
*   Halberds and possibly spear-heads.

Many of these weapons, such as the swords and halberds, are leaf-shaped. However, certain features—such as unpierced loops and overshort tangs—suggest they may have been manufactured for ceremonial purposes rather than battle. Some straight-edged specimens resemble ancient bronze swords from China. While bronze arrow-heads are found in both the soil and Yamato sepulchres, other bronze weapons do not occur in Yamato sepulchres.

Some theories suggest these weapons were "mallet-headed swords" (kabutsuchi) used by Keiko's soldiers around A.D. 82 against the Tsuchi-gumo.

## Regional Variations and Related Implements
In other historical contexts, bronze was utilized for specific military equipment. The Assyrians, for example, used helmets made of bronze. In other regions, bronze weapons were eventually superseded by iron; in Japan, the Yamato race utilized iron swords, though approximately ten percent of these were double-edged with a central ridge, which is considered a hammered-iron survival of the earlier bronze leaf-shaped weapon.

Outside of the specific "Bronze Age" classification, various cultures utilized similar metal tools for ritual or combat. The Scythians used awls or daggers to prick their bodies to mingle blood with wine during the making of oaths, subsequently dipping a javelin, battle-axe, arrows, and a sword into the mixture. In other regions, daggers and swords were crafted from steel, such as those found in old Kermán.
