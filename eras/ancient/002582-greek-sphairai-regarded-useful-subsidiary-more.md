# Sphairai

In the context of ancient Greek culture, the term *sphairai* (Greek: [Greek: sphairai]) refers to two distinct applications: the use of balls in physical activity and a specific type of equipment used in pugilism.

## Ball Games and Physical Culture
Among the Greeks, games played with balls (*sphairai*) were viewed as a useful subsidiary to more violent athletic exercises. These activities were utilized as a means to render the body graceful and keep it supple. Such games were generally left to girls and boys.

While regular rules for these games have left little to no trace, the *Onomastikon* of Pollux of Naucratis preserves the names of various forms of ball play. These include:
*   **Aporraxis**: The act of putting the ball on the ground using the open hand.
*   **Ourania**: A game where the ball is flung into the air to be caught by two or more players.
*   **Phaininda**: A game of catch involving two or more players that utilized feinting as a test of skill and quickness.

The use of balls for recreation is noted in other early cultures and texts. Egyptian monuments portray forms of ball games, and they are played by the least advanced savage tribes in the present day. In the works of Homer, Nausicaa is described as playing at ball with her maidens in the land of the Phaeacians, and the figures Halios and Laodamas are noted for performing ball play accompanied by dancing before Odysseus and Alcinous. In contrast, the scriptures of the Hebrews contain no mention of the ball.

## Pugilistic Equipment
The term *sphaerae* (Greek: [Greek: sphairai]) also refers to a specific variety of the *caestus* (or *cestus*), a boxing-glove or gauntlet used by ancient pugilists. The *caestus* existed in several forms:
*   **Meilichae**: The simplest and least dangerous variety, consisting of raw hide strips tied under the palm while leaving the fingers bare. These were used for practice in the *palaestrae*.
*   **Sphaerae**: A more formidable version used for serious contests, which were sewn with leather-covered small metal balls.
*   **Murmekes**: Also known as "limb-breakers" (*guiotoroi*), these were the most terrible variety, studded with heavy nails.

To protect the forearm from heavy blows, these gloves utilized straps (*himantes*) of varying lengths, some of which reached as far as the elbow.

## Related Terminology
The *caestus* used in boxing is distinct from the *cestus* (from the Greek *kentein*, meaning embroidered), which is an adjective used as a noun to describe a girdle. Specifically, the girdle of Aphrodite was believed to possess the power to excite love.
