# Babylonia

Babylonia was a region of Mesopotamia located between the Persian Gulf and the upper waters of the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. Characterized by a climate of enduring sunlight and a constant water supply, the area provided highly favorable conditions for early human settlement. According to Herodotus, wheat in Mesopotamia yielded two hundredfold to the sower, and Pliny noted that it was cut twice, subsequently providing good fodder for sheep. The region was further enriched by many sorts of fruits and abundant palms.

## Geography and Early Settlement
The environmental stability of the region between the Euphrates and Tigris allowed Neolithic men to transition from a wandering existence to permanent settlements. Because the area provided trustworthy year-round supplies of food, water, and fodder, populations multiplied and became denser than previously known. This security allowed for the development of substantial housing and the extermination of wild beasts over large areas, resulting in a society where ordinary people could move through towns and fields without the need for weapons.

## Architecture and Art
The culture of Babylonia was essentially literary, lacking the specific artistic spirit found in Greece or Egypt. Due to a scarcity of stone and an abundance of clay, the Babylonians relied on brick for construction. Babylonian temples were massive, shapeless structures of crude brick supported by buttresses, utilizing drains—one of which at Ur was made of lead—to carry off rain.

The reliance on brick fostered the early development of columns and pilasters, as well as the use of enamelled tiles and frescoes. Walls were often brilliantly colored and occasionally plated with gold or bronze. Painted terra-cotta cones were also embedded into plaster. 

In the arts, Babylonia favored figures in the round over bas-relief; early examples from Tello are described as realistic but somewhat clumsy. The lack of stone made pebbles precious, leading to a high level of perfection in gem-cutting, exemplified by seal-cylinders from the age of Sargon of Akkad. While early metallurgic specimens are rare—with the exception of the silver vase of Entemena—later periods saw great excellence in the creation of gold bracelets and ear-rings, as well as skilled work in copper.

## Influence on Assyria
Assyria acted as a "servile pupil" of Babylonia in many respects, particularly in architecture. Assyrian palaces and temples were built of brick, even though stone was the natural material available in the north. Assyrians even maintained the use of brick platforms, which were necessary for the marshy soil of Babylonia but unnecessary in Assyria. Over time, Assyrian architects moved away from this influence, employing stone for walls and using sculptured, colored stone slabs instead of the painted walls common in Chaldaea.

## Economic and Political Decline
During the reign of Assur-bani-pal, Assyria engaged in a long struggle with Elam. Although Assyria succeeded in leveling the capital of Susa (or Shushan) and wasting the country with fire and sword, the conflict left the Assyrian empire maimed and exhausted. 

The imperial exchequer was drained of wealth, and the fighting population was depleted. The provinces of Babylonia and Elam had been so devastated that they could yield nothing to supply the needs of the imperial exchequer. This economic exhaustion, combined with a shortage of troops to garrison conquered populations, left Assyria ill-prepared to defend its frontiers against the Scythians (referred to by the Babylonians as the Manda).

## Persian Conquest
During the expansion of the Persian Empire, Cyrus marched along the left bank of the Euphrates toward Babylonia. After a grueling thirteen-day march through a desolate desert characterized by wormwood and aromatic shrubs—where soldiers were forced to live on meat due to exhausted flour—the Persian forces reached Pylæ. This location marked the entrance to the cultivated territory of Babylonia, where the army halted for several days to refresh themselves. Later, the king of Babylonia had specially caused a long trench to be dug for the protection of the region, though he eventually shrunk from defending it.
