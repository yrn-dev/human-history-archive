# Proceedings of the Confederacy Under Athens as Head

## Formation and Early Organization
The confederacy under Athenian leadership emerged following a shift in Grecian politics after the repulse of Xerxes. Initially, there had been a steady tendency toward a Pan-Hellenic league presided over by Sparta. However, this union broke up into a schism, creating a bifurcation where Athens and Sparta divided the Grecian world between them. This transition was accelerated by the misconduct of the Spartan Pausanias. While commanding the united Greek fleet in Asia, Pausanias engaged in treasonable correspondence with Xerxes and established a mastery in Byzantium. The Athenians, already recognized as heads of the confederacy, were forced to expel him by force. Consequently, the Asiatic Greeks, breaking away from Pausanias, entreated Athens to organize a new confederacy and act as the presiding city (*Vorort*).

The resulting Confederacy of Delos was framed on principles of equal rights and constant control by its members, attracting the spontaneous adhesion of many maritime and insular Greeks near the Aegean sea. The members swore a perpetual and peremptory oath that allowed for neither evasion nor retirement. The confederacy was organized for common and pressing objects, specifically the necessity of continued action against the Persians following the battles of Mykale and Plataea.

## Administration and the Role of Athens
In the early years, Athens acted as the leader with a level of conduct characterized by the probity of Aristeidês, who handled the initial assessment of tribute raised by the synod of Delos. Athens served as the guardian of the Aegean sea against piracy. During this period, Athens undertook several operations, including actions at Skyros, Eion, and Karystus. Under the leadership of Kimon, the confederacy achieved a significant victory over the Persians at the river Eurymedon.

The administration of the confederacy evolved over time. Initially, the synod of Delos held importance, but it gradually declined and vanished. A significant shift occurred when the allies themselves demanded the substitution of money-payments for personal service. This arrangement suited the interests and feelings of Athens and contributed to the city's maritime improvement. The confederate fund was eventually transferred from Delos to Athens; this move was proposed by the Samians, the second power in the confederacy, who believed the funds would be safer in Athens than on an island requiring a permanent garrison and squadron.

## Expansion and Internal Conflict
As the confederacy grew, Athens faced the first signs of internal instability. Naxos was the first member to revolt, though it was subsequently reconquered. Later, Thasos revolted, leading to a two-year blockade and siege by Athenians under Kimon. Following its reduction, Thasos was dismantled and disarmed. Athens also attempted to found a city at Ennea Hodoi on the Strymon above Eion, but the attempt failed and the settlers were slain.

Over time, a distinction emerged between the original confederacy of Delos and the Athenian empire that grew out of it. Athens began to employ the tribute and force of its subject-allies in wars against other Greeks to sustain Athenian predominance. This use of force for purposes where allies had nothing to gain and everything to fear led to growing unpopularity. While the general citizenry of subject cities often felt indifference toward Athens, positive hatred was felt primarily by those who were not her subjects, and revolts were typically driven by small parties of ambitious leading men.

## Later Developments and Decline
In later periods, such as the formation of a new confederacy in 378 B.C., Athens initially showed prudent self-restraint. The city formally proclaimed that no Athenian citizen should possess or cultivate land outside of Attica, renouncing the previous system of *kleruchies* (out-possessions). However, this restraint was eventually abandoned. The Athenian maritime force began to be used for the recovery of lost possessions and the enlargement of the confederacy, ignoring the prohibition against *kleruchies*.

This shift caused offense to prominent members of the maritime confederacy. Additionally, Athens experienced increasing alienation from Thebes, exacerbated by the Theban violent proceedings against Plataea in 372 B.C. and disputes over the relief of Korkyra. By the time the Macedonian prince Philip, son of Amyntas, emerged as an aggressor twelve or thirteen years later, the force of Athens had been misemployed and broken into fragments, leaving the city unable to repel him.
