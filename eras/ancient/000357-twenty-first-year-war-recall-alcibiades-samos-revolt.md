# Twenty-first Year of the War: Recall of Alcibiades and Political Upheaval

The twenty-first year of the Peloponnesian War was characterized by shifting alliances, the strategic maneuvering of Alcibiades, and significant constitutional volatility within Athens and its military encampments.

## Peloponnesian Naval Movements and the Revolt of Rhodes
During this period, the Peloponnesians faced difficulties in maintaining a united fleet due to a lack of funds and the unreliable nature of Tissaphernes as a paymaster. Consequently, they dispatched Clearchus, son of Ramphias, with forty ships to Pharnabazus, who was prepared to provide pay. While the majority of these ships, including those of Clearchus, were forced into Delos and later Miletus by a storm, ten ships under the Megarian Helixus successfully reached the Hellespont and effected the revolt of Byzantium. This prompted the Athenian commanders at Samos to send a squadron to guard the Hellespont, leading to a naval encounter before Byzantium involving eight vessels from each side.

Simultaneously, the Peloponnesians sailed to Rhodes following invitations from principal local figures. They hoped to secure an island with strong land and sea forces and to maintain the fleet through their own confederacy rather than relying on Tissaphernes. After arriving at Camirus with ninety-four ships—causing alarm among the unfortified and uninformed inhabitants—the Lacedaemonians assembled the residents of Camirus, Lindus, and Ialysus. The Rhodians were persuaded to revolt from Athens and joined the Peloponnesians. Although the Athenians attempted to forestall this move, they arrived too late and subsequently waged war against Rhodes from Samos, Cos, and Chalce.

## The Recall of Alcibiades to Samos
At Samos, the Athenian chiefs, led by Thrasybulus, remained determined to recall Alcibiades. Believing that their salvation depended on bringing Tissaphernes over to the Athenian side, they held an assembly where the soldiery voted for Alcibiades' recall and amnesty. Upon his arrival at Samos, Alcibiades addressed the assembly, deploring his banishment and magnifying his influence with Tissaphernes. 

Alcibiades made extravagant promises to the army, claiming Tissaphernes had assured him that the Athenians would never lack supplies—even if Tissaphernes had to coin his own silver couch—and that the Phoenician fleet at Aspendus would be delivered to Athens. However, Alcibiades stated that Tissaphernes would only trust the Athenians if Alcibiades were recalled to serve as their security. His strategy was to increase his credit with the army, incite their hopes, and make the oligarchical government in Athens fear him to hasten the dissolution of the clubs.

## Constitutional Shifts in Athens
In Athens, the political landscape underwent a dramatic transformation. Upon receiving certain news, the Athenians manned twenty ships and convened an assembly at the Pnyx. They deposed the Four Hundred and transferred the government to the Five Thousand, a body consisting of all citizens who could provide a suit of armor. This new constitution decreed that no one should receive pay for holding office, under penalty of being held accursed. This period was regarded as one of the best governments Athens had experienced, as it effectively fused the high and low classes, allowing the state to recover from previous disasters. Under this government, the Athenians voted to recall Alcibiades and other exiles, urging them to return and devote themselves to the war.

## Oligarchical Intrigues and the Role of the King
Despite the democratic restoration in the city, oligarchical designs persisted in the camp at Samos. Some individuals interviewed Alcibiades, who offered to make Tissaphernes and the King friends of the Athenians if the democracy were abolished. This proposal appealed to the higher class, who had suffered most from the war and hoped to seize government. Emissaries formed a club at Samos, telling the soldiers that the King would provide money if Alcibiades were restored and the democracy abolished. While the multitude was initially irritated, the prospect of Persian pay kept them quiet.

However, the general Phrynichus opposed these proposals. He argued that Alcibiades sought only his own restoration and cared nothing for the form of government. Phrynichus further reasoned that the King would not trust the Athenians over the Peloponnesians, who had not injured him, and that allied states would not prefer servitude under an Athenian oligarchy or democracy over their current freedom.

In Athens, envoys from Samos and Pisander argued that the King's alliance was essential to overcome the Peloponnesians, given the enemy's superior ship count and financial backing. Despite opposition from the Eumolpidae and Ceryces—who cited the mysteries as the cause of Alcibiades' banishment—Pisander insisted that the safety of the state was more pressing than the form of government, arguing that a more moderate government with offices in fewer hands was the only way to gain the King's confidence.
