# Irrigation in Human History

Irrigation has served as a critical tool for transforming arid regions into productive land. When water can be distributed across dry surfaces, the land typically becomes extremely productive. This is particularly effective where rivers cross deserts at a level near the general surface, as seen in parts of the Great Basin of North America and the valley of the Nile. Conversely, irrigation becomes too expensive when a river has cut deeply and flows far below the surface. In other regions, such as parts of Australia, artesian wells have been utilized to achieve similar ends.

## Legal and Social Frameworks
In certain historical legal codes, irrigation was considered indispensable, and its maintenance was strictly regulated. If an irrigator caused a flood by leaving a runnel open or neglecting to repair a dyke, they were legally required to compensate neighbors for damaged crops. Failure to do so could result in the irrigator and their family being sold to cover the costs. Furthermore, the theft of agricultural implements, such as water-buckets or watering-machines, carried heavy fines.

The social organization of agriculture often involved complex contracts. While slaves were numerous, hired labor was frequently required, particularly during harvests. This labor was governed by contracts, often with advance payment and guarantees of fulfillment. Animals, including asses and oxen, were hired for various tasks, including the operation of watering-machines, ploughing, threshing, and carting. Statutory wages were fixed for field-labourers, ox-drivers, and sowers.

## Engineering and Infrastructure
Ancient hydraulic engineering is evidenced by the stone aqueducts found above Hit. Attributed by Arabs to the Persians (referred to as the "times of the ignorant") when they were fire-worshippers, these firmly cemented structures were placed at right angles to the current. They narrowed to approximately twenty inches or two feet at the top and extended from 200 to 1,200 yards toward the interior. While many are in ruins, some have been maintained for irrigation or grinding corn.

In other cultures, such as under the rule of Nintoku, riparian works and irrigation improvements were conducted on a large scale. However, records regarding the repairs of the Manda river-bank mention the "heinous custom" of sacrificing human lives to ensure the success or speed of public works.

## Regional Applications and Development

### North America
In Idaho, irrigation became vital in the arid southern region. Initial efforts were simple and individual, centering around the head waters of streams. This evolved into co-operative colonization, leading to the construction of irrigation canals with large head gates and dams. The Federal Reclamation Act (1902) and the Carey Act (1894) marked a significant era of development. The Twin Falls project, utilizing the Snake river, irrigated over 200,000 acres by 1903-1905. Additionally, the Minidoka project in Cassia and Lincoln counties, completed in 1907, commanded an irrigable area of 130,000 to 150,000 acres.

In the Mississippi valley, water is drawn via siphons or flumes in the levees. However, drainage difficulties and flood risks have made the extension of this practice unprofitable. In the prairie region, irrigation was almost exclusively for rice farms. Between 1899 and 1902, the irrigated rice area grew by 92.9%, with the majority of the 387,580 total irrigated acres located in the parishes of Vermilion, Acadia, and Calcasieu. During this period, construction costs rose 87.7%, reaching $12.25 per irrigated acre in 1902.

### Asia
In India, the Ganges Canal is described as a source of "unspeakable blessings" for a vast population, though some observers noted that canal irrigation could lead to sanitary issues if natural drainage was interfered with, leading to the saturation of retentive soils. In the Bombay Presidency, the water supply generally does not support large irrigation works, with the exception of Sind. In Bengal, irrigation is primarily used as a protection against famine; otherwise, it is not considered remunerative due to the damp climate and abundance of rivers. However, canals on the Sone river in southern Behar and in Orissa were constructed for both navigation and irrigation.

## Historical Narratives
Irrigation figures in ancient narratives as a means of survival and social ascent. An ancient Sumerian inscription regarding Sargon I, the founder of the Akkadian Sumerian Empire, describes how Sargon was abandoned in a basket of reeds on a river. He was rescued by "Akki the irrigator," who reared him and trained him as a gardener before Sargon eventually became king.
