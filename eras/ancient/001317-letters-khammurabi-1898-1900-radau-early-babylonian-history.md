# Letters of Khammurabi and Early Babylonian History

The historical record of early Babylonia is reconstructed through a vast array of archaeological and textual evidence, including contract tablets, royal charters, and the personal correspondence of its monarchs. Central to this record are the autograph letters of Khammurabi (also written as Ammurapi or Khammuram), which provide direct insight into the administration and geopolitical reach of his empire.

## The Reign of Khammurabi
Khammurabi was the sixth king of the first Babylonian dynasty and the son and successor of Sin-muballidh. He is identified in some contexts as the Amraphel mentioned in Genesis xiv. 1. His reign was marked by a struggle for independence and regional dominance. During this period, the Elamites, led by King Kudur-Lagamar (Chedor-laomer), occupied Babylon and destroyed the temple of Bel-Merodach.

Khammurabi eventually retrieved his fortunes, achieving a decisive victory over the Elamite forces in the thirtieth year of his reign (dated to 2340 B.C. in some sources). Following this victory, he spent two years incorporating Yamutbal and Larsa into his dominion. This process transformed Babylonia into a single monarchy with Babylon as its metropolis. Under his rule, Babylonian authority extended as far as the shores of the Mediterranean, and the empire maintained constant intercourse with Syria and Canaan.

## Administrative and Diplomatic Correspondence
The archive contains a significant number of autograph letters written by Khammurabi. These documents serve as evidence of the political structure of the era. For example, one specific letter orders the dispatch of 240 soldiers from Situllum and Assyria, which serves as proof that Assyria was a dependency of Babylon at that time. 

The relationship between the two regions was close; the archive notes that the Assyrian language was practically identical to the Babylonian, and the Assyrians were considered the same people as the Babylonians, albeit with some foreign admixtures.

## Legal and Social Integration
Khammurabi is credited with welding his vast empire together through a uniform system of law. While earlier Babylonian cities had their own laws—which protected the rights of foreign women married to Babylonian husbands and allowed groups of aliens to enter the city—Khammurabi's "Code" provided a more systematic legal framework.

The study of Babylonian law is supported by thousands of "contracts," which include bonds, receipts, accounts, conveyances, and the actual legal decisions rendered by judges in law courts. These, combined with royal rescripts and private letters, illustrate a society with a population composed of many races and incessant intercommunication between cities.

## Religious Transformation
The era of Khammurabi marks a distinct epoch in the religion of the Euphrates valley. Before his unification of the states (c. 2250 B.C.), religion was centered around various cities such as Ur, Nippur, Erech, Agade, Eridu, Sippara, Kutha, and Shirgulla. In these centers, a chief deity and a female consort presided over a jurisdiction limited to the political extent of that district.

Khammurabi pursued an avowed policy to centralize this system. He raised Marduk, the patron deity of Babylon, to the head of the entire Babylonian pantheon. This religious shift mirrored the political reality of Babylon becoming the recognized center of the entire Euphrates valley.

## Chronological Perspectives
The dating of Khammurabi's reign has been a subject of scholarly discussion. Prior to 1907, he was commonly dated to 2376-2333 B.C. or 2285-2242 B.C. However, the discovery of inscriptions showing that the second dynasty was partly contemporaneous with the first and third dynasties led to a downward revision of these dates. More recent adoptions, such as those by Thureau-Dangin and Ungnad, place his reign between 2130 and 2088 B.C. Other records vary on the length of his reign, with some lists assigning him 43 years and others 55 years.
