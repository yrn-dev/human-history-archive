# Papyrus and the Evolution of Early Writing Materials

## The Nature and Origin of Papyrus
Papyrus is a plant characterized by triangular stalks that shoot from the root to a height of six or seven cubits. As a writing material, it was made from the pith of a reed found primarily in Egypt. The process involved dividing the bark of the papyrus into thin leaves or flakes suitable for writing. It is believed that papyrus has been used as a writing material as early as 4000 B.C.

The material was so central to early literacy that it influenced the terminology of later languages. The Greeks used the words *bublos* or *biblos* to refer to papyrus, from which they formed the word *biblion* for a book. This Greek term is the origin of the English word "Bible."

## The Papyrus Prisse and Early Egyptian Manuscripts
One of the earliest direct ancestors of the modern printed book is a roll of eighteen columns written in Egyptian hieratic writing, dating to approximately the 25th century B.C. This manuscript, known as the Papyrus Prisse, is preserved in the Musee de Louvre in Paris and contains the maxims of Ptah-hetep. 

Beyond literary maxims, papyrus was used extensively for administrative purposes. Egyptian records were commonly written on this material, and contemporary imagery depicts scribes using papyrus to keep accounts of grain quantities entering and leaving government store-houses.

## Forms and Preservation of Papyrus Documents
During the period when papyrus was the predominant writing material, the standard form of a book was the *volumen*, or roll, which was wound around one or more sticks. There were three primary methods of writing upon these rolls:
1. Short lines written at right angles to the length of the roll.
2. Long lines extending the entire length of the roll.
3. Short lines parallel to the length of the roll, with spaces left on either side of each column.

To protect these documents, Egyptian papyrus rolls were generally kept in roll form, bound with papyrus tape, and frequently sealed with Nile mud. These rolls were often stored within rectangular hollows carved into wood.

While some papyrus documents were later bound using a "stabbed" method—where cord or fibre was run through holes in the substance of the roll—this was rare because papyrus is too brittle to retain threads properly. An example of this binding exists in a Coptic papyrus from about the 8th century found at Thebes.

## Transition to Other Materials
Papyrus remained the usual vehicle for writing until the early centuries of the Christian era. Its use persisted in specific contexts, such as pontifical bulls until A.D. 1022, and in Coptic manuscripts as late as A.D. 1250.

However, the supremacy of papyrus was eventually challenged by other materials:
* **Parchment:** In the 2nd century B.C., Eumenes II, king of Pergamus, improved the preparation of sheep and calf skins for writing because papyrus had become hard to procure. This material, known as *pergamentum* (parchment), contributed to the rise of the *codex* (the modern book form) as it superseded the *volumen*.
* **Paper:** The manufacture of paper was learned by the Arabs from the Chinese via Central Asia and subsequently passed to Europeans. Following the Arab conquest of Egypt, Europe was cut off from its papyrus supply, making the adoption of paper essential for the later development of printing and popular education.

## Other Early Writing Media
Before and alongside the use of papyrus, other materials were employed for recording information. In Babylonia, as early as 2400 B.C., revenue accounts and legal decisions were inscribed in cuneiform on clay tablets, which were stored in jars on shelves. By the 7th century B.C., Sargan (721-705 B.C.) founded a library of such tablets at Nineveh.

Other early media included:
* **Palm leaves and tree bark:** Used before the introduction of paper.
* **Wax tablets:** Wooden leaves (diptychs) hollowed and filled with blackened wax. These were used in Rome and are considered prototypes of the modern book. The earliest known Latin manuscript is a record of payment to Umbricia Januaria in A.D. 55, scratched into wax.
* **Bark books:** Used by uncultured nations for medical receipts or magical formulae, often folded like a concertina.
