# Steward

The term "steward" refers to a variety of administrative, judicial, and ceremonial roles across different historical contexts, ranging from royal household officials and judicial officers in England to land administrators in Japan and symbolic roles in religious literature.

## The Lord Steward and Lord High Steward of England

In England, the Lord Steward is a prominent official within the king's household, serving as the first dignitary of the court. This official is always a peer, a member of the government, and a privy councillor. Historically, the office held cabinet rank until 1782. The Lord Steward is appointed personally by the sovereign and carries a white staff as a warrant of authority. Various historical texts refer to the role by different names: the *Statutes of Eltham* call the official "the lord great master," while the *Household Book* of Queen Elizabeth uses "the lord steward." In 1539, an act of Henry VIII described the role as the "grand master or lord steward of the king's most honourable household."

The Lord Steward presides at the Board of Green Cloth, which also includes the comptroller and treasurer of the household. While these three officials are typically peers or sons of peers and members of the ministry, their theoretical duties are often performed in practice by the master of the household. The master of the household is a permanent officer who resides in the palace and presides over the daily dinners of the suite.

The Lord High Steward also performs specific ceremonial and judicial functions. At coronations, the steward's ancient services have become largely obsolete; the banquet and procession from Westminster Hall were abandoned upon the accession of William IV. However, new functions have emerged; for the coronation of Edward VII, the Duke of Marlborough served as steward and carried "St Edward's crown" during the procession in Westminster Abbey.

## The Steward's Court and Trials of Peers

The steward's court was historically used for the proceedings against peers. This court was often preferred over trials in parliament because, following the Southampton case, the steward could select only a few peers to be summoned, making it easier for the king to secure convictions. This practice was partially changed by the Treason Act of William III, which required all peers of parliament to be summoned at least twenty days before trials for treason or misprision of treason.

The rules of the steward's court varied depending on whether parliament was in session. Lord Chancellor Jeffreys ruled during the trial of Lord Delamere in 1685 that if a trial occurred during a parliamentary recess, the steward acted as the judge of the court. In this capacity, the steward's warrant convened the prisoner, his summons convened the peers, and he held sole authority over questions of law, though he did not vote on the verdict of guilty or not guilty. Conversely, during a session of parliament, all peers served as both judges and triers, and the steward acted only as chairman, voting alongside the other lords. Since 1685, all trials of peers have occurred before the lords in parliament, with the most recent being that of Earl Russell in 1901, where Lord Chancellor Halsbury was appointed lord high steward. The steward is addressed as "his grace," carries a rod of office, and the commission for the role is dissolved by breaking this rod.

## Hereditary Stewardship and Political Claims

The hereditary stewardship was once held by the earls of Leicester. Simon V. de Montfort, the last of these earls to inherit the role, served as steward at the coronation of Eleanor, queen of Henry III. Simon believed his hereditary stewardship entitled him to a high official position in England, and following his victory at Lewes, he appeared as steward of England in official documents. After his death at Evesham, the stewardship was granted for life to his son, Edmund of Lancaster. Edmund was succeeded by Thomas, earl of Lancaster, who received a grant for himself and his heirs from Edward II. Thomas claimed that the steward was the supreme judge in parliament and the second personage in the realm, a claim that received slight recognition in a statute passed against the Despencers in the first year of Edward III.

## Land-Stewards in Japan

In Japanese history, land-stewards were officials appointed to manage manors. Initially, Yoritomo appointed retainers as land-stewards to exercise general supervision and collect taxes without owning the estates. Later, following the Shokyu struggle, the Bakufu confiscated approximately three thousand manors from hostile courtiers and distributed them among the Hojo, the Minamoto, and their partisans. These recipients became both owners and land-stewards, known as *shimpo-jito* ("newly appointed land-stewards"), to distinguish them from the *hompo-jito* ("originally appointed").

Land-stewards were responsible for collecting taxes, paying their own salaries, and maintaining peace and order within their assigned manors. They were responsible only to Kamakura and remained outside the jurisdiction of the Imperial Court. However, the land-steward system faced difficulties and created friction, leading to their withdrawal from all manors except those administered or owned by Taira.

## Other Applications of the Term

### Sports Administration
In the context of horse racing, the Jockey Club is governed by three stewards, one of whom retires annually. These stewards supervise racing business and grant licenses to jockeys, trainers, and other officials. They serve as *ex officio* stewards for Doncaster, Goodwood, Epsom, and Ascot. Other racing meetings are controlled by stewards—usually patrons of the Turf—who adjudicate objections and settle disputes, reporting final decisions to the Jockey Club stewards.

### Religious and Legal Contexts
In rabbinical literature and related texts, the term "stewards" is used symbolically. One account of the fall of Jerusalem describes priests casting the keys of the sanctuary into heaven and telling the Lord to guard His own house because the priests were found to be "unfaithful stewards."

In legal history, the term appears in specific cases. For example, the case of Dr. Steward cast suspicion on the character of Francis Bacon, as it appeared Bacon may have allowed a judgment to be set aside and a matter reopened due to a request from Buckingham.
