# The Nineveh Hypothesis: Relocating the Hanging Gardens

In previous installments, we explored the traditional narrative of the Hanging Gardens as a romantic gift from King Nebuchadnezzar II to his Median wife in Babylon. However, the persistent silence of Babylonian records—which meticulously document every other major construction project of the era—has led modern historians to question not just *if* the gardens existed, but *where*.

While the classical Greek historians insisted the gardens were in Babylon, a compelling scholarly shift suggests we have been looking at the wrong city entirely.

## The Case for Nineveh

The "Nineveh Hypothesis," most notably championed by Dr. Stephanie Dalley of Oxford University, proposes that the legendary gardens were actually located in Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire, rather than Babylon. This theory resolves the archaeological void in Babylon by aligning the legends with the actual records of the Assyrian King Sennacherib.

Sennacherib, who ruled in the 7th century BCE, left behind detailed inscriptions describing a "wonder" of a palace complex. His records describe a massive botanical garden featuring exotic flora brought from all corners of the empire, designed to mimic a mountain landscape. Unlike the vague accounts of Babylon, Sennacherib’s texts provide a concrete blueprint for a lush, elevated paradise.

## Engineering the Impossible

The primary challenge of any "hanging" garden in the arid Near East was irrigation. To lift water from the river to high terraces, a sophisticated system was required. In Babylon, no such infrastructure has been found. In Nineveh, however, archaeological evidence reveals a vast network of canals and aqueducts.

Most significantly, evidence suggests the use of an early version of the Archimedes screw—a device capable of lifting water vertically. While traditionally attributed to the Greeks centuries later, Assyrian reliefs suggest Sennacherib’s engineers had mastered this technology to keep his gardens green amidst the desert heat.

## A Case of Mistaken Identity

How did the gardens migrate from Nineveh to Babylon in the historical record? Historians suggest that later Greek writers, who relied on second-hand accounts and confused the two great Mesopotamian capitals, simply attributed the wonders of the Assyrians to the more famous Babylonians. By the time the accounts were codified, the "Hanging Gardens of Babylon" had become a fixed literary trope, regardless of the geographical reality.

### Key Facts
* **Traditional Location:** Babylon (modern-day Iraq), attributed to Nebuchadnezzar II.
* **Proposed Location:** Nineveh (modern-day Mosul, Iraq), attributed to Sennacherib.
* **The Discrepancy:** No contemporary Babylonian texts mention the gardens, while Assyrian texts describe them in detail.
* **Technological Feat:** The use of aqueducts and potential screw-pumps for irrigation.
* **Scholarly Debate:** The debate centers on whether the gardens were a physical reality or a poetic exaggeration of Mesopotamian horticulture.

### Did You Know?
* The term "hanging" is a mistranslation of the Greek word *kremastos*, which more accurately means "overhanging" or "tiered," describing terraces rather than plants suspended in mid-air.
* Sennacherib’s gardens were designed to be a "Palace without Rival," intended to showcase the power of the Assyrian Empire over nature.
* The gardens are the only one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World for which a definitive archaeological site has never been universally agreed upon.
