# THE DAIKA REFORMS

The Daika reforms, occurring during the Daika era (645–650), represented a fundamental political development in Japanese history. Initiated during the reign of the thirty-sixth sovereign, Emperor Kotoku (A.D. 645–654), these reforms served as a transition from a Japanese system based on heredity to a Chinese system based on morality. The reforms were heavily influenced by the Tang polity, copying its model to centralize power and restructure the administration of the empire.

## Central Government and Administration
Prior to the Daika reforms, the sovereign's contact with his subjects was conducted solely through the medium of the *o-muraji* or the *o-omi*. Imperial commands were transmitted and enforced by these figures, and the Throne did not practically possess the prerogative to nominate them. 

The Daika reforms altered this structure by creating a cabinet and a body of officials who were entrusted with definite functions. Unlike the previous system, these officials were appointable or removable by the sovereign at will. As part of this reorganization of the central government, which included the establishment of boards and bureaux, the office of the Left Minister (*Sa-daijin*) was created.

## Land Ownership and Taxation
A primary feature of the Daika development was the metamorphosis of land ownership and the status of the population. The entire nation became the public people of the realm, and all land—specifically "wet fields"—became the property of the Crown. This removed land entirely from the control of the hereditary nobility, who were relegated to the rank of State pensioners.

Under this new system, land was partitioned among the people on leases that were terminable after six years. While the Crown claimed ownership of wet fields, no restrictions were imposed on the ownership of uplands, which belonged to those who reclaimed them. 

The system of taxation was also overhauled. Previously, State charges on land produce were limited to military operations and religious observances, and required the intervention of *uji* heads. Following the reforms, the hereditary nobility's interest in taxes was limited to the realization of their sustenance allowances.

## Provincial and Local Organization
To implement these changes, the state conducted an accurate census of the population and a survey of the land to fix the boundaries of villages, districts, and provinces. 

The empire was divided into provinces (*kuni*) categorized into four classes: great, superior, medium, and inferior. While the term "province" was ancient, the number of *kuni* was largely reduced during the surveys and re-adjustments of the Daika era (from 144 in the time of Emperor Keitai to a lower number). Each *kuni* was placed under a governor (*kokushi*) appointed for a four-year term based on competence. This replaced the *miyatsuko*, who had previously administered the *kuni* by hereditary right.

Provinces were further subdivided into districts (*kori*), which were originally called *agata* (meaning "arable land"). These districts were categorized into five classes: great, superior, medium, inferior, and small. The *agata* were further subdivided into *mura* (village) and *inaki* (granary). While *nushi* had previously overseen the *agata*, *inaki*, and *mura*, the Daika system placed each district under the administration of a *cho* (chief).

## Legal Framework and Later Evolution
The legislation of the Daika reformation included a penal law (*ritsu*) that was undoubtedly copied from Tang legislators, with modifications made only to the degrees of punishment. The general code was also partially exotic in character but underwent sweeping alterations to ensure conformity with Japanese traditions and customs.

Despite the intent of the Daika code—which enacted that all profits from reclaimed lands must be shared with farmers—the system faced abuses. Provincial and district governors often used forced labor to create large private estates on uplands, defying the code.

The administrative systems of the Daika era were later completed during the reign of the fortieth sovereign, Emperor Temmu (A.D. 673–686). Temmu asserted the authority of the Court to an unprecedented degree, relying on coercion and military assistance rather than the conciliation used by the original Daika reformers. He specifically avoided rewarding his supporters with official posts to prevent the recurrence of abuses his predecessors had sought to eradicate.
