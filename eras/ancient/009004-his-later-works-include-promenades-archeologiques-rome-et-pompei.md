# Promenades archéologiques: Rome et Pompei

*Promenades archéologiques: Rome et Pompei* is a work authored by a French writer noted for applying lucidity and elegance with seriousness to weighty literary matters. The work was first published in 1880, with a second series following in 1886.

## Author and Literary Contributions
The author of *Promenades archéologiques: Rome et Pompei* focused primarily on the reconstruction of the elements of Roman society. In addition to this work, his bibliography includes several other titles:
* *L'Afrique romaine, promenades archéologiques* (1901)
* *La Fin du paganisme* (published in two volumes in 1891)
* *Le Conjuration de Catilina* (1905)
* *Tacite* (1903), which was translated into English by W.G. Hutchison in 1906.

Beyond his focus on Roman themes, the author also produced monographs on Saint-Simon (1892) and Madame de Sevigne (1887). He died in June 1908.

## Historical Context of Rome and Pompey
While the author's work examines Roman society, the archive contains extensive records regarding the historical figures of Rome and Pompey the Great. During the middle of the first century B.C., the Roman Senate served as the nominal center of government, though power was effectively divided among the First Triumvirate consisting of Caesar, Pompey, and Crassus. This coalition was formed to end the rule of the oligarchy after the senate had alienated Pompey by refusing to grant lands to his soldiers and failing to ratify his acts.

Following the death of Crassus at Carrhæ by the Parthians, Caesar and Pompey became rivals. Pompey aligned himself with the republican side, and laws were passed to bring Caesar to trial for disobedience to the senate and breaches of law. In 49 B.C., Caesar crossed the Rubicon, stating, "The die is cast," and marched toward Rome and Pompey.

## The Conflict Between Caesar and Pompey
The ensuing conflict saw Pompey utilize forces including two legions in Campania and eight legions in Spain commanded by Petreius and Afranius. Caesar rapidly outmaneuvered Pompey's lieutenants in Spain, receiving their submission on August 2nd after reaching Ilerda on June 23rd. 

Pompey eventually embarked for the East, where his name held the greatest prestige. Caesar followed, attempting to invest Pompey's lines at Dyrrhachium, but was defeated due to Pompey's superior force. However, at the battle of Pharsalus in the plain of Thessaly, Pompey was decisively defeated. He fled to Egypt, where he was murdered.

## Aftermath and Legacy
Following his victory over Pompey, Caesar was appointed dictator first for ten years and later for life in 45 B.C., effectively becoming the monarch of the empire. He adopted the throne and sceptre and introduced the Egyptian concept of a god-king, leading to a statue being erected in his honor with the inscription "To the Unconquerable God." This shift caused a final protest from the remnants of Roman republicanism; Caesar was eventually stabbed to death in the Senate at the foot of the statue of his rival, Pompey the Great.

The conflict between these two figures was later dramatized in the *Pharsalia* by Lucan. This work sketches the causes of the war, the crossing of the Rubicon, Pompey's retirement to Greece, and the eventual assassination of Pompey in Egypt.
