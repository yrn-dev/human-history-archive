# Corstopitum and the Northern Roman Frontiers

## The Station of Corstopitum
Corstopitum, also known as Corchester, was a Roman station located half a mile west of the modern market town of Corbridge in Northumberland, England. Situated at the junction of the river Cor (a tributary of the Tyne) and the river Tyne, the site served as a significant military base. Excavations conducted starting in 1907 revealed the station's importance, uncovering remains of several buildings that fronted a broad thoroughfare. Among these was the largest Roman building discovered in England, with the exception of the baths at Bath.

The site featured two granaries, which indicate that Corstopitum functioned as a base for the northward operations of Antoninus Pius. Following the loss of these conquests, the station ceased to be a military center, and its military buildings were repurposed for civilian occupation. A hoard of gold coins wrapped in lead-foil was discovered hidden in a wall at the site in 1908. Corstopitum ceased to exist early in the 5th century and was never occupied again.

## The Advance of Antoninus Pius
Around 142, the successor to Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, advanced from the frontier of the Solway and the Tyne. Acting through his general, Lollius Urbicus, the Roman forces moved toward the isthmus between the Clyde and the Forth, a distance of 36 miles across. This area had previously been fortified by Agricola.

In this region, the Romans constructed a continuous rampart with a preceding ditch. This rampart was built using regularly laid sods, a method likely similar to that used for Hadrian's wall. This construction method was verified by excavations carried out between 1891 and 1893.

## Forts and Infrastructure of the Northern Frontier
To support the rampart, approximately a dozen fair-sized forts were built, some abutting the wall and others positioned close behind it. A connecting road ran from end to end of the frontier. While much of the work has been lost in agricultural districts near the ends of the line, traces of the road, ditch, and wall remain visible in the covers of Bonnyside (3 miles west of Falkirk), at Westerwood, and on Croyhill near Kilsyth.

Three of these forts have been excavated, revealing the standard features of Roman *castella*, though they vary in design:
*   **Bar Hill:** Covering three acres, this fort was nearly five times the size of the earlier fort built by Agricola on the same site. It featured turf ramparts, wooden barrack-rooms, and a stone storehouse, bath, and headquarters building.
*   **Castle Cary:** This fort covered nearly four acres and featured massive, well-dressed masonry in its ramparts. Its north face aligned with the frontier wall.
*   **Rough Castle:** Located near Falkirk, this was a smaller fort notable for its earthen and turf-built ravelins and ramparts, as well as a series of defensive pits similar to Caesar's *lilia* at Alesia.

## Regional Road Systems
The Roman road system in northern Britain consisted of three principal routes. One road led north from York, passing through Ebchester, Lanchester, Binchester, Piers Bridge, and Catterick Bridge to reach the wall and Scotland. Branches from this route through Chester-le-Street provided access to the Tyne mouth at South Shields and the Tyne Bridge (Pons Aelius) at Newcastle.

A second route turned northwest from Catterick Bridge, crossing the Pennine Chain via Brough-under-Stainmoor, Bowes, and Rokeby. It descended into the Eden valley and reached Hadrian's wall near Carlisle (Luguvallium) before passing to Birrens.

A third, more complex route started from Chester and followed the western coast. This route is characterized by the presence of numerous forts, including Ribchester (Bremetennacum), Manchester (Mancunium or Mamucium), Old Penrith (Voreda), and Brougham Castle (Brocavum). Western branches of this system reached Maryport (Uxellodunum), Hardknott above Eskdale, Waterhead on Ambleside, and Watercrook near Kendal.
