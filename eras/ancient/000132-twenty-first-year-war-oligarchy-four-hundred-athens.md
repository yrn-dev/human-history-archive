# TWENTY-FIRST YEAR OF THE WAR: OLIGARCHY OF FOUR HUNDRED AT ATHENS

The twenty-first year of the war was marked by a period of extreme political instability in Athens, characterized by a rapid rally from military disaster followed by an internal coup that installed the oligarchy of the Four Hundred. This political upheaval occurred between 412 B.C. and 411 B.C., bringing the city to the brink of ruin.

## The Athenian Rally and the Role of Alkibiadês
Following the catastrophe near Syracuse in September 413 B.C., Athens appeared to be facing irreparable ruin. However, the city underwent an energetic and rapid rally, enabling it to achieve a victory over the Milêsians upon landing near Milêtus in September 412 B.C. Despite impaired resources and a purely defensive system, Athens maintained a tolerable struggle against numerous and bold enemies.

During this period, Alkibiadês, who had escaped an order from Sparta to be killed, retired to the Persian satrap Tissaphernês and became his adviser. Alkibiadês advised Tissaphernês not to assist either Grecian party heartily, though his own leanings favored Athens to facilitate his own restoration. While acting as a negotiator for Tissaphernês at Magnesia, Alkibiadês opened correspondence with Athenian officers at Samos and originated the scheme for an oligarchical revolution in Athens.

## The Conspiracy of the Four Hundred
The conspiracy was arranged between Alkibiadês and Athenian officers, utilizing "hetæries," or political clubs, to organize the oligarchical faction. Peisander was dispatched to Athens to push the conspiracy forward. Despite strong opposition from the people regarding both the conspiracy and the restoration of Alkibiadês, the assembly eventually voted to relinquish their democracy. This unwilling vote was secured through the promise of Persian aid for the war.

As the plot progressed, Peisander organized the oligarchical clubs into action against the democracy. After Peisander left Athens for Samos, the management of the conspiracy was taken over by Antiphon, Theramenês, and Phrynichus. The oligarchy of the Four Hundred was eventually installed in the senate-house around February or March 411 B.C.

## Internal Dissension and Failure of the Oligarchy
The Four Hundred's hold on power was fraught with tension and strategic failures. Negotiations between Peisander and Alkibiadês were hindered by the latter's tricks; Alkibiadês exaggerated his demands to intentionally break off negotiations, which caused indignation among the oligarchs. Consequently, Peisander and his colleagues persisted with the conspiracy without Alkibiadês.

The oligarchs attempted to extend their influence beyond Athens, seeking to subvert the democracy at Samos through the assassination of Hyperbolus and others. However, the democracy at Samos was sustained by the Athenian armament. Similarly, trierarchs such as Eratosthenês and Iatroklês, returning from the Hellespont, attempted to turn their squadrons to the cause of the conspirators but were thwarted by the "inflexible democracy" of their own seamen.

Furthermore, the Four Hundred attempted to effect similar revolutions in various allied dependencies to secure their attachment to the new regime. This strategy backfired; rather than strengthening the maritime empire, it weakened it. The newly created oligarchies became more anxious for complete autonomy than the previous democracies had been. At Thasos, for instance, recalled exiles began active preparations for revolt, building new triremes and fortifications.

## Restoration of Democracy
The oligarchy of the Four Hundred lasted for four or five months, ending in July 411 B.C. when the democracy was restored. The period was characterized by danger and distraction that nearly placed Athens within the grasp of its enemies. 

Years later, during the oligarchy of the Thirty, Theramenês claimed that the Athenian people had adopted the Four Hundred because they were told the Lacedæmonians would never trust a democracy. However, archival records indicate this assertion was incorrect and a "loose assertion" by a speaker transferring present feelings to past incidents.

## Legacy and Later Impact
The faction that had supported the Four Hundred remained a presence in Athenian politics. Seven years after the installation of the Four Hundred, during the period when Lysander was demolishing the walls of Athens, the remnants of this faction and various exiles—including members of the Four Hundred—returned to the city. Many of these individuals had previously served at Dekeleia as part of the force blockading Athens. They viewed the prostrate condition of the city and the end of the Athenian democracy with delight, as it fulfilled the goals they had aimed for when they originally constructed the fortress of Ecteioneia to ensure their own power.
