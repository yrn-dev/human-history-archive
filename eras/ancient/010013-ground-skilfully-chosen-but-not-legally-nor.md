# The Theory of Royal Supremacy during a Royal Minority

## Conflict over Ecclesiastical Jurisdiction
During the accession of Edward VI, Bishop Bonner and Gardiner faced a dilemma regarding royal supremacy. While Bonner had previously been an active agent in establishing this supremacy, he began to doubt it upon observing how a Protestant council utilized such power. Consequently, either Bonner or Gardiner developed a specific theory: that royal supremacy was in abeyance during a royal minority.

The archive notes that the ground for this theory was skilfully chosen, but it was not legally nor constitutionally tenable. This lack of tenability was evidenced by the fact that both Gardiner and Bonner had sought fresh licences from the young king to exercise their ecclesiastical jurisdiction. This created a logical contradiction: if the king was supreme enough to confer jurisdiction, he was likewise supreme enough to order the visitation and issue the injunctions that Bonner opposed. Furthermore, if a royal minority caused an abeyance of supremacy in ecclesiastical matters, the same would have to apply to the temporal sphere, which would result in anarchy.

## Resistance and Legal Consequences
This constitutional question led to a direct conflict between Bonner and the government of Edward VI. In August 1547, Bonner resisted a visitation and was committed to the Fleet, though he was later released. He subsequently took an active role against the government during the parliament of November 1547.

Between November 1548 and March 1549, Bonner emerged as a leading opponent of the Book of Common Prayer and the first Act of Uniformity. After these were enacted into law, Bonner neglected to enforce them. On September 1, 1549, the council required him to maintain at St Paul's Cross that royal authority remained as great as if the king were forty years of age. Bonner failed to comply with this requirement. Following a trial lasting seven days, an ecclesiastical court presided over by Cranmer deprived him of his bishopric and sent him to the Marshalsea.

## Final Deprivation and Imprisonment
The fall of Somerset in the month following Bonner's deprivation initially raised his hopes, leading him to appeal from Cranmer to the council. However, the Protestant faction eventually gained the upper hand. On February 7, 1550, the council, sitting in the Star Chamber, confirmed Bonner's deprivation and condemned him to perpetual imprisonment.
