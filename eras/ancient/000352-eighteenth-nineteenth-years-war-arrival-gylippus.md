# Eighteenth and Nineteenth Years of the War—Arrival of Gylippus at Syracuse

## Lacedaemonian Invasions and Athenian Response
During the summer of the seventeenth year of the war, the Lacedaemonians and their allies invaded Argos, resulting in the devastation of much of the country. In response, the Athenians dispatched thirty ships to aid the Argives. This action constituted an overt breach of the treaty between Athens and the Lacedaemonians. Prior to this event, Athenian cooperation with the Mantineans and Argives had been limited to descents on the coast of the Peloponnese and incursions from Pylos, specifically avoiding Laconian territory despite frequent requests from the Argives to land heavy infantry there. 

Under the leadership of Demaratus, Laespodius, and Phytodorus, the Athenians landed at Prasiae, Epidaurus Limera, and other locations to plunder the land. This provided the Lacedaemonians with a stronger pretext for hostilities. Following the withdrawal of the Athenian fleet and the Lacedaemonian forces, the Argives conducted an incursion into the Phlisaid, where they killed inhabitants and ravaged the land.

## The Command of Gylippus
Following information received from Alcibiades, the Lacedaemonians focused their efforts on sending aid to the Sicilians and the fortification of Decelea. Gylippus, son of Cleandridas, was appointed to command the Syracusans. He was instructed to coordinate with the Corinthians and the Syracusan people to arrange the fastest possible delivery of succours to the island. Gylippus requested two ships from the Corinthians to be sent immediately to Asine, while the remainder of the fleet was to be prepared for a later sailing.

## Arrival at Syracuse and Military Impact
Gylippus arrived at a critical juncture in the conflict. Before his arrival, the Corinthian commander Gongylus reached Syracuse in a single ship. Gongylus intervened in a Syracusan assembly that was considering ending the war, reassuring the citizens that Gylippus and further vessels were approaching. This news restored the courage of the Syracusans, who marched to meet Gylippus.

On his way, Gylippus captured Ietae, a fort belonging to the Sicels. He then arrived at Epipolae via Euryelus and advanced against the Athenian lines. At the time of his arrival, the Athenians had nearly completed a double wall of six or seven furlongs leading to the great harbour, though a small section by the sea remained unfinished. Other parts of the circle toward Trogilus were either half-finished or completed, and the danger to Syracuse had been significant.

## Conflict and Generalship
Upon entering the town, Gylippus led the Syracusans in an attack on the Athenians. He sent a herald to Nikias offering a five-day armistice on the condition that the Athenians collect their effects and leave Sicily. Nikias refused to answer this message. Some Athenian soldiers mocked the situation, questioning if a single Spartan cloak and staff had made the Syracusans so strong, noting that Athens had previously imprisoned three hundred such men. Some sources, such as Timæus, suggest the Sicilian Greeks initially ridiculed Gylippus for his avarice and his long hair and Spartan cloak. However, they eventually rallied around him, viewing his cloak and staff as symbols of Spartan authority.

In the first battle following his arrival, the Athenians were victorious and killed several Syracusans, including Gongylus. However, the following day, Gylippus utilized the same ground, horses, and arms to defeat the Athenians, demonstrating his qualities as a general. Philistus, a Syracusan eye-witness, noted that nothing could have been achieved without Gylippus.

## The Nineteenth Year and the Dekeleian War
The winter of 414-413 B.C. was marked by extensive military preparations leading into the nineteenth spring of the war, a period some historians refer to as the beginning of the Dekeleian war. While Eurymedon traveled to Syracuse with ten triremes in midwinter, Demosthenês worked to assemble a second armament for the spring. 

Athens sent twenty triremes around the Peloponnese to Naupaktus to block Corinthian reinforcements from leaving the Corinthian gulf. In response, the Corinthians prepared twenty-five triremes to convoy hoplites. Simultaneously, levies of hoplites were organized in Lacedæmon, Bœotia, Sikyôn, and Corinth for the armament to Syracuse and the occupation of Dekeleia. During this time, Gylippus worked actively to encourage all of Sicily to take a decisive part in the struggle.
