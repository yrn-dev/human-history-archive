# Athletic Metaphors in Christian Asceticism

## Origins of Ascetic Terminology
The practice and theory of asceticism, which involves religious self-mortification and bodily abstinence, draws its foundational terminology from ancient Greek sources. The term is derived from the Greek verb *askeo*, meaning "I practise," which led to the noun *askaesis* and the adjective *askaetikos*. This terminology embodies a metaphor originating from the *palaestra*, or ancient wrestling-place. In these athletic settings, victory was granted to those individuals who had most effectively trained their bodies.

## Application to Christians and Other Groups
The use of metaphors taken from athletic contests was not limited to a single group. These technical terms of Greek philosophic asceticism were first employed by the Neo-pythagoreans and the Cynics. Subsequently, these athletic metaphors were continued in the traditions of Christians and the Greek Jews.

## Nature and Purpose of the Metaphors
While Christians and other ascetic practitioners adopted these terms from the athletic arena, the archive notes that these were strictly metaphors. The actual practice of asceticism, when worthy of the name, is not about physical sport but possesses a moral purport. It is based on an eternal contrast between two propositions: "This is right" and "That is pleasant."

The essence of healthy asceticism lies in the discipline of the feelings and the training of character. This allows an individual to prefer a larger good over a momentary impulse. While primitive asceticism sometimes included self-torture or abstentions that appeared valueless for character training, the goal of great religious reformers—including Paul, Moses, Mani, Mahomet, St Francis, and Luther—was to direct the ascetic instinct toward higher aims, replacing ritual purity with true personal holiness.
