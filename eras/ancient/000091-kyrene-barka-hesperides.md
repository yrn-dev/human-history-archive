# KYRENE AND BARKA — HESPERIDES

The region encompassing Kyrênê, Barka, and Hesperides constitutes a maritime stretch of the African coast extending from the island of Platea (Gulf of Bomba) in the east to Hesperides (modern Bengazi) in the west. This area is characterized by mountains of considerable elevation, reaching their highest point near Kyrênê, and is interspersed with productive valleys and plains. This geography contrasts sharply with the interior country to the south and west, which is largely low, sandy, and destitute of trees.

## Geography and Environment
The maritime region is marked by frequent ravines that carry winter torrents into the sea, ensuring a supply of water throughout the year. This availability of water, particularly the inexhaustible Fountain of Apollo and other springs in the mountains, attracts Bedouin Arabs every summer when interior supplies fail. In ancient times, this same environmental advantage placed nomadic Libyans in a state of dependence on Barka and Kyrênê.

The distance between Hesperides and Ptolemeta (the port of Barka) is fifty-seven geographical miles across a fertile plain. Between these two lies the ancient Teucheira, located approximately thirty-eight miles from Hesperides. The road from Barka to Kyrênê shows ancient marks of chariot-wheels; it passes through the plain of Mergê before becoming woody and hilly, eventually clearing as it approaches Kyrênê, where valleys produce barley and hills provide cattle pasturage.

Kyrênê itself was situated about ten miles from the sea and eighteen hundred feet above the Mediterranean, providing a commanding view of the coast. Its sheltered port was called Apollonia. While the soil around Kyrênê is partly sandy and calcareous, it is noted for its vigorous vegetation and fertility, though ancient sources considered it inferior to the lands near Barka, Hesperides, and the region near Kinyps.

## Foundation and Political Development
Kyrênê was founded by Battus from the island of Thêra. Because Thêra was a colony of Lacedæmon, some sources, such as Isokratês, describe Kyrênê as a colony of Lacedæmon. The city was initially considered in relation to Irasa, which possessed the fountain Thestê, but the colonists ultimately chose the site of Kyrênê.

Barka was founded later as a Greco-Libyan city in the territory of the Libyan Auschisæ. Its establishment occurred during the reign of Arkesilaus the Second (approximately 554-544 B.C.) after the prince's brothers revolted and seceded with a portion of the citizens and Libyan Periœki. Barka was located about twelve miles from the coast and approximately seventy miles west of Kyrênê by sea.

Hesperides was the most westerly of the Grecian colonies in the region and existed by about 510 B.C. In the era of the Romans, these cities—Kyrênê, Apollonia, Ptolemais, Teucheira, and Berenikê (or Hesperides)—were referred to as the Pentapolis. However, in the time of Herodotus, it is possible that only three of these were spoken of as a Tripolis. Ptolemais, originally the port of Barka, eventually became autonomous and surpassed Barka in importance.

## Interaction with Libyan Tribes
The Greek colonies appropriated and interacted with various native Libyan tribes. Kyrênê took over the maritime territory of the Asbystæ, while the Auschisæ occupied the region south of Barka near Hesperides, and the Kabales resided near Teucheira in Barka's territory.

The Libyan Nomads lived in twisted tents, fed on meat and milk, and wore goatskins. They were noted by Herodotus for having better health than any other known people. They bred excellent horses and operated four-horse chariots that were admired by the Greeks; the magnates and princes of Barka and Kyrênê often used these horses to achieve success in the Greek games. Additionally, the Nasamônes tribe made annual journeys from the coast to the Oasis of Augila to purchase or gather dates.

## Persian Influence and Conflict
The region faced significant turmoil during the Persian era. Arkesilaus the Third sent his submission to the Persian king Kambysês. Subsequently, a Persian expedition advanced from Egypt against Barka. According to Polyænus, the city was captured through perfidy. Pheretimê, the mother of Arkesilaus, treated the prisoners with extreme cruelty, crucifying chief opponents around the walls and affixing the breasts of their wives to the walls.

Except for the Battiads and those not involved in the death of Arkesilaus, the inhabitants were enslaved and taken to the Persian empire. Darius assigned these captives a village in Baktria, which retained the name Barka. During this campaign, the Persian army reached as far as Hesperides and subjected many Libyan tribes. Consequently, Kyrênê, Barka, and these tribes served as auxiliaries and tributaries to Xerxês during his expedition against Greece.
