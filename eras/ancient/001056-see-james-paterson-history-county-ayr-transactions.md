# Ayrshire

Ayrshire is a south-western county of Scotland. It is bounded to the north by Renfrewshire, to the east by Lanarkshire and Dumfriesshire, to the south-east by Kirkcudbrightshire, to the south by Wigtownshire, and to the west by the Firth of Clyde. The county's coastline extends 70 miles and includes several offshore features: Horse Island off Ardrossan, Lady Island 3 miles south-west of Troon, and the conspicuous rock of Ailsa Craig, located 10 miles west of Girvan. The total area of the county is 1,142 square miles (724,523 acres).

## Geography and Topography
The surface of Ayrshire is generally undulating, featuring a small mountainous tract in the north and a larger one in the south and south-east. The highest peaks include Black Craig (2,298 ft.), located 5 miles south-east of New Cumnock; Enoch (1,865 ft.), 5 miles east of Dalmellington; Polmaddie (1,750 ft.), 2 miles south-east of Barr; Corsancone (1,547 ft.), 3 miles north-east of New Cumnock; and Stake, situated on the border of Ayrshire and Renfrewshire.

The county is served by six notable rivers: the Doon, Girvan, Garnock, Irvine, Stinchar, and the Ayr. The Ayr is the longest, rising at Glenbuck on the Lanarkshire border and flowing 38 miles into the Firth of Clyde. Other streams mentioned in the verse of Burns include the Cessnock, the Afton, and the Lugar. The region also contains many lochs, the largest being Loch Doon, which is 5.5 miles long and serves as the source of the river Doon.

Historically, the shire was divided into three districts: Cunninghame (north of the Irvine), Carrick (south of the Doon), and Kyle (between the Irvine and the Doon). Kyle was further split by the river Ayr into Kyle Stewart and King's Kyle. Politically, the county is divided into North Ayrshire (comprising Cunninghame) and South Ayrshire (comprising Kyle and Carrick).

## History
Traces of Roman occupation exist in the county. During the campaigns of Agricola, the region was held by the Damnonii; their town of Vandogara is identified with a site at Loudoun Hill near Darvel, where a serious encounter with the Scots occurred. Following the Roman withdrawal, Ayrshire became part of the kingdom of Strathclyde and later came under the sway of Northumbrian kings.

Significant historical events include the 9th-century slaying of the Scottish king Alpin at Dalmellington and the 1263 battle of Largs, where the Scots under Alexander III defeated Haakon of Norway. In the following generation, William Wallace campaigned in the shire, burning the barns of Ayr where the forces of Edward I were located and surprising the English garrison at Ardrossan. Robert Bruce, who was earl of Carrick and is alleged to have been born at Turnberry Castle, defeated the English at Loudoun Hill in 1307. Later, Cromwell visited the county, demolishing Ardrossan castle and reportedly using its stones to build a fort at Ayr.

Between 1660 and 1688, the county largely supported the Covenanters. On July 20, 1680, their leader Richard Cameron was killed at Airds Moss, a morass between the Lugar and the Ayr, during one of the movement's heaviest reverses.

## Population and Administration
As of 1901, the population of Ayrshire was 254,468 (up from 226,386 in 1891), with a density of 223 persons per square mile. In 1901, only 17 persons spoke Gaelic exclusively. The county returns two members to parliament for North and South Ayrshire. Ayr and Irvine are royal burghs within the Ayr parliamentary group, while Kilmarnock is a parliamentary burgh of the Kilmarnock group.

Administrative structures include a sheriffdom with resident sheriffs-substitute at Kilmarnock and Ayr, who also sit at Girvan, Cumnock, Beith, and Irvine. Education is managed under school-board jurisdiction, with secondary schools in Beith, Kilmarnock, Irvine, and Ayr. The Kilmarnock Dairy School, established in 1899, is part of the West of Scotland Agricultural College. Technical education is subsidized at local centers, covering subjects such as engineering, mining, plumbing, gardening, agriculture, and science and art.

## Economy and Industry
Ayrshire is Scotland's principal mining county and possesses the second largest coalfield. Major coal districts include Cumnock, New Cumnock, Muirkirk, Hurlford, Galston, Kilmarnock, Dreghorn, Kilbirnie, Dalry, Beith, Stevenston, Coylton, Irvine, Drongan, Maybole, Patna, Dalmellington, and Ayr. Ironstone is found primarily at Cumnock, Dreghorn, Kilbirnie, Dalry, Coylton, and Patna, with blast furnaces located in most of these towns. Additionally, the Water-of-Ayr stone, a valuable whetstone, is quarried at Bridge of Stair.

Manufacturing is diverse:
* **Textiles:** Cotton factories and bleachfields are at Catrine; carpet, woollens, blanket, and shoe factories are in Kilmarnock and Ayr. Hosiery and fabrics are produced in Stewarton, Beith, Kilmaurs, Kilbirnie, and Dalry. Lace curtains are manufactured in Darvel, Newmilns, and Galston.
* **Other:** Cabinet-making is a significant industry in Beith, and chemicals are traded in Irvine. Explosives and dynamite are produced in the sandhills near Stevenston.
* **Maritime:** Shipbuilding occurs at Irvine, Ayr, Troon, and Fairlie (noted for yachts). Major ports include Troon, Irvine, Girvan, Ayr, and Ardrossan. Fishing is conducted via the Ballantrae and Greenock fishery districts.

## The Town of Ayr
Ayr serves as the county town and is a royal, municipal, and police burgh. Located at the mouth of the river Ayr, it had a population of 29,101 in 1901. The town is known for its beautiful sands and the river Ayr, which is crossed by a railway viaduct and three bridges: the Victoria Bridge (1898), the New Brig (1788), and the Auld Brig, which is said to date from the reign of Alexander III. The Auld Brig was closed for public safety in 1904.

Notable landmarks include the county buildings, designed after the Roman temple of Isis, and the town buildings with a 226 ft. spire. The Academy, rebuilt in 1880, replaced a 13th-century grammar school in 1764. The Gothic Wallace Tower stands on the site of an older building; it features a statue of Sir William Wallace by James Thorn. Other statues in the town depict General Smith Neill, the 13th earl of Eglinton, and Burns. The Carnegie free library was established in 1893. The *Ayr Advertiser*, first published on August 5, 1803, was the county's first newspaper.

## Antiquities
The county contains various antiquarian remains, including:
* **Castles:** Dunure, Ardrossan, Portencross, Dundonald, Turnberry, and Loch Doon.
* **Other Sites:** Cairns in Sorn and Galston; camps on the hills of Dundonald and Knockgeorgan attributed to the Danes or Norwegians; and a Roman road extending from Ayr through Dalmellington and Dalrymple toward the Solway.
* **Religious Ruins:** The haunted church of Alloway and the abbeys of Crossraguel and Kilwinning.
