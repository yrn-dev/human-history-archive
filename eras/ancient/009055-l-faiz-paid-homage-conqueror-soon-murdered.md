# Homage and Political Authority

In the historical record, the act of paying homage is frequently associated with the establishment of political legitimacy, the recognition of sovereignty, and the administration of justice. It appears across various cultures as both a formal requirement of governance and a symbolic gesture of submission or respect.

## The Nature of Kingly Office and Homage
The institution of kingship is often characterized not by its external splendor or the reverence and exterior homage paid to it by subjects, but by the practical services and security it provides to a nation. In an ideal kingly office, the monarch serves as a source of protection and asylum, particularly for the poor and weak, sacrificing personal ease to shelter others from "storms and tempests."

To facilitate the execution of laws and the defense of the state against internal factions and external invasions, the title of king is bestowed, accompanied by the erection of a throne and the granting of a sceptre. In such systems, homage is paid to the monarch, who is provided with guards for security and assigned officers. These rulers are invested with full powers to administer justice, symbolized by the sword, which is used to punish crimes and restrain injustice.

## Homage in Regional Sovereignties
Historical records indicate that homage was often used to define the relationship between different territories or rulers. In the history of Alban, English chroniclers state that Kenneth II (who died in 995) paid homage to King Edgar in exchange for the cession of Lothian, though these accounts are noted as potentially being influenced by controversies regarding the position of Scotland.

In other contexts, the refusal to pay homage served as a marker of cultural or religious distinction. For instance, while the Romans protected the rites of the people they despised, the posterity of Abraham would have been viewed with abhorrence by their own brethren had they paid homage to the Jupiter of the Capitol, despite the fact that Augustus had ordered sacrifices for his own prosperity in the temple of Jerusalem.

## Religious and Moral Homage
Beyond political structures, homage is described in spiritual and ethical terms. In certain historical perspectives, those who knew the true God addressed him in their doubts and wants to obtain his will and succour. Conversely, those who followed "fictitious and deceitful deities" paid them homage, only to receive error, illusion, and fraudulent imitations of divine conduct in return.

In a moral sense, historians such as Tacitus suggest that the purpose of recording history is to make a judicious choice of extraordinary events—both good and evil. This process is intended to create a public abhorrence for vice and to ensure that the "public homage" justly due to virtue is paid.

## Cultural Practices of Homage and Ritual
In Korea, from the 10th to the 14th century, Buddhism was a dominant force, though it was later discredited. In its place, Confucianism became the official cult. Within this system, all officials are required to offer homage and sacrifices at stated seasons within Confucian temples, as Confucian ethics form the basis of social order and morality.

## Political Transitions and Violence
The transition of power often involved the sudden shift of homage or the violent removal of those in power. In Syracuse, Kallippus obtained the government after murdering Dion, but he eventually lost his power and was murdered by Leptines and Polyperchon using the same short, carved Laconian dagger used to kill Dion. Similarly, Kenneth II of Alban was slain treacherously by his own subjects at Fettercairn through an intrigue involving Einvela, daughter of the earl of Angus.

In Central Asia, the region of Sogdiana (also known as Sughd or Transoxiana) saw frequent shifts in dominion. It was influenced by the conquests of Alexander the Great, the Seleucidae, and the Yue-chi under King Kadphises I, whose dominions extended from Bokhara to the Indus. The region was later reconquered by the Sassanian dynasty of Persia and subsequently became a center of Islam following the Arab conquest of Persia between 640 and 642. Later, the region was subdued by Malik Shah of the Seljuk dynasty, Mahommed Shah Khwarizm in 1216, and Jenghiz Khan in 1220, before eventually falling to Timur (Tamerlane), who established his capital at Samarkand.
