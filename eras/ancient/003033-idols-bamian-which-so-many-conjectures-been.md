# The Idols of Bamian

The idols of Bamian are colossal figures carved into the cliffs of a valley in the Hazara country of Afghanistan. Located approximately 80 miles northwest of Kabul, the site sits on the primary road leading toward Turkestan at the northern foot of the Koh-i-Baba, a prolongation of the Indian Caucasus.

## Geography and Site Characteristics
The valley of Bamian is situated at a height of approximately 8,500 feet. It is drained by a river that serves as one of the primary sources of the Aksarai (or Sarkhab), which is an important tributary of the Upper Oxus. The surrounding terrain is characterized by high passes on the Kabul side reaching 11,000 and 12,000 feet.

The cliffs lining the valley feature precipitous faces that have been pierced by a vast number of ancient cave-dwellings over a distance of 6 to 7 miles; some of these excavations remain occupied. The hills are composed of pebbles and indurated clay, which facilitated the creation of these dwellings. The remains of the old city are marked by walls and mounds, while an isolated rock in the center of the valley contains ruins of what is believed to have been the acropolis, known locally as Ghulgulah.

## The Colossal Idols
The most famous remains at the site are two colossal standing idols carved into the north side of the valley's cliffs. These figures measure 173 feet and 120 feet in height. Both the images and the niches in which they are cut were coated with stucco. The larger idol features an as-yet-uninterpreted inscription above it. On either side of its niche, staircases lead to a chamber near the head of the figure, which shows evidence of gilding and elaborate ornamentation in azure. These chambers have been utilized by the amir as store-houses for grain. Additionally, the surfaces of the niches were painted with figures.

Beyond these two primary statues, a third colossus, slightly smaller than the 120-foot figure, is located in one of the branch valleys. There are further indications of other idols and niches in the area.

## Identification and History
While many conjectures were previously made regarding the nature of these figures, they have been ascertained to be Buddhist. This identification is based on the narrative of the Chinese pilgrim Hsuan-Tsang, who observed the idols in their splendor in A.D. 630. This was further verified by officers who discovered additional Buddhist excavations and caves in the Sarikol and Balkhab valleys.

Historical records of the city of Bamian are relatively obscure. It cannot be identified with any city in classical geography and was certainly not Alexandria ad Caucasum. The first known mention of the city is by Hsuan-Tsang, at a time when Bamian served as the head of one of the small states resulting from the breakup of the empire of the White Huns. Later, until A.D. 1214, it was the seat of a branch of the Ghori dynasty ruling over the basin of the Upper Oxus (Tokharistan).

The city was besieged and eventually annihilated in 1222 by Jenghiz Khan, who was provoked by the death of a favorite grandson caused by an arrow fired from the city walls. Evidence from the ruins at Ghulgulah and local traditions suggest the city was rebuilt after the Mongol period before perishing again. In 1840, during the British occupation of Kabul, the site was the location of a military action where Colonel William H. Dennie routed Dost Mahommed Khan and several Uzbeg chiefs.

## The Recumbent Figure
Located approximately 2 miles east of Bamian was a recumbent figure representing Sakya Buddha entering Nirvana (the act of death). According to the accounts of Hiuen Tsang (Hsuan-Tsang), this figure was approximately 1,000 feet in length. Modern travelers have not alluded to any traces of this statue. It is hypothesized that the figure was constructed from rubble and plaster—a method used for similar Nirvana figures in Indo-China—which would account for its lack of durability.
