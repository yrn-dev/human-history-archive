## Regional History of Bahraich
In the district of Bahraich (also spelled Bharaich), a town in the Fyzabad division of the United Provinces in British India, historical records indicate limited knowledge of the area prior to the Mahommedan invasion in A.D. 1033. During that year, Masaud, described as a champion of Islam, was defeated and slain by the nobles of Bahraich. Despite this event, Mahommedans did not establish authority over the country until the middle of the 13th century.

The district later saw the arrival of Rajput adventurers, known as Raikwars, who became masters of the western portion of the district around 1450 and maintained control into the modern era. In 1816, the Nepal *tarai* was ceded to the British via the treaty of Segauli, though it was returned in 1860. The district was also a site of significant fighting during the Mutiny; following the conflict, large portions of the land were distributed as *jagirs* to loyal chiefs, which led to the creation of the *taluqdari* estates.

Currently, the town of Bahraich is situated on the river Sarju and has flourished since the opening of the railway. It is home to a Mussulman monastery, the ruined palace of a nawab of Oudh, and a mission of the American Methodists. The tomb of Masaud serves as the most popular place of pilgrimage in Oudh, attracting both Hindus and Mahommedans. As of 1901, the population was 27,304.

## Philosophical and Ethical Works

In 1040, he authored a treatise in Arabic titled *Duties of the Heart*, which is regarded as one of the most influential and significant works of the middle ages for the Israelites. 

Bahya's work presents an intensely spiritual conception of religion and incorporates elements of impassioned mysticism. He viewed the Law, in the rabbinical sense, as an integral part of the inner life of the Israelite. His treatise is organized into ten distinct parts:

* Contemplation
* Worship
* Trust
* Consecration
* Humility
* Repentance
* Self-Examination
* The Ascetic Life
* The Love of God

## Botanical and Linguistic Notes on Incense
In Classical Latin, the word *thus* or *tus* was used generally for all types of incense. The term *Libanus* specifically referring to frankincense appears only in the Vulgate. The Romans referred to "ground pine" or "ground frankincense" (*Ajuga chamaepitys*) as *Tus terrae*. Other related terms include *Libanotis*, used for a plant that smells like frankincense, and *Libanios*, used for a fragrant Thasian wine.

The term *Olibanum* (derived from *Oleum Libani*), which is the common commercial name for frankincense, was used in a bull issued by Pope Benedict IX in 1033. Other variations include "European frankincense," a name applied to *Pinus Taeda* and the resinous exudation of Norwegian spruce firs (*Abies excelsa*), also known as "Burgundy pitch." In America, the "incense tree" is identified as *Icica guianensis* and the "incense wood" as *I. heptaphylla*.
