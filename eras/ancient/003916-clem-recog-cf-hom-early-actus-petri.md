# Barnabas

Barnabas was a figure in early Christian history, identified in the Acts of the Apostles as the cousin of Mark. He is described in Acts iv. 36 as the "son of exhortation."

## Missionary Work and Relationship with Paul
Barnabas was associated with Paul in several missions. Around A.D. 46, Barnabas and Paul conducted a relief visit to Judaea, which was accompanied by Mark. Subsequently, Barnabas and Paul embarked on a joint mission beyond Syria, with Mark serving as their assistant to handle travel-related personal duties and work.

During this mission, after the group left Cyprus and arrived at Perga in Pamphylia, Mark withdrew and returned to Jerusalem. This event led to a later disagreement between Paul and Barnabas. When Paul proposed revisiting the sites of their joint labors following the Jerusalem council (Acts xv.), he refused to take Mark again, doubting his reliability. Barnabas, however, supported his younger kinsman, and the two of them sailed away to Cyprus to resume their work.

## Later Life and Traditions
Following his departure for Cyprus, historical records of Barnabas become sparse. Some evidence regarding his later activities is derived from tradition and specific early texts:

*   **Financial Principles:** According to 1 Cor. ix. 6, Barnabas followed the principle of self-support during his mission work.
*   **Colossae:** Records from Col. iv. 10 indicate that by approximately A.D. 60, Barnabas was well known and respected at Colossae.
*   **Alexandria:** Clement of Alexandria regarded Barnabas as one of the seventy. Tradition, cited in *Clem. Hom.* i. 8 and ii. 4, suggests he traveled to Alexandria. This tradition is further supported by the ascription of the Alexandrine *Epistle of Barnabas* to him.
*   **Rome and Milan:** There is stronger and more varied evidence for a visit to Rome, found in *Clem. Recog.* i. 7, *Clem. Hom.* i. 7, the early *Actus Petri Vercellenses*, and the late Cypriot *Encomium*. Later tradition also suggests he may have visited Milan.

## Authorship and Death
There is a Western tradition, beginning with Tertullian (*De Pud.* 20), that ascribes the Epistle to the Hebrews to Barnabas. This may be an inference based on the epistle's self-description in xiii. 22 as a "word of exhortation," linking it to Barnabas's title as the "son of exhortation."

The date of Barnabas's death remains uncertain, though it is probable he was no longer living when the Acts was written (c. A.D. 75-80). While some legends claim he performed missionary labors in Cyprus and suffered martyrdom at Salamis, these accounts are considered late and untrustworthy.
