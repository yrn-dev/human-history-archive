# BONONIA

Bononia, known in the modern era as Bologna, was the chief town of ancient Aemilia in Italy. Historically, it served as a critical meeting-point for the road systems of north Italy, a role mirrored by the modern city's status as an important railway centre.

## Early Origins and Pre-Roman Period
Classical writers attributed the origin of Bononia to the Etruscans, stating it was founded from Perusia by Ocnus (or Aucnus) under the name Felsina. However, recent excavations have revealed a more complex early history. Discoveries include approximately 600 ancient Italic (possibly Ligurian) huts and cemeteries dating from 800 to 600 B.C. These were followed by an Umbrian period that immediately preceded the Etruscan civilization, which existed from roughly 600 to 400 B.C. Evidence of the Etruscan presence is further supported by an extensive necropolis found on the site of the modern cemetery, as well as other finds on the Arnoaldi Veli property and in the public garden.

## Roman Integration and Infrastructure
Bononia first appears in the historical record in 196 B.C., at which time it was in the possession of the Boii and had likely already changed its name. It became a Roman colony in 189 B.C. and later acquired Roman citizenship in 90 B.C.

The city's importance was solidified by its strategic location on the Via Aemilia. In 187 B.C., this road connected Bononia with Placentia and Ariminum. In the same year, a road was constructed to Arretium, and another was likely built around 175 B.C. leading to Aquilelia.

During the Roman Imperial period, the city continued to develop:
* **Military Use:** In 43 B.C., Mark Antony used Bononia as his base of operations against Decius Brutus and settled colonists there.
* **Augustan Improvements:** Emperor Augustus added further colonists and constructed a new aqueduct from the Letta, a tributary of the Rhenus. This aqueduct was later restored to use in 1881.
* **Imperial Aid:** Following a fire in A.D. 53, the emperor Claudius provided a subvention of 10 million sesterces (L1,087,500).

## Late Antiquity and Transition
Bononia was one of the most significant cities of ancient Italy. Its strength was evidenced in 410 A.D. when it successfully resisted Alaric, preserving its existence while other areas faced general ruin. Following this period, the city belonged to the Greek exarchate of Ravenna. While there are no Roman remains currently standing above ground, various discoveries have been made within the city walls.

## Modern Bologna
The modern city maintains its historical role as a transport hub. The main railway line from Milan divides at Bologna, with one branch following the path of the ancient Via Aemilia toward Brindisi, Ancona, and Rimini, while another heads through the Apennines toward Rome and Florence. Additional lines connect the city to Padua, Ferrara, and Portomaggiore, with steam tramways serving Malalbergo, Pieve di Cento, and Vignola. The city is also noted for its local specialities, including liqueurs, *tortellini* (a type of macaroni), and *salami* or *mortadella* (Bologna sausage).
