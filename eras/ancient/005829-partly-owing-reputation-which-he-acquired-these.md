# The Acquisition and Nature of Reputation

In the records of human history, reputation is often established through a combination of professional output, political affiliation, personal character, and the quality of one's associations. The archive's sources illustrate how this standing is acquired across various fields, including law, politics, theology, and military leadership.

## Reputation in Law and Academia

The acquisition of reputation in the legal field often stems from a mixture of public activity and specialized scholarship. Petrus Baldus de Ubaldis (1327–1406), an Italian jurist from Perugia, achieved a great reputation among his contemporaries. This standing was due in part to the fame he gained through his consultations—five volumes of which were published in 1589—and in part to the active role he played in public affairs. Despite this contemporary fame, the archive notes that his extant works hardly bear out the scale of that reputation, as many were left incomplete.

In a broader institutional sense, the University of Bologna acquired a European reputation as a school of jurisprudence. This was established under the influence of Pepo, the first known teacher of Roman law at Bologna (circa 1076), his successor Irnerius, and their followers known as the glossators.

## Political and Intellectual Standing

Reputation can also be a catalyst for political advancement. In the case of the individual who rose to the presidency of the Constituent Assembly during the Revolution of 1848, his elevation was partly owing to the reputation he had acquired through his publications. However, this was further bolstered by his connections to secret societies hostile to the government of Louis Philippe and his association with the *National* newspaper. Despite this initial standing, he proved to lack the qualities necessary for such a tempestuous and difficult situation, leading to a short tenure and a failure to be re-elected after the assembly's dissolution.

In the realm of historical scholarship, the reputation of Grote rests mainly on his work, *The History of Greece*. Even half a century after its production, the work remained a text-book. While some portions became obsolete due to the discovery of the Aristotelian *Constitution of Athens* and advancements in archaeological and epigraphic research, the work is noted for its comprehensive scope and its role as one of the first studies of Greek history based on modern historical science and real evidence.

## Religious and Theological Influence

Within the Christian Church, reputation may be tied to the perceived authority of a text or the character of an author. The Gospel of St. Mark, for instance, historically aroused less interest and attachment than the first and fourth Gospels, partly because its author did not bear the name of an apostle. However, it has recently acquired new importance through critical inquiries suggesting that the other two synoptic Gospels are based upon it, or a document it represents, making it a more primary source of information.

Conversely, Ephraem the Syrian, a 4th-century saint from Mesopotamia, became one of the most influential Syriac authors. His widespread fame as a preacher, poet, commentator, and defender of orthodoxy is attributed to several factors: the "vast fertility of his pen" (credited by the historian Sozomen as having written 3,000,000 lines), the elegance of his style, his poetic inspiration, the consistency and strength of his personal character, and his ardour in defending the creed formulated at Nicaea.

## Military and Regional Reputation

Reputation also manifests in military leadership and regional productivity. Philip of Macedon's reputation for indefatigable activity and generalship was felt everywhere. This was complemented by the reputation of his assembled officers and soldiers, including the hypaspistæ and the lochages (front-rank men of the phalanx), who were a mix of chosen Greeks and native Macedonians. Philip further increased his military capabilities by gaining ascendency in Thessaly, where the philo-Macedonian party appointed him federal chief (or Tagus), granting him access to their cavalry and common funds from market-dues and customs.

In a non-political context, reputation can be tied to agricultural output. In the county of Caithness, the adoption of a policy of large holdings on long terms—advocated by James Traill of Ratter in 1824—allowed farmers to invest capital and labour. Consequently, the county acquired a remarkable reputation for its produce, specifically beans, potatoes, turnips, barley, and oats.
