# The Psalms

The Psalms are a collection of sacred writings that have functioned as separate books in early biblical traditions and continue to serve as a central element of various liturgical practices.

## Composition and Authorship
According to the writings of Josephus, the Book of Psalms was composed by David. Josephus suggests that David wrote these works generally at the end of his life or after his wars had concluded, rather than at various times prior. This view is shared by the authors of the known books of the New and Old Testaments, as well as the Apostolical Constitutions, who do not ascribe the Psalms to any author other than David.

Other historical estimates regarding the dating of the text vary. Some sources indicate that several Psalms were written later than the time of Alexander, while the Book of Daniel is dated to between 167-5 B.C.

## Biblical Context and Instrumentation
In early biblical history, the Bible appeared to consist primarily of the Pentateuch (the first five books of the Old Testament). However, the Psalms existed as separate books, alongside others such as Proverbs and Chronicles, before being incorporated into the present Hebrew Bible.

The text of the Psalms also provides references to ancient musical instruments. Specifically, Psalm xxxiii. 2 mentions the "kinnor, nebel and asor," while Psalm xcii. 3 mentions the "nebel and asor," and Psalm cxliv. refers to the "nebel-asor." The term *asor* is translated as "an instrument of ten strings." It is likely a variant of the *nebel*, which Josephus notes typically had twelve strings. In the Septuagint, the term is rendered as *en dekachordo* or *psaltaerion dekachordon*, and in the Vulgate as *in decachordo psalterio*.

## Liturgical Use
In liturgical traditions, the day is divided into "hours," each with its own office. These offices are composed of several constituent parts, including lessons, hymns, responsories, antiphons, collects, versicles, little chapters, and psalms.

Within these offices, certain Old Testament passages are admitted as psalms, including:
* The Song of Moses (Exodus xv.)
* The Song of Hannah (1 Sam. ii.)
* The prayer of Habakkuk (iii.)
* The prayer of Hezekiah (Isaiah xxxviii.)

Additionally, New Testament passages such as the Benedictus, the Magnificat, and the Nunc dimittis are also categorized as psalms.

In practice, the multiplication of festivals for saints—which often utilize the same special psalms—leads to the constant repetition of approximately one-third of the Psalter. This results in the remaining two-thirds being recited rarely. This differs from the *Proprium de Tempore*, which, if followed, would provide equal opportunities for the recital of every psalm.

## Comparative References
The structure of other sacred texts has been compared to the Psalms. For example, Sura i. of the Koran is placed at the beginning of its collection not only because it expresses important articles of the faith and praises Allah, but in a manner similar to how Psalm i. is in praise of the righteous man.
