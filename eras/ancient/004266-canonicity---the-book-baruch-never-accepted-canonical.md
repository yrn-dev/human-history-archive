# Canonicity

Canonicity refers to the process and status of books being recognized as authoritative within a religious collection. The formation of these collections often involves successive stages of recognition and varying degrees of acceptance across different traditions and historical periods.

## The Old Testament Canon
The collection of the Old Testament is understood to have developed through three successive stages of historical collection. The first part to be definitely recognized as authoritative, or canonized, was the Law. Internal evidence suggests the Law may not have been formally accepted as a whole before 444 B.C. The "Prophets" were the next to be accepted as canonical, reaching completion and recognition around 250 B.C. The final group, a more miscellaneous collection known as the Hagiographa, was recognized last, between approximately 150 and 100 B.C.

There is no formal historical account regarding the formation of the Old Testament canon. Some patristic writers refer to a legend found in the apocryphal book of 2 Esdras, written toward the end of the 1st century A.D., which claims that Ezra miraculously rewrote the law and dictated 94 books to five scribes. These 94 books allegedly included the 24 books of the Old Testament (by Jewish reckoning) and 70 apocryphal books of "esoteric wisdom." However, this account is considered unworthy of credit and does not provide factual evidence regarding the redaction or completion of the canon.

## The Book of Baruch
The Book of Baruch is a deutero-canonical book of the Old Testament. Its placement varies by version: the Septuagint (LXX) places it between Jeremiah and Lamentations, while the Vulgate places it after Lamentations. The book is characterized by a plurality of authorship due to the poor coherence of its parts. Its contents include a historical preface describing the book's origin and purpose, and a confession of sin used by the Palestinian Remnant. This confession, sent from Babylon to Jerusalem, acknowledges national sin and views the Exile as a righteous judgment.

The canonicity of the Book of Baruch has been a subject of divergence:
* **Palestinian Jews:** The book was never accepted as canonical by Palestinian Jews (as noted in Baba Batra 14^b).
* **Early Church:** The *Apostolic Constitutions* state it was read in public worship on the 10th day of Gorpiaeus, though this statement is regarded as hardly correct.
* **Later Christian Traditions:** The book was in general use in the church until its canonicity was rejected by Protestant churches. Conversely, it was accepted by the Roman church at the council of Trent.

Versions of the Book of Baruch include two Latin versions, a Syriac, and an Arabic. The Latin version in the Vulgate is considered tolerably literal and dates to a time prior to Jerome. A later, less literal Latin version was published by Jos. Maria Caro in 1688. The Syriac and Arabic versions found in the London Polyglot are literal. Additionally, a Hexaplar-Syriac version was produced by Paul, bishop of Tella, in the early 7th century.

## Christian Canonization Processes
The decisive influence in fixing the Christian canon and doctrine occurred between 325 and 450, driven by theologians and bishops. This process was supported by the practices of the greater churches and the "instinctive judgment" of the faithful. This religious instinct is viewed as having played a more significant role than quasi-scientific criticism, which would have been inadequate at the time. While the resulting canon of Christian Sacred Books is not claimed to be infallible, experience suggests that any mistakes made were unimportant and were rectified over time.

## Buddhist Canonical Texts
In the Buddhist tradition, the Buddha taught through conversation, embodying doctrines in set phrases called *sutras*. Due to a lack of suitable writing materials, these *sutras* served as *memoria technica*—catchwords that recalled fuller expositions. The Buddha used the conversational idiom of Pāli rather than the Sanskrit used by the Brahmins.

Following the Buddha's death, his disciples collected these sayings into the Four Nikāyas (collections), which reached their final form approximately fifty or sixty years later. A supplementary Nikāya was added, containing sayings and verses attributed to the disciples. These were handed down by memory for a generation or two, supplemented by written memoranda and running commentaries. By the 3rd century B.C., during the time of Asoka, slight additions were still being made to the supplementary Nikāya. Following a schism in the community about one hundred years after the Buddha's death, the two resulting schools each maintained their own arrangement of the canon in Pāli or an allied dialect.
