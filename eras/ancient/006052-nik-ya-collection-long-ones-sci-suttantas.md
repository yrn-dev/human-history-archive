# Nikāya

The Nikāyas are the four primary collections of canonical Pāli texts that preserve the teachings of the Buddha. These works are not books in the modern sense, but rather memorial sentences or verses designed to be learned by heart. Because they were intended for oral transmission rather than reading, their style and arrangement are subordinated to the necessity of memory.

## Structure and Composition
The basic unit of these collections is the *sutra* (Pāli: *sutta*), which is typically a very short piece, often occupying only one or two pages and containing a single proposition. When several suttas containing similar propositions are collected within the framework of a single dialogue, the result is called a *suttanta*. While a typical suttanta is about a dozen pages long, some are longer. A collection of these suttantas constitutes a Nikāya.

The texts were developed through a gradual process of accumulation. First, the suttas emerged as set phrases embodying doctrine. These were then incorporated into episodes, which were subsequently woven together into suttantas. Finally, these suttantas were grouped into the Nikāyas. Parallel to this prose development was the use of verses, which were adopted because they assisted memory and allowed adherents to express feelings of ecstasy and enthusiasm regarding the reforming doctrines.

## The Four Nikāyas
The four collections are organized primarily by length and subject:

*   **Dīgha Nikāya**: Known as "the Collection of Long Ones," this is the first of the four Nikāyas and contains the longest suttantas. It includes a prose account of the Buddha's "going forth" and the *Mahāparinibbāna Suttanta*, which details the Buddha's last days.
*   **Majjhima Nikāya**: The "Collection of the suttantas of Medium Length," containing texts shorter than those in the Dīgha but longer than those in the remaining two collections. It contains texts regarding the birth of the Buddha and a long episode covering his years of study, penance, the attainment of Buddhahood, and the conversion of his first five converts.
*   **Anguttara Nikāya**: This is the longest book in the Buddhist Bible, filling 1,840 pages (8vo). It organizes Buddhist doctrine—specifically psychology and ethics—into numbered classes. It begins with units, followed by pairs, trios, and so on.
*   **Samyutta Nikāya**: The final collection arranges the doctrine by subject. It consists of 55 *Samyuttas* (groups), where suttas on the same subject or those addressed to the same type of people are grouped together.

Together, the Dīgha and Majjhima Nikāyas contain 186 dialogues. In these, the Buddha or one of his leading disciples is represented as discussing ethical, philosophic, or religious points.

## Language and Historical Development
The Buddha taught by conversation, utilizing set phrases (*sutras*) that he would enlarge upon. While writing was known during his time, a lack of suitable materials made lengthy books impossible. Unlike the Brahmins, who used Sanskrit (a dead language), the Buddha purposely used Pāli, the ordinary conversational idiom of the day.

Following the Buddha's death, his disciples collected these sayings. The Nikāyas are believed to have reached their final form approximately fifty or sixty years after his death, though they reached their present shape before the 4th century B.C. For a generation or two, they were handed down by memory, potentially aided by written memoranda and running commentaries. About one hundred years after the Buddha's death, a schism in the community led to two different schools, each maintaining its own arrangement of the Pāli canon.

## Supplementary Texts and Abhidhamma
Beyond the four primary Nikāyas, a supplementary collection called the *Khuddaka Nikāya* was created. This includes the *Khuddaka Patha*, which contains a profession of faith and a list of the thirty-four constituents of the human body, and the *Dhammapada*, a collection of 423 verses on Buddhist ethics and self-training.

Additionally, seven later works on *Abhidhamma* were added to the canonical books. These provide a more classified and elaborate exposition of the Dhamma (doctrine). One such manual, the *Dhamma Sangani* (translated as *Buddhist Psychology*), is estimated to date from the 4th century B.C.
