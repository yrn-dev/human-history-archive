# Archelaus's Ethnarchy Is Reduced Into A Roman Province

The transition of the ethnarchy of Archelaus into a Roman province marked a significant shift in the administration of the territories previously held by Herod. This change followed a period of governance characterized by internal conflict, accusations of brutality, and the eventual banishment of Archelaus.

## Establishment of the Ethnarchy
Following the death of Herod, Caesar divided the kingdom. Archelaus was appointed as Ethnarch over one half of the territory, with the promise that he might be granted the dignity of king if he proved himself worthy. His dominions included Judea, Idumea, and Samaria, as well as the cities of Jerusalem, Joppa, Sebaste, and Strato's Tower. While the revenue of this land is cited as four hundred talents in some records and six hundred talents in others, Caesar granted Samaria a mitigation of one quarter of its taxes because they had not joined the general revolt.

The other half of Herod's kingdom was divided into two tetrarchies. One was given to Philip, encompassing Auranitis, Trachonitis, Batanea, and parts of Zeno's house (or the House of Zenodorus) about Jamnia, with a revenue of one hundred talents. The second was given to Herod Antipas, who governed Galilee and Perea with a revenue of two hundred talents. Additionally, the Grecian cities of Hippos, Gadara, and Gaza were separated from the kingdom and added to the province of Syria.

## Governance and Controversies
During his tenure, Archelaus engaged in several significant public works and political maneuvers. He rebuilt the royal palace at Jericho and established a village named Archelais. To water the palm trees he planted there, he diverted half of the water that previously supplied the village of Neara. In the religious sphere, he removed Joazar, the son of Boethus, from the high priesthood on accusations of assisting the seditious, replacing him with his brother Eleazar. However, Eleazar was later replaced by Jesus, the son of Sie.

Archelaus's personal life also drew criticism. He married Glaphyra, the daughter of Archelaus, king of Cappadocia. Glaphyra had previously been married to Alexander, the brother of Archelaus (and son of Herod), and later to Juba, king of Libya. Because marrying a brother's wife was considered detestable among the Israelites, this union was viewed as a transgression of the law of their fathers.

## Banishment and Fall
The administration of Archelaus was marked by the "barbarous" treatment of both the Israelites and the Samaritans, stemming from old quarrels. This led to the dispatch of ambassadors to Caesar to lodge complaints against him. In the ninth year of his government, Archelaus was banished to Vienna, a city in Gaul, and his effects were seized for the treasury of Caesar.

Prior to his summons to trial, Archelaus reportedly experienced a vision of nine large ears of corn being devoured by oxen. While various diviners offered different interpretations, Simon of the Essens sect suggested that the ears represented years and the oxen represented a mutation of things. He predicted that Archelaus would reign for as many years as there were ears of corn before facing a change in fortune. Five days after this interpretation, Archelaus was called to his trial.

## Transition to Roman Province
Following the removal of Archelaus, his portion of Judea was reduced to a Roman province. Caesar appointed Coponius, a member of the Roman equestrian order, as procurator. Coponius was granted the power of life and death.

During this period of Roman provincial administration, the other sons of Herod, Philip and Antipas, continued in their respective tetrarchies. Philip built the city of Julias in lower Gaulonitis and the city of Cesarea in the region of Paneas at the fountains of Jordan. Herod Antipas built the city of Tiberius in Galilee and another city called Julias in Perea.

## Social and Religious Climate
The transition to Roman rule coincided with internal social unrest. A Galilean named Judas led a revolt, arguing that his countrymen were cowards for paying taxes to the Romans and submitting to mortal men as lords instead of God.
