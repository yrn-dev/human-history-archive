# Gaul

Ancient Gaul was a vast region encompassing the territory between the Ocean, the Rhine, the Alps, and the Pyrenees. Its extent was greater than that of modern France, including areas such as the cantons of Switzerland, the duchy of Savoy, the four electorates of the Rhine, and territories including Flanders, Brabant, Hainault, Luxemburgh, and Liege.

## Geography and Roman Administration
Under the laws introduced by Augustus, Gaul was divided into provinces based on national distinctions, the course of rivers, and the progress of the legions. This division organized what had previously been over a hundred independent states. The six provinces during the reign of the Antonines were:
*   **The Narbonnese:** Including Provence, Dauphiné, and Languedoc.
*   **Aquitaine:** Extending from the Pyrenees to the Loire.
*   **The Celtic (or Lyonnese):** The region between the Seine and the Loire.
*   **The Belgic:** Located beyond the Seine; historically bounded by the Rhine, though Germans had occupied a significant portion of this territory before the age of Julius Caesar.
*   **Upper and Lower Germany:** The frontier along the Rhine from Leyden to Basil.

## People and Culture
The chief tribes of Gaul belonged to the same Celtic people as those who had temporarily occupied north Italy and those who settled in Asia Minor as Galatians. In Italy, the Insubrians—a powerful Celtic tribe at the foot of the Alps—were known to enroll mercenaries called Gæsatæ.

The Gauls held an ancient reputation that caused significant terror among the Romans, who feared them more than any other nation. This fear was rooted in the fact that the Gauls had once captured the Roman city. Consequently, Rome passed a law exempting priests from military service except in the event of another war with Gaul. During the outbreak of such a conflict, the Romans followed prophecies from the Sibylline books, which included the burial of two Greeks and two Gauls alive in the Cattle Market. To this day, religious mysteries are performed in November in accordance with these prophecies.

The religious life of the Celts was characterized by the priesthood of Druids. As in Britain, Druids in Gaul taught secret lore, wielded great influence, and practiced magical arts and barbarous rites of human sacrifice, though they generally did not participate in politics.

## Lugudunum
Lugudunum (or Lugdunum), a Celtic place-name meaning "hill or fort of the god Lug (or Lugos)," was used for several towns, the most prominent being the city at the confluence of the Rhone and Saône, now known as Lyons. In Roman times, it was the most important town in western Europe and the capital of all northern Gaul.

The city consisted of two distinct elements: a self-governing Roman *colonia* (municipality of Roman citizens) on a hill near the present Fourviéres, and the Temple of Roma and Augustus. The latter served as a center for the 64 Gallic cantons across the three provinces of Belgica, Lugudunensis, and Aquitania (the *Tres Galliae*), who sent delegates every summer to celebrate the worship of the emperor. Lugudunum possessed a mint and controlled the trade passing from northern Gaul to the Mediterranean. Its wealth was substantial; for example, its inhabitants provided large subscriptions for the relief of Rome after the city was burnt during the reign of Nero.

## Roman Conquest and Transition
Julius Caesar established his reputation as a military leader in Gaul, where he drove back a German invasion and incorporated the entire region into the Roman Empire. Following the Roman conquest, the Celtic language was preserved in regions such as Armorica.

The influence of Gaul extended to the architectural styles of Roman Britain. Private houses in towns like Silchester—characterized by rows of rooms with corridors or large square open yards—find their real parallels in Gaul. These are considered Celtic types that were modified for Roman use, compared by some to Indian bungalows. While their internal fittings, such as mosaics, frescoes, and hypocausts, were Roman, the basic structural types were Gallic.

The Roman era in the region eventually faced instability. In the early 5th century, the Teutonic conquest of Gaul served to cut Britain off from Rome, contributing to the eventual collapse of Roman administration in the British Isles.
