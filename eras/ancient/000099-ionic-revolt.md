# Ionic Revolt

The Ionic Revolt was a significant conflict involving the Asiatic Greeks and the Persian Empire, serving as a point of confluence between the histories of the European and Asiatic Greeks. The revolt was characterized by a series of uprisings across Asiatic Greece, the involvement of mainland Greek city-states, and a subsequent Persian campaign of suppression.

## Origins and Outbreak
The revolt was precipitated by the actions of Aristagoras of Milêtus. Following an expedition against Naxos—undertaken by Aristagoras with the assistance of Artaphernês, the satrap of Sardis—the mission failed due to a dispute between Aristagoras and the Persian general Megabatês. Fearing the consequences of this failure, and encouraged by Histiæus, Aristagoras determined to revolt against Persia. 

The uprising began with the deposition and seizure of the despots in various cities, starting with the revolt of Aristagoras and the Milesians. This movement quickly extended throughout Asiatic Greece.

## Search for Allies
Seeking external support, Aristagoras first approached Sparta, but the Spartans refused to assist him. The Spartans had no kinship with Ionia beyond common Hellenism and were not in hostile relations with Persia; consequently, they viewed meddling in the Asiatic war as provoking a new enemy.

Aristagoras then proceeded to Athens, which was the second power in Greece and the mother-city of Asiatic Ionia. The Athenians were more receptive for several reasons: they already faced hostility from the Persian satrap, they were influenced by the instigations of Hippias, and they viewed the revolt as a form of indirect protection. Additionally, the removal of the Ionic fleet from Persian control provided a strategic relief to Athens. 

The Athenians resolved to send twenty ships under Melanthius to aid the revolted Ionians. Herodotus describes these ships as "the beginning of the mischiefs between Greeks and barbarians." Aid was also obtained from Eretria.

## Military Operations and Persian Response
With the support of Athenian and Eretrian allies, Aristagoras marched to Sardis. The Greeks burned the town, but were subsequently defeated by the Persians and forced to retreat. Following this defeat, the Athenians abandoned the alliance.

The revolt continued to spread to Byzantium and Cyprus. In response, the Persians summoned the Phenician fleet and sent an armament against Cyprus. Despite aid sent by the Ionians, the Persians were victorious and reconquered the island. The Persians then achieved further successes against the revolted coast of Asia Minor, leading Aristagoras to lose courage and abandon the country.

## The Siege of Milêtus
During the later stages of the conflict, Histiæus returned from Susa. Suspected by Artaphernês, he fled to Chios. After failing to gain admission into Milêtus, he led a small piratical squadron. 

The Persians assembled a large force, supported by the Phenician fleet, to conduct the siege of Milêtus. The allied Grecian fleet mustered at Ladê to oppose them. However, the Grecian forces suffered from a lack of discipline and command. While the Phôkæan Dionysius was allowed to assume command, the Grecian crews refused to act under him. This incapacity and disorder were further exacerbated by the treachery of the Samians and attempts by the Persians to disunite the allies using exiled despots.

## Chronology and Aftermath
Precise dating of the revolt is a subject of debate among chronological critics. While the battle of Marathon is fixed for August or September 490 B.C., estimates for the revolt vary. Some suggest it began in 502–501 B.C., with the siege of Milêtus concluding around 496–495 B.C. Herodotus notes that Milêtus was taken in the sixth year after the revolt.

Following the suppression of the revolt, the satrap Artaphernes rearranged and remeasured the territories of the Ionic cities (approximately 493 B.C.) and established a tribute assessment for each. According to Herodotus, these assessments remained unchanged in the Persian records until his own time, though the actual payment of these tributes was not enforced until shortly before 412 B.C., when the Athenian empire had weakened.

The suppression of the revolt also had indirect effects on European Greece. Miltiadês, who had been driven out of the Chersonese by the Persians during the suppression of the Ionic revolt, later used his fame from the conquest of Lemnos to secure his election as one of the ten generals for the year of the Marathonian combat.
