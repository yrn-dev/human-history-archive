# Calumnies Against The Sons Of Mariamne and Antipateris

## Origins of Hostility
The sons of Mariamne inherited the hatred that had been directed toward their mother. Having considered the magnitude of Herod's crime in putting her to death, the sons viewed their father as an enemy. This suspicion began during their education in Rome and intensified upon their return to Judea as they reached manhood.

Their return from Italy initially made them conspicuous and popular among the multitude, as they were seen as possessing royal dignity and great fortune. This popularity provoked envy in Salome, the king's sister, and others who had previously raised calumnies against Mariamne. These individuals feared that if the sons of Mariamne attained government, they would be punished for the wickedness committed against their mother. Consequently, they began scattering stories among the people, claiming the sons were displeased with Herod's company because he had murdered their mother. While these claims had a true foundation, they were used as a motive to make Herod hate his sons.

## Marriages and Increasing Tension
Despite the suspicions, Herod initially respected his sons and provided them with wives when they reached a suitable age. Aristobulus was married to Bernice, the daughter of Salome, and Alexander was married to Glaphyra, the daughter of Archelaus, king of Cappadocia.

As the sons grew older, they became bolder in their speech and continued to harbor hatred in their minds. This boldness provided a handle for those who calumniated them to tell the king that treacherous designs were being laid against him. Specifically, it was reported that the son-in-law of Archelaus intended to flee and accuse Herod before Caesar.

## The Rise of Antipater
In response to these reports, Herod restored Antipater—his son by Doris—to favor as a defense against his other sons. Antipater, who was born of a mother of no family, used his abilities to flatter his father and contrive various calumnies against his brethren. He told stories about them himself and employed other persons to raise further stories.

The sons of Mariamne were unable to contain their indignation at the rise of Antipater. Through the combined use of flattery and calumny, Antipater eventually cut his brothers off from any hope of succeeding to the kingdom. He was publicly named in Herod's will as the successor and was sent to Caesar with royal ornaments and marks of royalty, though without the diadem. Antipater's influence was such that he even succeeded in introducing his mother back into Mariamne's bed. His efforts eventually brought matters to a point where the king considered putting his other sons to death.

## Further Accusations and Conflict
Additional calumnies continued to inflame Herod's mind. Reports reached the king that his sons perpetually spoke of their mother and cursed him during their lamentations for her. It was further alleged that when Herod gave Mariamne's garments to his later wives, the sons threatened that these wives would soon be clothed in hair-cloth instead of royal garments.

Internal conflicts also contributed to the tension. Aristobulus raised a quarrel with his mother-in-law, Salome, and frequently upbraided his wife, Bernice, for the meanness of her family. He complained that while he had married a woman of low family, his brother Alexander had married one of royal blood. In response, Salome informed Herod that Alexander had threatened the mothers of his other brothers, claiming that upon ascending the crown, he would force them to weave with maidens and turn his brothers into country schoolmasters.

## Aftermath and Legacy
Antipater's success in securing the succession led to an intolerable hatred of him by the nation, as it was widely known that he had contrived the calumnies against his brethren. Despite his title, Antipater lived in terrible fear of the posterity of the sons who had been slain. Alexander had two sons, Tigranes and Alexander, and Aristobulus had three sons (Herod, Agrippa, and Aristobulus) and two daughters (Herodias and Mariamne).

To secure his position and appease potential enemies, Antipater used obsequiousness and large sums of money to gain favor with Pheroras, Caesar's friends in Rome, and Saturninus and his friends in Syria. However, these presents were viewed as being born of fear rather than generosity, which only increased the bitterness of those he did not pay and failed to earn the goodwill of those he did. This fear was exacerbated by the observation that the king showed repentance for killing the fathers of the orphans by demonstrating commiseration toward them.
