# From the Battle of Marathon to the March of Xerxes against Greece

## The Battle of Marathon and Its Aftermath
The battle of Marathon represented the first defeat the Persians ever received from the Greeks in the field. Prior to this engagement, the tide of Persian success had been uninterrupted, particularly during the ten years preceding the battle, during which the cruel extinction of the Ionic revolt had heightened Greek alarm. The Athenian effort to encounter the invaders—led by the general Miltiades—required extreme courage, as evidenced by the division of opinion among the ten Athenian generals. Despite this, the Athenians achieved victory, repulsing the Persian general Datis.

Following the battle, two thousand Lacedemonians arrived in Attica. Although they arrived too late to participate in the combat, they visited Marathon to view the bodies of the slain and commended the Athenians for their work. This victory briefly unified Greece through a shared sense of fear.

## The Transition of Persian Power
The news of the defeat at Marathon reached the Persian monarch, Darius, simultaneously with reports of a rebellion in Egypt. Darius, whose reign lasted thirty-six years, died while undecided as to which of these two directions to turn. Before his death, he had nominated his son Xerxes as his successor. Xerxes was the son of Queen Atossa, and her influence ensured his preference over his elder brother, Artabazanes.

Upon ascending the throne, Xerxes first addressed the Egyptian rebellion by establishing a Persian satrap there. He then spent four years preparing a second, more massive attack upon Greece to fulfill the revenge his father had sought against Athens.

## Preparations for the Second Invasion
Xerxes resolved to assemble the entire force of his empire, directing satraps and sub-governors throughout Asia to provide ships, horses, and troops. This immense levy agitated the empire for three years. According to Herodotus, Xerxes led out nations from across Asia, exhausting almost every water source except the great rivers. The requirements for the expedition were diverse: some nations provided ships, some provided cavalry, others furnished vessels to carry horses, and some provided ships of war for bridges or vessels for provisions.

Unlike Datis, who had traversed the Aegean to reach Eretria and Marathon, Xerxes planned a dual assault involving both a land force and a fleet. The fleet consisted of 1,2007 ships of war, along with numerous vessels of burden and service. To ensure the subsistence of his army, Xerxes displayed significant forethought by establishing large magazines of provisions—including flour and other essential articles from Egypt and Asia—at maritime stations from the Hellespont to the Strymonic gulf.

## The March of Xerxes
In the autumn of 481 B.C., the Persian army assembled near Sardis. A large portion of the force had gathered at Kritala in Kappadokia, where Xerxes joined them while traveling from Susa. He marched through Lydia and Phrygia, passing through towns such as Kallatêbus, Kolossæ, Anaua, and Kelænæ, before establishing winter quarters at Sardis.

Xerxes' land force crossed the Hellespont and marched through Thrace and Macedonia. This route was conducted through subject territory, supplemented by additional contingents levied along the way and Thracian volunteers seeking plunder. The Thracians held the road he traveled with such solemn reverence that it was protected from tillage and intruders even in the days of Herodotus.

As Xerxes reached the northern boundary of what was properly called Hellas, he came within sight of Mount Olympus. He was conducted further by the Thessalian Aleuadæ and the Macedonian princes, the last of his western tributaries. At this stage of the march, the Persian advance appeared to face no significant difficulty.
