# Og and the Region of Bashan

## Geography and Boundaries of Bashan
Bashan is a region located east of the Jordan River, situated toward the river's source and generally north of the territory of Gilead. Its boundaries are not precisely defined, but historical indications suggest Mount Hermon served as a northern limit. The region is characterized by volcanic origins, including the Jebel ed-Druz group of extinct volcanoes, which results in extraordinarily fertile soil capable of yielding barley a hundred-fold and wheat eighty-fold. Historically, the area was noted for its mountains, its lions, and specifically its oaks, which were used to make oars and are often mentioned alongside the cedars of Lebanon. The region was also proverbial for its cattle, which were regarded as ferocious.

Later accounts, such as those by Josephus, divide Bashan into four provinces:
*   **Gaulanitis:** Extending from the Jordan lakes to the Haj Road, further divided into Sogana and Gamalitis.
*   **Trachonitis:** Adjoining the territory of Damascus and corresponding to the modern Leja.
*   **Auranitis:** Located north of Gilead and south of the Jaulan.
*   **Batanaea:** Situated east of the Hauran plain and the Leja, including the Hauran mountain.

## Og and the Israelite Conquest
The name Bashan first appears in Hebrew history during the wanderings of the Israelites. According to the narrative in Numbers xxi. 33, after the Israelites routed Sihon, king of the Amorites, they turned toward the land of Bashan. There, they were met at Edrei by Og, the king of Bashan, who was defeated and slain. 

Og is described as being of gigantic stature, a characteristic attributed to his status as a remnant of the aboriginal Rephaim, a race known for their lofty height. He dwelt at Ashteroth, and the region of Argob—containing sixty cities with walls and brazen bars—is associated with his kingdom. Following the conquest, the territory was allotted to the eastern division of the tribe of Manasseh. One of the cities of refuge, Golan, was located within Bashan.

## Historical and Archaeological Analysis
The account of Og and the Israelite invasion of Bashan is a subject of scholarly dispute. Some analysts suggest that the details regarding Og's "bedstead" and his immense size are suggestive of legend. Furthermore, critics point to the remoteness of Bashan from the regions where the Israelites wandered and the lateness of the documents recording the wars of Og. Despite these disputes, the story is deeply embedded in Hebrew tradition, and an invasion of Bashan prior to the crossing of the Jordan is considered a possibility.

Archaeologically, there are no tangible traces of Og, his people, or the Israelites who supplanted them. While various sites have been proposed as the "giant cities of Bashan," such as the remains in the Leja and Hauran, these have been identified as Greco-Roman structures dating no earlier than the time of Herod. Specifically, the identification of Argob with the Leja is now generally abandoned. Some specific site identifications remain tentative: Edrei is sometimes equated with Ed-Dera'a due to a subterranean city of unknown date, and Salecah is linked to the ruin of Salkhat.

## Later History and Administration
In the era of Solomon, the region of Argob in Bashan was assigned to Ben-Geber, one of Solomon's lieutenants. Later, during the reign of Jehu, the country was taken from the Israelites by Hazael, king of Syria. Hazael spoiled the eastern parts of the country beyond Jordan—including Bashan, Gilead, and the lands belonging to the Gadites, Reubenites, and the half tribe of Manassites—burning and offering violence to the region while Jehu failed to defend it.

In more recent history, Bashan became known as a refuge for robbers and outlaws. Strabo mentioned a great cave in Trachonitis capable of holding 4,000 robbers. The Hauran region has also become a principal habitation for the sect of the Druses.
