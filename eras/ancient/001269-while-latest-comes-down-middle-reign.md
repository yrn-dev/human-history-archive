# Chronology and Historical Records

The methods of recording time and the preservation of historical annals vary significantly across different civilizations, ranging from the use of official lists and astronomical events to the recording of dynastic reigns and literary eras.

## Assyrian and Babylonian Chronology
In Assyria, later chronology was established through the use of *limmi* (archons), who gave their names to the years of their office. These lists, some of which were found in the library of Nineveh, date as far back as 911 B.C. and continue until the middle of the reign of Assur-bani-pal. This system of reckoning time by *limmi* was an Assyrian innovation that extended back to the early days of the monarchy; cuneiform tablets from the 15th century B.C. indicate that Assyrian settlers in the colony of Kara Euyuk near Kaisariyeh also utilized this method. Because of these lists, Assyrian chronology is considered certain from 911 B.C. to 666. Additionally, a solar eclipse visible in the month Sivan in 763 B.C. has been calculated to have occurred on June 15 of that year.

Babylonia employed a different system where years were dated by distinguishing chief events, a practice also seen in Egypt during the Old Empire. The government determined these events and notified officials, as evidenced by a notice sent to Babylonian officials in Canaan during the reign of Samsu-iluna, son of Khammurabi. Babylonia maintained registers divided by reigns, which were later used to compile dynastic lists detailing the duration of dynasties and individual kings.

## Egyptian and Hellenic Perspectives on Time
The Egyptians claimed to possess certain knowledge of history because they consistently wrote down the years as they passed. According to Egyptian records reckoning down to the reign of Amasis, the god Pan is described as very ancient, being one of the "eight gods." Heracles is ranked among the "twelve gods," and Dionysos is of the third rank, born of the twelve gods. The Egyptians reckon Dionysos to have existed for fifteen thousand years down to the reign of Amasis.

These Egyptian accounts differ from the perspectives of the Hellenes. The Hellenes consider Heracles, Dionysos, and Pan to be the latest-born of the gods. According to Hellenic tradition:
* Dionysos, born of Semele (daughter of Cadmos), was born approximately 1,600 years before the time of Herodotus.
* Heracles, son of Alcmene, was born approximately 900 years prior.
* Pan, born of Penelope and Hermes, came into being about 800 years before the time of Herodotus, appearing after the wars of Troy.

## English Literary and Prose Eras
The history of letters in England is marked by distinct transitions in language and style. While English poetry has roots in heathen times, effective English prose began during the reign of Alfred. Earlier vernacular prose existed, such as the laws of Aethelberht of Kent. Although it is unlikely these laws were written during the lifetime of King Aethelberht (who died in 616) or Augustine (who died A.D. 604), they were known to Bede.

Between the 7th and middle of the 9th century, Latin remained the preferred language for literary works. Alfred sought to combat the ignorance of spiritual guides and the needs of the laity, fostering the development of vernacular prose.

A later continuous period known as the English Renaissance spans from the publication of More's *Utopia* (1516) to Milton's *Samson Agonistes* (1671). This era, which includes "Elizabethan" literature, is characterized by a wealth of production and a distinct break from the preceding middle ages, though some medieval elements survived. These included the tales of Troilus and Arthur, the matter of Geoffrey of Monmouth, and the use of allegory. For example, Spenser used allegoric romance and Shakespeare utilized the histories of Lear, Macbeth, and Troilus. The arrival of Dryden marked a new phase of the Renaissance, while a significant break occurred around 1660, leading into the "Augustan" or classical age.

## Regional and Dynastic Records
In Persian history, the people of Shabánkára (also referred to as Soncara or Soucara) are noted for their influence. Their princes, of the Faslúyah family, were influential before the Mahomedan conquest, though they became prominent during the Mongol era. Shabánkára was taken from the Buyid Dynasty in 1056 by Fazl ibn Hassan and later taken by the Modhafferians in 1355. The Modhafferians, who ruled in Kermán, Fars, and ’Irák, were established after the death of Abu Saïd in 1335 and were eventually subjugated by Timur in 1392. The capital, I′g (or Irej), was captured by Hulaku in 1259. While the dynasty seemed extinguished after a 1311 rebellion, a descendant attempted a revival in the middle of the 14th century. The latest historical mention of the name appears in the year H. 807 (1404) in Abdurrazzák’s *History of Shah Rukh*.
