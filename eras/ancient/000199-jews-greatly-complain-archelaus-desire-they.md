# The Jewish Petition Against Archelaus

## Civil Unrest and the Role of Varus
Following the reign of Herod, the region experienced significant instability. After Archelaus departed, the nation entered a state of tumult. Varus, the president of Syria, intervened to punish the authors of the disturbance and restrain the sedition. To maintain order and keep the people quiet—who were described as being fond of innovation—Varus left one legion of his army at Jerusalem as a garrison before returning to Antioch.

Despite the presence of the legion, sedition persisted. Sabinus, a procurator of Caesar, remained in Jerusalem and caused great distress to the people. Driven by extraordinary covetousness and a love of gain, Sabinus used force to seize citadels and zealously searched for the king's money to seize it by force. He further oppressed the people by arming a multitude of guards to protect him, which eventually led the people to rebel.

## The Embassage to Rome
With the permission of Varus, an embassage of the people was sent to Rome to petition for the liberty of living by their own laws. This delegation consisted of fifty ambassadors, who were joined in Rome by more than eight thousand of the people already residing there.

Caesar convened a council of his friends and the principal Romans in the temple of Apollo, a structure he had built and adorned at vast expense. The ambassadors and the multitude of the people stood together to present their accusations against Archelaus. Archelaus attended the proceedings with his friends, but his own kinsmen refused to join him due to their hatred and envy of him. However, these relatives also declined to assist the ambassadors, fearing it would be a disgrace in the eyes of Caesar to act in opposition to a man of their own kindred.

## Competing Interests and Claims
During these proceedings, Philip, the brother of Archelaus, arrived from Syria. He had been sent by Varus for two reasons: to assist Archelaus and to ensure that if Caesar distributed Herod's possessions among his posterity, Philip would obtain a share.

Simultaneously, Antipas traveled to Rome to strive for the kingdom. He argued that a former testament naming him king was valid over the later testament that favored Archelaus. Antipas was supported by Salome, his mother, Ptolemy (a highly trusted friend of Herod), and the orator Ireneus. Many of Archelaus's kindred, who hated him, shifted their support to Antipas. While these relatives primarily desired to live under their own laws and be subject to a Roman governor, they viewed Antipas as the preferred alternative should that request fail.

## Internal Demands and Legal Disputes
Prior to the Roman petitions, the people had approached Archelaus with demands for revenge regarding those slain by Herod, specifically mentioning Matthias and those condemned for pulling down the golden eagle. They requested that Archelaus punish those who had been honored by Herod and replace the high priest with one of greater purity and more agreeable to the law.

Archelaus was offended by the importunity of these requests, as he intended to travel to Rome for Caesar's determination. He sent his general to persuade the people that the deaths of their friends had been according to the law and urged them to remain quiet and unanimous until he was established in government by Caesar's consent.

## Outcome and Subsequent Governance
After hearing the petitions, Caesar did not grant the request to be made subject to Roman governors; instead, he distributed Herod's dominions among his sons according to his own pleasure.

In later periods of Roman administration, tensions regarding the law continued. Under the governorship of Pilate, a conflict arose when Pilate attempted to force the people to admit Caesar's images into the city. When surrounded by soldiers with naked swords, the people fell in vast numbers and exposed their necks, declaring they were ready to be slain rather than allow their law to be transgressed. Surprised by this, Pilate ordered the ensigns to be removed from Jerusalem.
