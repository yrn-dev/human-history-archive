# The History and Use of Books

The history of books encompasses the evolution of their physical forms, the methods of their collection, and the diverse ways in which they have been studied and utilized by individuals and institutions across different civilizations.

## Ancient Libraries and Early Formats
In ancient Egypt, Ptolemy I established a great library that functioned as both a state library and a state publishing entity on an unprecedented scale. This institution was designed to be encyclopedic; any stranger bringing an unknown book to Egypt was required to have it copied for the collection. To manage this, a staff of copyists produced duplicates of necessary and popular works. Under the leadership of Callimachus during the reigns of Ptolemy II and III, the library's accumulations were systematically catalogued.

During this era, books were not composed of pages but were rolled. This format required readers to tediously roll the material forward or backward to find specific passages, a process that caused wear on both the reader and the book. To minimize this waste of time, Callimachus divided long works, such as the History of Herodotus, into separate rolls, which are referred to as "books" or volumes.

## The Evolution of Printing and Production
Early block-books, which are believed to be more ancient than those printed with moveable types, bear a precise resemblance to Chinese block-books. These books feature impressions on only one side of the paper, with blank sides placed opposite each other or pasted together during binding. Rather than printer's ink, they utilized a thin brown paint or color similar to Indian ink. It is suggested that this process was copied from Chinese specimens brought by early travelers, and some traditions suggest that Guttemberg may have based his art on specimens brought home by Marco Polo.

Even after the invention of printing reduced the cost of books by approximately 80%, the practice of book-collecting in the modern sense did not immediately emerge.

## Book Ownership and Collecting
Historically, a distinction existed between the student who desired a well-printed copy of a favorite author and the collector who cared more for the external appearance of books than the act of reading. In 1345, the *Philobiblon*, attributed to Richard de Bury, focused on the delights of literature. Conversely, Sebastian Brant later argued that possessing books was a poor substitute for actual learning.

Modern book-collecting grew later. Some early owners, such as Jean Grolier (1479-1565), bought books to encourage contemporary printers or to provide friends with Renaissance scholarship rather than out of antiquarian instinct. Other figures, including the historian Jacques Auguste de Thou (1553-1617), Archbishop Cranmer, and Lords Arundel and Lumley, assembled the best learned literature of their time, binding them in leather and decorating them with heraldic badges and coats of arms.

In other contexts, books were shared as common property. The brothers Wilhelm and Jacob Grimm lived under one roof and held their books and property in common throughout their lives.

## Study and Application of Texts
The purpose of reading has varied widely across history. Some individuals viewed reading as a means to virtue or action. One historical figure read philosophical treatises only if they taught virtue and studied passages of Homer to excite manly courage. He also studied military tactics through the history of Alexander the Great and Evangelus's treatise, though he believed reading was a waste of time unless it led to action.

In the study of epic poetry, such as the *Iliad*, critics have analyzed whether the work was a single projection or a series of additions. Some suggest the *Iliad* began as a narrower plan (an *Achillêis*) and was subsequently enlarged by successive additions, whereas the *Odyssey* appears to be of one projection from the beginning.
