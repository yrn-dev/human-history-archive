# Trine Immersion in Early Christian Baptism

Trine immersion refers to the practice of dipping a person into baptismal water three times. This rite was a significant feature of early Christian baptism, though its origins and the interpretations of its meaning varied across different traditions and eras.

## Interpretations of Trine Immersion
The practice of trine immersion has been explained through several different theological lenses. Tertullian provides one of the earliest explanations, linking the three immersions to the triple invocation of the baptismal formula. He stated, "Not once, but thrice, for the several names, into the several persons, are we dipped." Jerome similarly asserted that the three plunges were intended to show forth the "one sacrament of the Trinity."

Conversely, other figures in the East and West—including Athanasius, Cyril of Jerusalem, Gregory of Nyssa, Theophylactus, and Leo of Rome—viewed trine immersion as a symbol of the three days that Christ spent in entombment. This specific interpretation was enjoined in the Armenian baptismal rubric and in an epistle from Macarius of Jerusalem to the Armenians (circa 330). Armenian writers further associated this practice with the concept of baptism into the death of Christ.

## Origins and Pagan Influence
While various theological explanations exist, some evidence suggests the practice may predate these interpretations. Basil confessed his ignorance regarding the origin of trine immersion, suggesting that the rival explanations were aetiological—invented to justify a custom already in place. 

The archive indicates that the church may have adopted this custom from its pagan medium, as threefold lustrations were common in Greek and Latin antiquity. Examples of this are found in the writings of Ovid, Horace, and Persius. Virgil also wrote of a "pure wave" that carried companions around three times. A scholiast on Horace, Acro, noted that people plunged themselves thrice when expiating their sins.

## The Baptismal Formula and Rituals
The use of trine immersion did not always coincide with a uniform baptismal formula. While the *Teaching of the Apostles* prescribes baptism in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, it also mentions baptism into the name of the Lord. In the 3rd century, baptism in the name of Christ alone was widespread; Pope Stephen declared such baptisms valid in opposition to Cyprian of Carthage. Later, Pope Nicholas (858-867) also allowed baptism to be valid *tantum in nomine Christi*. However, Basil condemned baptism into the Lord alone as insufficient.

Other elements of the baptismal rite included:
* **Exorcism:** The *Didache* and Justin prescribed fasting to encourage the exit of evil spirits, based on the belief that demons preferred well-fed bodies. Over time, these exorcisms grew longer.
* **Anointing and Blessing:** Basil noted that the blessing of the baptizand, the water, and the oil of anointing, as well as the formal renunciation of Satan, were handed down through a "silent and sacramental tradition."
* **Age of Baptism:** In early Syrian, Armenian, and Greek rites, infants were blessed and sealed with the sign of the cross on the eighth day, acting as an analogue to Christ's circumcision, with the prayer that they enter the church at the proper time.

## The Baptistery and Physical Practice
The administration of baptism often took place in a separate hall or chapel known as a baptistery (or *photisterion* in the Greek Church). These were commonly circular buildings, though some had twelve or eight sides. The baptistery consisted of an ante-room for the instruction of catechumens and an inner apartment containing the baptismal font (*piscina* or *kolumbethra*).

In these fonts, those being baptized were immersed thrice. The fonts were initially made of stone, though metals were used later. Because the bishop typically baptized all catechumens in his diocese only three times a year, baptisteries were often attached to cathedrals rather than parish churches. During the months when no baptisms occurred, the doors of the baptistery were sealed with the bishop's seal.
