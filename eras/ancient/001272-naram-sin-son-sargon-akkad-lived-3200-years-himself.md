# Naram-Sin

Naram-Sin was a monarch of the Semitic Empire of Akkad, the son and successor of Sargon of Akkad. He is recognized as a famous conqueror and a great architect.

## Military Conquests and Imperial Titles
Naram-Sin continued the military successes established by his father. He led marches into Magan, where he took the king of that region captive. Following these achievements, he assumed the imperial title of "king of the four zones."

## Divine Status and Art
In a practice similar to that of his father, Naram-Sin was addressed as a god and was specifically called "the god of Agad" (Akkad). These divine honors are noted as being reminiscent of those claimed by the Pharaohs of Egypt, whose territory at the time adjoined that of Babylonia.

Artistic remnants from his reign include a finely executed bas-relief found at Diarbekr, which bears a striking resemblance to early Egyptian art in several features. Additionally, a stele glorifying King Naram-Sin was discovered in 1898 among the ruins of Susa, Persia. In terms of dress, evidence from the time of Naram-Sin shows the use of a tunic or skirt that reached the knees and was held up by ornamental shoulder-bands.

## Administration and Infrastructure
The empire under Sargon and Naram-Sin was integrated through a system of roads that supported a regular postal service. The administration utilized clay seals as stamps, some of which are preserved in the Louvre and bear the names of both Sargon and Naram-Sin. 

Furthermore, a cadastral survey appears to have been instituted during this era. Documents related to this survey mention Uru-Malik, a governor of the land of the Amorites (the Babylonian term for Syria and Palestine), whose name suggests a Canaanitish origin.

## Chronology and Historical Debate
The dating of Naram-Sin's reign is a subject of historical discussion based on a statement by Nabonidus (also referred to as Nabu-na'id). Nabonidus claimed that 3200 years separated his own age from that of Naram-Sin. Based on this statement alone, some sources have placed the date of Sargon at 3800 B.C.

However, this figure has not been universally accepted by scholars:
* **Lehmann-Haupt** suggested the text was misengraved and that the number should be read as 2200 instead of 3200.
* **Winckler** viewed the statement of Nabonidus as an uncritical exaggeration.
* Other theories suggest that the scribes of Nabonidus may have mistakenly reckoned several contemporaneous early dynasties as being consecutive.

Despite these debates, archaeological evidence, such as large bricks stamped with the names of Sargon and Naram-Sin found at Niffer, helps anchor their place in history.

## Family and Succession
Naram-Sin had a son named Bingani-sar-ali, though it is not currently known if he succeeded his father on the throne. Naram-Sin had another son who served as the high-priest of the city of Tutu. He also had a daughter, Lipus-Eaum, who was a priestess of Sin.
