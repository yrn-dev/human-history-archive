# Carelessness and Negligence in Legal and Social Contexts

The concept of carelessness, whether manifesting as clerical errors in financial instruments, professional negligence in ancient codes, or perceived laxity in political and religious spheres, has historically resulted in varied legal and social consequences.

## Banking and Financial Instruments
In the context of banking, the carelessness of a customer when filling out cheques has occasionally allowed third parties to fraudulently increase the original sum drawn. In the case of *Colonial Bank of Australasia v. Marshall* [1906], the judicial committee of the privy council determined that providing such facilities for forgery did not constitute a breach of the customer's duty to the banker. Consequently, the banker was not entitled to debit the customer for more than the original amount of the cheque. Furthermore, current authorities suggest that a customer's dealings with their pass-book cannot be used to prevent them from disputing unauthorized payments listed within it.

Regarding the collection of cheques, the custom of bankers is factored into the determination of a "reasonable time" for presentation. Under the Bills of Exchange Act, a holder of a cheque may employ a banker for collection without risking their rights against prior parties if the cheque is dishonoured. In such instances, the banker, acting as an agent, must provide notice of dishonour to either the customer (the principal) or the parties liable on the bill. While the usual practice is to return the cheque to the customer—a custom validated by sec. 49, subs. 6 of the Bills of Exchange Act—this provision appears to overlook cheques payable to bearer or those that became payable to bearer via blank endorsement before reaching the customer.

## Professional Liability and Ancient Codes
Historical legal codes have imposed severe penalties for carelessness and neglect, particularly when such failures led to physical harm or death. For example, an unskilful physician whose negligence resulted in the loss of a limb or life faced the penalty of having his hands cut off. In cases involving the loss of a slave's eye, the physician was required to pay half the slave's value. 

Similar strict liabilities applied to other trades:
* **Veterinary Surgeons:** If a surgeon caused the death of an ass or an ox, they were required to pay quarter value.
* **Builders:** Careless workmanship resulting in death could lead to the builder losing their own life or the life of their child. They were also required to replace lost goods or slaves and were obligated to repair any defects or rebuild the house.
* **Boat-builders:** These professionals were required to provide a one-year warranty to make good any damage or defects in construction.

## Custody of Valuables and Bank Liability
Banks often receive a customer's valuables for safe custody as a facility driven by competition. The liability of the banker for the loss of these goods depends on the nature of the loss. According to a memorandum from the Central Association of Bankers, a distinction is made between:
1. **Wrongful Conversion:** If valuables are delivered to the wrong person by mistake, the bank is liable regardless of negligence.
2. **Loss or Theft:** If goods are stolen, lost, destroyed, or fraudulently abstracted, the banker is viewed as a gratuitous bailee. In these cases, the banker is only liable if they failed to exercise the care an ordinary prudent man would take of his own valuables. This assessment takes into account the nature of the goods and the bank's access to exceptional protection, such as strong-rooms.

## Social and Political Observations
Carelessness has also been noted in social and political observations. In ancient Greece, elder men frequently visited gymnasia to listen to the repartees of youth, doing so with an intensity that was not "languid [or] careless," but rather as if they were the instructors and captains of the youth. In the political sphere, Kleon expressed suspicion that politicians like Nikias and Lachês would be "over-indulgent and careless" in securing Athenian interests if they were selected as commissioners for peace and alliance with Sparta.

In a religious context, it is noted that "the careless Polytheist," when faced with unexpected terrors and lacking protection from philosophers or priests, was often subdued by the threat of eternal torture. This fear could lead such individuals to conclude that the Christian religion was the safest and most prudent party to embrace.
