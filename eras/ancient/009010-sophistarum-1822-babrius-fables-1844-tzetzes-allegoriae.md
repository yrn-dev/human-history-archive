# Sophistarum, Babrius, and Tzetzes

The study and preservation of later Greek literature, including the works of Babrius and Tzetzes, as well as the historical categorization of the *Sophistarum*, have been significantly advanced by the efforts of classical scholars and the discovery of ancient manuscripts.

## The Works of Jean François Boissonade
Jean François Boissonade (1774–1857) was a prominent French classical scholar who dedicated much of his career to the study of later Greek literature. After a period in public service and a tenure as secretary to the prefecture of the Upper Marne, Boissonade focused on Greek studies, eventually becoming a professor at the faculty of letters at Paris and later succeeding J.B. Gail in the chair of Greek at the College de France. He also served as the perpetual secretary of the Academie des Inscriptions and librarian of the Bibliotheque du Roi.

Boissonade produced several critical editions of Greek texts. In 1822, he published *Vitae Sophistarum* by Eunapius and *Epistolae* by Aristaenetus. His later contributions include the *Fables* of Babrius (1844) and Tzetzes' *Allegoriae Iliados* (1851). He also compiled a 24-volume *Collection of Greek Poets* and produced the *Anecdota Graeca* (1829–1833) and *Anecdota Nova* (1844), both of which are considered important for the study of Greek grammarians and Byzantine history.

## Babrius and the Greek Fables
Babrius was an author of a collection of fables written in Greek. While very little is known about his life, it is suspected that he was a Roman, possibly named Valerius, who lived in the East, likely in Syria. His work is written in choliambic verse—a "limping" or imperfect iambic metre characterized by a spondee as the last foot, which was originally used for satire. The style of these fables is described as elegant, correct, and terse.

The dating of Babrius' work has been a subject of speculation due to an address in the text to "a son of King Alexander." This has led to proposed dates ranging from the 3rd century B.C. to the 3rd century A.D. Some suggest the Alexander mentioned is Alexander Severus (A.D. 222–235), while others identify the "son" as a possibly fictitious person named Branchus, for whom Babrius may have been a tutor. Because Babrius does not appear in ancient writings before the 3rd century A.D., and based on his style, scholars generally place him in that period.

The recovery of Babrius' work involved significant scholarly effort. Richard Bentley first identified traces of versification in prose Aesopian fables, extracting verses he attributed to Babrius. Later, in 1842, a Greek named M. Minas discovered a manuscript of Babrius in the convent of St Laura on Mount Athos. This manuscript, now held in the British Museum, contains 123 fables arranged alphabetically (breaking off at the letter O) out of a presumed original 160. Babrius is also believed to have been the source of inspiration for the Latin fabulist Avianus.

## Tzetzes and the Chiliads
John Tzetzes was a monk and author of a 12th-century miscellaneous work known as the *Chiliads*. In this work, Tzetzes relates a romantic or moral tale regarding the blindness and beggary of Belisarius, written in ten political or vulgar verses. This account was later imported into Italy via Greek manuscripts and repeated by writers such as Pontanus, Volaterranus, and Crinitus before the end of the 15th century.

Despite recording this tale, Tzetzes also noted in other chronicles that Belisarius did not actually lose his sight and had recovered both his fortunes and his fame.

## The Sophistarum
The term *Sophistarum* relates to the professional wisdom of the Greeks. According to Lord Bacon in the *Novum Organon*, this type of wisdom was "professoria" and spread into disputations, a method he viewed as contrary to the inquiry of truth. The label *Sophistarum* was used by those who considered themselves philosophers to cast contempt upon ancient rhetors, including figures such as Polum, Hippiam, Protagoram, and Gorgiam. This categorization was extended to include a wide range of successors, including Epicuro, Zenoni, Aristoteli, Platoni, Theophrasto, Carneadi, and Chrysippo.
