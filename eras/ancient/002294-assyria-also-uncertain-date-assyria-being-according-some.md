# Assyria

Assyria was one of the two great empires that developed on the banks of the Tigris and Euphrates. Historically and geographically, it was closely intertwined with Babylonia, with the power of one often serving as a measure of the weakness of the other. While classical accounts frequently prioritize Assyria, deciphered inscriptions indicate that for much of its history—with the exception of some seven or eight centuries—Assyria may be described as a dependency or province of Babylonia. Babylonia served as the mother country, providing the religion, culture, literature, arts, and sciences from which the Assyrians derived their own. The two peoples shared similar language and race.

## Geography and Origins
The country of Assyria, known in Assyro-Babylonian literature as *mat Assur* ("land of Assur"), took its name from the ancient city of Assur. This city was located at the southern extremity of Assyria proper. The territory was initially bounded to the south by the lower Zab river and to the north by the Zagros mountain range in the region of modern Kurdistan. The kingdom grew from the primitive settlement at Assur through a gradual process of colonization within the rich vales of the middle Tigris region, an area watered by the Tigris and several tributaries, most notably the lower Zab.

## Social and Military Structure
Assyria differed essentially in character from Babylonia; while Babylonia was a land of agriculturists and merchants, Assyria was described as an organized camp. Its dynasties were founded by successful generals, and the Assyrian king functioned as an autocratic general. In the early days, a feudal nobility supported the king, but from the reign of Tiglath-pileser III, an elaborate bureaucracy was established. The royal palace was separate from the temples of the gods and was more sumptuous than those temples.

Socially, Assyria did not have castes. Its social hierarchy was dominated by a military aristocracy, contrasting with the priesthood that dominated Babylonia. While both nations utilized a large body of slaves, Assyria had relatively few commercial and agricultural classes. Scribes held a more important position in Assyria than they did in Babylonia.

The Assyrian army was raised in part by conscription, and Assyria is credited with organizing the first standing army. Under the kings of the second empire, the military underwent several improvements:
* Cavalry superseded the use of chariots.
* Tiglath-pileser III introduced high boots and saddles for riders.
* Sennacherib created a corps of slingers.
* The army utilized battering-rams, baggage-carts, and tents during marches.
* The *tartan*, or commander-in-chief, was the highest-ranking official after the king.

## Imperial Expansion and Conflict
Assyria exerted significant power over Syria, Phoenicia, and the Levant. In 842, King Shalmaneser attacked Syria after a coalition against Assyria revolted. During this period, Jehu, king of Israel, paid tribute to Assyria. Shalmaneser also conducted a hostile campaign against Phoenicia; according to the archives of Tyre, he besieged the city for five years, blocking its rivers and aqueducts, though the Tyrians survived by digging wells.

Tiglath-pileser III (referred to in the Bible as "Pul") is noted as the first Assyrian monarch to appear on the Hebrew horizon. In 738 B.C., Menahem bought him off with a thousand talents of silver. In 732, Tiglath-pileser III reduced Damascus after being appealed to for protection by Ahaz, king of Judah.

Assyrian aggression continued through the reigns of subsequent monarchs. In 721 B.C., the kingdom of Israel was swept into captivity. Sennacherib campaigned in 701 B.C. during the reign of King Hezekiah, though he was later murdered by his own sons. Following this, Esarhaddon conquered Egypt.

## Decline and Fall
The collapse of Assyria was precipitated by the draining of its fighting population during the age of Assur-bani-pal. Following his death, the empire broke up rapidly. Under his successor, Assur-etil-ilani, Scythians penetrated Assyria, burning Calah. Although the walls of Nineveh protected the remnants of the Assyrian army, the resources of the state were at a low ebb, as evidenced by the small size and architectural poverty of a new palace built among the ruins of a neighboring city.

The last king of Assyria was likely Sin-sar-iskun (possibly the Sarakos of Berossus), the brother of Assur-etil-ilani. He attempted to restore ruined temples and their priests, linking the god Assur of Nineveh with Merodach of Babylon. However, war broke out between Sin-sar-iskun and Nabopolassar of Babylonia. Aided by the Scythian king of Ecbatana, Cyaxares, the Babylonians captured and destroyed Nineveh and other cities in northern Babylonia that had sided with Assyria. This event marked the end of the Assyrian empire.

## Other Historical Notes
Assyria is noted for the early customary use of iron weapons, occurring two or three hundred years before such weapons became common in China. In various historical and religious texts, "Assyria" is sometimes interpreted differently; some suggest it refers to the ancient realm of Nineveh, while others suggest it refers to the Seleucid kingdom of Syria.
