# Maspero

Gaston Maspero was a scholar and author whose work focused extensively on the history, archaeology, and religion of ancient Egypt. His contributions span a wide range of publications, including detailed catalogues, geographical lists, and historical analyses of the Egyptian empire.

## Chronological Systems
Maspero is associated with a specific school of thought regarding the dating of early Egyptian history. His chronological system is grouped with those of Sayce, Rogers, Winckler, and Delitzsch. These systems are based on a shared principle: they first established a fixed date for the conclusion of Dynasty III and subsequently utilized the figures provided in the Kings' List without amendment to define earlier periods. In doing so, these scholars did not attempt to reconcile their findings with other conflicting data.

## Published Works and Research
Maspero authored numerous significant works detailing various sites and aspects of Egyptian civilization. His publications include:
*   *Le Sérapéum de Memphis* (beginning in 1857)
*   *Dendérah* (1873-1875), published in five folios and one 4to
*   *Abydos* (1870-1880), published in two folios and one 4to
*   *Karnak* (1875), published in folio and 4to
*   *Deir el-Bahari* (1877), published in folio and 4to
*   *Listes géographiques des pylônes de Karnak* (1875), published in folio
*   *Catalogue du Musée de Boulaq* (six editions published between 1864 and 1876)
*   *Aperçu de l'histoire d'Égypte* (four editions published between 1864 and 1874)
*   *Les Mastabas de l'ancien empire* (1883), which he edited

Additionally, Maspero wrote *Dawn of Civilization* and *New Light on Ancient Egypt*.

## Analysis of Amenophis IV
In *New Light on Ancient Egypt*, Maspero provides an account of the conflict between Amenophis IV and the priesthoods, specifically the priests of the great god Ammon Ra, the Lord of Karnak. Maspero suggests that the origins of this quarrel may lie in the priests' opposition to and annoyance of Tii, a beautiful Syrian subject and the mother of Amenophis IV, with whom Amenophis III had made a love match. Maspero posits that Tii may have inspired her son with a fanatical hatred of Ammon Ra.

This conflict contributed to the actions of Amenophis IV, who sought to end sectarian worship and close all Syrian and Egyptian temples to establish the worship of a single god, Aton, the solar disk. As part of this transition, the Pharaoh moved his capital from Thebes to Tel Amarna and changed his name from Amenophis (consecrating him to Ammon) to Akhnaton (the Sun's Glory).
