# Immersion and Aspersion in Christian Baptism

The administration of baptism in the early Christian church involved various methods of applying water, primarily categorized as immersion (submersion) or aspersion (pouring or sprinkling). The historical application of these methods evolved over several centuries, influenced by available facilities, liturgical rubrics, and the age of the candidates.

## Early Practices and the Didache
Early sources, including the New Testament, Justin, Tertullian, and the *Didache*, do not mandate the use of a font. Instead, these sources generally contemplate the use of "living" or running water. This practice aligned with a Jewish rule requiring that water used in ablutions run over and away from the parts of the body being washed. Consequently, records of martyrdom up until the age of Decius describe baptisms taking place in the sea, lakes, and rivers. In certain exceptional circumstances, a martyr's death was considered sufficient, or they might be sprinkled with their own blood.

The *Didache* specifically instructs to "pour water on the head." This method of pouring is reflected in Christian sculptures and paintings from the 1st through the 10th centuries, which depict the baptizer pouring water from a bowl or the hand over the head of a baptizand who is standing in the water.

## Evolution of Immersion
While some early literary notices mention submersion, these are often viewed as rhetorical rather than conclusive evidence of a required practice. Furthermore, the ruins of the earliest fonts indicate they were not deep or large enough to facilitate total submersion. Although the rubrics of certain manuscripts enjoin total immersion, this practice did not enter general vogue until the 7th century. This shift occurred as adult baptism became rarer, allowing the Greek word *baptizo* to be interpreted in a manner suited for infants.

The *Key of Truth*, a manual used by the old Armenian Baptists, provides an archaic prescription where the penitent enters the water on their knees to the middle, at which point a bishop or the "elect one" pours water over the head.

## Liturgical Developments and Traditions
Over time, the baptismal rite incorporated additional elements. Basil, in his work *On the Holy Spirit*, noted that several features—including trine immersion, the blessing of the baptizand, the blessing of the oil of anointing and baptismal water, the use of anointing oil, and the formal renunciation of Satan and his angels—were passed down through a silent, sacramental tradition and unpublished teaching.

Other early features of the rite included the use of fonts, the status of the baptizer, and the practice of exorcism. The *Didache* and Justin prescribed fasting for exorcism to encourage the exit of evil spirits, based on the belief that demons prefer a well-fed body over an emaciated one. While exorcisms grew longer over time, the archaic rite found in the *Key of Truth* does not include these elements.

## Denominational Perspectives
The term "Baptists" refers to a body of Christians who hold the distinctive view that baptism should be administered only to believers. The mode of administration varies among these groups:
* **Modern Baptists:** These groups distinguish themselves by the practice of immersion.
* **Mennonites:** Some Baptists, such as the Mennonites, continue to practice baptism via sprinkling or pouring.

Immersion is not exclusive to modern Baptists; it is recognized as a legitimate mode by Paedobaptists and is practiced exclusively by a large portion of paedobaptist Christendom, such as the Orthodox Eastern Church.

## Related Terminology
In the context of the Roman Catholic Church, the term "aspersion" refers to the ceremony of sprinkling the people with holy water before High Mass. This is derived from the Latin *aspergere* ("thou wilt sprinkle"). The ceremony utilizes a vessel called an *aspersorium* and a brush known as an *aspergillum* or aspersoir.
