# Biblical Studies and Related Historical Records

The study of biblical texts and their historical contexts involves the analysis of ancient scriptures, the identification of geographical landmarks, and the examination of parallel narratives in other ancient civilizations. These studies often draw upon commentaries, linguistic analysis, and archaeological evidence to interpret the events and objects described in the Bible.

## Biblical Literature and Commentaries
A wide array of scholarly works serves as an introduction to the biblical texts for English readers, including the commentaries of W. E. Barnes in the *Cambridge Bible* and J. Skinner in the *Century Bible*. Other significant academic contributions include the works of A. Kuenen, J. Wellhausen, H. Winckler, B. Stade, and S. R. Driver. For those studying the Hebrew text, sources such as Klostermann's *Sam. u. Könige* (1887), C. F. Burney's *Notes on the Hebrew Text* (1903), and the edition by Stade and Schwally in Haupt's *Sacred Books of the Old Testament* (1904) are utilized.

The development of the Bible's composition is also a subject of study. At one period, the Bible appears to have consisted primarily of the Pentateuch (the first five books of the Old Testament), while other books, such as Proverbs, the Psalms, and Chronicles, existed as separate entities before being incorporated into the present Hebrew Bible.

## Geographical and Cultural Identifications
Researchers seek to identify specific locations and objects mentioned in biblical narratives.

### Mount Hor
Mount Hor is identified in the Bible as the site of Aaron's death, located on the edge of the land of Edom. Since the time of Josephus, it has been identified as *Jebel Nebi Harun*, a twin-peaked mountain reaching 4,780 feet above sea level. However, some investigators, such as H. Clay Trumbull, suggest the Jebel Madara instead. A second Mount Hor is mentioned as being on the northern boundary of the prospective conquests of the Israelites; this is possibly Hermon, though some suggest it may be *Hadrach*, a northern country near Damascus.

### The Kinnor
The *kinnor* is the Hebrew name for an ancient stringed instrument, first mentioned in Genesis (iv. 21) and typically translated as "harp." Evidence from the history of musical instruments suggests the *kinnor* is the Greek *cithara*. This instrument was used by Semitic races before 2000 B.C. and appeared in Hittite sculptures and on Hebrew coins.

## Historical Narratives and Parallelisms
Biblical accounts often share similarities with other ancient records. The stories of the Flood, Adam and Eve, and the Creation of the World run closely parallel to Babylonian legends, suggesting these were common beliefs among Semitic peoples. Similarly, the stories of Samson and Moses have Babylonian and Sumerian parallels. 

In the case of the Israelites, the narrative becomes more specific starting with Abraham, a patriarchal Semitic nomad who may have lived during the days of Hammurabi in Babylon. According to the book of Genesis, Abraham traveled through Canaan, and the God of Abraham promised this land to him and his children.

## Military and Political Analysis
Scholars use biblical texts to analyze ancient conflicts. For example, discussions regarding a "Cushite" in the narrative may refer to an Arabian or an Ethiopian. W. E. Barnes notes in the *Cambridge Bible* that if Zerah is interpreted as a Sabaean or Ethiopian prince, certain narrative difficulties are removed, as no king Zerah of Ethiopia is known during that period. Furthermore, while the identification of such figures with Osorkon I or II is considered untenable due to the weakness of Asa, it is noted that desert hordes frequently troubled Judah and may have allied with Philistine towns, particularly if the battle occurred at Mareshah.
