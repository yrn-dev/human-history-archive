# Platonic Theory of Ideas

The Platonic theory of Ideas posits a fundamental distinction between the phenomenal world and a higher realm of substantive existences. Within this framework, Ideas are understood as substantive existences that exist apart from the phenomenal world.

## Conceptual Nature of Ideas
In the Platonic system, the "Idea" is contrasted with the *eidolon*. The *eidolon* represents a fleeting and transient image of the real thing, serving as a false appearance. This distinction is highlighted in the *Republic* (vii. 516 A), where the process of purification involves first clearing away shadows and subsequently removing the images (*eidola*) of humans and other things from the waters.

## Divine Mind and the Order of Nature
Later interpretations of Platonism, specifically those received by Bonaventura via St. Augustine, the Alexandrian school, and the author of mystical works attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite, further define the location and nature of these Ideas. According to this perspective, Ideas do not exist *in rerum natura* (in the nature of things). Instead, they exist as thoughts of the divine mind. These divine thoughts served as the blueprints or models according to which actual things were formed.

## Relationship to Sokratês and Platonism
The origin of these theories is often discussed in the context of Plato's dialogues featuring Sokratês. While some of Plato's works, such as the "Apology of Sokratês," "Kriton," and "Phædon," are viewed as moral pictures of the man Sokratês, other works are identified as the product of Plato's own speculative genius. The hypothesis of Ideas as substantive existences apart from the phenomenal world is categorized as belonging to Plato's own distinct opinions rather than those of Sokratês.

## Influence on Later Thought
The Platonic conception of Ideas and the soul has influenced various subsequent philosophical and religious movements:

*   **Gnosticism:** In the 2nd century, Carpocrates and his son Epiphanes led a school based mainly on Platonism. They utilized doctrines of pre-existence of souls and reminiscence. In their view, the world was formed by inferior spirits out of harmony with a supreme unity. Those who remember their pre-existing state can attain a contemplation of this unity.
*   **Scholasticism:** Bonaventura adopted the theory of Ideas as thoughts of the divine mind, which significantly influenced his philosophy. He contrasted this mystical and Platonizing mode of speculation with the Aristotelian scholasticism represented by Thomas Aquinas and Roger Bacon.
*   **Early Modern Philosophy:** Francis Bacon borrowed the term *Idola* from Plato. In the *Novum Organum*, Bacon uses the term in connection with Platonic Ideas, contrasting the two by treating *Idola* as false appearances.
