# Paternity and Children of Slaves

In various historical legal and social systems, the status of children born to slaves or through unions involving slaves varied significantly depending on the governing codes and the nature of the relationship between the parents.

## Legal Status and Legitimacy
Under certain legal codes, there was no hindrance to a man having children by a slave girl. In such instances, the children were considered free. While the mother could be pledged, she could not be sold if she had borne her husband children, and she attained her freedom upon the death of her master. These children could be adopted or legitimized through the father's acknowledgment before witnesses. Once legitimized or adopted, they ranked equally in the sharing of their father's estate. However, if they were not adopted, the children of the legal wife were granted first choice and divided the estate before them.

In other arrangements, free women were permitted to marry slaves and could be dowered for such marriages. The children resulting from these unions were free. Upon the death of the slave husband, the wife recovered her dowry and half of the assets acquired during the wedlock for herself and her children, while the slave's master claimed the remaining half as the heir.

## Social Origins and the Slave Population
The presence of slaves and their children in society often stemmed from warfare and economic hardship. In earlier periods of war, captive women and children were assimilated into the victorious tribe, while captive men were often tortured or sacrificed. Later, captives with exceptional arts or gifts were spared to serve as slaves.

The slave population grew not only through the birth of children to slaves but also through other means:
* **Debt and Insolvency:** Individuals could be enslaved to discharge debts, and insolvent debtors often faced slavery.
* **Family Sales:** Parents sometimes sold their children into slavery, and brothers might sell their orphan sisters.
* **Self-Sale:** Free men lacking a means of livelihood occasionally sold themselves into slavery.
* **Fixed-term Slavery:** Craft apprenticeship functioned as a form of fixed-term slavery.

## Domestic Arrangements and Concubinage
In societies where monogamy was the rule, a childless wife might provide her husband with a maid—who was not a wife—to bear children. These children were reckoned as the wife's children. The wife remained the mistress of the maid and could degrade her back to slavery for insolence, though she was prohibited from selling the maid if children had been born to the husband.

If a wife provided a maid for this purpose, the husband was not allowed to take a concubine. If she refused, he could. A concubine was a free woman and a wife, though of a lower rank than the first wife. Concubines were often dowered, their children were legitimate, and they could only be divorced under the same conditions as a primary wife. In all these domestic scenarios, the children were considered legal heirs and legitimate.

## Conditions of Slave Life and Family Stability
The experience of slave families varied by era and region. In later Babylon, elaborate laws protected the lives and property of slaves. However, in the Roman Empire during the age of the Antonines, conditions for slaves were precarious. A lack of security—specifically the fear that husbands and wives would be separated—contributed to a steady depopulation of the empire, as people, including slaves, refused to have children due to a lack of pride or reasonable hope for them.
