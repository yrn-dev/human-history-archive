# Eccentricity and Insanity

In historical and medical contexts, the terms eccentricity and insanity often overlap, appearing in descriptions of political leaders, artists, medical conditions, and individual personalities. While eccentricity may refer to peculiar habits or traits, it has frequently been associated with mental instability or specific pathological states.

## Historical and Political Figures
The Roman Empire provides several examples of the intersection between leadership and mental instability. Caligula, who ruled from 37 to 41 A.D., is described as having been insane, though the empire continued to function during his four years of eccentricity. His reign ended when he was murdered in his palace by his servants. In contrast, his successor Claudius (41 to 54 A.D.) was considered personally uncouth but was a hardworking and capable administrator who expanded the empire's westward boundary by annexing the southern half of Britain. Prior to Caligula, Tiberius (14 to 37 A.D.) was viewed as a capable ruler, though he was intensely unpopular in Rome due to personal tyrannies, cruelties, and an addiction to "gross and abominable vices."

## Art and Performance
Eccentricity has also been a defining characteristic of figures in the arts, sometimes leading to accusations of insanity. Max Klinger, a German painter, sculptor, and etcher born in 1857, created a sensation at the 1878 Academy exhibition in Berlin with his "Fantasies upon the Finding of a Glove" and "Series upon the Theme of Christ." The daring originality and eccentric nature of these works led to an outburst of indignation, and Klinger was voted insane. Despite this, the Berlin National Gallery purchased the "Glove" series. Klinger's work was characterized by a vivid, sometimes morbid imagination and a "Goyaesque" turn of mind.

In the theater, Junius Brutus Booth (1796-1852), a London-born actor who later moved to America in 1821, was highly popular for his roles as Hamlet, Richard III, and King Lear. However, his eccentricities were noted to border on insanity. This was particularly evident in his "excited and furious fencing" while performing as Hamlet and Richard III, which often forced the actors playing Laertes and Richmond to fight in "deadly earnest" for their lives.

## Medical and Psychological Perspectives
The relationship between eccentricity and mental health is explored in the study of epilepsy. While some individuals with epilepsy maintain normal mental integrity, many experience a general mental condition that is unfavourably affected. This often manifests as peculiarities such as diminishing intelligence, defective memory, or irregularities of temper, where the patient becomes perverse, irritable, or eccentric.

Furthermore, epilepsy can lead to "maniacal excitement," known as *furor epilepticus*. These attacks involve violent acts that make the patient dangerous. In some cases, individuals may be seized by a sudden impulse to use dangerous violence without malevolent intention, often retaining no memory of the event. Such states of mind have been linked to the commission of heinous crimes, raising significant medico-legal questions regarding criminal responsibility.

## Definitions and General Observations
The term "idiosyncrasy" is derived from the Greek *idiosynkrasia* (a peculiar habit of body or temperament). It refers to a physical or mental condition peculiar to an individual, often taking the form of a special susceptibility to stimuli—such as abnormal discomfort caused by certain colors, odours, or the presence of a cat. The term is also used generally to describe any peculiarity of appearance or eccentricity of character.

Other instances of eccentricity appear in social and cultural observations. Marco Polo, during his missions for Kúblái Khan, recorded "eccentric traits of manners" and curious facts from regions such as Yun-nan to delight the Emperor, who expressed disgust for commissioners who spoke only of official business. In military contexts, General von Pelkmann, the Deputy-Governor of Metz, was described as a "very eccentric" little man with decided views on the universe and the creation.

Finally, in the study of suicide, Professor Morselli suggests that when self-immolation is accomplished through prolonged agony or painful means, it is typically the act of a mind disordered by madness, morbid excitement, or fanaticism. However, the practice of *seppuku* is distinguished from this, as it requires utmost *sang froid* and is categorized by Dr. Strahan as "Rational or Quasi" suicide rather than "Irrational or True" suicide.
