# Festus Succeeds Felix Who Is Succeeded By Albinus

The administrative transition of the procuratorship of Judea during the reign of Nero involved a succession of officials, including Felix, Porcius Festus, and Albinus, whose varying styles of governance significantly impacted the stability of the region.

## The Transition from Felix to Festus
Following the tenure of Felix, Porcius Festus was sent by Nero to serve as the successor procurator. The transition was marked by legal grievances; the principal Jewish inhabitants of Caesarea traveled to Rome to accuse Felix of misgovernment. While these accusations could have led to his punishment, Felix was acquitted due to the influence of his brother, Pallas, who was held in high honor by Nero. Pallas had previously served as a minister and favorite under Claudius.

Chronological records regarding the exact date of this replacement vary among critics, with some suggesting dates as early as A.D. 55 and others as late as A.D. 60 or 61. Analysis of the influence of Pallas and the later influence of Poppaea suggests the recall of Felix and the arrival of Festus likely occurred between the years 58 and 59.

## The Administration of Porcius Festus
Upon taking office, Festus focused his efforts on maintaining order and correcting those who caused disturbances within the country. His administration was characterized by a crackdown on criminal elements; he successfully captured and destroyed a great many of the robbers operating in the region.

However, during the period when Felix and Festus served as procurators, the region also saw the rise of various impostors, murderers, and robbers. Additionally, during Festus's time, a conflict arose in Caesarea. Two principal Syrians persuaded Burrhus—Nero's secretary for Greek epistles and tutor—by providing a large sum of money to disannul the equality of privileges previously enjoyed by Jewish citizens. This led to an imperial epistle that sparked disorder and eventually kindled a war among the Jews of Caesarea.

## The Procuratorship of Albinus
Following the death of Festus, Caesar sent Albinus to Judea as the new procurator. In contrast to his predecessor, Albinus did not execute his office with the same diligence. He is described as having a hand in every named form of wickedness. In his political capacity, Albinus engaged in the plunder and theft of the substance of others and burdened the nation with taxes.

Albinus further corrupted the legal system by allowing the relations of prisoners—those incarcerated for robbery by former procurators or city senates—to redeem them for money. Consequently, only those who could not pay remained in prison. This environment empowered seditious elements in Jerusalem; principal seditious leaders purchased permission from Albinus to continue their practices. These individuals, acting as tyrants or arch-robbers, led bands of robbers to plunder those living quietly. This atmosphere of tyranny forced victims to remain silent and compelled others to flatter Albinus out of fear. These conditions are noted as the seeds that eventually brought the city to destruction.

## Religious and Legal Conflicts under Albinus
The beginning of Albinus's tenure coincided with a period of internal religious and legal volatility. Upon the death of Festus and while Albinus was still traveling to Judea, the king deprived Joseph of the high priesthood, granting the position to the son of Ananus, who was also named Ananus. This younger Ananus was a member of the Sadducees, a sect known for being very rigid in judging offenders.

Taking advantage of the power vacuum between Festus's death and Albinus's arrival, Ananus assembled a sanhedrim of judges. He brought before them James, the brother of Jesus (who was called Christ), and several companions, accusing them of breaking the law and delivering them to be stoned. This action was disliked by the more equitable citizens, who petitioned King Agrippa to intervene. Some citizens met Albinus during his journey from Alexandria to inform him that Ananus had illegally assembled the sanhedrim without the procurator's consent. Albinus complied with these concerns and wrote to Ananus in anger, threatening punishment. As a result, King Agrippa removed Ananus from the high priesthood after only three months and appointed Jesus, the son of Damneus, in his place.
