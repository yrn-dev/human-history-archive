# The Relationship Between God, Mediator, and Man

## The Triadic Formula of Bonald
In the writings of Bonald, a leading figure of the traditionalist or theocratic school, social and political philosophy is rooted in the principle of the divine origin of language. Bonald posits that the first language contained the essence of all truth, a premise from which he deduces the infallibility of the church, the supreme authority of the Holy Scriptures, and the existence of God.

Central to Bonald's speculations is a formula of constant application based on a triad consisting of cause, means, and effect. He observes this pattern repeated across different spheres of existence:
*   **The Universe:** The first cause is the mover, movement serves as the means, and bodies are the result.
*   **The State:** Power acts as the cause, ministers as the means, and subjects as the effects.
*   **The Family:** This relation is exemplified by the father, mother, and children.

Within this framework, the first term relates to the second as the second relates to the third. Bonald applies this specific logic to the religious world through a triad consisting of God, the Mediator, and Man. In this arrangement, God is to the Mediator (the God-Man) as the God-Man is to Man. Using this basis, Bonald constructed a system of political absolutism.

## Theological Perspectives on the Divine and Human
Different historical and philosophical systems have proposed varying interpretations of the relationship between the divine and humanity.

### Christian and Gnostic Interpretations
In the early Christian era, Jesus referred to himself as both the Son of Man and the Son of God. Later, followers such as Paul emphasized his divine nature, leading to extensive arguments regarding whether Jesus was identical to or separate from God. By the fourth century, these debates over the nature of God often overshadowed the original teachings of brotherhood, service, and charity.

Marcion presented a dualistic view influenced by Gnosticism. He taught that a "good God" and His crucified Son offer salvation to those who renounce the Demiurge and perform good works of love. In Marcion's system, only the spirit of man is saved, while the material body perishes. He distinguished between the "good God" of spirit and the Old Testament God, whom he viewed as the god of matter. Marcion asserted that the good God does not judge but manifests goodness; those who do not believe the gospel are simply removed from His presence and fall under the power of the Demiurge, who punishes them in hell.

### Greek Mythology and Philosophy
Greek myths often juxtapose Gods, Heroes, and Men. In some narratives, such as those attributed to Hesiod, the calamitous state of humanity is linked to the malevolence of Zeus. While Prometheus, a Titan god, acted as the protector of the human race, his attempts to cheat Zeus regarding sacrificial victims provoked Zeus's wrath. This resulted in the theft of fire and a subsequent snare laid for Epimêtheus, which wrecked human happiness.

Conversely, Epicurean philosophy sought to establish man's independence from the gods and fatality. Epicurus rejected the Stoic doctrine of Fatalism, arguing that man could withdraw from external influences and "live like a god among men" as a sage.

## Philosophical and Social Conceptions of Man and God
Various thinkers have explored the intersection of human consciousness and divine force. Descartes' metaphysical theology relegated all forces in their ultimate origin to God, who communicates and sustains motion in the universe. This allowed Descartes to apply mathematical laws and mechanics to explain physical phenomena, while maintaining that humans are thinking beings.

In other philosophical traditions, the aim of the philosopher is to transcend the natural instincts of self-interest and self-preservation to embrace the whole world. By growing into the "mind of God" and aligning the human will with the will of nature, a sage is described as "leaving manhood for godhead."

From a social history perspective, the relationship between man and the divine often manifested in land ownership and labor. Over time, the common man found that the land he cultivated belonged to a god, a king, or an official, requiring him to pay fractions of his produce or provide labor to his masters.
