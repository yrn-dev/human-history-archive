# BARTER

Barter, derived from the French *barater* meaning to truck or exchange, is the system of exchanging commodities for other commodities. This method of trade stands in contra-distinction to the exchange of commodities for money.

## General Characteristics and Evolution
Barter represented the simplest form of trading among primitive communities. Due to the inconveniences associated with this system, early civilizations eventually adopted metals to serve as mediums of exchange. Despite the evolution of currency, barter remains common in dealings with uncivilized peoples. In many countries, traders find that the most effective way to conduct exchanges is to provide commodities such as ornaments, tools, and weapons, as these items are more readily accepted than money.

## Historical Applications in Ancient Civilizations
In the earliest ages of recorded history, individuals wishing to dispose of their goods were required to use barter. In ancient cities such as Babylon and Thebes, most trade was conducted through this method. In Babylon, while gold and silver ingots were used for exchange and bankers stamped their names and weights on these lumps of metal, barter remained prevalent. Furthermore, most workers and servants in these societies were slaves who were paid in kind rather than with money; the decline of slavery coincided with the rise of money.

In ancient China, barter was the primary means of disposal for goods in the earliest ages. This was eventually succeeded by a variety of mediums of exchange, including pieces of deerskin, linen, stamped silk, and shells. These were later replaced by copper discs with round holes, which preceded the copper coins with square holes and inscriptions used in later centuries. Other early forms of coinage in China were cast in the shapes of "trousers" and "knives," some of which featured gold inlays.

## Regional and Cultural Examples
Barter continues to be observed in various global contexts and specific populations:

*   **Central Asia:** In the provinces of the Oxus and Badakhshan, market days are universal. On these days, people from the surrounding countryside assemble in bazaars to exchange goods, generally through barter. This has been noted specifically at Talikan.
*   **The Kara-Kirghiz:** This nomadic group, consisting of over 800,000 people divided into the "Right" (On) and "Left" (Sol) sections, conducts trade primarily by barter. Dealers from Russia, Turkestan, and China provide manufactured goods in exchange for cattle.
*   **International Trade Hubs:** In the past, Mecca served as the world's greatest mart for exchanging goods from the West for those of the East. Large quantities of precious stones, perfumes, silks, and spices were bartered there, having been transported by caravan from the north-eastern regions of farther Asia.

## Legal and Linguistic Distinctions
The term *barater* (or *bareter*) also appears in legal contexts. In English criminal law, "common barratry" is the offense of constantly stirring up quarrels and inciting disturbances of the peace in courts or elsewhere. This is a cumulative offense requiring proof of at least three commissions of the act and is punishable by imprisonment and fine. Under a 1726 statute, legal professionals found guilty of common barratry were disabled from practicing law.

In the context of marine insurance, barratry refers to fraud committed by a ship's master against the insurers or owner for personal benefit. Continental jurists apply a broader definition, viewing barratry as any willful act by the crew or master that damages the charterers or owners, regardless of the motive.
