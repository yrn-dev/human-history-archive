# Uncial Manuscripts

In the study of ancient texts, uncial (or majuscule) refers to a specific style of writing. This style is distinguished from cursive or minuscule writing and serves as one of the primary methods for classifying manuscripts, alongside the material used (such as papyrus, vellum, parchment, or paper) and the contents of the document.

## Characteristics and Dating
The identification of a manuscript's date often relies on the specific forms of its letters. For example, a manuscript featuring a "fine uncial" may exhibit traits of a distinctly Ptolemaic type. Such documents may be distinguished from earlier Ptolemaic manuscripts of the 2nd or 3rd century B.C. by specific letter forms. One such indicator is the Upsilon, which may feature a shallow curve on the top of the upright—a form associated with manuscripts from the 1st century B.C. and distinct from the more fully formed Upsilon used during the Roman period. 

Another significant indicator is the Xi; a specific form of this letter began to disappear around 50 B.C., replaced by a version where the middle stroke connects with the other two. Based on these palaeographical indications, certain manuscripts can be dated as not later than the middle of the 1st century B.C.

In the context of New Testament manuscripts, a general rule suggests that those written in uncial style date from before the 9th century, while those from the 10th century onward are written in minuscule.

## New Testament Uncials
Greek uncials dating back to the 4th century were previously considered the most important source of evidence for textual criticism. However, some scholars now argue that versions in other languages and quotations by early writers are more important, as they suggest the existence of text types from the 2nd and 3rd centuries that differ from the extant Greek manuscripts.

There are said to be 129 uncial manuscripts of the New Testament, though achieving an accurate count is difficult. Notable examples include:

*   **Codex Vaticanus (Greg. B):** A 4th-century uncial manuscript written in three columns with forty-two lines per column. It originally contained the entire Bible, though several sections of the New Testament are now missing. It was written by three scribes and corrected by two others—one contemporary with the writer and another from the 10th or 11th century.
*   **Codex Ephraemi Syri Rescriptus (Greg. C):** A 5th-century uncial palimpsest. Originally containing the whole Bible, it now retains 145 leaves of the New Testament and 64 leaves of the Old Testament.

## Classification and Notation
The notation of these manuscripts is complex, with two primary systems in use:

1.  **The Traditional System:** Used since the time of Wetstein, this system employs capital letters from the Latin, Greek, and Hebrew alphabets to designate uncials, while Arabic figures are used for minuscules. This system is represented by scholars such as Gregory and Scrivener.
2.  **The von Soden System:** Introduced in 1902, this system ignores the distinction between uncial and minuscule, prioritizing date and content. Manuscripts are divided into three classes: New Testaments ($\delta$), Gospels ($\epsilon$), and Acts, Epistles, and Apocalypse ($\alpha$). Numbers are attached to these letters to indicate the century of origin and the specific contents.

## Statistical Distribution
Based on analysis by von Soden, the distribution of New Testament manuscripts varies by century and content. For instance, the number of Gospel manuscripts increases significantly over time, from 3 in the 4th century to 282 in the 12th century. In contrast, New Testament manuscripts as a whole show a peak in the 14th century with 47 recorded copies.
