# Early Human and Regional History

The reconstruction of the earliest periods of existence and human civilization relies on a variety of sources, ranging from the geological record to ancient literary monuments and archaeological remains.

## The Geological Record
Knowledge of life preceding human memory and tradition is derived from the "Record of the Rocks." This involves the examination of fossils and markings—including bones, shells, stems, fruits, and footmarks—preserved in sandstone, limestone, slate, and shale. These remains exist alongside evidence of the earliest rain-falls and tides. Because sedimentary rocks have been distorted, crumpled, and mixed, significant effort has been required to order this record. The total span of time represented by these rocks is estimated at 1,600,000,000 years.

## Early Mesopotamian Civilizations
In its earliest known period, Babylonia consisted of several independent states separated by boundary stones and canals. Its culture emerged from two primary centers: Nippur in the north and Eridu in the south. 

Nippur grew around the sanctuary of El-lil, the lord of the ghost-land, who provided mankind with incantations and spells to control spirits. In contrast, Eridu was the home of Ea, the god of light and beneficence. Ea is credited with the invention of writing, the creation of the first law-book, and the provision of sciences, industries, and arts to humanity. Eridu's status as a seaport facilitated foreign trade, which influenced its culture and its cosmology, which viewed the earth as having grown from the waters of the deep. Eventually, these two cultures coalesced, with the influence of Eridu predominating.

Babylonia is also noted for the early development of brickmaking. Well-burnt bricks were produced more than 6,000 years ago and were used extensively by the time of Sargon of Akkad (c. 3800 B.C.). The Babylonians and Assyrians became proficient in creating glazed or enameled bricks for wall decoration.

## Early China
The origins of Chinese civilization may have been a "brunette civilization" linked to the earliest Sumerian, Dravidian, and Egyptian civilizations, rather than being Mongolian. By 1750 B.C., China was organized as a system of city-states and small kingdoms that paid feudal dues to a priest emperor known as the "Son of Heaven." 

The Shang dynasty ended in 1125 B.C., followed by the Chow dynasty. While the Chow initially maintained unity, China gradually fragmented. Local rulers stopped paying tribute, and Hunnish peoples established principalities. By the sixth century B.C., a Chinese authority records the existence of five or six thousand independent states, a period termed the "Age of Confusion." 

Regarding industry, while the Chinese claim great antiquity for clay industries, it is probable that brickmaking and the art of glazed bricks traveled east from Babylonia through northern India and Persia. The Great Wall of China, constructed partly of burnt and unburnt brick, was built at a later date (c. 210 B.C.).

## Early Greece and the Homeric Age
The earliest phase of Grecian life is primarily known through the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*. These poems are monuments to an age that was poetical and religious but lacked a recording or philosophical nature. Because the period was "unrecording," there is no authentic transmitted knowledge, making it difficult to distinguish reality from fancy.

The historical age of Greece is generally marked as beginning in 776 B.C. This history is divided into six compartments, the first of which spans from 776 B.C. to 560 B.C., ending with the accession of Crœsus in Lydia and Peisistratus in Athens.

## Other Regional Developments
*   **India:** The regular Indian drama is generally dated no earlier than the 5th century A.D., though the play *Mrichchhakatika* (The Toy Cart) is believed to predate the works of Kalidasa.
*   **Bohemia:** The earliest genuine documents of the Bohemian language consist of legends (such as those of St. Dorothy and St. Catherine) and hymns. The rhymed chronicle of Dalimil, likely written during the reign of King John (1310-1346), records history from the earliest period to the reign of King Henry of Carinthia.
*   **Northern Tribes:** For the earliest history of northern nations, the use of fur pelts for tents, couches, and clothing was a necessity for survival against arctic winters. This practice eventually evolved into a system of barter and traffic with more temperate regions.
*   **Assyria:** Knowledge of early Assyrian history relies on cuneiform inscriptions. However, historians caution that monuments of art—such as sculptures of kings capturing cities—cannot be used as positive historical proof of conquest without corroborating evidence.
