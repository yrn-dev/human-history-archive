# The Investigation into the Death of Pheroras

## The Relationship Between Herod and Pheroras
Pheroras was the brother of King Herod and a partner in the affairs of the kingdom, though he did not hold the diadem. He served as a tetrarch of the land beyond Jordan, a position Herod had requested from Caesar on his behalf. Pheroras possessed a personal revenue of one hundred talents. 

The relationship between the brothers was marked by periods of both affection and severe conflict. Herod had previously attempted to arrange royal marriages for Pheroras, first with his own wife's sister and later with his eldest daughter, providing a dowry of three hundred talents for the latter. Pheroras refused to consummate the marriage with Herod's daughter due to his passion for a maidservant, which initially provoked Herod's anger. Although Herod eventually pardoned him for this, further enmity arose regarding Pheroras's wife. Herod accused the woman of offering affronts to his daughters, providing money to the Pharisees as rewards for actions against the king, and using love potions to turn Pheroras against him.

When Herod forced Pheroras to choose between his brother and his wife, Pheroras stated he would rather die than forsake her. Consequently, Herod cast both Pheroras and his wife out of the kingdom. Pheroras retreated to his tetrarchy in Perea, swearing he would not return until Herod was dead. Despite this, when Herod later fell ill, he sent for Pheroras, though the brother did not return. Later, Pheroras himself became sick; Herod visited and cared for him, but Pheroras died shortly thereafter.

## The Discovery of Poisoning
Following a pompous funeral in Jerusalem, two of Pheroras's highly esteemed freedmen approached Herod. They urged the king to avenge the "unreasonable and unhappy death" of his brother, claiming that Pheroras had been destroyed by poison. They reported that on the day before he fell sick, Pheroras had supped with his wife and consumed a potion brought in a type of food he did not usually eat.

The freedmen alleged that the potion had been brought from Arabia by a woman skilled in mixing drugs. While it was presented as a love potion, it was actually a deadly poison. This woman was said to be an intimate friend of a mistress of Sylleus, the procurator of Obodas, king of Arabia. The freedmen claimed that the mother and sister of Pheroras's wife had visited this woman and persuaded her to sell the potion, bringing it back just before the fatal supper.

## The Investigation and Torture
Provoked by these reports, Herod ordered the torture of the women slaves and some free women. Initially, none confessed. However, under extreme agony, one woman prayed that God would send similar agonies upon Antipater's mother, identifying her as the cause of their miseries.

This revelation led Herod to increase the tortures, which eventually uncovered a series of secret assemblies and "merry meetings." Through these inquiries, it was discovered that the poison had not been intended for Pheroras, but had been prepared by Antipater, the son of Herod, for his own father.

## The Designs of Antipater
The investigation revealed the deep-seated hatred Antipater bore for Herod. Through the testimony of tortured maid-servants, it was disclosed that Antipater and Pheroras had previously agreed to distance themselves from the king—Antipater by going to Rome and Pheroras to Perea. They viewed Herod as a "wild beast" given to murder, fearing that after he had slain Alexander and Aristobulus, he would eventually turn on them and their wives.

Antipater had lamented to his mother that he was aging while his father seemed to grow younger, fearing death would overtake him before he could truly be king. He believed that even if he succeeded Herod, the reign would be short because the sons of Alexander and Aristobulus were growing up. Furthermore, Antipater discovered that Herod's testament did not name his own children as successors, but instead named Herod, the son of Mariamne.

The inquiry also revealed that Herod had previously given Antipater a hundred talents specifically to ensure he had no intercourse with Pheroras. Despite this public injunction, Antipater had secretly attended night meetings with Pheroras and his wife.

## Consequences and Legal Actions
As a result of the discovery, Herod cast Doris, Mariamne, and their accomplices out of the palace. He also blotted his son Herod (the son of Mariamne) out of his testament. 

Antipater, who had already navigated from Rome to his father, was accused by Nicolaus of Damascus. He was subsequently condemned to die by both his father, Herod, and Quintilius Varus, the President of Syria. Antipater remained bound while the matter was reported to Caesar.
