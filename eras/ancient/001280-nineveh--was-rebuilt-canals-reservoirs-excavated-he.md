# Nineveh

Nineveh was an ancient city of the Assyrians. Its history is characterized by periods of construction, destruction, and modern archaeological rediscovery.

## Early History and Development
Early records indicate that a town named Nin—which likely provided the name for the later Nineveh—was rebuilt during the campaigns of E-anna-du. During this period of reconstruction, reservoirs and canals were excavated.

## The City and its Surroundings
Nineveh was a major center of Assyrian power. Some historical perspectives, such as those cited by Diodorus from Ktêsias, suggest the city occupied a prodigious space of 480 stadia, or nearly 60 English miles. While the exact boundaries are a subject of scholarly discussion, A.H. Layard hypothesized that the circuit of ancient Nineveh included the sites of Khorsabad, Kouyunjik, and Nimrud.

The city of Calah (known as *Kalah* in Assyrian inscriptions) was located 19 miles south of Nineveh at the angle formed by the upper Zab and the Tigris. Built by Shalmaneser I around 1300 B.C. as a residence city to replace the older Assur, Calah was later restored by Assur-nasir-pal around 880 B.C. Calah remained a residence for Assyrian kings until the close of the 7th century, when it was captured and destroyed by the Babylonians and Medes, sharing the same fate as Nineveh.

## Notable Events and Legends
Ancient reports describe the city's wealth and the lengths to which individuals went to acquire it. According to accounts reported by Herodotus, thieves once plotted to steal the great wealth of King Sardanapallos, son of Ninos. This wealth was stored in subterranean treasure-houses. To reach the king's palace, the thieves dug underground from their own dwellings. To conceal their activities, they carried the excavated earth to the Tigris river under the cover of night.

## Modern Archaeological Discovery
The modern understanding of Nineveh was greatly expanded through the excavations of A.H. Layard and P.E. Botta. These efforts coincided with the decipherment of cuneiform writing, allowing scholars to reconstruct the history of Babylonia and Assyria. A pivotal discovery by Layard was the library of Assur-bani-pal.

Further exploration was conducted between 1877 and 1879 by Hormuzd Rassam on behalf of the British Museum. Rassam's work in the neighborhood of Nineveh included excavations at the mounds of Balawat (called Imgur-Bel by the Assyrians), located 15 miles east of Mosul. These excavations revealed:
* A small temple dedicated to the god of dreams by Assur-nazir-pal III (883 B.C.), which contained a stone coffer or ark holding two rectangular alabaster tables with inscriptions.
* A palace that had been destroyed by the Babylonians and subsequently restored by Shalmaneser II (858 B.C.). From this site, bronze gates featuring hammered reliefs were recovered.
