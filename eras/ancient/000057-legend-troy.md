# Legend of Troy

The legend of Troy represents the culminating point of the Grecian epic, centered on two sieges and the eventual capture and destruction of the city of Troy. It is regarded as the greatest event in the ancient epic, surpassed only by the sieges of Thebes in the context of war between Greeks during the era of the Heroes.

## Epic Sources and Literary Tradition
The narrative of Troy was preserved through a variety of epic poems. While the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* are the most prominent, the archive identifies five other lost epics specifically concerning the legend of Troy: the *Cyprian Verses*, the *Æthiopis*, and the *Capture of Troy* (both attributed to Arktinus), the *lesser Iliad* (attributed to Leschês), and the *Returns* (attributed to Hagias of Trœzên). Additionally, Eugammôn wrote the *Telegonia* as a continuation of the *Odyssey*.

## Origins and the Outbreak of War
The foundations of the city are linked to Dardanus, a son of Zeus, and Ilus, the founder of Ilium. The walls of Ilium were built by Poseidôn, and the city was once captured by Hêraklês. The conflict leading to the city's destruction began with Paris, a descendant of Priam, who rendered a judgment on three goddesses and subsequently carried off Helen from Sparta. Helen is described as the offspring of Zeus and the daughter of Tyndareus, king of Sparta.

In response to the abduction, heroes from across Greece combined under the leadership of Agamemnôn to recover her. The Grecian host, which included figures such as Achilles and Odysseus, initially mistook Teuthrania for Troy. Their progress was further delayed at Aulis, involving Agamemnôn and Iphigeneia.

## The Siege and Fall of the City
The war is characterized by significant conflicts and losses. The *Iliad* indicates that the destruction of Troy was brought about because the Trojans stole Greek women. Key events of the siege include the killing of Hectôr by Achilles and the arrival of Trojan allies, including Memnôn—who was killed by Achilles—and Penthesileia. Following the death of Achilles, funeral games were held in his honor, leading to a quarrel over his panoply that resulted in the suicide of Ajax after Odysseus prevailed.

The city remained impregnable as long as it held the Palladium, a statue given by Zeus to Dardanus. Odysseus eventually penetrated the city in disguise and stole the statue. Helen, who recognized him, assisted Odysseus in planning the city's capture. The city finally fell through the stratagem of the wooden horse, leading to the destruction of Troy and the distribution of captives among the victors.

## Divergent Narratives and Variations
The archive notes significant discrepancies in the legend. While the Homeric version focuses on the abduction of Helen, Herodotus adopted an Egyptian version. This variation, originating with Stesichorus, suggests that Helen never left Sparta; instead, her *eidôlon* was taken to Troy. Another version claims Paris carried Helen to Egypt, where she remained under the detention of King Prôteus until Menelaus reclaimed her. Herodotus argued that the Trojans' inability to surrender Helen or prove her absence was a divine judgment for their misdeeds.

Other peculiar accounts exist, such as a story from Istrus claiming Paris was conquered by Achilles and Patroklus in Thessaly, and that Hectôr stormed Troezen to carry off Aithra.

## Aftermath and the Destinies of Heroes
Following the victory, Helen was restored to Menelaus and lived in dignity at Sparta before passing to a happy immortality. Menelaus himself faced hardships and storms in Phœnicia and Egypt before returning to Sparta. He eventually passed from life to the Elysian fields to dwell with the golden-haired Rhadamanthus.

Other heroes faced varied fates. Odysseus experienced final adventures and death, while Æneas and his descendants are the subject of various stories, with the Æneadæ appearing at Skêpsis. The archive notes that the Trojan war is essentially legendary and served as a vital item in Grecian national faith.

## Historical and Archaeological Perspectives
The historical reality of Ilium has been a subject of debate. While generally visited as the town of Priam and respected by Alexander and the Romans, its mythical legitimacy was questioned by Dêmêtrius of Skêpsis and Hestiæa. Strabo is noted as the only source believing in an "Old Ilium" as the real Troy, distinct from "New Ilium."
