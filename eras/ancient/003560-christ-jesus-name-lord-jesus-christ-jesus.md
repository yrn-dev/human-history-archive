# Jesus Christ

## Life and Teachings
Jesus was born in Judea during the reign of Augustus Cæsar, the first Roman Emperor, and appeared as a prophet in Judea during the reign of Tiberius Cæsar. He was approximately thirty years of age when he began preaching in the fashion of preceding Jewish prophets. His personal teachings emphasized the universal Fatherhood of God and the implicit brotherhood of all men, asserting the sacredness of every human personality as a living temple of God.

The spirit of these teachings was opposed to the subjugation of man by man and to outrages upon human dignity, such as the gladiatorial combats of the arena. Jesus also challenged the intense tribal patriotism and restrictive family loyalties of the Israelites. He taught that the kingdom of Heaven would be the family of his followers; when his mother and brothers sought to speak with him, he indicated that whoever does the will of his Father in heaven is his brother, sister, and mother.

## Role in Early Christian Doctrine
While the four gospels provide the personality and teachings of Jesus, the broad lines of Christian belief were laid down in the epistles written by his immediate followers. St. Paul, a former persecutor of the disciples who was converted after the crucifixion, was a primary architect of Christian doctrine. Although Paul never saw or heard Jesus preach, he taught that Jesus was the promised leader of the Israelites and the promised Christ. Paul further developed the belief that the death of Jesus served as a sacrifice for the redemption of mankind, similar to sacrificial victims of primordial civilizations.

Following the death of Christ, the first generation of Christians lived in an "eschatological" state, expecting the Coming of the Messiah in power and glory. Because they believed this event was near, they generally did not produce written records for two decades, as they expected to be caught up to meet the Lord in the air.

## Baptism and the Name of Jesus
The practice of baptism evolved from the baptism of John. While John baptized in the name of the coming Messiah without identifying him as Jesus of Nazareth, the apostolic age established this identification. The standard formulas used during this period included "into Christ Jesus," "of Jesus Christ," "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ," or "of the Lord Jesus Christ." Paul explained these formulas as being equivalent to "into the death of Christ Jesus," symbolizing a believer being raised from death into everlasting life and ensuring a real union with him.

There is scholarly debate regarding whether Jesus himself instituted baptism. While the fourth gospel suggests he baptized on a scale greater than John, it also notes that only his disciples baptized. However, texts in Mark and Matthew attribute the injunction to baptize to Jesus. Some critics questioned these utterances, but citations from Eusebius Pamphili and Aphraates suggest a consistent usage of "in My Name" or "believe in Me."

## Theological Interpretations of Rebirth
Certain traditions and writers viewed the baptism of Jesus in the Jordan as a spiritual rebirth. Based on a reading of Luke iii. 22 ("Thou art my beloved Son, this day have I begotten Thee"), the adoptionist school of Christology suggested Jesus was reborn of the Spirit as an adoptive Son of God and Messiah. This concept influenced early Christian art, where Christ was symbolized as a fish. Tertullian wrote, "We little fishes, after the example of our Fish Jesus Christ, are born in the water." Similarly, Hilary wrote that Jesus became the Son of God after being "born again" through baptism.

## Rituals of Illumination
In the early church, the rite of baptism was often called "Illumination" because those who learned the faith were illuminated in their understandings. According to Justin, the process involved the name of God the Father, the name of the Holy Ghost (who foretold things about Jesus through the prophets), and the name of Jesus Christ, who was crucified under Pontius Pilate.

Justin asserted that this baptism was the "water of life" and the only means to purify those who had repented, contrasting it with rites that cleansed only the flesh. He remarked that those who approached God through Jesus Christ received a spiritual circumcision, rather than a carnal one, similar to that of Enoch.
