# Bononia

## Origins and Early History
Bononia, known today as Bologna, was the primary town of ancient Aemilia in Italy. Classical writers attributed its origins to the Etruscans, stating it was founded by Aucnus or Ocnus from Perusia under the name Felsina. However, recent excavations have revealed a different chronological sequence. These findings include approximately 600 ancient Italic (possibly Ligurian) huts and cemeteries from the Umbrian and preceding periods, dating from roughly 800 to 600 B.C. The Umbrian period immediately preceded the Etruscan civilization, which lasted from approximately 600 to 400 B.C.

Evidence of Etruscan presence is further supported by the discovery of an extensive necropolis on the site of the modern cemetery, as well as other sites located on the Arnoaldi Veli property and in the public garden.

## Roman Period and Infrastructure
Bononia first appears in the historical record in 196 B.C., at which time it was already possessed by the Boii and had likely changed its name. It became a Roman colony in 189 B.C. The town's importance was solidified by its strategic position within the road system of north Italy. In 187 B.C., it was connected to Placentia and Ariminum via the Via Aemilia, and a road to Arretium was constructed in the same year. A further road to Aquilelia was potentially built in 175 B.C.

The town acquired Roman citizenship in 90 B.C. Later, in 43 B.C., Mark Antony utilized Bononia as his base of operations against Decius Brutus and settled colonists there. The emperor Augustus subsequently added more colonists and constructed a new aqueduct sourced from the Letta, a tributary of the Rhenus. This aqueduct was restored to use in 1881, as recorded by G. Gozzadini in *Notizie degli Scavi*.

## Imperial and Late Antiquity
During the Roman Empire, the city continued to be significant. Following a fire in A.D. 53, the emperor Claudius provided a subvention of 10 million sesterces (L1,087,500). Bononia's status as one of the most important cities of ancient Italy was mirrored by the status of modern Bologna.

In the later stages of the Roman era, the city demonstrated resilience, successfully resisting Alaric in 410 and maintaining its existence during a period of general ruin. Following this, Bononia became part of the Greek exarchate of Ravenna.

## Archaeological Remains
While there are no remains of the Roman period currently standing above ground, various discoveries have been made periodically within the city walls.
