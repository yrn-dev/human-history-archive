# On Change of Names

*On Change of Names* is a tract written by Philo (20 B.C.–A.D. 40). The work provides depictions of a life that, in Philo's view, impersonated the highest wisdom. These descriptions are inspired by a respectable form of cynicism that had become deeply rooted among the Greek Jews of that era.

## The Ideal of Wisdom and Asceticism
In *On Change of Names*, Philo describes a company of the wise and good who, acting of their own free will, divested themselves of excessive wealth and rejected things that are dear to the flesh. Philo contrasts the physical state of these disciples of wisdom with that of athletes. While athletes are lusty and of good habit because they have fortified the body to serve as a servant to the soul, the disciples of wisdom are described as wasted and pale, appearing in some manner as skeletons. This physical condition is the result of their decision to sacrifice their entire bodily strength to the faculties of the soul.

## The Therapeutae
Philo's favorite ascetics were the Therapeutae, whose primary center was located in Egypt. This group practiced a rigorous form of renunciation, abandoning all property and the temptations associated with it. Their commitment to this lifestyle involved the irrevocable abandonment of their previous social and familial ties, including:
* Parents and children
* Wives and brothers
* Throngs of kinsmen
* The intimacy of friends
* The fatherlands where they were born and bred

This model of renunciation is noted as an ideal similar to that of early Christian renunciation, though it functioned independently of the influence of Jesus.
