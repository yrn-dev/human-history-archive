# Pennsylvania Excavations at Nippur (1888-1895)

The excavations at Nippur conducted under the auspices of the University of Pennsylvania represent a significant effort in the archaeological exploration of ancient Babylonia. These operations, which began in 1888 and continued through 1895 (with broader excavation activity in the region extending to 1900), focused on uncovering the remains of one of the most important political and religious centers of the ancient world.

## Leadership and Personnel
The expedition was led by Rev. John Punnett Peters, Ph.D., D.D., who served as the Canon Residentiary of the Cathedral of New York and was formerly a Professor of Hebrew at the University of Pennsylvania. Peters was in charge of the expedition from 1888 to 1895. Another key figure in the systematic uncovering of the site was Mr. J. H. Haynes, who worked to remove layers of debris and cut sections into the ruins down to the virgin soil.

## The City and the Temple of En-lil
The excavations focused heavily on the remains of the great temple of En-lil. In Babylonian religion, En-lil was the patron deity of Nippur and was ideographically written as Bel, a title signifying "lord" or "master" *par excellence*. Inscriptions discovered during the excavations by Peters and Haynes indicate that Bel of Nippur was regarded as the head of an extensive pantheon, holding titles such as "father of the gods," "king of heaven and earth," and "king of lands."

The chief temple of this deity was known as E-Kur, which means "mountain house." This edifice held such sanctity that Assyrian and Babylonian rulers continued to restore and embellish it until the latest days. The term E-Kur eventually came to designate the entire sacred precinct, which included a court of chapels and temples dedicated to other gods and goddesses. The "mountain house" designation may have referred to a staged tower, known specifically as "Im-Khar-sag," which was built to imitate a mountain and featured the sacred shrine at its summit.

## Archaeological Findings and Stratigraphy
The work at Nippur provided critical data regarding the chronology and urban development of the region. Excavations revealed that by a very early period—prior to 3000 B.C.—Nippur had already become the center of a political district of considerable extent. Other sources indicate the site may date as early as 5000 B.C., or potentially 1,000 years prior to that.

Stratigraphic analysis conducted by the expedition uncovered a platform of large bricks located midway in the mound. These bricks were stamped with the names of Naram-Sin and Sargon of Akkad (dated to 3800 B.C.). The debris above this platform measured 34 feet in thickness, with the topmost stratum dating no later than the Parthian era. It is calculated that the 30 feet of debris located beneath the pavement represents a period of approximately 3,000 years.

## Related Publications
The findings and experiences of the expedition were documented in several works. Rev. John Punnett Peters authored *Nippur, or Explorations and Adventures on the Euphrates* (published in 1897). Additionally, H. V. Hilprecht provided detailed accounts of the work in *The Babylonian Expedition of the University of Pennsylvania* (published from 1893 onwards).
