# Virgil, Sallust, Terence and Cicero

The writers Virgil, Sallust, Terence, and Cicero represent a group of foundational Latin authors whose works served as primary resources for later grammarians, critics, and printers. Their influence extended from the early Roman era through the middle ages and into the early modern period.

## Influence on Latin Grammar and Criticism
The works of these four authors were frequently used as benchmarks for Latinity and linguistic study. Arusianus Messius, a Latin grammarian who flourished in the 4th century A.D., authored a small extant work titled *Exempla Elocutionum*. Dedicated to the consuls Probinus and Olybrius for the year 395, this work consists of an alphabetical list—primarily of verbs that allow for more than one construction—utilizing examples drawn from Virgil, Sallust, Terence, and Cicero. This specific collection of four authors is referred to by Cassiodorus, the only writer to mention Arusianus, as the "Quadriga."

Other grammarians also focused on these authors. Aemilius Asper, a Latin grammarian possibly living in the 2nd century A.D., wrote commentaries on Virgil, Sallust, and Terence. Fragments of his work on Virgil indicate that Asper possessed taste and good judgment as both a commentator and critic.

## Literary Relationships and Comparisons
The archive records various intersections between these authors and their contemporaries. Cicero, for instance, commented on the comic poet Caecilius Statius. While Cicero admitted with some hesitation that Caecilius might have been the chief of the comic poets in *De Optimo Genere Oratorum*, he considered Caecilius to be inferior to Terence in terms of Latinity and style, attributing this to Caecilius's foreign extraction.

Virgil's work was deeply influenced by the earlier poet Ennius. Ennius's *Annales*, a narrative poem in eighteen books, was admired by both Lucretius and Cicero. Virgil frequently imitated the *Annales* and incorporated much of Ennius's sentiment and phraseology into his own national epic, purifying the original material and realizing it in an artistic shape. Additionally, Virgil maintained a friendship with Ovid and C. Cornelius Gallus; the tenth Eclogue of Virgil is addressed to Gallus.

## Later Scholarship and Commentary
The study of these authors continued through the 4th century A.D. with Aelius Donatus, a teacher of rhetoric and tutor to St Jerome. Donatus produced a commentary on Terence, which was a compilation from other sources, and wrote a life of Virgil along with an introduction and preface to his work. Fragments of Donatus's notes on Virgil were preserved, though they were severely criticized by Servius. Another writer, Tiberius Claudius Donatus, who lived approximately fifty years after Aelius, wrote a commentary on the *Aeneid* titled *Interpretationes*, though it is regarded as being of less value than the work of Servius.

In the 1st century A.D., the historian and grammarian Quintus Asconius Pedianus compiled historical commentaries on the speeches of Cicero. Using sources such as Tiro's life of Cicero and the *Acta Publica* (Gazette), Asconius provided notes on legal and antiquarian matters for five of Cicero's speeches: *in Pisonem*, *pro Scauro*, *pro Cornelio*, *in toga Candida*, and *pro Milone*. Other works attributed to Asconius include a defense of Virgil against detractors and a life of Sallust.

## Printing and Translation
In the 18th century, the English printer John Baskerville produced high-quality editions of these authors. After publishing a *Virgil* in royal quarto in 1577, Baskerville later issued a series of quarto editions of Latin authors that included Sallust, Terence, and Virgil. These works are noted as admirable specimens of typography.

The influence of these authors also reached the Dutch school of Middelburg. Jakob Westerbaen, a successful disciple of the writer Jakob Cats, produced translations from Terence and Virgil, among other Latin authors such as Ovid, Seneca, and Juvenal.
