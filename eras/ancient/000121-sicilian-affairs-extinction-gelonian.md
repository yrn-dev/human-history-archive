# Sicilian Affairs After the Extinction of the Gelonian Dynasty

The extinction of the Gelonian dynasty at Syracuse led to a broader political transformation across Sicily, characterized by the expulsion or retirement of despots from various Grecian cities. This period marked a transition toward free and self-constituted governments, though the process was complicated by significant social and proprietary upheavals.

## Political and Social Reorganization
The transition from single-headed rule to popular government involved more than a change in political structure; it included large-scale transfers of property and inhabitants. Previous despots—including Gelo, Hiero, Thêro, Thrasydæus, and Thrasybulus—had exiled many original citizens and replaced them with mercenaries and new citizens devoted to their interests. 

Once the "iron hand of compression" was removed, bitter conflicts erupted between the dispossessed proprietors, the old citizens, and the new settlers. These dissensions were particularly acute in Syracuse. While some mercenaries introduced by Thrasybulus retired to the Hieronian city of Ætna, a large body of citizens introduced by Gelo and Hiero remained. Of the ten thousand enrolled by Gelo, more than seven thousand were still present after the revolution.

To commemorate the revolution, the Syracusans convened a public assembly to establish an annual festival called the Eleutheria, featuring sacrifices and solemn matches, and to erect a colossal statue of Zeus Eleutherius. In determining the new political constitution, a strong reaction against the former dynasty led the assembly to declare all new citizens domiciliated under Gelo and Hiero ineligible for honor or magistracy.

Following a period of severe commotion, an accommodation was reached. Adherents of the expelled dynasty were resettled in the territory of Messêne and in the re-established city of Kamarina, located on the eastern portion of the southern coast near Syracuse.

## Internal Conflicts and the Sikel People
Syracuse continued to experience political dissensions, during which the practice of ostracism was tried and subsequently abandoned. Despite these internal struggles, the city maintained significant power and conducted foreign exploits, including conquests in the interior of Sicily.

In the interior, the Sikel prince Duketius founded a new Sikel town called Palikê. Following his exploits, Duketius was defeated and taken prisoner by the Syracusans. Although he was spared and sent to Corinth, he broke his parole and returned to Sicily, where he eventually died.

## Regional Relations and the Peloponnesian War
The relationship between the Sicilian Greeks and the Peloponnesian Greeks became closely intertwined during the interval between 461 and 416 B.C. Initially, the Dorian cities in Sicily attacked the Ionian cities, prompting the Ionian cities to seek assistance from Athens. This led to a series of Athenian interventions:
* A first expedition under Lachês.
* A second expedition under Pythodôrus, which resulted in indecisive operations near Rhegium and Messênê.
* A larger fleet led by Eurymedon and Sophoklês.

A congress of Sicilian cities was eventually held at Gela, where a speech by Hermokratês led to a general peace between the Sicilian cities. Eurymedon acceded to this peace and withdrew the Athenian fleet, an action that caused displeasure among the Athenians.

Further instability arose in Leontini, where the Leontine Demos was expelled with the assistance of Syracuse. The exiled Demos sought help from Athens, which sent Phæax to observe the situation. Additionally, a war broke out between Selinus and Egesta; the Egestæans applied to Athens for aid, with Alkibiadês warmly supporting their cause.

## Intellectual and Regional Developments
During this era, Sicily experienced an intellectual movement featuring figures such as Gorgias, Korax, Tisias, and Empedoklês. Agrigentum also experienced a period of prosperity and power.

Along the coast of Italy, the city of Rhegium, under despots Anaxilaus and Mikythus, remained closely involved in Sicilian politics. In approximately 473 B.C., Mikythus forced three thousand Rhegians to serve as auxiliaries for the Tarentines in an expedition against the non-Hellenic Iapygians. The expedition was a disaster; the Iapygians defeated the Grecian forces, resulting in a slaughter that Herodotus described as the greatest Hellenic slaughter within his knowledge. The loss of many opulent citizens in Tarentum strengthened the Demos and made the city's constitution more democratical.
