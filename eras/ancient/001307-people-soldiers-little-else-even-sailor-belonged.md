# The Social and Military Character of Ancient and Historical Populations

## The Military State of Assyria
In the ancient Near East, the sister-states of Babylonia and Assyria exhibited fundamentally different societal characters. While Babylonia was characterized as a land of agriculturists and merchants, Assyria functioned as an organized camp. The nature of leadership in Assyria was rooted in military success, as its dynasties were established by successful generals. In contrast, Babylonian leadership was established by priests through revolution, and the Babylonian king remained a priest under the influence of a powerful hierarchy.

The Assyrian king operated as an autocratic general of an army. In the early periods, he was supported by a feudal nobility, and from the reign of Tiglath-pileser III onwards, an elaborate bureaucracy was established. The king's palace was separate from the temples of the gods and was more sumptuous than those temples. The general population of Assyria consisted of soldiers and little else; notably, even the role of the sailor belonged to Babylonia. This extreme specialization in warfare contributed to the sudden collapse of Assyria once its fighting population was drained during the age of Assur-bani-pal.

## Social Classes and Recruitment in Early Civilizations
In early civilizations, certain social classes were characterized by their lack of fixity and their nature as recruited groups. These included servants, gang labourers, slaves, gang soldiers, and to a lesser extent, sailors (specifically galley rowers). These groups did not easily form homes or act as distinct breeding classes. Instead, they were replenished across generations through impressment from among cultivators, the failures of other classes—particularly small retailers—and the acquisition of captives.

Priesthoods and servants were also considered recruited classes. Many priesthoods were celibate, and servants, living in the households of others, generally did not rear large families of their own.

A distinction existed within the maritime community. While galley rowers were recruited, the navigating and shipowning seamen of ports such as Sidon and Tyre formed a different social stratum. Shipowners transitioned into the mercantile class, while navigators formed a peculiar community in great seaports, where they maintained homes and passed the secrets of seacraft to their sons.

## Maritime and Military Organization in Other Cultures
Different societies developed unique systems for managing their sailors and soldiers:

*   **Carthage:** The Carthaginians maintained powerful armies described as the flower of all armies in the universe. They utilized mercenary forces, funded by flourishing foreign commerce, which allowed them to wage war without depopulating their cities, disturbing peaceful artificers, or interrupting their navy and manufactures. Their vast coastlines allowed them to quickly levy experienced captains, pilots, and a sufficient number of rowers and sailors.
*   **Hydra:** In the city of Hydra, which originated from a colony of boatmen from the Morea, sailors were viewed as a distinct and respectable class. They were characterized as sedate, well-bred, informed, and speculative. Hydra employed a unique payment system where freight was treated as common stock. After deducting victualing charges, the remainder was split equally: one half for the captain and ship, and the other half shared equally among the crew regardless of rank or age.
*   **Syracuse:** In Syracuse, the "mob of sailors" and lower classes exerted political influence, at one time interfering in the appointment of commanders. Under the leadership of Herakleides, sailors and soldiers were susceptible to harangues that could incite mutiny against leaders like Dion.
*   **Ancient Greece:** During the Peloponnesian War, military necessity sometimes forced the adaptation of naval personnel. Demosthenes, facing attacks by land and sea, armed his sailors with poor shields made mostly of osier, as arms were unavailable in the desert location.

## Later Military and Regional Observations
In later historical contexts, the relationship between soldiers, sailors, and the state continued to evolve. In Dalmatia between the 15th and 17th centuries, despite the neglect of popular education and the presence of a small opulent minority, the soldiers and sailors of Dalmatia serving abroad maintained loyalty to the Venetians, fostered by 150 years of war against a common Turkish enemy.

In more modern conflicts, such as the World War, the management of soldiers and sailors shifted toward repatriation and the respect of graves. Agreements between Allies and Germany mandated the maintenance of graves for both soldiers and sailors buried on their respective territories and the repatriation of remains.
