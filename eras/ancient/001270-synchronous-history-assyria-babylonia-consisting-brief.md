# Synchronous History of Assyria and Babylonia

The "Synchronous History of Assyria and Babylonia" is a literary work of an ambitious character, taking the form of a chronicle. Written by an Assyrian, the text consists of brief notices detailing the various occasions on which the kings of Assyria and Babylonia entered into relations with one another, whether those relations were hostile or otherwise.

## Historical Context of the Two Empires
Assyria and Babylon were two great empires that developed on the banks of the Euphrates and Tigris. Geographically and historically, the two states were closely intertwined, with the power of one often serving as a measure of the weakness of the other. While classical accounts frequently prioritize Assyria, the decipherment of inscriptions reveals that for most of their history—with the exception of some seven or eight centuries—Assyria was effectively a dependency or province of Babylonia.

Babylonia is described as the mother country, and the Assyrians derived their culture, religion, literature, arts, and sciences from their southern neighbors. The two populations were similar in language and race. Despite these similarities, the character of the two states differed essentially: Babylonia was a land of agriculturists and merchants, whereas Assyria functioned as an organized camp.

## Political and Social Structures
The governance of the two states reflected their differing characters. In Babylonia, revolutions raised priests to the throne, and the king remained a priest under the control of a powerful hierarchy. In contrast, Assyrian dynasties were founded by successful generals. The Assyrian king acted as an autocratic general of an army, supported by a feudal nobility in early periods and an elaborate bureaucracy from the reign of Tiglath-pileser III. onwards.

Socially, neither country utilized a caste system. In Babylonia, the dominant class was the priesthood, which included doctors. In Assyria, this was mirrored by a military aristocracy. While both nations possessed large populations of slaves, as well as artisans, poets, musicians, and money-lenders, the commercial and agricultural classes were less numerous in Assyria. Scribes held a more important social position in Assyria than they did in Babylonia.

## Military Development and the Rise of Assyria
Assyria eventually grew in power at the expense of Babylonia. Early in its history, under Khammurabi, a Samsi-Hadad (or Samsi-Raman) served as a vassal-prince at Assur. The monarchy's foundation is attributed to Zulilu, who lived after Bel-kapkapi or Belkabi (1900 B.C.).

The shift in power became evident when the Kassite king of Babylonia married the daughter of the Assyrian king Assur-yuballidh. Following the murder of the Kassite king by a court faction, Assur-yuballidh marched into Babylonia to install Burna-buryas on the throne. After Burna-buryas's 22-year reign, the Assyrians ceased to be nominal vassals. Shalmaneser I (1300 B.C.) openly claimed supremacy in western Asia, founding Calah and extending the empire through campaigns against wild northern tribes and the Hittites. The transition of power was completed when Shalmaneser's son, Tukulti-In-aristi, conquered Babylon and executed its king, Bitilyasu.

Assyria's military strength was based on a standing army, which was further improved by the kings of the second empire. Cavalry replaced chariots, and Tiglath-pileser III introduced high boots and saddles for riders. Sennacherib established a corps of slingers. The army utilized battering-rams, baggage-carts, and tents during marches, led by a commander-in-chief known as the *tartan*.

## Chronology and Record Keeping
The two states employed different systems for recording time. Assyria used the *limmi* (archon) system, where years were named after the official in office. This system dates back to the early monarchy and was used as far away as the colony at Kara Euyuk in the 15th century B.C. Lists of *limmi* from the library of Nineveh allow for a certain chronology from 911 B.C. to 666 B.C. One such record notes a solar eclipse visible in the month Sivan, 763 B.C. (calculated as June 15th).

Babylonia instead dated years by the chief events that distinguished them, a practice also seen in the Old Empire of Egypt. The government determined these events and notified officials; one such notice from the reign of Samsu-iluna (son of Khammurabi) was discovered in the Lebanon. Babylonian records were kept in registers divided by reigns, which were later used to compile dynastic lists.

## Decline and Fall
The decline of these states began in the 7th century B.C. Following the death of Assur-bani-pal, the Assyrian empire began to break up. Under Assur-etil-ilani, Scythians penetrated Assyria and burned Calah. The subsequent palace built among the ruins was small and architecturally poor, reflecting the depleted resources of the state.

The final period of Assyrian rule involved a struggle between the last king, likely Sin-sar-iskun, and the Babylonian viceroy Nabopolassar. War broke out between them, and the Babylonians were aided by the Scythian king of Ecbatana, Cyaxares. The Scythian army captured and destroyed Nineveh and several northern Babylonian cities that had sided with Assyria, bringing the Assyrian empire to an end. The ruins of these two civilizations eventually became the foundation for the Persian empire.
