# The Four Nikāyas and Early Buddhist Records

The Four Nikāyas are the primary collections of the canonical Pāli texts, which reached their present shape before the 4th century B.C. These records were not books in the modern sense but were memorial sentences or verses designed to be learned by heart. Because writing materials were lacking, the Buddha—who taught exclusively through conversation—embodied his doctrines in set phrases known as *sutras* (Pāli: *suttas*). These served as *memoria technica* or catchwords that recalled fuller expositions.

## Structure and Composition of the Nikāyas

The canonical literature evolved through a gradual process of compilation. The earliest records were the *suttas*, which embodied doctrine in set phrases. These were then grouped into episodes, which were further woven together to form *suttantas*. Finally, these *suttantas* were organized into the Nikāyas.

The four collections are categorized as follows:

*   **Dīgha Nikāya**: Known as the "Collection of Long Ones," it contains the longest *suttantas*.
*   **Majjhima Nikāya**: The "Collection of the suttantas of Medium Length," containing texts shorter than those in the Dīgha but longer than ordinary *suttas*. Together with the Dīgha Nikāya, it contains 186 dialogues featuring the Buddha or his leading disciples.
*   **Anguttara Nikāya**: The longest book in the Buddhist Bible, filling 1840 pages. It organizes Buddhist doctrine, specifically ethics and psychology, into numbered classes (units, pairs, trios, and so on) to aid memory.
*   **Samyutta Nikāya**: This collection arranges the doctrine by subject, consisting of 55 *Samyuttas* (groups) where *suttas* on the same topic or addressed to the same type of people are grouped together.

## Literary Forms and Oral Tradition

The early Buddhist community possessed a great love of poetry, and verses were adopted to assist memory and express feelings of ecstasy and enthusiasm inspired by the reforming doctrines. The oldest verses are lyrics expressing emotion or "pregnant thought." These were often accompanied by prose frameworks stating when, why, and by whom they were uttered.

The dialogues within the Nikāyas are characterized by a dignified style and a method of Socratic questioning. They are often arranged by length rather than subject. While the Buddha used the ordinary conversational idiom of the day (Pāli) rather than the "dead language" of Sanskrit used by the Brahmins, later Buddhist Sanskrit texts include the *Mahāvastu* (probably 2nd century B.C.) and the *Lalita Vistara* (probably 1st century B.C.).

## Biographical Episodes in the Canon

The canonical Pāli texts preserve several episodes regarding the life of the Buddha:

*   **Birth**: Recorded in the *Majjhima Nikāya* and *Anguttara Nikāya*.
*   **Adoration of the babe**: An old ballad found in the *Sutta Nipāta*.
*   **Youth at home**: Found in *Anguttara Nikāya*, i. 145.
*   **The going forth**: This event is preserved as an old ballad in the *Sutta Nipāta* and as a prose account in the *Dīgha Nikāya*.
*   **Early Ministry**: The *Majjhima Nikāya* and *Vinaya* detail the years of study, penance, the attainment of Buddhahood and Nirvāna, and the conversion of the first five converts.
*   **Later Life**: The *Vinaya* records a visit to Kapilavastu, while the *Dīgha Nikāya* (specifically the *Mahāparinibbāna Suttanta*) documents the Buddha's last days.

## Transmission and Schisms

Following the Buddha's death, his disciples collected his sayings into the Four Nikāyas, though they likely did not reach their final form for fifty or sixty years. A supplementary Nikāya was added containing sayings ascribed to disciples, with additions made as late as the 3rd century B.C. during the time of Asoka. 

For several generations, these texts were handed down by memory, supplemented by written memoranda and running commentaries. Approximately one hundred years after the Buddha's death, a schism occurred in the community. Each resulting school maintained its own arrangement of the canon in Pāli or an allied dialect.
