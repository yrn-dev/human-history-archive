# Baiae and its District

Baiae was an ancient city located in Campania, Italy, situated 10 miles west of Neapolis on the *Sinus Baianus*, a bay on the western coast of the Gulf of Puteoli. Originally perhaps serving as the harbour of Cumae, Baiae remained part of Cumae's territory and never developed into an independent town. The city is said to be named after Baios, the helmsman of Ulysses, whose grave was located there.

## Natural Characteristics and Appeal
Baiae was primarily renowned for its mild climate, luxuriant vegetation, and variety of warm sulphur springs, which were noted for their curative properties. Despite these attractions, the low ground suffered from some malaria during the summer months. Because of these features, the area became a favorite residence for Roman emperors and a destination for the wealthy, particularly during the latter part of the Republic. Ancient writers frequently commented on the immorality and luxury associated with life in Baiae during both the republican and imperial eras. By the time of Strabo, the city had grown to a size comparable to Puteoli.

## Architectural Remains and Bathing Establishments
The district is characterized by the remains of splendid villas and baths, the foundations of which were frequently extended far into the sea. While many ruins are fragmentary and difficult to identify, several significant structures persist:

*   **Domed Buildings:** At Baiae, there are three large, lofty domed buildings constructed of brick and *opus reticulatum*. Although popularly called temples, they are actually the remains of *nymphaea* or baths. These early Roman domes featured pierced openings or windows, suggesting they served as *frigidaria*. Specific halls include the Temple of Apollo (internal diameter of 100 ft), the Temple of Diana (96 ft), the Temple of Mercury (66 ft), and the Temple of Venus (60 ft). The vaults were constructed of tufa with cement and horizontal brick courses.
*   **Bacoli:** This area contains several ruins, including a small theatre to the north of the village (known as the tomb of Agrippina), a large villa beneath the village, and two water reservoirs. One reservoir, the Cento Camerelle, lies to the east, while the *piscina mirabilis* to the south measures 85 by 230 ft and features a vaulted ceiling.
*   **Other Sites:** Remains are also found at Punta dell' Epitaffio.

## Notable Villas and Residents
Many prominent figures of the Roman Republic and Empire maintained estates in the region:

*   **Julius Caesar:** Possessed a villa whose remains are likely the large substructures on the ridge above the 16th-century castle. Hadrian died in this villa in A.D. 138.
*   **Nero:** Built a massive villa, likely on the site now occupied by the castle.
*   **Alexander Severus:** Constructed large buildings in the area for his mother.
*   **The Villa of Marius:** This luxurious retirement was later purchased by Lucullus and eventually became imperial property. It was the site of the death of Tiberius. The villa is referred to as both *Baiana* and *Misenensis*; scholars disagree on its location, with some placing it at Bacoli and others on the promontory south of Misenum.
*   **Bauli:** A villa resort near Baiae, named for the *Boaulia* (stalls) where Hercules concealed the oxen of Geryon. It is identified by some as the modern village of Bacoli and by others as Punta dell' Epitaffio. Pompey and Hortensius both held villas here. Hortensius's villa, located on the shores of the Lacus Lucrinus, was known for its tame lampreys and served as the setting for a dialogue in Cicero's *Academica Priora*. This property later became imperial property and was the site where Nero murdered Agrippina. Caligula also constructed a bridge of boats from Bauli to Puteoli.

## Lucrinus Lacus
The Lucrine Lake (Lucrinus Lacus) is located about half a mile north of Lake Avernus, separated from the Gulf of Pozzuoli by a narrow strip of land. This strip is traversed by the Via Herculanea, an embankment traditionally attributed to Heracles. In Roman times, the lake was a popular spot for pleasure excursions from Baiae and was famous for its oyster-beds, the foundation of which is attributed to Sergius Orata around 100 B.C. The state let out the lake's important fisheries to contractors. The banks were lined with villas, including Cicero's Academia on the eastern bank; however, this villa and the village of Tripergola disappeared in 1538, the same year the lake's size was reduced by the rise of the Montenuovo crater.
