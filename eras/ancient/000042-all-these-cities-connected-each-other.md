# Intercity Connections and Urban Infrastructure in Antiquity

The connectivity between cities and the internal organization of urban centers in the ancient world were facilitated through various means, including commercial treaties, religious alliances, diplomatic pacts, and sophisticated street planning. These connections ranged from the physical layout of individual cities to the complex geopolitical networks of the Mediterranean and the Near East.

## Commercial and Religious Networks in Egypt
In Egypt, during the reign of Amasis (570-526 B.C.), the pharaoh extended the privileges of Greek merchants at Naukratis. Amasis permitted various Grecian towns to establish religious establishments for their visiting citizens and sanctioned the creation of a formal, organized emporium or factory. This factory was linked to a large religious precinct known as the Hellênion, which was funded jointly by nine Grecian cities. These included four Ionic cities (Chios, Teôs, Phôkæa, and Klazomenæ), four Doric cities (Rhodes, Knidus, Halikarnassus, and Phasêlis), and one Æolic city (Mitylênê).

While the Hellênion provided protection and control for most Greek merchants, three powerful cities—Samos, Milêtus, and Ægina—founded their own separate temples at Naukratis. These separate establishments were likely connected to commercial facilities and protection, allowing these cities to act as individual guarantees for their own citizens' merchandise and conduct.

## Diplomatic and Federal Alliances in Greece
Connectivity between Greek cities often manifested as formal alliances or shared religious obligations. In one instance, a treaty of peace and alliance was concluded between the Athenians, Argeians, Mantineians, and Eleians. This agreement lasted for one hundred years and stipulated that the parties would not bear arms against one another for purposes of damage. The treaty included reciprocal obligations; for example, if an enemy invaded Attica, the other three cities were required to provide the most vigorous aid possible at the invitation of Athens.

In the Ægean, islands and seaports were connected through trade, common apprehensions, and feelings of fellowship. These cities formed a confederacy with a synod at Delos, which served as a federal tribunal. This board of arbitration was essential for maintaining common action against Persia, requiring members to forego private war and submit differences to peaceable arbitration. Over time, the judicial functions of this synod and the common treasure were transferred to Athens as the confederacy evolved into an Athenian empire.

Furthermore, early Greek connectivity was fostered by the habit of common sacrifice. Small-scale fraternity between villages was expressed through the sending of a sacred legation, or Theôria, to festivals. This practice established truces and connected communities with the local gods of their neighbors. These traditions eventually expanded from village festivals to town festivals, culminating in the massive confluence of the Pythian and Olympic games. The city of Elis enforced the Olympic truce, imposing heavy fines—even on Lacedæmon—for violations.

## Urban Planning and Physical Connectivity
The physical connectivity within ancient cities was often characterized by rigid geometric planning. In Babylon, the city was designed as a great square with twenty-five gates on each side. Fifty streets, each fifteen miles long, ran in straight lines from these gates to the opposite side, crossing each other at right angles. This layout divided the city into 676 squares, each with a circumference of two miles and a quarter. Additionally, four half-streets, 200 feet broad, ran along the walls.

Similarly, in Japan, main streets connecting opposite gates varied in width from 80 to 170 feet. These streets divided the city into nine districts, which were further subdivided into four divisions each. Each division consisted of four streets, and each street contained four rows of eight "house-units," measuring 50 by 100 feet. Minor streets and lanes crossed the main thoroughfares at right angles.

In Rome, the Imperial gardens—including those of Sallust, Lucullus, Agrippa, Domitian, and Caius—skirted the city. These gardens were connected to each other and to the Imperial palace via streets and bridges thrown over the Tiber.
