# Jaincoa and Jaungoikoa

The terms *Jaincoa* and *Jaungoikoa* are the names used by the Basques to refer to the Deity. These terms may be translated as "lord" or "master," or specifically as "Lord of the high." In the dialect of Roncal, the word *Goikoa* refers to "the moon," which would render the term *Jaungoikokoa* as "Lord of the moon." The root term *Jaun*, meaning lord or master, is also used in a secular context; for example, the phrase *Etcheko Jauna* refers to the lord or master of the house and is applied to every householder.

## Basque Religious Context
Information regarding Basque religion prior to the arrival of Christianity is limited. Available records suggest a belief system involving the worship of the moon, the sun, the elements, and the morning star. Additionally, there was a belief in the immortality of the body if it remained unburied and unburnt. 

During the Roman period, local Basque deities were assimilated into the Roman pantheon. Latin inscriptions from this era identify deities such as *Herauscorrtsehe* and Deo *Baicorrixo* (Baigorry). Other Iberian inscriptions have been discovered in ancient sepulchres and on monuments in northern Spain, including the *toros de guisando* (stone boars or bulls). Some figures found at the *Cerro de los Santos* in Murcia reflect the physical type of modern Basques, though they are found alongside figures of various other types.

## Comparative Concepts of "Lord" and "Master"
The concept of a deity identified as "Lord" or "Master" appears in various other historical and religious contexts within the archive.

### Babylonian and Phoenician Traditions
In Babylonian religion, the chief deity was known as Bel, a name that serves as a title meaning "lord" or "master" *par excellence* rather than a genuine name. Bel is the counterpart to the Phoenician Baal. The deity's real name is sought in En-lil, where the first element also carries the meaning of "lord." Bel of Nippur was regarded as the head of an extensive pantheon and held titles such as "father of the gods," "king of heaven and earth," and "king of lands." His chief temple, E-Kur (meaning "mountain house"), was a sacred precinct that Babylonian and Assyrian rulers sought to restore and embellish.

### Other Interpretations
The term "Lord of the High House" or "Lord of Heaven" is one possible explanation for the name Beelzebul, derived from the use of *zebul* in Rabbinical writings to mean "house," "temple," or "the fourth heaven," and in 1 Kings viii. 13 to mean "height." Conversely, other interpretations suggest Beelzebul means "Lord of Dung" (from the Targum word *zebel*) or "Chief of false gods."

## The Lord in Sacred Texts
In the Qur'an, the "Lord of the worlds" is presented as the sender of messengers to humanity. These messengers deliver reminders and messages from their Lord to warn people and offer sincere, faithful advice. The texts describe the Lord as the "Cherisher of the worlds" and the source of mercy and bounties. One such messenger reminds his people of how the Lord made them inheritors or successors after the people of Noah (Nuh), granting them a tall stature or excellence in make. The texts emphasize the command to worship Allah alone, contrasting this with the cults of ancestors.

## Secular and Administrative Use of "Lord" and "Master"
The terms "lord" and "master" are also used to denote high-ranking officials and social hierarchies:

*   **English Household:** The Lord Steward of England is a peer and privy councillor who serves as the first dignitary of the court. In the *Statutes of Eltham*, he is referred to as "the lord great master," and in an act of Henry VIII (1539), he is called the "grand master or lord steward." While the Lord Steward holds the appointment, the practical duties are often performed by the master of the household.
*   **Governance in India:** The title "Lord" was used by Governor-Generals of India, such as Lord Dalhousie and Lord Canning. In the case of Lord Dalhousie, his administration was characterized by a masterful spirit and a desire to be recognized as the master of the government.
*   **Samurai Code:** In the context of Bushido, the relationship between a subject and their lord or master involved duty and loyalty, but not the sacrifice of conscience. A subject who used servile compliance to steal a master's affection was termed *chô-shin*, while one who used unscrupulous fawning was called *nei-shin*.
