# Persia, Northern India, and China

## Historical Connections and Migrations
The ancient civilizations of China and Babylonia are generally admitted to have had some connection, though the precise nature of this relationship remains uncertain. It is clear that the Chinese entered their present territory along the course of the Hwang-ho at an unknown period, possibly around 3000 B.C., arriving from the west. 

Technological exchange also appears to have moved eastward across Asia. The art of making bricks, practiced by all civilized nations of antiquity, likely originated in Babylonia, where well-burnt bricks were used more than 6000 years ago and extensively during the time of Sargon of Akkad (c. 3800 B.C.). It is believed that the knowledge of brickmaking, and specifically the highly developed art of glazed bricks, traveled from the west through Persia and northern India to reach China.

## The Development of the Chinese State
China is the oldest of existing states, with authentic history extending back slightly beyond 1000 B.C. Early developments included the Shang dynasty, characterized by priest-emperors who presided over a loose-knit empire of subordinate kings and performed seasonal sacrifices. This era produced bronze vessels of high workmanship, indicating centuries of preceding civilization.

By approximately 220 B.C., the state of Tsin (or Chin) rose to prominence, forming a homogeneous empire that expanded southward. A significant architectural feat of a comparatively late period (c. 210 B.C.) was the construction of the Great Wall of China, which was built partly of burnt and unburnt brick.

China reached peaks of power under several dynasties:
*   **Han Dynasty (c. 200 B.C.–A.D. 220):** Extended boundaries in the north.
*   **T'ang Dynasty (A.D. 618–906):** Dominions extended across central Asia to northern India. During the 7th through 9th centuries, China was the most secure and civilized country in the world, with influence extending through tributary Turkish tribes to Persia and the Caspian Sea. This era saw the first use of tea, the manufacture of paper, the start of wood-block printing, and a revolution in thought brought by Buddhism.
*   **Sung Dynasty (960–1127):** Reunited China proper, though it later split into southern (Chinese) and northern (Tatar) kingdoms.
*   **Ming Dynasty:** A native dynasty that succeeded the Mongol rule of Kublai Khan. The Ming reigned for nearly 300 years and sent expeditions reaching East Africa, Ceylon, and India.

The imperial house was eventually founded by the Manchus, a northern tribe that captured Peking in 1644.

## Central Asian Invasions and Influence
Central Asia has been a source of significant historical upheaval. Tribes linguistically divided into groups such as Huns, Mongols, and Turks frequently formed states and launched invasions. Key conquests included the Mogul conquest of India, the Mongol conquest of China under Kublai Khan, and the later Manchu conquest of China. Additionally, numerous lesser invasions affected Persia, India, and China.

## Religious and Missionary Expansion
Islam has been a major transforming force in western Asia, subjugating much of the region including Persia and India. In India, Islam is strong in the central and northern regions but weaker in the south. In Persia, Arabia, Afghanistan, and Turkey, Islam is paramount. While Islam spread to the Malay Archipelago—where it overwhelmed Hindu civilization—and reached the southern Philippines, it made no progress in Japan or Indo-China. In China, although there is a large Moslem population, Chinese influence forced Islam to exist as a creed parallel to other religions, such as Buddhism.

Christian missions also penetrated these regions. The Dominicans reached central Asia, and St. Hyacinth traveled into northern China, Tibet, and Tatary. From the 14th century to the middle of the 17th, the Dominicans maintained numerous missions in China, India, and Persia.

## Geography and Natural History
The regions are characterized by distinct climatic zones. A hot, dry desert tract extends from Arabia through south Persia and Baluchistan to Sind. In contrast, a region of high temperature and heavy summer rains encompasses southern China, the Indo-Chinese peninsula, and India. 

Botanically, southern China is hardly distinct from the great Indian region, with about three-fifths of Hong Kong's flora species being common to the Indian region. North China's flora is akin to that of Japan and shows relations to the American continent, with temperate forms extending to the Himalaya and as far as Kashmir.

## Modern Imperialism
In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, European powers sought to carve up Asia for exploitation. Germany seized Kiau Chau in 1898, Britain seized Wei-hai-wei, and Russia took Port Arthur. These actions led to a surge of hatred toward Europeans in China, resulting in the 1900 attack on European legations in Pekin. Subsequently, the Russians seized Manchuria and the British invaded Tibet in 1904.
