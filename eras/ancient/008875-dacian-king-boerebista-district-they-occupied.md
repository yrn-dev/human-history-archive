# The Dacians and the Province of Dacia

## Characteristics of the Dacian People
The Dacians were described as the most warlike of men, residing beyond the Danube. They were characterized by their strength and fierceness, as well as a notable contempt for life. This attitude was rooted in a warm persuasion regarding the transmigration of the soul and its immortality. In later periods, the Taifalæ, who inhabited the region that now forms the principality of Wallachia, were considered by some to be the last remains of this great and powerful nation, also referred to as the Daci or Dahæ.

## The Reign of Decebalus and Roman Conquest
During the reign of the Roman emperor Domitian, the Dacians had insulted the Majesty of Rome with impunity. This state of affairs changed with the accession of Trajan, a military emperor with the education of a soldier and the talents of a general. Trajan launched the first of his exploits against the Dacians, leading to a memorable war that lasted five years, punctuated by a very short suspension of hostilities.

The Dacian king, Decebalus, proved to be a rival worthy of Trajan. Decebalus utilized every resource of policy and valor in his efforts to maintain the fortune of his people. However, because the emperor could exert the full force of the Roman state without control, the conflict ended in the absolute submission of the barbarians.

## The Province of Dacia
Following the conquest, the new province of Dacia was established. The province was approximately thirteen hundred miles in circumference. Its natural boundaries were defined by the Euxine Sea, the Lower Danube, the Teyss (or Tibiscus), and the Niester. A military road was constructed during this period, the vestiges of which can be traced from the banks of the Danube to the neighborhood of Bender.

## Territorial Changes and the Gothic Influence
The status of the province changed under the emperor Aurelian, who withdrew Roman forces from Dacia and tacitly relinquished the province to the Vandals and Goths. This decision was based on the judgment that the frontiers of the monarchy should be contracted. 

The Dacian subjects were removed from distant possessions they could not defend or cultivate, moving to the southern side of the Danube to increase the populousness and strength of that region. This fertile territory, which had been turned into a desert by repeated barbarous inroads, was restored through their industry, and a new province of Dacia was created to preserve the memory of Trajan's conquests.

Despite this migration, a considerable number of inhabitants remained in the old country, fearing exile more than the rule of a Gothic master. These "degenerate Romans" continued to serve the empire by introducing the useful arts, agriculture, and the conveniences of civilized life to their Gothic conquerors. Over time, an intercourse of language and commerce developed across the Danube. Eventually, Dacia became an independent state and served as a firm barrier for the empire against invasions from the savages of the North.

The various colony that filled the ancient province eventually blended into one people. While they acknowledged the superior authority and renown of the Gothic tribe and claimed a Scandinavian origin, the Goths themselves held a vain persuasion that their ancestors had once been seated in the Dacian provinces. This belief was fueled by the resemblance of the name of the Getæ, leading the Goths to believe their ancestors had checked the arms of Darius and Sesostris and received instructions from Zamolxis.
