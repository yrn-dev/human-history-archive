# Distinct Classes in Human History and Science

The archive's records indicate that the concept of "distinct classes" appears across various domains of human history, including social hierarchies, professional divisions, philosophical categories, and biological substances.

## Social and Hereditary Classes

In various historical societies, populations were divided into classes based on birth, land ownership, or political status.

**Early English and Roman Societies**
In early English society, a distinction existed between the *gesiðcund* and *ceorlisc* classes. While the exact nature of this difference is not clearly explained, it was hereditary and of considerable antiquity; they may be defined as gentry and peasants. Within these, further distinctions existed based on land. The *twelfhynde* possessed at least five hides of land (essentially a village), while the *sixhynde* were landless or possessed less. Among the *ceorlisc*, the *gafolgelda* (tribute-payer) possessed at least a hide, whereas the *gebur* owned no land and received his outfit as a loan from his lord.

In Rome, the early state was defined by a struggle between the patricians (the old nobility, also called *patres*) and the plebs (the commonalty). Originally, the term *populus* referred only to the privileged patrician class, and the plebs were not considered part of it. However, after the law of Licinius Stolo in 366 B.C., plebeians gained access to the consulship and higher honors. This led to the creation of a new class called *nobiles*, consisting of those who had achieved these honors and their descendants.

**Ancient Greece and the Middle East**
In Lacedæmon (Sparta), the *Periœkus* was a native of the soil subject to city law, distinct from the Metic. In contrast, Aristotle indicates that in Krête, there was no such intermediate class; instead, there were only two classes: free citizens and serf-cultivators (Helots).

In Bagdad, the governing pasha and higher officials generally came from Constantinople, while many other Turkish officials came from Kerkuk. These officials formed a class distinct from the native Arab population. This distinction was further reinforced by religious differences, as the Turks were Sunnites and the Arabs were generally Shi'ites.

**Japanese Feudatories**
In Japan, those possessing a fief of 10,000 *koku* or more were called *daimyo*. This group included the *Sanke*, *Sankyo*, *fudai* (hereditary vassals), *tozama*, and the *gokemon* (governor of Echizen). The *daimyo* were further divided into three classes by fief size:
*   **Kokushu** (provincial barons): Revenues of at least 300,000 *koku*.
*   **Joshu** (castle-owning barons): Incomes between 100,000 and 300,000 *koku*.
*   **Ryoshu** (castleless barons): Revenues between 10,000 and 100,000 *koku*.

## Professional and Technical Classifications

**The English Bar**
The legal profession in England is divided into several special classes of business. The most prominent distinction is between barristers practicing in chancery and those practicing in the courts of common law. Within the bar, there is a higher rank known as king's or queen's counsel, who lead in court and provide opinions but do not accept pleading or conveyancing. Historically, there was a separate class of special pleaders and conveyancers who held licenses to practice under the bar without being "called," though these duties are now part of the work of junior barristers.

**Medieval Naval Armament**
Up to the middle of the 16th century, medieval galleys were classified by the number of rowers on one bench pulling through a single *portella* (rowlock-port). The Italians used classical terms for these classes: *Biremes* (two men and oars per bench), *Triremes* (three men and oars), and *Quinqueremes* (five men and oars). Records from Marino Sanudo the Elder note that before 1290, most galleys going to the Levant were Biremes, but after that date, the *ai Terzaruoli* (three oars and men per bench) became the standard.

## Philosophical and Scientific Categories

**Socratic Phenomena**
The philosopher Socratês divided phenomena into two distinct classes to address the discrepancy between scientific and religious views. The first class consisted of phenomena where the connection between antecedent and consequent was invariable and ascertainable by human study. The second, more comprehensive class consisted of phenomena reserved for the unconditional agency of the gods, where results could only be known through prophecy, omens, or inspired communication. Socratês believed physics and astronomy belonged to the divine class, making human research in these fields impious and fruitless.

**Biological Opsonins**
In the study of opsonic action—the effect of serum on bacteria to make them more susceptible to phagocytosis by white blood corpuscles—it is admitted that two distinct classes of substances are involved. One class consists of thermostable immune opsonins developed through action. These differ from normal serum opsonins, which are labile and destroyed when heated to 55°C.

**Mythological Figures**
In the study of Grecian antiquity, some scholars, such as Mr. Clinton, propose that personages in the mythical world are divisible into two classes: those who are real and those who are fictitious. This differs from the view of ancient historians like Herodotus and Hellanikus, who assumed all mythical characters in their sequence were real persons for the purpose of chronological calculation.
