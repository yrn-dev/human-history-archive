# Nineteenth Year of the War: Arrival of Demosthenes and the Defeat at Epipolae

The nineteenth spring of the Peloponnesian War, also referred to by some historians as the beginning of the Dekeleian war, was preceded by a winter of extensive military preparations (414-413 B.C.). During this period, the Syracusan war became absorbed into a general conflict rekindling across Greece. While Eurymedon led ten triremes to Syracuse in midwinter, Demosthenês worked throughout the winter to organize a second armament for the early spring. Simultaneously, twenty Athenian triremes were sent to Naupaktus to block Corinthian reinforcements from the Corinthian gulf, while the Corinthians prepared twenty-five triremes to convoy their hoplites. In Lacedæmon, Bœotia, Sikyôn, and Corinth, levies of hoplites were gathered for the Syracuse armament and the occupation of Dekeleia. In Sicily, Gylippus actively encouraged Sicilian towns to participate in the struggle.

## Naval Conflict in Syracuse
The Athenian forces in Syracuse faced significant challenges. Gylippus initiated attacks at sea, and although the Athenians were initially victorious in a naval combat within the harbor, Gylippus successfully captured Plemmyrium. This capture increased the confidence of the Syracusans in their own naval capabilities.

The Athenians suffered further disadvantages in the Great Harbor, where their standard naval tactics were impossible due to the narrow space. The Syracusans improved their ships to better suit these conditions and threatened the Athenian naval station. Despite additional preparations by Nikias, the Athenians suffered a complete defeat.

## Arrival of Demosthenês
Following the Athenian defeat, Demosthenês arrived in the Great Harbor with the second armament, having voyaged from Korkyra. His entry had an imposing effect and revived the courage of the Athenian forces. Demosthenês implemented decisive resolutions and formulated new plans to alter the course of the campaign.

## The Defeat at Epipolae
Demosthenês executed a nocturnal march intended to surprise Epipolæ and turn the Syracusan line of defence. While the operation achieved partial success at first, it ultimately ended in a complete and ruinous defeat. The subsequent flight of the Athenians was characterized by disorder and great loss.

This defeat led to a divide among the Athenian generals. Demosthenês insisted that the forces depart from Sicily, or at the very least, remove themselves from the Great Harbor. Nikias opposed these views, refusing to consent to the removal. Due to the obstinacy and infatuation of Nikias, the armament remained in the Great Harbor, neither acting nor retiring. Eventually, Nikias consented to a retreat, and orders were circulated privately. However, the retreat was postponed following an eclipse of the moon, which was viewed as a sign.

## Aftermath and Syracusan Victory
The Syracusans, emboldened by their success, maintained an aggressive posture. In the final engagements, the Syracusans gained victory after hard fighting. The Athenians fled toward their station, passing between merchantmen. The Syracusan ships pursued them, but were stopped by beams armed with dolphins suspended from the merchant vessels. In the excitement of victory, two Syracusan vessels were destroyed by these beams, and one was captured with its crew.

The Syracusans sank seven Athenian vessels, disabled many others, and took most of the remaining men prisoner or killed them. Following this, the Syracusans retired and set up trophies for both engagements, confident in their superiority by sea and hopeful for equal success on land.
