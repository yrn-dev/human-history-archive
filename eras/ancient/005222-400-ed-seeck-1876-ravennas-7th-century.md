# Historical Records and Art of the 4th to 7th Centuries

## Classical References to Early Britain
The study of early Britain relies on several principal references from classical writers, including Tacitus, Ptolemy, Julius Caesar, Strabo, Cassius Dio, Diodorus, and the elder Pliny. In addition to these authors, historical data is drawn from specific lists and documents. These include the *Notitia Dignitatum*, dated to approximately A.D. 400 (edited by Seeck in 1876), and the *Antonine Itinerary*, which dates to roughly A.D. 210-230 (edited by Parthey in 1848). Further references are found in the Ravennas, described as a 7th-century *rechauffé* (edited by Parthey in 1860).

These passages have been collected in various scholarly works, such as Petrie's *Monumenta Hist. Britann.* (1848) and Holder's *Altkeltische Sprachschatz* (1896-1908). Additionally, Roman inscriptions have been compiled by Hübner in the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latin.* vii. (1873) and through supplements in *Ephemeris epigraphica* by Haverfield and Hübner. Other relevant records include Hübner's *Inscript. Britann. Christianae* (1876) and J. Rhys's work on Pictish inscriptions in the *Proceedings Soc. Antiq. Scotland*.

## Mosaics and Artistic Traditions in Ravenna
Ravenna serves as a significant site for the study of ancient mosaics, which utilized materials ranging from marble to colored and gilt glass. While glass mosaics were used as early as the Augustan age, and gilt tesserae appeared in the 1st or 2nd century, Ravenna contains prominent examples from later periods.

The mausoleum of Galla Placidia in Ravenna features interior mosaic work dating to the 5th century. The city is also home to many magnificent examples of mosaics from the 6th century. Furthermore, a mosaic from Ravenna was transported to Germany two generations ago and has been recently rediscovered.

## Religious and Monastic Developments
Around A.D. 400, Rufinus translated the Rules of St Basil into Latin. These rules became the foundation for life in certain Italian monasteries and heavily influenced the ground-ideas of the Rule of St Benedict. By the 6th and 7th centuries, Greek monasteries were established in south Italy, Sicily, and Rome. 

In the subsequent centuries, specifically the 8th through 10th, invasions by Arabs, Saracens, and others drove crowds of fugitives from Sicily and Greece into southern Italy. This led to the establishment of numerous Basilian monasteries peopled by Greek-speaking monks in Calabria, which eventually spread as far north as Rome. One such survivor of this tradition is the monastery founded by St Nilus around A.D. 1000 at Grottaferrata in the Alban Hills.

## Other 4th to 7th Century Developments
The period saw various cultural and regional shifts across Europe and the Mediterranean:
*   **Literature:** Around A.D. 400, the pagan writer Nonnus composed a fantastic epic regarding the god Dionysus's triumphal progress to India; Nonnus also wrote a voluminous commentary on the gospel of St John as a Christian. In the 7th century, Georgius Pisides wrote lengthy iambic poems detailing the martial deeds of emperor Heraclius.
*   **Architecture:** The cathedral on the island of Torcello near Venice was originally constructed in the 7th century. It is noted for its apse arrangements, including a bishop's throne in the center of the arc and six rows of stone benches for presbyters.
*   **Demographics:** In the region of Carniola, the Slavonic population settled during the end of the 6th century and the beginning of the 7th century.
*   **Art:** In Rome, the great apse-mosaic of S. Pudenziana dates to approximately A.D. 400.
