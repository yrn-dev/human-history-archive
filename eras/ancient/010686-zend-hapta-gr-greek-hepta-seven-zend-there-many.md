# Zend

Zend refers to both an ancient language and a collection of canonical books associated with the followers of Zoroaster. The term itself signifies "life" or "living," and the books are regarded as containing the "word of life." These writings are also referred to as the *Zendavesta* or *Avesta*; while *Zend* can refer to the language or the collection, *Avesta* specifically means "revelation," "oracle," or "word," representing the collection of Zoroaster's books as the revelation of Ormuzd.

## Linguistic Characteristics and Classification
Zend is classified as a member of the Indo-European family of languages and is part of the Aryan group. It is described as the ancient language of Media, showing affinities with the dialects of Georgia and Armenia. While some scholars, such as Sir W. Jones and Mr. Erskine, have considered Zend a derivative of Sanskrit, others, like the linguist Rask, view Zend and Sanskrit as sister dialects.

The Aryan group, including Zend, is distinguished from other Indo-European languages by four primary characteristics:
1. The confusion of original long and short *e* and *o* sounds with the original long and short *a* sound.
2. The representation of the short schwa-sound by *i* (for example, *pita* for "father").
3. The transformation of original *s* into *s* after certain consonants, *i*, and *u*.
4. The use of the suffix *-nam* for the genitive plural of stems ending in a vowel.

Zend specifically differs from Sanskrit in several ways. In Iranian languages like Zend, the Aryan voiced aspirates (*gh, dh, bh*) are confused with original *g, d, b*. Additionally, Aryan breathed aspirates (*kh, th, ph*) generally become spirants. A notable shift occurs where Aryan *s* becomes *h* initially before vowels and in some medial cases; for instance, the Sanskrit *sapta* becomes *hapta* in Zend (similar to the Greek *hepta* for "seven"). Zend also features numerous vowel changes not shared with Old Persian, some resulting from epenthesis or umlaut, as seen in the word *airya-* compared to the Sanskrit *arya*.

## The Zend Avesta and Zoroastrianism
The teachings of Zoroaster (the Greek form of the Iranian *Zarathustra*) are preserved in the *Zend Avesta*. Central to this religion is the opposition between two deities: Ormuzd, the god of truth, light, frankness, and the sun, and Ahriman, the god of darkness, night, diplomacy, cunning, and secrecy. The religion is characterized by a sacerdotal and ceremonial system that utilizes altars with sacred fires and temples, though it eschews the use of images. A distinctive practice is the prohibition of burying or burning the dead; the Parsees, the last surviving Zoroastrians, place their dead in "Towers of Silence" for vultures.

The *Zend Avesta* describes a social structure for the ancient inhabitants of Iran. This includes a fourfold division consisting of artificers, agriculturists, warriors, and priests. There is also a sevenfold division corresponding to the seven servants of Ormuzd, known as amschespands. This structure is attributed to a tradition from the golden age of Diemschid (or Jemshid) rather than an invention of Zoroaster.

The priestly caste, the Magi, was further subdivided:
* **Herbeds**: Disciples.
* **Mobeds**: Masters.
* **Destur Mobeds**: Complete masters.

Only the Destur Mobeds were permitted to read the liturgies of Ormuzd, predict the future, and carry sacred objects such as the *barsom* (a bunch of twigs), the *havan* (cup), and the *costi* (girdle).

## Scholarly Study and History
The study of Zend in Europe was significantly advanced by several scholars. Eugène Burnouf brought scientific knowledge of the language to Europe by deciphering Zend manuscripts brought to France by Anquetil du Perron. Burnouf published the *Vendidad Sade* and a commentary on the *Yaçna*, a liturgical book of the Parsees. James Darmesteter, who taught Zend at the Ecole des Hautes Etudes, published a complete translation of the *Zend Avesta* between 1892 and 1893. Darmesteter argued that the extant texts were more recent than commonly believed, dating the bulk to the 3rd century A.D. and the earliest to the 1st century B.C.

Other contributors include Hermann Brockhaus, who published the text of the *Zend-Avesta* using Latin letters. While some critics, such as Richardson, once claimed that Zend was an invented language created by the Magi, scholars like Kleuker proved it was a living, spoken tongue. By the time of the Arsacides, however, Zend had already become a dead language in the regions where the events of the *Zendavesta* occurred. The language of the commentary, Pehlvi, is a more modern idiom that also ceased to be a living tongue many ages ago.
