# Basilian Monasticism

Basilian monks are those who adhere to the rule of Basil the Great. The reconstruction of monastic life established by St. Basil serves as the foundation for monasticism within the Slavonic and Greek Churches, although the monks themselves do not use the term "Basilians."

## Origins and Principles
After leaving Athens, Basil visited monasteries in Palestine and Egypt. In Syria and Palestine, he observed that monastic life was becoming increasingly eremitical, often characterized by extreme bodily austerities. Around 360, Basil established his own monastery in Pontus, near Neocaesarea, specifically to oppose these tendencies.

Basil asserted that the cenobitical (communal) life was superior to the eremitical (hermit) life. He introduced several new departures in monachism, including the requirement that monasteries be located near towns. He maintained that work should be an integral part of monastic life—not merely as a way to pass time, but for its own sake and to benefit others. Furthermore, he taught that austerities and fasting must not interfere with work or prayer.

The life established by Basil was strictly cenobitical, featuring common meals, common work, and common prayer performed seven times a day. Despite these innovations, it remained an austere, contemplative life dedicated to heavy field-work, the reading of Scriptures, and prayer.

## The Basilian Rules
The "Rules" (categorized as the Longer and Shorter) are viewed as catechisms of the spiritual life rather than corporate regulations for community operation. While the authorship of some ascetical writings under his name has been questioned, the current tendency is to recognize the two sets of Rules as his. Additional insight into his monastic system is found in his correspondence with St. Gregory Nazianzen.

In the Basilian system, there were apparently no formal vows. Instead, the focus was placed on the cultivation of monastic virtues, including personal poverty, self-denial, chastity, obedience, and other spiritual disciplines.

## Propagation and Influence
The propagation of Basilian monachism was aided by the suitability of the institute to European ideas. In provinces neighboring Cappadocia, Sozomen notes there were no hermits, only cenobites. While a preference for eremitical life persisted in the east, the Basilian cenobitic form eventually prevailed throughout Greek-speaking lands through civil legislation (the Justinian Code) and ecclesiastical legislation (the council of Chalcedon).

Basilian monachism spread from Greece into Russia and Italy. Around 400, Rufinus translated the Rules into Latin, leading to their adoption in certain Italian monasteries. St. Benedict was familiar with these texts, referring his own monks to the "Rule of our holy Father Basil"; it is noted that Benedict owed more of his Rule's ground-ideas to Basil than to any other monastic legislator.

## History in Italy and the East
During the 6th and 7th centuries, Greek monasteries existed in south Italy, Sicily, and Rome. Between the 8th and 10th centuries, invasions by Arabs, Saracens, and others drove crowds of fugitives from Sicily and Greece into southern Italy. From the mid-9th century, Greek-speaking monks established numerous Basilian monasteries in Calabria, which eventually spread north to Rome. While most ceased to exist by the 18th century, the monastery founded by St. Nilus around 1000 at Grottaferrata in the Alban Hills survives today. These Italian houses were significant for their scriptoria; many chief collections of Greek manuscripts in western Europe, including those at the Vatican and the Bessarion at Venice, originated from the spoils of these monasteries.

In the Orthodox Church, monks hold significant ecclesiastical importance because bishops must be celibate, and therefore all bishops are recruited from the monks. Additionally, small congregations of Uniat Basilians exist outside the Orthodox Church, with Catholic Basilian monasteries located in Rumania, Galicia, Hungary, and Poland, as well as among the Uniat Syrians or Melchites.

## Basilian Nuns
Basilian nunneries existed from the beginning, with St. Basil's sister, St. Macrina, establishing a nunnery under Basil's direction. Nuns follow a purely contemplative life and are numerous throughout the East. In Russia, where there are approximately one hundred nunneries, nuns are prohibited from taking final vows until they reach the age of sixty.
