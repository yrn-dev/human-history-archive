# Baal

The term *Baal* is a word meaning "lord," serving as an ordinary title or word for a deity. It is frequently used to designate a local deity, as evidenced by place names such as Baal Hermon and Baal Hazor, or Beth Baal Meon (the House or Temple of the Baal of Meon).

## The Tyrian Baal and Phoenician Worship
The Tyrian Baal, also known as Melkart ("king of the city"), was worshipped in a magnificent temple in insular Tyre founded by Hiram. This deity was often identified with the Olympian Zeus or the Greek Heracles. The solar nature of Melkart is highlighted by an annual feast celebrating his awakening shortly after the winter solstice. In Tyre, as among the Hebrews, Baal was represented by symbolical pillars—one of gold and one of smaragdus—which are associated with the Pillars of Hercules.

The worship of the Tyrian Baal spread to all Phoenician colonies. In Carthage, Baal-Ammon was worshipped as an associate deity ([Greek: *theoi paredroi*]) alongside the great lady Tanit. Votive stelae from Carthage record invocations to both, describing Tanit as the manifestation or face of Baal (*Tanti-Pene-Baal*). In other Phoenician contexts, such as Paphos, the cult of a Baal or Astarte might be centered on a conical stone or pillar, which served as the object of public pilgrimage and adoration.

## Baal in the Israelite Context
In the history of the Israelites, the worship of Baal is presented as a foreign influence. King Jehu, seeking to extirpate the house of Ahab, targeted the priests and prophets of Ahab's god, who was called Baal. Jehu used a ruse, claiming he would worship twice as many gods as Ahab had, to gather the priests of Baal into their house. Once they were assembled and confirmed to be free of foreigners, Jehu's soldiers slew the prophets to vindicate the laws of the country.

Similarly, the house of Baal built by Jehoram and Athaliah to honor Ahab was demolished by Jehoiada and the people, who sought to return to the worship of the God of their fathers and the laws of Moses.

## Baalzebub and Other Variations
The Old Testament mentions Baalzebub as the god of the Philistine city of Ekron, whom King Ahaziah ben Ahab consulted regarding his health. While some suggest *Zebub* refers to a specific place, it is commonly identified with the Hebrew word for "flies." In this context, Baalzebub is viewed as the Baal to whom flies belong; flies being symbols of the sun's warmth. Other interpretations suggest the "Fly-god" may be an "averter of insects," similar to the Greek *Zeus apomouios* or a Hercules *muiagros* worshipped in Rome. Other suggestions include the identification of Baalzebub with the Egyptian dung-beetle (*Scarabaeus pillularius*). Some scholars, such as Cheyne, argue that Baalzebub is a contemptuous modification of the name Baalzebul. Other compounded names include Baalgad, the Baal of Fortune.

## Heliopolis and the Roman Period
The city of Baalbek (ancient Heliopolis) in Coelesyria is named for an earlier native term containing the element Baal. While the present buildings date from Antoninus Pius and Septimius Severus, a Baal temple likely existed earlier, where the Emperor Trajan consulted an oracle. 

The larger of the two Roman temples was dedicated to Jupiter (Baal), who was identified with the Sun and associated with Mercury and Venus. Locally, Jupiter-Baal was depicted as a beardless god wearing long scaly drapery, holding a whip in his right hand and lightning and ears of corn in his left, supported by two bulls. This specific representation entered European worship during the 3rd and 4th centuries A.D. Due to the "extreme licence" of the worship at Heliopolis, the Emperor Constantine built a basilica to curb the cult of Venus, and Theodosius later erected another basilica in the main court of the Jupiter temple.
