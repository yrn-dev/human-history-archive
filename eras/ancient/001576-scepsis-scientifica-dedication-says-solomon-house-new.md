# Solomon and the Construction of the Temple

The historical and textual records concerning Solomon, king of Jerusalem, focus heavily on his wisdom, his diplomatic relations with neighboring powers, and his extensive building projects, most notably the Temple of God and his own royal palace.

## The Construction of the Temple
Solomon designed and built a house for God, a project that had been foretold to his father, David. While David had made great preparations for the building and constructed the first altar on Mount Moriah, he was hindered from building the temple itself by continual expeditions and wars. Solomon, enjoying a period of peace, was able to undertake the enterprise.

The construction required specialized materials and labor. Solomon entered into an agreement with Hiram, the king of Tyre, who provided cedar and pine wood from Mount Lebanon, as well as significant quantities of gold and silver. Solomon utilized the Sidonians for the cutting of wood, noting their superior skill in the craft. In exchange for these contributions, Solomon provided Hiram with annual shipments of corn, wine, and oil. He also granted Hiram twenty cities in Galilee, though Hiram found these cities unsatisfactory and referred to them as the land of Cabul, a Phoenician term meaning "what does not please."

The temple was completed in twenty years. Its design included a sanctuary of forty cubits and a more secret inner chamber of twenty cubits. This secret chamber contained two solid gold cherubims, each five cubits high with wings stretching five cubits. Their wings touched the northern and southern walls and met in the center to cover the ark. The temple was characterized by extreme opulence; the floors were laid with gold plates, and virtually every internal and external part of the structure was covered in gold. The doors were made of cedar overlaid with gold sculptures, and the interior featured veils of scarlet, purple, blue, and soft linen decorated with flowers.

## The Royal Palace and Other Works
Beyond the temple, Solomon built himself a royal palace described as very costly and splendid. He also focused on the fortification of Jerusalem and the construction of several great cities. His reign was marked by the subjugation of some of the Canaanites.

## Wisdom and Diplomacy
Solomon was regarded as exceptionally sagacious and understanding. This reputation was evidenced by his interactions with King Hiram of Tyre, who sent him sophisms and enigmatical sayings. Solomon used his reasoning to solve these problems and reveal their hidden meanings. Similarly, records from the Tyrian archives mention Abdemon, a youth who conquered difficult problems commanded by Solomon.

Solomon's international standing was further reflected in his marriage to a daughter of a Pharaoh from the XXIst Dynasty. This union is noted as a sign of the decline of Egyptian prestige, as the Pharaohs of earlier eras, such as Amenophis III, had refused to grant Egyptian princesses in marriage to foreign monarchs.

## Later Reign and Legacy
Despite his wealth and wisdom, Solomon's later years were marked by his love for women, which incensed God and led to the rise of Ader and Jeroboam against him. The splendors of his house and the temple were eventually looted by the Pharaoh Shishak, the founder of the XXIInd Dynasty, who took advantage of a developing cleavage between Israel and Judah to capture Jerusalem.

In later historical contexts, the scale of Solomon's work served as a benchmark for other rulers. The Byzantine Emperor Justinian, upon completing the Cathedral of St. Sophia in Constantinople, claimed to have vanquished Solomon in the magnitude of his architectural achievement.

## Influence on Later Thought
In the sphere of natural science, the "dream of Bacon" (referring to Francis Bacon) sought to revive a spirit of method and observation. Bacon's work, particularly *New Atlantis*, provided the impetus for the foundation of the Royal Society. His experimental method of natural philosophy was praised by figures such as Dr. Thomas Sprat and later hailed by Voltaire and the French encyclopaedists as the foundation of experimental philosophy. While the scientific landscape shifted toward the clear, uniform prose of Newton and Locke, some transitional writers, such as Joseph Glanvil in *Scepsis scientifica* (1665), maintained a different style of reflection.
