# Early Babylonia and the Sumerian Civilization

The early civilization of Babylonia is identified as Sumerian. This civilization was developed by a race that spoke an agglutinative language, which some sources suggest may have been allied to the idioms of central Asia or the ancestors of the Huns and Turks. The Sumerians are credited with the foundation of the country's chief cities and the essential elements of its civilization, including the invention of pictorial hieroglyphs that later evolved into cuneiform characters.

## Geography and Early Settlement
The alluvial plain of Babylonia was known as Edin (or the Sumerian Gu-Edinna), a term referring to "the plain" on the western bank of the river. Southern Babylonia was more comprehensively called Kengi ("the land") or Kengi Sumer ("the land of Sumer"), eventually becoming known simply as Sumer. In contrast, the northern region was referred to as Urra (meaning "clayey soil" or "highlands") and later as Akkad, the Semitic form of Agade, which was the capital of the elder Sargon.

Archaeological excavations at Eridu have revealed a Neolithic agricultural stage that predates the Sumerian foundations and the invention of writing or bronze. In this pre-Sumerian stage, crops were harvested using earthenware sickles. Some researchers believe these people were proto-Elamites rather than Sumerians, noting similar remains at Susa, the chief city of Elam. Estimates suggest Eridu was located on the seacoast around 6500 B.C., though dates vary among authorities.

## Engineering and Infrastructure
The Sumerians were responsible for significant engineering works designed to regulate the environment of the river valley. These included the drainage of marshes and the construction of canals to regulate the overflow of the rivers. These infrastructure projects provided the physical foundation for the region's development.

## The Transition to Semitic Rule
At a remote period, Semites—likely originating from Arabia—entered the plain of Babylonia. While the exact timing is uncertain, the cuneiform writing system was still evolving when it was borrowed and adapted by the Semitic newcomers. The Semitic Babylonian language was profoundly influenced by Sumerian, borrowing words and grammatical usages, while Sumerian also showed traces of Semitic influence in its earliest texts.

Although the Semites eventually conquered the Sumerian cities and became the ruling race, the Sumerian civilization prevailed over the simpler Semitic culture. The newcomers adopted the Sumerian language and cuneiform writing, using them as the languages of power and knowledge. Sumerian continued to serve as the language of law and religion long after the Semites had established political dominance.

## Political Evolution and Empires
Early Babylonian states grew around sanctuaries where the god was nominally the ruler, and the human *patesi* (high-priest) served as viceregent. Over time, these high-priests assumed the title and functions of king. However, when Babylonia was unified under Semitic princes, the earthly king was viewed as an incarnate god, though the city of Babylon maintained a tradition where Bel-Merodach adopted the king to validate his right to rule.

The rise of Sargon I (with a traditional date of 3750 B.C.) led to the creation of an empire that combined Sumer and Akkad, a title that thereafter denoted the whole of Babylonia. Later, as the Sumerian Akkadian Empire lost its vigour, it was pressured by the Elamites from the east and the Semitic Amorites from the west. The Amorites initially settled in the town of Babylon and, after a century of warfare, established the first Babylonian Empire under King Hammurabi around 2100 B.C.

## Education and Culture
Education in Babylonia was of very old standing and was general across the population, unlike in the northern kingdom of Assyria, where it was confined to a single class. Libraries were common in temples and towns. Both men and women learned to read and write, which required knowledge of the extinct Sumerian language and a complex syllabary. 

Students utilized vocabularies, grammars, interlinear translations, and commentaries to understand older texts. Literature was primarily inscribed on clay tablets using a metal stylus, though papyrus was also used. In later periods, under the second Assyrian empire, Aramaic became a required subject for the educated class due to its role in diplomacy and commerce. Later, under the Seleucids, Greek was introduced, and some tablets show Sumerian and Assyrian words transcribed in Greek letters.
