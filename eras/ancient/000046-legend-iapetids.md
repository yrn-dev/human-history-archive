# LEGEND OF THE IAPETIDS

The Iapetids are a group of divine figures appearing in Greek mythology, specifically identified as the sons of the Titan god Iapetus. While their descriptions vary across different ancient sources, they are most prominently detailed in the Hesiodic *Theogony*.

## The Sons of Iapetus
According to the Hesiodic *Theogony*, the sons of Iapetus are Atlas, Menœtius, Promêtheus, and Epimêtheus. These figures appear in various capacities across the epic and didactic poetry of the era, though there are notable discrepancies between the accounts provided by Hesiod and those provided by Homer.

In the works of Homer, the representation of the Iapetids is significantly more limited. In the *Odyssey*, Atlas is the only son of Iapetus mentioned, although Homer does not explicitly identify him as the son of Iapetus. The Homeric Atlas is described as one who knows the depths of the entire sea and maintains the tall pillars that keep the heaven separate from the earth. Regarding Iapetus himself, the *Iliad* places him in Tartarus alongside Kronos.

## Promêtheus and the Human Condition
Promêtheus is a central figure in the legends of the Iapetids, particularly concerning his interactions with Zeus. The narratives describe a process of counter-manœuvring between Promêtheus and Zeus. This conflict leads to the punishment of Promêtheus, who was confined to a specific locality.

The legends associated with Promêtheus also involve the creation or introduction of Pandôra. In the *Theogony*, the poet presents a general feeling that while man is wretched, Zeus is not to blame for this state. Instead, the narratives highlight the mischiefs arising from women, a theme further explored in the legend of Pandôra as found in the "Works and Days" (specifically verses 95 and 705).

## Literary Context and Variations
The legends of the Iapetids are preserved in different types of early Greek literature, which often present differing perspectives. The "Works and Days" is characterized as the earliest didactic poem and is noted for its ethical vein of sentiment, though it remains intersected by the mythical. This poem differs from both the *Theogony* and the works of Homer.

The character of the "Works and Days" has been assimilated by Isokratês to that of Phokylidês and Theognis. Furthermore, the fable regarding Pandôra in the "Works and Days" (v. 200) has been compared to fragments from Simonidês of Amorgos concerning women, as well as fables in Archilochus regarding the ape and the fox.
