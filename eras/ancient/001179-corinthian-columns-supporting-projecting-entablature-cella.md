# Architectural Elements of the Cella and Entablature

In classical architecture, the cella serves as the inner chamber of a temple, while the entablature constitutes the superstructure supported by columns. These elements vary in decoration and composition across different historical periods and structures.

## The Entablature
The entablature is the architectural term for the superstructure carried by columns in the classic orders. It typically consists of three distinct members:
*   **The Architrave:** Also known by the Greek term *epistyle*, this is the supporting member that is carried from wall, pier, or column to column.
*   **The Frieze:** The decorative member of the superstructure. While common, it is sometimes omitted, as seen in the entablature of the portico of the caryatides of the Erechtheum or the archaic temple of Diana at Ephesus. It is also absent in Lycian tombs, which were rock reproductions of early Ionian timber structures.
*   **The Cornice:** The projecting and protective member of the assembly.

## The Cella and its Integration
The cella is the central room of a temple, and its relationship with the surrounding architecture varies by design. In the temple of Bacchus, the cella walls were connected to the peristyle via a coffered ceiling containing slabs featuring the heads of emperors and gods. This temple's cella is currently ruinous but once featured engaged columns supporting rich entablatures and inner wall-reliefs. Access to this cella was through an exquisite doorway with a cracked door-lintel that displays an eagle on the soffit.

In other structures, such as the small late imperial age temple known as Barbarat al-atika, the cella is semicircular. This specific cella is decorated internally with arcading and pillars, and externally with a frieze. This building's preservation is attributed to its later use as a church for St Barbara, a local martyr.

In the colossal Olympieum (the temple of Olympian Zeus), the cella served as the location for a chryselephantine statue of Zeus, dedicated by Hadrian in A.D. 129. Similarly, in the Hephaesteum, a sculptured zophoros—a decorative band—was placed above the exterior of the cella walls, though it only extended across the east and west fronts and the eastern ends of the sides.

## Corinthian Columns and Supporting Structures
Corinthian columns are frequently used to support entablatures in both pagan and early Christian architecture. 

### Temple Examples
*   **The Temple of Bacchus:** This peripteral temple featured a peristyle of 46 Corinthian columns, each over 52 ft. in height. These columns supported an entablature 7 ft. high that featured a double frieze.
*   **The Olympieum:** This structure possessed 104 Corinthian columns standing 56 ft. 7 in. high. These were arranged with 56 columns in double rows at the sides and 48 in triple rows under the pediments.
*   **Barbarat al-atika:** This small temple features a peristyle of eight Corinthian columns that support a projecting entablature.

### Early Christian Basilicas
Corinthian columns were also central to the design of Roman basilicas. In the original Old St Peter's, four dividing colonnades each consisted of twenty-two Corinthian columns; those positioned closest to the nave supported horizontal entablatures. 

However, later reconstructions of Christian basilicas in Rome often displayed a decadence in art due to a lack of architectural knowledge. Builders used materials from desecrated public buildings and temples, resulting in colonnades where columns of different diameters and heights were used together. In some instances, different orders, such as Composite, Ionic, and Corinthian, stood side by side, as seen at S. Maria in Trastevere. In other cases, such as at S. Lorenzo fuori le Mura, pilasters were placed in a horizontal position to serve as entablatures.
