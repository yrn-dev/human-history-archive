# Shalmaneser II

Shalmaneser II was an Assyrian monarch who ascended to the throne in 858 B.C., succeeding his father, Assur-nazir-pal III. He presided over a long reign lasting 35 years, during which the Assyrian capital was transformed into a type of armed camp.

## Military Campaigns and Expansion
Under the rule of Shalmaneser II, the Assyrian armies marched out of the capital annually to destroy and plunder. His military efforts extended to several regions:

*   **Babylonia:** Shalmaneser II occupied Babylon and reduced the country to a state of vassalage.
*   **Syria:** In 853 B.C., Shalmaneser II shattered a confederacy of Syrian princes. This coalition was led by Benhadad of Damascus and included Ahab of Israel. Later, in 842 B.C., Shalmaneser II took advantage of the assassination of Ben-Hadad by Hazael to attack Syria again. While he wasted the country, he was unable to capture the capital city. Twelve years after the initial defeat of the Syrian confederacy, the forces of Hazael were annihilated, and the ambassadors of Jehu of Samaria brought tribute to the "great king."

## Architecture and Archaeological Discoveries
Archaeological excavations conducted by Hormuzd Rassam between 1877 and 1879 provided physical evidence of Shalmaneser II's contributions to Assyrian infrastructure. At the mounds of Balawat (known to the Assyrians as Imgur-Bel), located 15 miles east of Mosul, Rassam discovered a palace. Although this palace had been destroyed by the Babylonians, it was restored by Shalmaneser II in 858 B.C.

From this restored palace came the bronze gates featuring hammered reliefs, which are currently housed in the British Museum. These gates consisted of two leaves, each measuring approximately 27 feet in height and 8 feet 4 inches in width. The gates were encased in bronze bands or strips, 10 inches high, which were decorated with repousse figures. The wooden doors were roughly 3 inches thick, while the hanging stiles measured over 14 inches in diameter.

## Succession and Internal Conflict
The final years of Shalmaneser II's life were marked by instability due to a rebellion led by his eldest son. This revolt was nearly fatal to the king's authority, as the pretender gained the support of Arbela, Assur, and other locations. The rebellion was eventually suppressed with difficulty by Shalmaneser's second son, Samsi-Raman (also referred to as Samsi-Hadad). Following the resolution of the conflict, Samsi-Raman succeeded Shalmaneser II in 824 B.C.
