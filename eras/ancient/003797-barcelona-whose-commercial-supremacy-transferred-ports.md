# Barcelona

Barcelona is a flourishing city and the principal seaport of Spain, located in eastern Spain at 41deg 23' N. and 2deg 11' E. on the Mediterranean Sea. Since 1833, it has served as the capital of the province of Barcelona, and it was formerly the capital of Catalonia. The city is situated on the sloping edge of a small plain between the Llobregat river to the south and the Besos river to the north. Its geography is defined by the fortified hills of Montjuich (650 ft.) to the south-west, the heights of Tibidabo (1745 ft.) to the west, and the Montanas Matas to the north-east. The urban landscape consists of a central city featuring huge factories and a busy port, surrounded by modern suburbs and gardens of almost tropical luxuriance. As of 1900, the population was 533,000.

## Early History and Roman Era
The city's ancient name, Barcino, is traditionally connected to Hamilcar Barca of Carthage, who is credited as its founder in the 3rd century B.C. Following the Roman conquest, the city was named Julia Faventia—later Augusta and Pia—by Augustus (27 B.C.-A.D. 14) and granted the status of a Roman colony. The Roman city was located on Monte Taber and grew rapidly to become a leading mart of the western Mediterranean by the 2nd century A.D., rivalling Massilia (Marseilles) and Tarraco (Tarragona).

## Medieval and Early Modern Period
The bishopric of Barcelona was established in 343. During the Visigothic period, the city served as a temporary capital in 415 and 531, and hosted church councils in 540 and 599. In 713, the city—then called Barcinona or Bardjaluna—was captured by the Moors. It passed to the dominion of the Franks in 801 along with the rest of Catalonia, and from 874, the counts of Barcelona ruled as independent monarchs.

The city reached the zenith of its wealth and fame following the 1149 union between Aragon and Catalonia. During this era, Barcelona's merchant ships traded from the Baltic and North Sea in the west to Alexandria in the east, competing with the maritime powers of Venice, Ragusa, and Genoa. In 1258, James I. of Aragon authorized the city to issue the *Consulado del Mar*, a code of maritime law that many European states recognized as authoritative. The city also pioneered the practice of marine insurance and maintained consuls at major Mediterranean commercial centers.

## Political Shifts and Decline of Supremacy
The union of Aragon and Castile in 1479 began to favor other Spanish cities. Barcelona's commercial supremacy was subsequently transferred to the ports of western Spain following the discovery of America in 1492. This shift led to resentment toward the "Castilian" government, with some citizens favoring annexation by France. This tension culminated in 1640 when Barcelona became the center of a Catalonian rebellion against Philip IV and sought French protection. Although it returned to its allegiance in 1652, the city was captured by the duke of Vendome in 1697.

## Port Development and Infrastructure
Historically, the port of Barcelona was merely an open beach sheltered by hills. While Don Alphonso V granted a license to build a mole in 1438 and the Moll de Santa Creu began in 1474, the city was still described by travelers as lacking a harbor well into the 17th century. For a long period, a sand-bank blocked large vessels from entering the inner area.

Modern improvements included the construction of a mole from the foot of Montjuich and an extension of the former mole to create a shelter for heavy battleships. Systematic improvements began in 1873, adding new quays and storage. By 1903, the port featured an inner harbor (18 to 30 ft. deep at low tide) and an outer harbor (20 to 35 ft. deep). In 1904, 8,075 vessels totaling nearly 5,000,000 tons entered the port. The city is well-connected by rail to Perpignan in France, Saragossa, and Madrid, and utilizes tramway lines to connect the port to suburbs such as Gracia.

## Industry and Modern Commerce
Barcelona has been the commercial and industrial center of eastern Spain since the 12th and 13th centuries. Despite a temporary check caused by the Spanish-American War of 1898, the city remained economically powerful; by 1899, it paid over L550,000 in industrial and commercial taxes, exceeding 11% of the kingdom's total. By 1904, it was a regular port of call for 35 shipping companies and the headquarters for 13 others, including the Transatlantic Mail Company.

The city's industrial workforce exceeds 150,000 people. While the primary industries are the weaving and spinning of silk, cotton, and wool, the city has expanded into nearly every industry where native capital can compete with foreign interests, aided by protectionist tariffs instituted in 1892. Principal exports include machinery, boots, shoes, leather goods, furniture, barrels, cigarette-paper, soap, cotton goods, olive-oil, cereals, and wines. Imports consist largely of combustibles, raw materials, comestibles, and foreign manufactured goods. Additionally, Barcelona serves as a trade hub for nearby islands, importing fresh fish and lobsters while providing the islands with timber, coal, sugar, petroleum, leather, and cotton.
