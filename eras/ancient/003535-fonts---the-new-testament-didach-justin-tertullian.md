# Fonts and Early Christian Baptism

## Early Practices and the Use of Water
In the earliest stages of the Christian faith, the use of a formal font was not enjoined. Sources including the New Testament, the *Didache*, Justin, and Tertullian generally contemplate the use of living or running water for baptism. This practice aligned with a rule among the Bani Israel (sons of Israel) that water used for ablutions should run over and away from the parts of the body being washed.

Consequently, baptisms were frequently conducted in natural bodies of water. Records of acts of martyrdom as late as the age of Decius describe baptisms taking place in the sea, lakes, and rivers. Tertullian, writing around 200 A.D., noted that it made no difference whether the washing occurred in a spring, a ditch, a pool, a river, or the sea. He specifically compared those baptized by Peter in the Tiber to those baptized by John in the Jordan.

## Rituals and Methods of Baptism
The Greek terms *baptismos* and *baptisma*, found in the New Testament, signify "ceremonial washing." The root verb *baptizo* is derived from *bapto*, meaning to "dip." While later manuscript rubrics enjoined total immersion, this did not become general vogue until the 7th century, coinciding with the increasing rarity of adult baptism.

Earlier evidence suggests a variety of methods. The *Didache* instructs to "pour water on the head." This is supported by Christian sculptures and pictures from the 1st to the 10th century, which depict the baptizer pouring water from a bowl or the hand over the head of a person standing in water. Similarly, the *Key of Truth*, a manual for old Armenian Baptists, describes a process where the penitent advances on his knees into the middle of the water to have water poured over his head.

## Evolution of the Baptismal Font
The transition to formal fonts occurred gradually. Evidence from ruins indicates that the earliest fonts were not deep or large enough to insist upon complete submersion. By the time of Eusebius, the church of Tyre appeared to have a font. Later, between 311 and 335 A.D., the church order of Macarius, bishop of Jerusalem, specified that the font should be placed behind and to the right hand of the altar within the same building. However, Macarius also noted that a font was not essential in cases of illness, stating that the "Holy Spirit is not hindered by want of a vessel."

## Baptism in Exceptional Circumstances
In certain extreme cases, such as martyrdom, the standard requirements for baptism were modified. A martyr's death was considered sufficient in itself, though in some exceptional instances, it sufficed for a martyr to be sprinkled with his own blood.

The necessity of the ritual for salvation was a point of discussion. Nearchus (c. 250) addressed the concerns of his unbaptized friend Polyeuctes on the scaffold. Nearchus argued that when the Lord healed the blind, He did not ask if they had been baptized or mention the "holy mystery," but instead asked only if they possessed true faith and believed He was able to perform the healing.

## Comparative Rituals and Context
The early Church Fathers were aware that ritual washing for the removal of sin existed in other religions. Tertullian observed in his tract *On Baptism* that the mysteries of Apollo and Eleusis involved baptism (*tinguntur*), and that votaries of Mithras and Isis were initiated *per lavacrum*, or "through a font." He noted that these groups believed such rituals provided regeneration and exemption from the guilt of perjuries. Tertullian further remarked that among the ancients, those who had committed homicide sought waters that could purge them of their guilt.
