# Sicilian Affairs Down to the Close of the Expedition of Timoleon

The history of the Sicilian Greeks between 353 and 336 B.C. is characterized by a transition from a period of despotism, impoverishment, and suffering to an era of renovated freedom and comparative happiness. This transformation was primarily achieved through the efforts of the liberator Timoleon.

## The Government of Dion
Prior to the success of Timoleon, the Sicilian Greeks were influenced by the actions of Dion. Though Dion had imbibed public-spirited aspirations and a hatred for government by will—influenced by his teacher, the philosopher Plato—his practical approach to leadership was flawed. Having been raised in a despotic family under the elder Dionysius, Dion lacked experience in the exigencies of a community of freemen. 

Dion believed that while despotism was negative, a thoroughly popular government was also undesirable. Consequently, he viewed himself not as a servant of the people, but as a despotic lawgiver who would determine the amount of liberty and the laws to be sanctioned for the community. This approach led him to establish a government based on pure force. During his tenure, he made a martyr of Herakleides and continued on a path of decline. While Plato mourned Dion's death and attributed his failure to external factors, Dion's career ended in a manner that contrasted sharply with the later success of Timoleon.

## The Expedition of Timoleon
The mission of Timoleon was fraught with difficulty from its inception. The enterprise required significant enthusiasm because the rewards were doubtful and the obstacles formidable. Before preparations were even complete, the situation appeared hopeless due to communications from Hiketas. 

Hiketas had sent a second mission retracting his previous statements and requesting that Corinth send no expedition. He claimed that because Corinthian aid had not arrived in time, he had been forced into an alliance with the Carthaginians. This alliance prohibited Corinthian soldiers from setting foot in Sicily. While this news exasperated the Corinthians and increased their desire to remove Hiketas, it also diminished their zeal for active service. It appeared that Timoleon, leading a small force, would be unable to reach the Sicilian shore against a numerous and active Carthaginian fleet, and that upon arrival, he would find the Syracusan people helpless and lacking important friends.

## Achievements and Legacy of Timoleon
Despite these obstacles, Timoleon succeeded where others had failed. He is recognized by Polybius as one of the most complete men of action to play a part in Sicilian affairs, alongside Pyrrhus and Hermokrates. Timoleon's achievements included:
* The removal of the despots.
* The subduing of the foreign enemy.
* The re-colonization of the largest among the ruined cities.
* The restoration of the constitution and laws to the Sicilian Greeks.

Unlike many other Greek leaders and generals of the period who dealt with Sicilian affairs, Timoleon acted virtuously from his own nature and did not take bribes.

## The Timoleontion and Final Honors
The Syracusan people expressed their gratitude toward Timoleon through significant public honors. Upon his death in 336 B.C. (or 337 B.C. according to some sources), the people of Syracuse solemnized his funeral at a cost of two hundred minæ. They passed a vote to honor him perpetually with gymnastics, music, and horse and chariot races.

A sepulchral monument was erected in the agora of Syracuse with an inscription praising his role in restoring their laws and defeating their enemies. This site was expanded to include palæstræ for the exercise of youths and porticos for business and conversation. The entire complex of buildings became known as the Timoleontion.
