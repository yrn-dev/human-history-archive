# History and Development of Roads

The development of roads has evolved from ancient strategic military networks and early trails to scientifically engineered systems designed for heavy transport and urban utility.

## Ancient and Military Road Systems
In antiquity, road construction focused on utility, convenience, and ornament. Caius implemented a system where roads were constructed in straight lines through the country, utilizing a combination of tight-rammed masses of earth and quarried stone. To maintain a level appearance, depressions were filled and bridges were constructed over deep ravines or winter torrents. These roads were measured in miles—with the Roman mile being slightly less than eight Greek stadia—and marked with stone blocks to indicate distance. Additionally, stones were placed at shorter intervals on both sides of the road to allow travelers to mount horses without assistance.

Military road networks were also extensive in Britain, particularly in the northern hill-districts and Wales. In Wales, roads connected Chester to Carnarvon (via a fort at Caer-hyn) and ran along the south coast from Caerleon-on-Usk through Cardiff to Carmarthen. A third road near Cardigan Bay connected these northern and southern routes, while an interior system included points such as the Gaer near Brecon and Caer-gai on Bala Lake. In northern Britain, three primary roads existed: one leading north from York to Scotland via Catterick Bridge and other forts; a second turning north-west from Catterick Bridge over the Pennine Chain to Hadrian's wall near Carlisle; and a third, more complex route from Chester along the western coast, passing through Manchester, Ribchester, and Old Carlisle.

## Early Trails and Regional Routes
In North America, early settlers of Kentucky utilized the Ohio River or the "Wilderness Road" via the Cumberland Gap. Marked by Daniel Boone in 1775, the Wilderness Road began as a mere trail from the Watauga settlement in east Tennessee to the Cumberland Gap, continuing through Bardstown, Danville, and Crab Orchard to the Falls of the Ohio. It remained passable only for horses and men until 1795, when it was converted into a wagon road.

In other regions, roads were often dictated by geography and season. In Asia Minor, the "winter-road" from Erzerum to Trebizond was the longest route, passing through Gumisch-Khana and following the valley, whereas other routes crossed the mountains. In Persia, the route from Yazd to Kermán via Báfk was the primary path before A.D. 1500, characterized by plains and groves of date-trees at Báfk. Following the construction of caravanserais by Sháh Abbás at Shamsh, Kermán-sháhán, and Zein ud-dín, travelers shifted to the eastern road via Anár and Bahrámábád.

## Engineering and Scientific Advancements
The 19th century saw a shift toward scientific road-making, led by the Scottish inventor John Loudon McAdam (1756–1836). After conducting experiments at Sauhrie and Falmouth, McAdam concluded that roads should be constructed of broken stone. His theories were detailed in his 1819 publication, *Practical Essay on the Scientific Repair and Preservation of Roads*, and his 1820 work, *Present State of Road-making*. Following a 1823 parliamentary inquiry, his methods were adopted by public authorities, and he was appointed general surveyor of roads in 1827.

Modern road engineering also accounts for "live loads" on bridges. In Switzerland, roads are categorized by importance to determine required strength. "Main Roads" are designed for a crowd load of 92 lb per sq. ft. or a 10-ton axle; "Secondary Roads" for 72 lb per sq. ft. or a 6-ton axle; and "Other Roads" for 51 lb per sq. ft. or a 3-ton axle. In England, provisions are made for even larger loads, such as traction engines with boiler trolleys or trucks loaded with granite.

## Road Maintenance and Urban Thoroughfares
Historically, road upkeep was often a matter of forced labor. In England, the common law held parish occupiers responsible for road upkeep. An act of 1555 required four days of forced labor, which was increased to six days under an act of Elizabeth. This burden was gradually reduced by the system of turnpikes starting in 1663. Eventually, the Highways Act of 1835 placed highways under the direction of a parish surveyor, funded by rates levied on land occupiers. In Scotland, statutory labor was established in 1719 and abolished in 1883.

In urban settings, such as the borough of Fulham in London, roads serve as primary thoroughfares for metropolitan movement. Key routes include the Fulham Palace Road, King's Road, and Fulham Road, which converge toward Putney Bridge, as well as Lillie Road and Wandsworth Bridge Road.
