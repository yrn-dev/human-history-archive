# Subjugation in Human History

The concept of subjugation appears across various eras and contexts in the archive, ranging from the expansion of ancient empires and the consolidation of medieval kingdoms to the internal power struggles of religious councils and the military campaigns of early modern states.

## Ancient and Classical Subjugations

In the ancient world, the Aryan peoples emerged as a dominant force from the ninth century B.C. onward. Over a period of six centuries, these peoples grew in power and enterprise, eventually subjugating the Egyptian, Semitic, and Aegean worlds. Despite this victory in form, a struggle between Egyptian, Semitic, and Aryan ideas and methods persisted long after the Aryan peoples held the sceptre.

In the Classical period, the Athenians transitioned from popular rulers to a more exacting power. While they initially formed alliances to liberate the Hellenes from the Mede, they later sought the subjection of their own allies. This process began with the subjugation of Naxos, an allied city that had left the confederacy. The Athenians also captured Eion from the Medes, enslaving its inhabitants, and enslaved the Dolopian population of the island of Scyros. The allies' inability to defend themselves, combined with Athenian severity regarding tribute and service, led to the enslavement of most confederates, with the exception of the Chians and the speakers of the record.

## Medieval Territorial Conquests

During the Middle Ages, subjugation was often driven by the desire for strategic resources or territorial security. Boleslaus III, King of Poland (1086-1139), devoted the main portion of his life to the subjugation of Pomerania to secure access to the sea. The Pomeranians, a pagan Slavonic race akin to the Wends, utilized their seaboard to maintain fleets and attack Polish territories. Boleslaus began his campaign in 1109 with a victory at Nackel. Despite a later rebellion by Prince Warceslaus of Stettin between 1120 and 1124—which resulted in the loss of 18,000 followers and the expatriation of 8,000 more—Boleslaus eventually achieved submission.

In Western Europe, Charlemagne expanded the Frankish kingdom through various struggles. In 774, he completely subjugated Lombardy, sending his father-in-law, the King of Lombardy, to a monastery. He further extended these conquests into Dalmatia in 776.

In the Iberian Peninsula, a Moslem army of Berbers and Arabs defeated the West Goths in 711, quickly bringing the whole country under their possession. By 720, the faith of Muhammad had reached the Pyrenees and pushed into France, appearing likely to subjugate Gaul as easily as it had the Spanish peninsula, before being halted by the kingdom of the Franks.

## Religious and Political Subjugation

Subjugation also occurred within the structures of ecclesiastical and political authority. During a period of conflict between a council and the papacy, the "fathers" of the council sought to curtail the resources and power of the papacy. They implemented disciplinary measures and abolished certain papal rights, such as annates and the "next presentation" to benefices (*gratiae expectativae*). Beyond these administrative reforms, the fathers continued to devote themselves to the subjugation of the Hussites.

## Early Modern Military Campaigns

In the 17th century, the Cossacks of Kharkov served as a strong advance-guard for Russian power, remaining faithful to the tsar during various rebellions. This relationship continued until the final subjugation of the entire southern region.

In the East, Japanese forces under the command of Ukita Hideiye engaged in campaigns in Korea. While some commanders, such as Konishi Yukinaga, wished to march northward to China immediately, Hideiye insisted on first subjugating the Korean provinces to secure supplies. However, the Japanese treated the Korean people so harshly that even peasants took up arms. This forced the Japanese to perpetually garrison the peninsula with a strong army and devote significant forces to guarding communication lines against guerilla bands.
