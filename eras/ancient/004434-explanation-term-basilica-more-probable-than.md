# Basilica

The term "basilica" has been applied to a variety of structures and legal documents throughout history, evolving from a Greek adjective meaning "royal" into a specific architectural form and a designation for imperial law.

## Etymology and Legal Usage
The word "basilica" is a Latinized version of the Greek adjective *basilike*, meaning "royal," which would have been paired with a feminine substantive such as *domus* or *stoa*. One theory suggests the term originated from the *basileios stoa* in Athens, where the court of the Areopagus assembled and the *archon basileus* conducted business. Similarly, Herod the Great constructed a grand structure along the southern edge of the Temple platform in Jerusalem called the *basileios stoa*, which featured a columned fabric with aisles, a nave, and clerestory lighting.

In a legal context, the term is used to distinguish the code of the emperor Leo. This code was originally titled *The Revision of the Ancient Laws* (*he anakatharsis ton palaion nomon*) and later *he hexekontabiblos*, reflecting its division into sixty books. Before the end of the 10th century, it became known as *ho basilikos* or *ta basilika*, elliptical forms of "the Imperial Law" (*ho basilikos nomos*) or "the Imperial Constitutions" (*ta basilika nomima*). This derivation is considered more probable than the theory that the code was named after the emperor Leo's father, Basil, as 11th and 12th-century Byzantine jurists ignored Basil's role in initiating the reforms. Furthermore, the father's name was written *basileios*, from which the adjective *basilikos* could not be easily derived according to ancient Greek linguistic rules.

## Roman Civil Architecture
In ancient Rome, a basilica was a place used for law business and the meeting of traders. The first Roman basilica was erected in 182 B.C. In municipal settings, such as the Forum of a town, the basilica served as a great hall for business and meetings. For example, the basilica at Calleva was a large hall 270 feet long and 58 feet wide, featuring two rows of Corinthian columns and a clerestory roof approximately 50 feet high. In such complexes, the rooms behind the basilica may have served as law courts, while other surrounding rooms functioned as shops.

According to the architectural schemes of Vitruvius, a basilica should be rectangular, with a length between two and three times its width. If the site required a greater length, the surplus was designated as *chalcidica* (open vestibules). The interior was divided into a central space (nave) and side aisles that were one-third the width of the center. The aisles were covered by a flat roof terrace (*contignatio*) with a parapet (*pluteum*) to hide pedestrians from the merchants below. A second row of columns, three-quarters the height of the first, stood atop this parapet, leaving spaces for light to enter the central space, which was covered by a roof called the *mediana testudo*.

## Transition to Christian Architecture
The relationship between the Roman civil basilica and the Christian church has been a subject of extensive discussion. While some once theorized that Christian churches were simply converted civil basilicas, there is no evidence for this regarding public basilicas. However, some private basilicas in palaces and mansions were granted to Christians; for instance, the basilicas of S. Maria Maggiore and S. Croce in Gerusalemme originated from the Liberian and Sessorian palaces, respectively. Additionally, the *Recognitions of Clement* mentions Theophilus of Antioch granting "the basilica of his house" for use as a church.

Most existing Christian basilicas were built from the ground up for sacred purposes. In Rome, builders often used materials from desecrated public buildings and temples, resulting in a lack of architectural unity where columns of different diameters, heights, and orders (such as Ionic, Corinthian, and Composite) were used in the same colonnade. In contrast, the basilicas of Ravenna exhibit greater harmony and design unity because they did not rely on such salvaged materials.

## Architectural Evolution and Examples
Early Christian buildings, appearing in the 2nd or 3rd century A.D., likely began as oblong interiors ending in an apse. After the time of Constantine, side aisles were added to accommodate growing numbers of faithful, giving them an appearance similar to civil basilicas. A key distinction is that early Christian basilicas in the west rarely had galleries over side aisles and were almost always dominated by a semi-dome apse terminating the central nave—a feature generally absent in normal columned civil basilicas.

Notable examples of basilican churches include:
* **The East:** The church of St Demetrius at Thessalonica, St Philip at Athens, Bosra in Arabia, Xanthus in Lycia, and the monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai.
* **North Africa:** Over 130 examples have been noted in Algeria, including a basilica adjoining the Forum at Timgad.
* **Europe:** The "Basse Oeuvre" at Beauvais (possibly Carolingian), an unaltered basilica from the time of Charles the Great at Michelstadt in Germany, and the 11th-century basilica of St Mauritius near Hildesheim. The form has also been revived in the modern basilica at Munich.
