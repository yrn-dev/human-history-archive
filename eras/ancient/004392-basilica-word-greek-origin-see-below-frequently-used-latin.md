# BASILICA

The term basilica is a word of Greek origin that was frequently employed in Latin inscriptions and literature. In its most literal sense, it denotes a large covered building designed to accommodate a significant number of people.

## Etymology and Origins
The word "basilica" is a Latinized version of the Greek adjective *basilikê*, meaning "royal." It is understood that a feminine substantive, such as *stoa* or *domus*, was originally implied alongside the adjective. 

One accredited, though unproven, theory suggests the term originated from a specific building in Athens known as the *basileios stoa*. This structure served as a place where the court of the Areopagus occasionally assembled and where the *archon basileus* conducted business. While the specific shape of this portico is unknown, the same name (*basileios stoa*) was applied to a grand structure built by Herod the Great along the southern edge of the Temple platform at Jerusalem. This later structure aligned with the Vitruvian architectural scheme, featuring a nave, aisles, and clerestory lighting. It is suggested that "basilica" may have become a specific architectural term derived from a single specimen that was accidentally named "royal," similar to how the term "labyrinth" developed.

## Function and Purpose
In the classical period, the basilica served as a covered forum. In early Greek and Italian history, political, commercial, and legal business was conducted in the open forum. As populations grew and business increased, additional accommodations became necessary. While theaters and amphitheatres handled games and performances, and markets served buyers and sellers, the basilica provided a roofed space for more important business.

Vitruvius describes the basilica as an adjunct to the forum. It was designed to provide merchants with shelter and quiet for their transactions. Additionally, the basilica functioned as a place of business and a court of law where legal tribunals were established. To facilitate these legal proceedings, the edifice sometimes included permanent fittings such as rectangular or curved platforms for the presiding magistrates.

## Architectural Form
The normal form of a basilica consisted of a rectangular space covered by a roof and surrounded by colonnades in two stories. This differed from the Greek agora, which remained open. The Vitruvian scheme specifically emphasizes a columned fabric. Evidence of the Roman basilica's form is preserved in the descriptions of Vitruvius, the ground-plans of two basilicas in Rome, and a well-preserved example at Pompeii.

## Derived Uses and Evolution
Over time, the term "basilica" expanded in a derived sense to describe any large structure, regardless of its location or primary function. Examples of these diverse applications include:
* **Private Palaces:** Used to describe a covered promenade or a hall of audience.
* **Specialized Facilities:** Used for a riding school (*basilica equestris exercitatoria*), a store or market for flowers (*basilica floscellaria*), or a store for other goods (*basilica vestiaria*).
* **Assembly Halls:** Used for halls of meeting for religious bodies.

Because of this broader application, the term naturally transitioned to describe the extensive buildings used for Christian worship during the era of Constantine and his successors. Many great Roman churches are referred to as basilicas and maintain a construction style that resembles the ancient versions of the building.
