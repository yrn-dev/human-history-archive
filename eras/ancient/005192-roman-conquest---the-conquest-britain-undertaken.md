# The Roman Conquest of Britain

The Roman conquest of Britain was a prolonged military and political process that began in the 1st century A.D., eventually establishing a province that encompassed all of England, Wales, and the Lowlands of Scotland up to the Firths of Edinburgh and Dumbarton.

## Initiation and Early Campaigns
While Julius Cæsar crossed the Straits of Dover twice in 55 and 54 B.C., he achieved no permanent conquest. The formal conquest was undertaken by Emperor Claudius in A.D. 43. This decision was driven by a general Roman policy of annexation and the specific political climate in Britain. The philo-Roman prince Cunobelin (known as Cymbeline) had been succeeded by two sons, Togodumnus and Caractacus, who were hostile to Rome. Additionally, a fugitive British prince, believed to be a Roman partisan and a victim of Cunobelin's sons, appealed for help. There was also a need to rectify a blunder made by Claudius's predecessor, Caligula.

The invasion began with Aulus Plautius landing a well-equipped army of approximately 40,000 men in Kent. The forces advanced on London, where Claudius himself arrived—the only 1st-century reigning emperor to cross the ocean. After crossing the Thames, the army moved through Essex and captured the native capital, Camulodunum (modern Colchester).

## Strategy of Expansion
From the bases at London and Colchester, the Romans employed a three-pronged strategy to subdue the island:
* **The Left Wing:** The Second Legion, led by Vespasian (the future emperor), subdued the south.
* **The Centre:** The Fourteenth and Twentieth Legions subdued the midlands.
* **The Right Wing:** The Ninth Legion advanced through the eastern part of the island.

Because the lowlands featured easy physical geography and a population that was either scanty or partly Romanized, the Romans met little obstacle. Within three or four years, the areas east of the Severn and south of the Humber were either directly annexed or placed under native client-princes as protectorates.

## Native Resistance and Roman Administration
Before the loss of their freedom, Britain was irregularly divided among thirty barbarian tribes. The most significant included the Iceni in Norfolk and Suffolk, the Silures in South Wales, the Brigantes in the North, and the Belgæ in the West. These tribes were characterized by valor and a love of freedom, but they lacked a spirit of union, often fighting singly or turning against one another. Despite the fortitude of Caractacus, the fanaticism of the Druids, and the despair of Boadicea, the steady progress of Imperial generals prevailed.

The Roman occupation of south Britain lasted from A.D. 43 to 409. During this period, Latin became the ordinary speech of the towns, and the provincial inhabitants became familiarized with the language.

## The Conquest of Caledonia
The region of North Britain, known to the Romans as Caledonia, proved more difficult to subdue. Around A.D. 83, the general Agricola invaded Caledonia, penetrating into Perthshire and fighting a decisive victory on the slopes of Mons Graupius. However, the home government recalled Agricola, and his conquests beyond the Tweed or Cheviot were abandoned.

Further attempts at annexation occurred around A.D. 140, when the district up to the Firth of Forth was annexed and the Wall of Antoninus Pius was constructed from sea to sea. This progress was temporary; following several risings, the land north of Cheviot was lost around A.D. 180-185.

## Final Frontiers and Withdrawal
In A.D. 208, Emperor Septimius Severus led an extensive punitive expedition against the northern tribes and invaded Caledonia. He focused on reconstructing Hadrian's Wall, rebuilding it entirely in stone to serve as the substantive frontier. Severus died at York in the fourth year of these operations. His successors, Severus Alexander and Caracalla (211-235), maintained this position and improved the frontier defenses. Hadrian's Wall remained the northern limit of Roman power in the extreme west for nearly two hundred years.
