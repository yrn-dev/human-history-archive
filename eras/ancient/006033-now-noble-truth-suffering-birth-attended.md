# The Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path

The teachings of the Buddha, presented as a new gospel to his brethren and ascetics, are centered on the concept of deliverance from life and its inevitable suffering. These teachings were delivered in the form of Four Noble Truths and the Noble Eightfold Path, which the Buddha claimed provided the "ambrosia of truth."

## The Nature of Suffering
The first Noble Truth concerns the reality of suffering. According to the teachings, birth is attended with pain, and suffering is inseparable from old age. Disease and death are described as painful. Furthermore, suffering arises from union with the unpleasant (or unloved) and separation from the pleasant (or loved). Any craving that remains unsatisfied is also considered painful. In summary, the conditions of individuality, referred to as the five aggregates of clinging or the fivefold clinging to the earthly, are painful.

## The Origin of Suffering
The second Noble Truth identifies the cause of this suffering as "craving thirst." This thirst for being is what causes the renewal of becomings and leads a person from birth to birth. This craving is accompanied by sensual delights and seeks satisfaction in various forms, specifically the craving for prosperity, the gratification of the senses, or the craving for a future life. It is characterized as a thirst for power, being, and pleasures.

## The Cessation of Suffering
The third Noble Truth focuses on the passing away of pain. This is achieved through the complete annihilation of desire—letting it go, expelling it, and ensuring it is given no room. The passing away of pain occurs when no passion remains and when one is emancipated from the "craving thirst." 

Once these truths are grasped, the craving for existence is rooted out and that which leads to renewed existence is destroyed, resulting in the end of birth. A disciple who possesses this way of Truth is assured of final salvation, meaning they are no longer liable to be reborn in a state of suffering, and places of woe—such as rebirth as a ghost, an animal, or in hell—are destroyed. One who has put off all hope, fear, lust, desire, and the will to exist as a sentient being reaches Nirvana, the heaven of extinction. Such a person may continue to tread the earth as a Brahman or saint, but they enjoy eternity and have quitted the transient.

## The Noble Eightfold Path
The fourth Noble Truth is the path that leads to the cessation of suffering. The Buddha discovered a "Middle Path" that avoids two extremes: the low and pagan ideal of devotion to attractions based on passions (fit for the worldly-minded), and the practice of asceticism, which is described as painful, ignoble, and unprofitable.

The Middle Path is the Noble Eightfold Path, which bestows understanding, opens the eyes, and leads to insight, peace, higher wisdom, and Nirvana. The eight components of this path are:
*   Right Views (or rectitude of faith)
*   Right Aspirations (or resolve)
*   Right Speech
*   Right Conduct (or action)
*   Right Mode of Livelihood (or living)
*   Right Effort
*   Right Mindfulness (or thought)
*   Right Rapture (or self-concentration)

## Historical Context
The Buddha presented these principles to five ascetics in the Deer-forest near Benares. Although the ascetics initially questioned how he could have won a mental victory after breaking his vows of bodily restraint, the Buddha explained that these truths were the means to end the weary path of transmigration. The teachings emphasize that participation in the outer world is not the self, but rather the means for the self to experience birth, death, emotions, change, and pain.
