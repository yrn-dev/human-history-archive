# Asia Minor, Syria, and Mesopotamia

The regions of Asia Minor, Syria, and Mesopotamia have served as central theaters for the development of early human civilization, the expansion of vast empires, and the intersection of diverse cultural and political powers.

## Early Civilizations and Material Development
Between 8000 and 6000 B.C., humans began transitioning from hunting and wandering to settled agriculture in areas where irrigation was possible. In Mesopotamia and the Nile Valley, this led to the formation of city-states. During this period, the Assyrians founded cities on the upper Tigris, while small communities grew toward civilization in the valleys of Asia Minor and along the Mediterranean shores and islands.

In more recent history, material development in Asia Minor, Syria, and Mesopotamia has been limited. Progress in this area has primarily been characterized by the promises associated with a German company's railway concession in the Euphrates Valley. While English surveyors in Palestine and beyond Jordan, as well as explorers in the regions between the Caspian and the Mediterranean, have obtained exact information, these efforts have contributed more to the elucidation of history than to the increase of material wealth or new commercial enterprises.

## Ancient Empires and Warfare
The history of these regions is marked by a thousand years of warfare between the civilizations of the Nile and Mesopotamia. Mesopotamia was ruled by Babylon, while the Syrians of Damascus and the Hittites achieved transitory predominance. The fortunes of the Assyrians of Nineveh fluctuated; at times Nineveh was a conquered city, and at other times the Assyrians ruled Babylon and attacked Egypt. The Syrians also conquered Egypt during certain phases. These conflicts involved the use of vast numbers of war chariots, as horses from Central Asia were introduced for use in war and glory.

Under the empire of Macedonia, these regions were organized into provinces. Asia Minor included Lydia, Caria, Lycia, Pamphylia, Great Phrygia, Paphlagonia, Cappadocia, and Phrygia on the Hellespont. The areas between the Taurus and Iran comprised Syria, Mesopotamia, Babylonia, Cilicia, and Susiana.

## The Parthian and Persian Eras
Following the successors of Macedon and Persia, the Parthian Empire established itself behind the Euphrates. The empire was defended by parching deserts that acted as an impenetrable barrier. Major cities of this era included Ctesiphon, Seleucia, Hatra, Nisibin, and Harran. These townships were supported by the cereal wealth of Mesopotamia, which was watered by ancient canals. While the agricultural population remained largely disconnected from high culture, the cultured citizens of Seleucia spoke Greek and criticized the tragedies and philosophies of Athens.

In a later period, the Turkish empire under Malek Shah experienced a period of unity that ended upon his death. This led to civil wars and the eventual separation of the Persian dynasty into several branches. The house of Seljuk saw the rise of three younger dynasties: those of Kerman, Syria, and Roum. The dynasty of Syria expelled the Arabian princes of Damascus and Aleppo, while the dynasty of Roum invaded the Roman provinces of Asia Minor. During this time, the sultan of Persia maintained supreme authority, receiving tribute and obedience from the emirs and Atabeks of Mesopotamia and Syria.

## Geography and Regional Divisions
Geographically, Mesopotamia is situated between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, with the city of Babylon located on the Euphrates and Nineveh on the Tigris. Armenia is separated from Mesopotamia by Mount Taurus and from Assyria by Mount Niphates. 

The borders of Asia are often viewed as arbitrary; during Greek and Turkish times, Asia Minor was more closely connected to the shores of Europe than to the lands to its east. Some perspectives suggest that Palestine and Asia Minor should be viewed as interacting with the Mediterranean countries rather than being strictly categorized as Asiatic.

## Modern Political Mandates
Following the Paris Conference and the creation of the League of Nations, the regions were subject to "mandates" that some viewed as camouflaged annexations. Under these arrangements, Britain was to have extensive mandates in Mesopotamia, while France was assigned mandates in Syria. During this period, strategic frontiers became a point of contention among Great Powers, exemplified by Greece landing troops in Asia Minor.
