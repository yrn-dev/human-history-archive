# A Description of Galilee, Samaria, and Judea

The regions of Galilee, Samaria, and Judea comprise a significant portion of the territory formerly subject to Herod. These areas have been characterized by their geography, their administrative divisions under Roman influence, and their varying levels of agricultural productivity and population.

## Galilee
Galilee is divided into two regions: Upper Galilee and Lower Galilee. The territory is encompassed by Syria and Phoenicia. Its northern boundaries are defined by the country of the Tyrians and the city of Tyre. To the west, it is bounded by Carmel—a mountain that previously belonged to the Galileans but later passed to the Tyrians—and the borders of the territory of Ptolemais. To the south, it is bordered by Scythopolis and Samaria, extending as far as the river Jordan. Its eastern borders include Ganlonitis, the kingdom of Agrippa, and Gadaris and Hippeae.

Lower Galilee extends in length from Zabulon to Tiberias, with Ptolemais as its maritime neighbor. Its breadth reaches from Bersabe to a village called Xaloth in the great plain. Upper Galilee begins its breadth at Bersabe and extends to the village of Baca, which serves as the divide from the land of the Tyrians. In length, it extends from Meloth to Thella, a village located near the Jordan.

Within this region, Sepphoris is noted as the largest city in Galilee, situated in a naturally strong location. Historically, some viewed Galilee as less happy and well-cultivated than Judea. Administratively, Galilee and Perea were once placed under the tetrarchy of Antipas, providing an annual tribute of two hundred talents.

## Samaria
Samaria is situated between the regions of Galilee and Judea. It begins at a village located in the great plain known as Ginea and concludes at the Acrabbene toparchy. The landscape of Samaria is characterized by hills and valleys, mirroring the nature of Judea.

The region is highly fruitful and moist enough for agriculture, possessing an abundance of both wild and cultivated autumnal fruit and trees. While Samaria is not naturally watered by many rivers, it receives ample rain-water, and its existing rivers are noted for having exceedingly sweet waters. Due to the quality of the grass, cattle in Samaria yield more milk than in other locations. Consequently, the region is very densely populated.

In terms of administration, Samaria was part of the ethnarchy of Archelaus. Because the inhabitants of Samaria did not join in a particular revolt with the rest of the multitude, Caesar granted them a mitigation of one quarter of their tribute.

## Judea
Judea, like Samaria, is a land of hills and valleys that is fruitful and moist, supporting agriculture and dense populations. Along with Samaria and Idumea, Judea was part of the ethnarchy of Archelaus. The cities of Jerusalem, Joppa, Sebaste, and Strato's Tower were among those that paid tribute to Archelaus.

The region has been the site of significant conflict and movement. During the siege of Jerusalem, vast numbers of people from Judea, Galilee, Samaria, Perea, and other remoter regions traveled to the city for the passover.

## Regional Interactions and Administration
The relationship between these regions has occasionally been marked by violence. One such instance occurred at a village called Geman in the great plain of Samaria, where a conflict erupted between Galileans and Samaritans after a Galilean was slain. This led to a violent march toward Samaria by a multitude from Jerusalem, led by Alexander and Eleazar the son of Dineus, who attacked the neighborhood of the Acrabatene toparchy, burning villages and killing inhabitants regardless of age.

Administratively, the Roman Empire exerted control through various officials. Vespasian, a Roman general, marched to Ptolemais with his army from Antioch (the metropolis of Syria). In Galilee, the citizens of Sepphoris sought peace with the Romans, receiving a Roman garrison and promising to assist Vespasian against their own countrymen. Other officials included Cumanus and Felix; while some accounts suggest Felix was once a procurator over Samaria only, other records and the observations of Josephus suggest he may have been a subordinate judge under Cumanus before becoming a procurator.
