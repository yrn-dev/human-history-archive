# Chondropterygii (Cartilaginous Fishes)

The Chondropterygii, also referred to as Selachii, comprise a group of cartilaginous fishes that include sharks, skates, and chimaeras. These animals are characterized by a well-developed skull that lacks membrane bones. Unlike other fish groups, they possess paired nasal apertures and both median and paired fins. A distinguishing feature of the males in this group is the presence of prehensile organs, known as claspers, located on the ventrals.

## Anatomy and Physiology
The skeletal structure of the Selachians is restricted to placoid elements. In these fishes, the bony skeleton is absent, and the skull is not composed of membrane bones. Aristotle noted that cartilaginous fishes lack an operculum over their branchiae. He further observed that the majority of these fishes lack pyloric caeca near the stomach.

The teeth of Chondropterygii are modified placoid elements. While many fishes have simple conical teeth, larger sharks, such as those of the genus *Carcharodon*, possess teeth that are flattened into serrated, trenchant blades. In contrast, certain rays, such as *Myliobatis*, have teeth that form flattened plates used for crushing the shells of mollusks.

Regarding reproduction, Aristotle categorized cartilaginous fishes as viviparous, with the exception of the sea-devil. However, some species, such as the dog-fish, are oviparous. In these cases, eggs are enclosed in semi-transparent horny cases called "mermaids' purses," which use tendril-like prolongations to moor themselves to fixed objects or sea-weed near the shore.

## Diversity and Species
The group includes a wide variety of forms, including true sharks, hammer-headed sharks, and dog-fish. The dog-fish is a name applied to several smaller shark species, such as the small-spotted dog-fish (*Scyllium canicula*) and the large-spotted or nurse hound (*Scyllium catulus*), both of which are known as ground-sharks. These species feed primarily on Crustacea and smaller fishes. Another prominent species is the picked dog-fish (*Acanthias vulgaris*), which is the most abundant British shark. It is characterized by a round, tapering body and two dorsal fins, each featuring a sharp, curved spine on the anterior edge.

## Distribution and Habitat
Many Selachians are pelagic fishes, meaning they are rapid swimmers capable of traveling for weeks without rest. This allows them to pass from one ocean to another, resulting in a distribution that is nearly world-wide. While most numerous between the tropics, they occasionally wander far north and south of their habitual ranges.

In specific regions, such as the seas of south Asia, true sharks and hammer-headed sharks are numerous. The dog-fish is also present, with one species extending from the Indian seas to the Cape of Good Hope.

## Evolutionary History
Fishes are considered the most archaic of existing craniates, providing evidence for the evolutionary history of morphological features in higher vertebrates. The earliest known backboned animals and first Vertebrata appeared in the Silurian division of Palæozoic rocks, estimated by some geologists to be five hundred million years old.

During the Devonian system, known as the "Age of Fishes," fishes allied to modern sharks and sturgeons were prevalent. These creatures inhabited the waters by browsing among seaweeds and preying upon one another. While most were only two or three feet long, exceptional forms reached lengths of twenty feet.

The evolution of the tail-fin within the shark group shows significant variety. While the majority possess heterocercal tails, other types exist, including the protocercal tail found in the living *Chlamydoselachus* and the fossil Pleuracanthids, as well as the practically homocercal tail of the ancient *Cladoselache*.
