# Almagest

The *Almagest* (also known as the *Great Syntaxis*) is a foundational work of astronomy authored by Ptolemy. It is described as the consummation of Greek astronomy and the centerpiece of the Ptolemaic system. This system was geometrically defensible and harmonized with appearances, appealing to the learned through its ingenuity and to the ignorant through its conformity to crude common sense.

## Astronomical Observations and Records
Ptolemy placed a high value on the comparison of astronomical observations taken at distant intervals. In the *Almagest*, he recorded the earliest Chaldaean astronomical observation that was both precise and of a date sufficient for scientific use: a lunar eclipse occurring on March 19, 721 B.C. This event took place in the 27th year of the era of Nabonassar. The text implies that Ptolemy had no access to observations more ancient than the era of Nabonassar; had he known of older observations meeting these scientific conditions, he would not have omitted them.

## Historical Chronologies
The *Almagest* served as a source for recording the dynasties of the Chaldaeans. Specifically, the 7th and 8th dynasties recorded by Ptolemy have proven to be faithfully recorded. These records include the following sequence of rulers and durations:

1. Nabonassar (747 B.C.): 14 years
2. Nadios: 2 years
3. Khinziros and Poros (Pul): 5 years
4. Ilulaeos: 5 years
5. Mardokempados (Merodach-Baladan): 12 years
6. Arkeanos (Sargon): 5 years
7. Interregnum: 2 years
8. Hagisa: 1 month
9. Belibos (702 B.C.): 3 years
10. Assaranadios (Assur-nadin-sum): 6 years
11. Regebelos: 1 year
12. Mesesimordakos: 4 years
13. Interregnum: 8 years
14. Asaridinos (Esar-haddon): 13 years
15. Saosdukhinos (Savul-sum-yukin): 20 years
16. Sinladanos (Assur-bani-pal): 22 years

## Transmission and Arabic Influence
Following the era of Ptolemy, the work had no successor, only commentators, including Hypatia (370-415) and her father, Theon of Alexandria (fl. A.D. 400). After the capture of Alexandria by Omar in 641, the scientific light of the city became extinct. However, the *Almagest* was later rekindled on the banks of the Tigris. 

Around the year 800, the first Arabic translation of the *Almagest* was produced by order of Harun al-Rashid. This led to further scientific developments in Baghdad, where Caliph al-Mamun established a grand observatory in 829. Several scholars utilized Ptolemy's work in this environment:
* Abd-ar-rahman al-Suf (903-986) revised Ptolemy's catalogue first hand.
* Abulwefa (939-998), a Persian native, conducted continuous planetary observations.

## Influence on Later Greek Astronomy
The revival of mathematics and astronomy among the Greeks was primarily driven by Perso-Arabian influence, with Ptolemy's *Great Syntaxis* operating under the oriental guise of the *Almagest*. While Arabia was a conduit, Persia was the most important direct source of this intellectual loan. 

By the close of the 13th century, the Greeks became acquainted with Persian astronomy. At the start of the 14th century, Isaac Argyrus and Georgius Chrysococca wrote astronomical treatises based on Persian works. Eventually, Byzantine scholars such as Nicephorus Gregoras and Theodorus Metochites returned to the original Greek sources.
