# Balaam and Laban

## The Identity of Balaam
In historical and critical analysis, there have been various attempts to identify the figure of Balaam. A Rabbinical identification (Yalkut) associates Balaam with Laban, the father-in-law of Jacob. This identification has also been revived by a modern critic, though from a different standpoint.

Other traditions, specifically those of the Mahommedans, present Balaam as one of the Anakim, the giants of Palestine. According to these accounts, Balaam read the books of Abraham, which provided him with the name Yahweh. Through this name, he was able to predict the future and receive whatever he requested from God. Some conjectures suggest that Balaam is identical to the Arabic wise man known as Luqman; this theory is based on the similarity of their fathers' names and the fact that "Luqman" means "swallower" or "devourer," a meaning that could be derived from Balaam via popular etymology.

Depending on the source, Balaam's origins vary:
* In one source of J, he is described as an Edomite.
* In another source of J, he is an Ammonite.
* In E, he is presented as an Aramaean or a native of the south of Judah.
* In the tradition of the Priestly Code, he is likely a Midianite.

Because these groups are either part of the Hebrew stock or closely connected to it, it is concluded that Balaam was an ancient figure of traditions common to all Hebrews and their allies before being appropriated by individual tribes.

## Laban and His Relation to Jacob
Laban was a resident of Haran in Mesopotamia and the brother of Rebeka, making him the uncle of Jacob. Jacob traveled to Haran to seek him out after fleeing from his twin brother, Esau, who had sought to kill him over disputes regarding the kingdom and blessings given by their father, Isaac.

Upon Jacob's arrival, Laban expressed great joy and offered him assistance. Jacob requested the hand of Laban's daughter, Rachel, in marriage. Laban agreed, provided Jacob stay with him for a time, as Laban regretted a previous alliance he had made by marrying his sister in the land of the Canaanites. Jacob agreed to serve Laban for seven years to demonstrate his virtue. However, on the wedding night, Laban secretly placed his elder daughter, Lea—who was not of a comely countenance—into Jacob's bed. When Jacob discovered the deception the following morning, Laban asked for pardon, claiming he was forced by necessity and assuring Jacob that he could still marry Rachel.

## Jacob's Service and Departure
Jacob served as the head shepherd of Laban's flocks for a total of twenty years. Eventually, Jacob desired to return home with his wives and children. When Laban refused to grant him leave, Jacob departed secretly, taking his wives, children, handmaids, and half of the cattle.

Rachel, the daughter of Laban, took her father's sacred images (gods) with her during the flight. She did this so that if they were pursued and captured by her father, she could use the images to obtain his pardon, despite Jacob having taught her to despise such worship.

## The Conflict and League at Gilead
Laban discovered the departure after one day and pursued Jacob with a band of men, overtaking them on the seventh day. Before Laban could act in wrath, God appeared to him in a dream, warning him to treat his son-in-law and daughters peaceably and to make a league with Jacob.

The following day, Laban accused Jacob of treating him as an enemy by driving away his cattle and persuading his daughters to flee. He specifically lamented the theft of the sacred paternal images worshipped by his forefathers. Jacob defended himself, stating that the love of one's native country is natural to all men.

Regarding the images, Laban searched for them, but Rachel hid them in her camel's saddle and claimed that her natural purgation prevented her from rising, leading Laban to stop the search. Ultimately, Laban and Jacob formed a league bound by oaths, promising that Laban would bear no malice and that Jacob would love Laban's daughters. They confirmed this league by erecting a pillar in the form of an altar on a hill, which became known as Gilead. After this feast, Laban returned to his home.

## Aftermath of the Departure
Following his departure from Laban, Jacob later discovered that Rachel had stolen the gods of Laban. Unaware of this until that moment, Jacob hid the images in the earth under an oak tree in Shechem while purifying his tents and offering sacrifices.
