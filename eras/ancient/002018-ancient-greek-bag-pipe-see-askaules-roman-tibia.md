# The Ancient Greek Bag-pipe and Roman Tibia Utricularis

The ancient Greek bag-pipe and the Roman *tibia utricularis* are classified as reed instruments inflated by the mouth. While they are grouped with other bag-pipe varieties, such as the Calabrian bag-pipe, it is not certain whether these specific ancient versions utilized drones.

## Terminology and Literary Evidence
The Greek term associated with the bag-piper is *askaules* (derived from *askos*, meaning bag, and *aulos*, meaning pipe), although there is no documentary authority to confirm its use. The term *askaulos*, which would naturally denote the bag-pipe itself, does not appear in the writings of classical Greek authors. Furthermore, the instrument is notably absent from the lists of pipes compiled by Athenaeus and Pollux.

Evidence for the instrument's existence in Greece is limited. A single passage from Dio Chrysostom, who flourished around A.D. 100, provides an unmistakable description of a performer skilled in playing a pipe with the mouth using a bag placed under the arm-pits. J.J. Reiske suggests that *askaulaes* was the Greek word for the piper. This is partially corroborated by the use of *ascaules* in Martial x. 3. 8 as a translation for the Latin *utricularius*. Because of this lack of literary presence among highly cultured Greeks, it is assumed that the bag-pipe remained a rustic instrument, used primarily by peasants and shepherds.

## Origins and Distribution
It is believed that the Greeks received some form of bag-pipe from either Chaldaea or Egypt, where it existed in the form of the *as-it*. While no bag-pipes are represented on known Egyptian or Assyrian monuments, the instrument can be traced through ancient Persia. Terracotta figures from the 8th century B.C. discovered at a *tell* (mound) in Susa depict musicians playing bag-pipes; these figures show a bag under the arm and a curved chaunter, though the insufflation tube is absent (though a round hole suggests one may have existed).

In the Roman context, the instrument is referred to as the *tibia utricularis*. Following the downfall of the Roman Empire, the *tibia utricularis* disappeared from the record. The bag-pipe likely persisted for a time among jugglers, actors, and itinerant musicians before reappearing in the early middle ages under the name *chorus*.

## Theoretical Connections and Debates
Some scholars have attempted to link the bag-pipe to the book of Daniel (iii. 5 and 15), suggesting that the term *sump[=o]ny[=a]* signifies a kind of bag-pipe. This theory posits that the Aramaic word is a loan-word from the Greek *sumphonia*. However, this argument is considered weak for several reasons:
* The date of the book of Daniel is controversial.
* The word *sump[=o]ny[=a]* may be a late interpolation.
* In verse 10, the term *sipp[=o]ny[=a]* is used, suggesting a derivation from the Greek *siphon* (pipe or tube).
* There is little evidence that *sumphonia*—which originally meant "a concord of sound"—was used to describe a specific instrument until the 2nd century B.C., and even then, the evidence is slight.

Curiously, in the middle ages, the bag-pipe was known in Spain and Italy—regions through which Eastern culture entered Europe—as *sampogna* or *zampogna*, terms that strongly recall the Chaldaean *sump[=o]ny[=a]*.

## Technical Characteristics
As a complex reed instrument, the bag-pipe serves as a link between the primitive organ and the *syrinx* by utilizing a reservoir for wind supply. This allows the performer to regulate sound-producing pressure using their arm. The general construction consists of an air-tight leather bag with three to five apertures containing stocks. These stocks hold three types of pipes:
1. A blow-pipe (insufflation tube) for filling the bag.
2. A chaunter (melody-pipe) with a double-reed and lateral holes.
3. Drones, which are cylindrical pipes that produce a single fixed note.

In the case of the Greeks and Romans, the practice of taking the reed directly into the mouth was common, which raises questions regarding the *raison d'etre* of early bag-pipes that lacked drones, as inflating a bag for a single chaunter is less satisfactory than direct blowing.
