# State of Society and Manners as Exhibited in Grecian Legend

The study of Grecian legend provides a primary starting point for understanding the history of the Greek people. While the specific historical facts within these legends—such as whether figures like Odysseus, Agamemnôn, or Hêraklês were real persons—may be impossible to determine, the legends themselves serve as a faithful mirror of the general state of early Grecian society. These fictions were conceived and assembled in conformity with the social phenomena and prevalent manners of the era in which the describers and their audiences lived.

## Governance and Social Structure
In the conceptions of Grecian legend, both human and divine societies are characterized by the personal rule of a legitimate sovereign. The king serves as the grand personal mover of heroic society. This authority is not derived from the special appointment of subjects, although the sovereign governs with their full consent. 

The influence of the prince is so pervasive that the race or nation is effectively absorbed into the individual. Eponymous persons act not only as princes but as representative unities and fathers, serving as the equivalent of the aggregate group to which they give their name.

This structure is mirrored in the divine realm. Zeus, the supreme god of Olympus, governs the agora of the gods in a manner similar to a human king. While he may hear dissentient opinions, be worried into compliance by Hêrê, or be wheedled by Aphroditê, his determinations remain conclusive, subject only to the overruling interference of the Fates (Mœræ).

## Public Life and the Agora
In early Grecian society, the agora served a specific function. While the feelings of assembled multitudes commanded some attention, the agora functioned primarily as a means of publicity, particularly in judicial matters more so than in political ones. This underscores the centrality of the king's personal power over collective democratic action in the legendary age.

## Religious Beliefs and Divine Character
The gods of Grecian legend are conceived as an enlarged human type. A recurring characteristic of these deities is their jealousy regarding the withholding of sacrifices and honors, or the presumption of mortals who contend with them. For example, the goddess Artemis sent the Kalydônian boar as a visitation upon Œneus because he honored other gods while omitting to sacrifice to her.

The legends also illustrate the intersection of religious observance and social consequence. In the Argeian territory, the daughters of Prœtos were smitten with leprosy and madness. According to Hesiod, this was due to their refusal to participate in Bacchic rites; other accounts suggest it was because they treated the equipment and statue of Hêrê with scorn. The cure for this malady required the intervention of Melampus, a renowned prophet and leech, who used prayer, sacrifice, or the ecstatic dances and shouting associated with the worship of Dionysos to appease the divine wrath.

## Individualism and the State
There is a common assertion that ancient societies sacrificed the individual to the state, a view that is preëminently true of Sparta and the ideal societies described by Aristotle and Plato. However, this is pointedly untrue of the Athenian democracy. In Athens, while patriotism was paramount, it did not absorb all activity. Athenian society exhibited a diversity of private pursuits, an unrestrained play of fancy, and a reciprocity of cheerful indulgence between individuals, protecting individual agency alongside public laws.

## Chronology and Historical Interpretation
Ancient chronologists, such as Hellanikus and Damastês, attempted to fix the date of the capture of Troy down to the exact hour, day, and month. Modern analysis, however, suggests these dates were often based on conjecture. For instance, Eratosthenês is thought by some to have chosen a middle point between the longer and shorter computations of his predecessors.

Because of these inconsistencies, the value of the legends lies not in their specific chronological facts, but in the rational general views they provide regarding the steps and causes by which early Grecian society attained its later positions.
