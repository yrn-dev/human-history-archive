# The History Of The Spurious Alexander and the Banishment of Archelaus

## The Spurious Alexander
Historical records describe a figure known as the spurious Alexander, a brother to Alexander. This individual was characterized as being of spurious birth and deficient in his understanding. Due to his weak intellect, the Persian governor of Caria sought him as a son-in-law. This prospect created suspicion for Alexander, who feared that Philip intended for Aridæus to receive the crown through such a noble match and the resulting support.

In response to these suspicions, Alexander dispatched a player named Thessalus to Caria to request that the governor overlook Aridæus and instead form an alliance with the lawful heir to the crown. While the governor, Pixodarus, was pleased with this proposal, Philip reacted with anger upon learning of it. Philip confronted Alexander in his apartment, accompanied by Philotas (the son of Parmenio), and reproached Alexander for his meanness of spirit and degeneracy in considering a marriage alliance with a man of Caria, whom Philip characterized as a slave of a barbarian king. Consequently, Philip ordered the Corinthians to send Thessalus to him in chains and banished several of the prince's companions, including Niarchus, Harpalus, Phrygius, and Ptolemy, though Alexander later recalled them and treated them with distinction.

## The Ethnarchy of Archelaus
Following the death of Herod, Archelaus entered upon his ethnarchy in Judea. During his rule, he took several administrative and personal actions. He removed Joazar, the son of Boethus, from the high priesthood on accusations of assisting the seditious, replacing him with Joazar's brother, Eleazar. However, Eleazar's tenure was short, as he was eventually replaced by Jesus, the son of Sie.

Archelaus engaged in significant building projects, including the magnificent rebuilding of the royal palace at Jericho. To support his plantings of palm trees in the plain, he diverted half of the water that previously supplied the village of Neara. He also established a village which he named Archelais.

## Marriage to Glaphyra
Archelaus committed a transgression of the law of the fathers by marrying Glaphyra, the daughter of Archelaus of Cappadocia. Glaphyra had previously been the wife of Alexander (the brother of Archelaus and son of Herod), with whom she had three children. Marrying a brother's wife was considered detestable among the Jews.

Glaphyra's history included a marriage to Juba, the king of Libya; following Juba's death, she returned to her father's home as a widow. Archelaus the ethnarch fell deeply in love with her, leading him to divorce his wife, Mariamne, to marry Glaphyra. Shortly after arriving in Judea, Glaphyra experienced a dream in which the deceased Alexander appeared to her. In the dream, Alexander reproached her for her impudence in choosing his brother as a third husband after her marriage to the king of Libya. He warned her that he would soon have her again, regardless of her will. Glaphyra died two days after recounting this dream.

## The Banishment of Archelaus
The government of Archelaus was marked by instability and perceived tyranny. In the tenth year of his rule, the principal men of Samaria and Judea, along with Archelaus's own brothers, accused him before Cæsar. The accusers cited his barbarous and tyrannical treatment of the people and his failure to adhere to Cæsar's commands to behave with moderation.

Cæsar, angered by these reports, summoned Archelaus's steward (also named Archelaus) and ordered that the ethnarch be brought to Rome immediately. Upon his arrival in Rome, Cæsar heard the testimonies of the accusers and the reply of Archelaus. As a result, Cæsar banished Archelaus to Vienna, a city in Gaul, and seized his money.

## Related Figures and Events
The period surrounding these events involved various political intrigues. Eurycles, described as a "pest" of the family, had forged vile accusations against Herod's sons. Although Herod found the proofs weak, he kept his sons in custody and rewarded Eurycles with fifty talents. Eurycles subsequently fled to Cappadocia, where he obtained money from Archelaus by falsely claiming he had reconciled Herod to Alexander. Eurycles later moved to Greece, where he was twice accused before Cæsar for plundering cities and inciting sedition in Achaia, eventually resulting in his own banishment.

Additionally, an ambassador from Cappadocia named Melas, a principal ruler under Archelaus, visited Herod. During this time, Herod questioned Alexander (who was in bonds) regarding a fight and plans to retire. Alexander denied any mischievous designs against his father and suggested that Tyrannus and Jucundus should have been examined more strictly, though they had been killed by Antipater.
