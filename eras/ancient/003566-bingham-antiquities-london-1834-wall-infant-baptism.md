# Bingham's Antiquities and W. Wall, On Infant Baptism

Within the historical study of early Christian rites and liturgical practices, the works of Joseph Bingham and W. Wall are cited as significant modern authorities. Bingham's *Antiquities* (London, 1834) and Wall's *On Infant Baptism* (London, 1707) are categorized among the most exhaustive literature regarding the sacrament of baptism.

## Joseph Bingham's Antiquities
Joseph Bingham's *Antiquities* (also referred to as *Christian Antiquities*) is utilized as a primary reference for the theory and practice of baptism in antiquity. His work is specifically noted for its detailed examinations in the tenth and eleventh books.

Bingham's research provides insights into the distinctions between different parts of divine service. He is cited as a learned source for explaining the "mysterious veil" and the distinction between the *missa fidelium* (the service for the faithful) and the *missa catechumenorum* (the service for the catechumens). Because of his Protestant perspective, his analysis of these public and secret divisions of service is presented as a reliable alternative to the accounts provided by Papist sources.

Additionally, Bingham's work addresses the specific rites associated with the creation of a catechumen. He notes that a particular rite was always employed in this process. This is illustrated by the case of Constantine, who received this rite for the first time immediately prior to his death and baptism. Beyond the sacrament of baptism, Bingham's *Antiquities* (specifically book xx) is listed as an authority regarding the Epiphany.

## W. Wall and the Study of Baptism
W. Wall's *On Infant Baptism*, published in London in 1707, is recognized as one of the most exhaustive modern works on the subject. It stands alongside Bingham's *Antiquities* as a key text for those researching the historical administration of the sacrament.

The broader context of the debate surrounding infant baptism is reflected in the history of English Baptist groups. For example, both the General Baptists (who were Arminian due to Mennonite Anabaptist influence) and the Particular Baptists (who were Calvinist and originated from the Independents) utterly rejected the baptism of infants. These groups focused their doctrinal disputes on three primary questions: the proper mode of baptism, the proper subjects for the rite, and the proper administrator.

## Ancient Baptismal Customs
The archive's sources provide further context on the ancient customs that Bingham and Wall examine. In antiquity, the sacrament of baptism—including when it was administered to infants—was immediately followed by holy communion and confirmation, a practice from which modern churches have materially departed.

Early rites in Syrian, Armenian, and Greek traditions included a service of sealing a child on its eighth day or giving the child a Christian name. During this rite, a priest would bless the infant and, except in Armenia, seal the child's forehead with the sign of the cross. This was accompanied by a prayer that the child enter the holy Catholic church at the proper time or in due season, serving as an analogue to the circumcision of Christ.

Furthermore, the practice of trine immersion is noted as being older than various rival explanations. This custom is believed to have been adopted from a pagan medium, as threefold lustrations were common in Greek and Latin antiquity, as evidenced in the writings of Horace, Ovid, Persius, and Virgil.
