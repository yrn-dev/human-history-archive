# Volksetymologie in Genesis xi. 9

The term *Volksetymologie* (folk etymology) refers to the popular or mistaken derivation of a word's origin. A notable instance of this is found in Genesis xi. 9 regarding the name of the city of Babylon.

## The Etymology of Babylon
The city of Babylon, located on the left bank of the Euphrates approximately 70 miles south of Bagdad, has been known by several names. The name "Babylon" is the Greek form of *Babel* or *Bab-ili*, which translates from Semitic as "the gate of the god." This was a translation of the original Sumerian name, *Ka-dimirra*. The deity referenced was likely Marduk (also known as Merodach), the city's divine patron.

Different historical sources provide varying interpretations of the city's name:
* **Assyrian Influence:** In an inscription by the Kassite conqueror Gaddas, the name appears as *Ba-ba-lam*, which seems to derive from the Assyrian word *babalu*, meaning "to bring."
* **Biblical Folk Etymology:** In Genesis xi. 9, a foreign *Volksetymologie* is present, deriving the name from *balbal*, meaning "to confound."

## Other Names and Titles of the City
Beyond *Bab-ili*, the city was known by several other designations:
* **Din-tir:** One of the oldest names, favored by poets, meaning "the life of the forest" in Sumerian, though a native lexicon translates it as "seat of life."
* **Su-anna:** A second name that may have originally referred to a specific quarter or separate village. In later inscriptions, this is often represented ideographically as *E-ki*, though its meaning and pronunciation are uncertain.
* **Uru-azagga:** A title meaning "the holy city," applied to Babylon and other cities within Babylonia.

The name *Ka-dimirra* (and the Semitic *Bab-ili*) likely originally referred to the *E-Saggila*, the "house of the lofty head." This was the temple dedicated to Bel-Merodach. The temple was founded in pre-Semitic times, and the city of Babylon grew around it. Because Merodach was the son of Ea, the culture god of Eridu, it is possible that Babylon began as a colony of Eridu.

## The Narrative of the Tower of Babel
The story associated with the "confounding" of languages in Genesis is believed by some to have been suggested by the temple towers of Babylon, specifically the *E-Saggila*. While the origin of the story has not been found within Babylonia itself, the event was eventually localized there.

The narrative presents the variety of languages and the dispersion of mankind as a curse. Some scholars suggest this may have stemmed from an ancient North Semitic myth. The account in Genesis is described as anthropomorphic and polytheistic. Other traditions regarding the tower's fall vary:
* **Cornelius Alexander and Abydenus:** These sources state the tower was overthrown by winds.
* **Yaqut and the Lisan el-'Arab:** These sources claim mankind was swept into a plain called "Babil" by winds and subsequently scattered in the same manner.

Similar myths regarding the confounding of language and the building of great structures to reach heaven appear in other cultures, including a tradition in Central America involving Xelhua and the pyramid of Cholula, as well as stories among the Africans of Lake Ngami, the Mongolian Tharus in northern India, and an Estonian myth known as "the Cooking of Languages."
