# Bactria

Bactria (also known as *Bactriana*) was an ancient region located between the Oxus (Amu Darya) river and the Hindu Kush (Paropamisus) mountain range. Its capital was the city of Bactra, known today as Balkh. The region is characterized by a moderate climate and fertile land with abundant water, though it is surrounded and limited by the Turanian desert.

## Early History and Tradition
Bactria was the home of one of the Iranian tribes. It is believed that the Aryans lived in the eastern part of Iran as a single people before separating into Iranians and Indians. While some have previously suggested that Bactria was the cradle of the Indo-European race, linguists now acknowledge this to be incorrect, stating that the Aryans likely originated in Europe.

In old times, the Bactrians and their neighbors were ruled by petty local kings rather than a single great empire. One such ruler was Vishtaspa, who served as the protector of Zoroaster. It is believed that Zoroaster preached and gained his first adherents in these regions, and his religion subsequently spread to the western parts of Iran. Although the sacred language of the Avesta has often been called "old Bactrian," scholars have abandoned this term.

Iranian traditions, including the *Avesta* and Firdousi's *Shahnama*, localize various myths and heroes in eastern Iran. These stories often depict kings of Iran fighting Turanians, which some authors have interpreted as evidence of an ancient, extensive Bactrian empire. However, this historical interpretation is considered a late reflex of the Achaemenid and Sassanid empires; no such great Bactrian empire actually existed. Additionally, accounts by Ctesias regarding a war between the king of Bactria and Ninus and Semiram are regarded as fantastic fiction, as Assyrian inscriptions show the Assyrians never entered eastern Iran.

## Persian and Macedonian Rule
Bactria was subjugated by Cyrus and subsequently became one of the satrapies of the Persian empire, though it is unknown if it was previously part of the Median empire. Following the defeat of Darius III by Alexander, the satrap of Bactria, Bessus, attempted to organize national resistance. Alexander conquered Bactria without much difficulty, though he faced stronger resistance further north in Sogdiana.

Under the Macedonian empire, Bactria became a province and eventually fell under the rule of Seleucus, the king of Asia. Seleucus I and his son Antiochus I established numerous Greek towns in eastern Iran, leading to a period where the Greek language was dominant.

## The Graeco-Bactrian Kingdom
Around 255 B.C., Diodotus, the satrap of Bactria, took advantage of the difficulties facing the Seleucid kings and attacks by Ptolemy II to declare independence and conquer Sogdiana, founding the Graeco-Bactrian kingdom. 

Following the defeat of Antiochus III by the Romans in 190 B.C., the Bactrian king Euthydemus and his son Demetrius crossed the Hindu Kush to begin the conquest of the Indus valley and eastern Iran. While they briefly wielded great power, the empire was soon destabilized by internal dissensions and usurpations. For example, while Demetrius was advancing into India, his general Eucratides seized the throne of Bactria. This pattern of usurpers proclaiming themselves kings and fighting one another undermined Greek dominance. During this decline, the kings abandoned the Attic standard of coinage in favor of a native standard, and the native language began to be used alongside Greek.

## Later Influence and Transition
In the second century B.C., Greek adventurers from Bactria raided into North India to establish short-lived kingdoms. These Greek-ruled Bactrian states became increasingly Orientalized over time. The movements of various tribes later facilitated the infiltration of Graeco-Bactrian civilization into India and helped maintain communications between the West and China.

Eventually, the region saw the southward movement of the Bactrians, Parthians, and Scythians. The Parthian dynasty of the Arsacids eventually rose to rule the east, while the Roman Empire ruled the west, with the two powers shifting boundaries across Armenia and Syria.
