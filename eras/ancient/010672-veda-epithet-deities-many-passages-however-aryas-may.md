# Arya as an Epithet of Deities

In the study of early Sanskrit writings, specifically within the *Rig Veda*, the term *arya* appears in a distinct capacity as an epithet used to describe deities. This usage is distinguished from the broader application of the word as a national or linguistic identifier.

## Linguistic Distinctions in Sanskrit
Within the Sanskrit language, scholars distinguish between two primary forms of the word. The first is *arya*, which serves as the origin of the term "Aryan." The second is *arya*, which is frequently employed in the *Rig Veda* as an epithet for the gods. 

However, linguistic analysis suggests further complexity. In various passages, the term *aryas* may be interpreted as the genitive form of *ari*. In this specific context, the word is explained as meaning "pious," "devoted," or "active."

## The Broader Use of Arya and Aryan
Beyond its use as a divine epithet, the term has been applied in several different ways by philologists and religious leaders:

*   **National and Linguistic Identity:** From its earliest occurrences, the word was used as a national name in Persia, Bactria, and India (appearing as *arya* in Sanskrit, *airya* in Zend, and *ariya* in Old Persian). Max Muller utilized "Aryas" as a general term for speakers of Indo-European (or Indo-Germanic) languages, regardless of their blood, color, or anatomical characteristics. In this sense, the term refers strictly to those who speak an Aryan language.
*   **The Arya Samaj:** In the 19th century, Dayanand Saraswati, a Guzerati Brahman, founded a reforming Hindu religious association called the Arya Samaj. He specifically chose the epithet "Arya" for the organization because he considered it more dignified than the term "Hindu," which he viewed as slightly contemptuous.

## Related Vedic Contexts
The *Rig Veda*, where *arya* appears as a divine epithet, is a central text in early Indian literature. According to Professor E.W. Hopkins, the text was composed in the district around Umballa, west of the great rivers. The *Rig Veda* features various prominent divinities, including Agni, Soma, Indra, and the Asvins. The Asvins are twin deities of light, described as honey-hued, bright lords of lustre, and physicians who protect the old and the weak.

## Etymological Debates
The exact origin of the root *ar-* remains a subject of scholarly debate. While Max Muller suggested a connection to *ira* (a Sanskrit word for earth), this is considered uncertain. Spiegel noted that while the word connects to a root *ar-*, there are several such roots with different meanings, and no certain criterion exists to decide which one is related. Other proposed connections, such as a link to *Erin* (Ireland), have been deemed impossible. There is, however, a possible connection to the *Ario-* found in Celtic and Germanic names, such as *Ariovistus*.
