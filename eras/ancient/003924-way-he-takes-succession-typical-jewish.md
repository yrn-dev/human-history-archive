# Interpretation of Jewish Institutions and Traditions

## Allegorical Interpretations in Alexandria
In Alexandria, a center where Judaism was immensely strong, certain writings emerged that sought to warn readers against compromising the Gospel with Judaism. The author of such works displayed an "astonishing familiarity with the Jewish rites," leading some scholars to suggest the writer may have been a converted Jew or a proselyte. This author utilized an extravagant allegorism to argue that there was no historical connection between the Gospel and Judaism.

Using the Rabbinic *Gematria*—a system based on the numerical values of letters—the author maintained that the Law and Prophecy, as intended by God, were never given to Israel as a people. According to this view, while the men of God in Israel understood the Divine oracles as pointing toward the Christian Covenant, the apostate people were led by an evil angel to follow the ceremonial letter of the Law. To illustrate this, the author took a succession of typical Jewish institutions—including the Sabbath, Temple, Covenant, Ablutions, Foods, and Circumcision—to demonstrate their spiritual counterparts within the ordinances of the New People, asserting that the Cross was prefigured from the beginning.

## Jewish Emblems and Philosophical Influence
The interpretation of Jewish symbols has varied based on the educational background of the interpreter. For instance, the mystical meaning of the garments of the high priest, the vessels, and the Jewish tabernacle were sometimes explained through Gentile philosophical notions. This approach is seen in the work of Philo and Josephus, both of whom were well-versed in heathen philosophy and learning.

However, other interpretations remained rooted in ancient Jewish notions. In the books of the *Jewish War*, written by a younger Josephus for a Jewish audience and before he was heavily influenced by Gentile books, different emblems are used. An example of this is the temple-candlestick; its seven lamps and seven branches were interpreted as emblems of the seven days of creation and rest, which in turn served as emblems of the seven planets.

## Political and Messianic Expectations
During the time of the trial and execution of the figure who proposed to fuse and enlarge human life, his disciples were influenced by a specific "old Jewish dream." This dream centered on the arrival of a Messiah—a king who would restore the fabled glories of David and overthrow the Roman overlord and the Hellenized Herods. Because of this expectation, the disciples disregarded the substance of his direct teaching, believing instead that he was part of a succession of kings and was using a singular, quasi-magic method to eventually ascend the throne of Jerusalem.

## Jewish Presence in Rome and Idumea
Historical records indicate that Jews and Syrians frequently migrated to Rome in large numbers during the Empire and the later Republic, where they earned livings in various ways. During the time of Cicero, Jews in Rome were known to cause disturbances in popular assemblies. Additionally, the Jewish community in Rome expressed great distress following the assassination of the Dictator Cæsar, who had favored the nation for their services during the Alexandrine War.

In other regions, the status of the Jewish nation expanded through proselytism. The Idumeans, for example, had been proselytes of justice since the days of John Hyrcanus. After approximately one hundred and ninety-five years, they were esteemed as part of the Jewish nation and were provided with a Jewish commander.
