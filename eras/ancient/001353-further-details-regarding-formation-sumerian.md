# Sumerian Civilization

The Sumerians established one of the earliest civilizations in lower Mesopotamia, in the region between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, which at the time flowed into the Persian Gulf via separate mouths. This society was characterized by the development of the first cities, temples, and systematic irrigation, marking a transition from barbaric village-towns to a complex social organization.

## Culture and Material Life
The Sumerians were a brownish people with prominent noses who shaved their heads and wore clothing made of wool. Their livestock included sheep, goats, asses, and cattle, though they did not possess horses. 

Due to a lack of stone in the Euphrates-Tigris valleys, clay became a fundamental element of Sumerian life. They utilized sun-dried brick to construct their buildings and great tower-like temples. At Nippur, they built a massive brick tower dedicated to their chief god, El-lil (Enlil), a structure believed to be the basis for the story of the Tower of Babel. Beyond architecture, the Sumerians used clay to create pottery, earthenware images, and thin tile-like cakes for writing. They did not use parchment or paper; instead, their letters, memoranda, and books were recorded on potsherds and clay tablets.

## Language and Writing
The Sumerian language consisted of accumulated syllables, similar to some contemporary Amerindian languages. This structure facilitated a syllabic method of writing, where pictures were used to represent similar things (a rebus system) to express ideas that could not be conveyed directly through images. This "cuneiform" writing was a precursor to later developments; the true alphabets of the later world derived from a mixture of Egyptian hieroglyphics and Sumerian cuneiform.

## Political Organization and Warfare
Sumeria was divided into city-states that frequently warred with one another. Their military capacity was maintained for many centuries, with soldiers fighting in close formation using shields of skin and long spears. For a significant period, Sumeria remained unconquered by any foreign race, though Sumerian city-states often conquered one another. Their civilization, shipping, and writing developed over a period that may be twice as long as the entire era from the Christian era to the present.

## Conquest and Assimilation
Around 2750 B.C., the Sumerian lands were conquered by Sargon, a Semitic leader of the Akkadians. Although Sargon was an illiterate barbarian, the Akkadians adopted the Sumerian language and writing, utilizing them as the speech of the learned and the officials. Despite the political conquest, Sumerian civilization prevailed over the simpler Semitic culture, and the Sumerian language remained the language of power and knowledge, similar to the role of Latin in middle ages Europe.

Following the decay of Sargon's empire after two centuries, the region faced incursions from the Elamites in the east and the Semitic Amorites in the west. The Amorites eventually established rule over Sumeria, making their capital at the previously small up-river town of Babylon. This first Babylonian Empire was consolidated by King Hammurabi (circa 2100 B.C.), who created the earliest known code of laws. Over time, the Semites and Sumerians assimilated, and the Babylonian Empire became Semitic in character and language.

## Onomastics and Historical Records
The study of Sumerian proper names presents significant challenges for modern scholars. In texts from the oldest historical periods, hundreds of genuinely Sumerian names appear, but determining their correct reading is difficult due to the multiplicity of phonetic values attached to the signs. Because knowledge of the ancient Sumerian language remains imperfect, scholars often disagree on readings. For example, a king of Ur previously read as Ur-Bau is now read as Ur-Engur, and a king of Erech read as Lugal-zaggisi is preferred by some as Ungal-zaggisi.

Further complications arise when determining if a name's bearer was a Sumerian or a Semite. If the bearer was a Semite, the signs might be read as Semitic equivalents or as an ideographic compound. An example is a Semitic ruler of Kish whose name was written Uru-mu-ush; while some initially read this ideographically as Alu-usharshid ("he founded a city"), scholarly opinion eventually accepted Uru-mu-ush (Urumush) as the correct designation.
