# Evolution of the Buddhist Suttas

The early Buddhist records were developed through a gradual process of accumulation and organization, moving from short, isolated statements of doctrine to complex collections. This evolution occurred in both prose and verse, reflecting the cultural preferences and mnemonic needs of the early Buddhist communities.

## Development of Prose Records
The earliest Buddhist records began as suttas, which were short statements of doctrine expressed in set phrases. These suttas were intended to be learned by heart rather than read, and they typically occupied only one or two pages, each containing a single proposition.

Over time, these records were expanded through a specific sequence of organization:
1. **Episodes:** Narrative frameworks were developed, stating when, how, and to whom a sutta was enunciated. These episodes often embodied several suttas and appeared in multiple locations.
2. **Suttantas:** Several episodes were woven together to form a suttanta, which generally spanned about a dozen pages.
3. **Nikāyas:** Finally, these were grouped into larger collections. The *Dīgha Nikāya* (the "Collection of Long Ones") contains the longest suttantas, while the *Majjhima Nikāya* (the "Collection of the suttantas of Medium Length") contains those shorter than the Dīgha but longer than ordinary suttas.

Together, the Dīgha and Majjhima Nikāyas contain 186 dialogues. In these texts, the Buddha—who refers to himself by the title Arahat—or one of his leading disciples is depicted in conversations regarding ethical, philosophic, or religious points.

## The Role of Verse and Poetry
Parallel to the evolution of prose suttas was the treatment of verses. Because there was a great love of poetry in the communities where Buddhism arose, verses were adopted to assist the memory and to express feelings of ecstasy and enthusiasm inspired by the reforming doctrines.

The oldest verses are lyrics that express deep thoughts or emotions. Most of these were not preserved alone; because they were often like puzzles and difficult to understand, they were usually accompanied by prose comments explaining the context of their utterance. In the latest books of the canon, the narrative portions also became verse, resulting in collections of ballads. The final stage of this evolution was the combination of these ballads into long epic poems.

## Key Poetical Collections
Several significant poetical works exist within the archive's records:
*   **Sutta Nipāta:** This collection contains fifty-five poems, most of which are short lyrics. It includes the *Song of Nalaka*, an ancient Pali ballad that mentions Lumbini, the garden where Gotama (the Buddha) was born.
*   **Jātaka:** This is a collection of 550 folk-tales from India. These verses are attributed to the Buddha in his previous births, identifying him as the hero of each story. In the Jātaka, the prose is preserved only within the commentary.

## Core Teachings and the Noble Truths
The foundation of the system is found in the "Noble Truths." One such truth concerns the passing away of pain, described as the emancipation from and getting rid of "craving thirst" so that no passion remains. The way to achieve this is the Noble Eightfold Path, which consists of:
*   Right Views
*   Right Aspirations
*   Right speech, conduct, and mode of livelihood
*   Right Effort
*   Right Mindfulness
*   Right Rapture

These simple statements are described as being pregnant with meaning, though they contain no mention of God, the soul, or the Buddha himself. The meanings of these ideas are elaborated upon and commented on extensively throughout the suttas.
