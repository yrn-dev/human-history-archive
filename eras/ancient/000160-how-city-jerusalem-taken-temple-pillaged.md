# The Capture of Jerusalem and the Pillaging of the Temple

The city of Jerusalem and its temple were subjected to multiple captures, sieges, and pillages throughout history by various foreign powers, including the Babylonians, the Greeks, and the Romans. These events often resulted in the destruction of fortifications, the removal of sacred vessels, and the forced displacement of the population.

## The Babylonian Conquest
One of the most devastating captures of Jerusalem occurred under Nebuchadnezzar, king of the Babylonians and Chaldeans. This event followed a period where a prince, having broken an oath of fidelity to Babylon to form an alliance with the king of Egypt, was chastised by the Babylonian king. Nebuchadnezzar laid siege to Jerusalem; although the arrival of an Egyptian army provided a brief hope for the besieged, the Egyptians were defeated. The siege of Jerusalem was renewed and lasted nearly a twelvemonth before the city was taken by storm.

The aftermath was marked by terrible slaughter. By the orders of Nebuchadnezzar, the nobles and principal men of Judah, as well as the two sons of Zedekiah, were killed in front of Zedekiah. The king himself had his eyes put out, was fettered, and carried to Babylon to remain in prison for the rest of his life. 

The city and temple were pillaged and burnt, and all fortifications were demolished. The king of Babylon dispatched Nebuzaradan, the general of his army, to carry out the pillaging of the temple, the burning of the royal palace, and the leveling of the city. Nebuzaradan removed the vessels of God made of gold and silver, specifically the golden tables, the candlesticks, the pillars of brass with their chapiters, and the large laver dedicated by Solomon. 

The temple was burned on the first day of the fifth month in the eleventh year of Zedekiah's reign (the eighteenth year of Nebuchadnezzar). This occurred 470 years, six months, and ten days after the temple was built. Along with the pillaged vessels, Nebuzaradan took captives to Riblah, including the high priest Seraiah, the priest Zephaniah, three temple rulers, an eunuch over the armed men, seven friends of Zedekiah, and his scribe. The heads of the high priest and rulers were cut off at Riblah, while Zedekiah and the other captives were led to Babylon.

## The Greek and Hellenistic Expeditions
In later periods, Jerusalem faced incursions by Greek leaders. Antiochus made an expedition against Jerusalem following quarrels regarding the high priesthood. He took the city and pillaged the temples, causing distress to the Israelites. During this time, many Israelites forsook the laws of their country. Simultaneously, the Samaritans adopted Greek customs and renamed their temple at Mount Gerizzim the Temple of Jupiter Hellenius.

Additionally, the archive records that Ptolemy, the son of Lagus, took Jerusalem and Judea through treachery and deceit, carrying many of the inhabitants away to be planted in Egypt.

## Roman Interventions and Pillaging
The Roman era saw further instances of the city being taken by force and the temple being plundered. Pompey besieged Jerusalem after the citizens shut their gates against him, eventually taking the city by force. 

Following the management of Jerusalem's affairs by Gabinius, the government was delivered to Crassus. The records state that Crassus came into Judea and pillaged the temple before marching against the Parthians, where he and his army perished.

## Restoration and Subsequent Conflict
Following the Babylonian destruction, Cyrus, king of Babylonia and Persia, commanded that the temple in Jerusalem be rebuilt. He ordained that the expenses be paid from the king's revenue and that the vessels pillaged by Nebuchadnezzar be restored to the people of Jerusalem. This task was overseen by Sanabassar, the governor and president of Syria and Phoenicia. 

However, the restoration process faced opposition. The Samaritans (also called Cuthearts) accused the Israelites of building the temple more like a citadel than a temple and persuaded Syrian governors to delay the work. Despite these challenges, the Israelites, led by Zorobabel and the high priest Jeshua, continued the work, citing the previous destruction by Nebuchadnezzar as a result of their fathers' impiety toward God.
