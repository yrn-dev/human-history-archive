# Grecian Epic and the Homeric Poems

The Grecian epic represents a primary category of early Greek poetry, characterized by its focus on great personages or significant events of legendary antiquity. At the forefront of these compositions are the *Iliad* and the *Odyssey*, which are associated with the name of Homer and cover separate portions of the comprehensive legend of Troy. These works serve as the type for the "heroic epic," distinguished from "genealogical" epics—such as the Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, *Eoiai*, and *Naupaktia*—which were tamer in spirit and lacked a concentrated focus on a limited number of contemporaneous characters.

## The Epic Cycle and Authorship
The Epic Cycle was an arrangement of poems organized according to the continuity of their narrative. While the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* are the only poems of the cycle that have been preserved, the cycle originally included various other works. In early antiquity, several epics were ascribed to Homer beyond the two primary poems, including the *Cyprian Verses*, the *Thebaïs*, the *Epigoni*, the *Capture of Œchalia*, the *Lesser Iliad*, the *Phokaïs*, and the *Amazonia*. Some critics even attributed the satirical *Margitês* and the *Batrachomyomachia* to him.

The *Thebaïs*, for instance, was mentioned as a work of Homer by the elegiac poet Kallinus (B. C. 640). Evidence from Herodotus regarding the expulsion of rhapsodes from Sikyôn by the despot Kleisthenês around B. C. 580 suggests that the *Thebaïs* and *Epigoni* were rhapsodized as Homeric productions at that time.

Later, Alexandrine critics, such as Aristarchus, began to regard only the *Iliad*, *Odyssey*, and the Hymns as genuine Homeric compositions. A group known as the *Chorizontes* (the Separators), led by Xenôn and Hellanikus, went further by arguing that the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* could not have been written by the same author.

## Composition and Transmission
The *Iliad* and *Odyssey* were originally intended for the ear, recited to assembled companies by singers and reciters known as rhapsodes, rather than read individually. This oral tradition was supported by bards of adequate memory, including blind bards.

The date of these poems is a subject of diversity of opinion, though the date assigned by Herodotus is considered the most probable, placing the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* between 850 and 776 B. C. There is significant debate regarding when these poems were first committed to writing. While some theories, such as those of Wolf and Lachmann, suggest they only reached their final form in the age of Peisistratus, other evidence suggests they were complete, well-defined wholes long before that period. For example, the *Æthiopis* of Arktinus, containing 9,100 verses, dates to more than two centuries before Peisistratus.

It is conjectured that manuscripts of the Homeric poems and other epics, such as the *Cypria* and *Thebaïs*, began to be compiled around the middle of the seventh century B. C. (between B. C. 660 and B. C. 630). This period coincided with the emergence of a narrow reading class and the opening of Egyptian commerce, which provided the papyrus necessary for writing.

## Relationship to Other Poets
Homer's work provided a foundation for subsequent poets. Hesiod, for example, took the brief antithesis between Zeus and Kronos (and the Olympic gods versus the Titans) found in Homer and expanded it into a systematized theogony. Similarly, other poets enlarged the Legend of Troy; Stasinus, Leschês, and Arktinus composed poems regarding events occurring before or after the *Iliad*.

Arktinus of Miletus is noted as a "disciple of Homer" who used the *Iliad* as a model for his *Æthiopis*, which served as a sequel. Unlike other Cyclic poems, the authorship of Arktinus's work remained distinct and was not merged into the legendary persona of Homer, suggesting a period in Ionia where Homer's immediate successors were still recognized as individual figures in tradition.

## Structure and Forms
The heroic epics are distinguished by their poetical unity. In contrast, biographical poems—such as the *Herakleia* or *Theseïs*, which recount the exploits of a single hero—occupy an intermediate position between the heroic and genealogical styles. Additionally, the hymns attributed to Homer are considered epical fragments that narrate specific adventures of the gods.

Regarding the length of lost works, the *Tabula Iliaca* records that the *Æthiopis* had 9,100 verses, though Proklus assigns it only four books. The *Ilias Minor* also consisted of four books, while the *Cyprian Verses* contained eleven.
