# ÆOLIC GREEKS IN ASIA

The presence of Greeks in Asia Minor is situated within a region that has historically served as a bridge between Europe and Asia. Geographically, the west coast of Asia Minor is characterized by a varied landscape of rugged mountains, fertile valleys, and an alternation of sea and promontory, which differs significantly from the monotonous scenery and severe climate of the central plateau.

## Regional Context and Terminology
The term "Asia" was used by the Greeks specifically to designate the country surrounding Ephesus, though it later acquired a broader geographical meaning. The region's history is marked by the rise and fall of small states and the movement of hostile armies rather than the stability of a single independent sovereign. 

Prior to and during Greek settlement, the area was inhabited by various indigenous nations. To the north were the Thracian race, and eastward of the River Halys—which served as an ethnographical boundary—were the Syro-Arabians. Other indigenous groups included the Phrygians, Lydians, and Mysians. The Phrygians, in particular, influenced early Greek colonists; the Greeks in Asia Minor adopted Phrygian worship and borrowed elements of the Greek musical scale from them.

## Greek Settlements in Asia Minor
Greek presence in Asia Minor included various groups and cities. While the archive notes the existence of twelve Ionic cities—such as Milêtus, Ephesus, Kolophôn, Smyrna, and Phôkæa—these were founded by diverse migrations of Greeks. These Ionic cities were often mixed with indigenous inhabitants and adopted the worship of Artemis and Apollo, which had existed on the Asiatic coast before the Greek emigrants arrived.

## Political Relations and Persian Hegemony
For a period, the Greeks in Asia Minor existed in a state of freedom. However, the historian notes that it was under the reign of Crœsus that Greeks were first subdued into tribute. This event initiated a series of hostile relations between the Greeks and the powers of Asia.

The rise of the Persian Empire under Cyrus, Cambyses, and Darius I further altered the status of these settlements. The Persians incorporated the civilization of Lydia and made all the Greek cities in Asia Minor tributary. Under Darius I (521 B.C.), the Persian empire extended its reach from the Dardanelles to the Indus.

## Liberation and Conflict
The Greek cities of Asia Minor eventually became the focus of liberation efforts by the Lacedæmonians, who viewed the enslavement of these Greeks by the Persians as a situation that required redress. The Lacedæmonian king Agesilaus crossed into Asia with a fleet, acting with vigour to defeat Tissaphernes and set the Greek cities free from Persian control. 

Despite these efforts, the Persian King Artaxerxes attempted to undermine Greek unity by sending Timokrates of Rhodes into Greece with large sums of money to bribe important persons and incite internal war among the Greeks, which eventually led to Agesilaus's departure from Asia.
