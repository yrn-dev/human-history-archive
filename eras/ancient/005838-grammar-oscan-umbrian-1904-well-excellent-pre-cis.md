# Grammar of Oscan and Umbrian (1904)

The *Grammar of Oscan and Umbrian*, published in Boston in 1904, is a philological work authored by Carl Darling Buck. Buck was an American philologist born in Bucksport, Maine, in 1866, who served as a professor of Indo-European comparative philology and Sanskrit at the University of Chicago. His scholarly contributions focused heavily on the Italic dialects, and the 1904 grammar serves as a lucid discussion of the phonology, accidence, and syntax of these languages.

## Context and Related Works
The *Grammar of Oscan and Umbrian* is part of a broader body of work by C. D. Buck on Italic linguistics. His previous publications in this field include *Der Vocalismus der oskischen Sprache* (1892) and *The Oscan-Umbrian Verb-System* (1895). Additionally, Buck authored a précis of the Italic languages for *Johnson's Universal Cyclopaedia* and collaborated with W. G. Hale on *A Latin Grammar* (1903). 

In the study of these dialects, Buck's work is often cited alongside other major scholarly contributions, such as R. von Planta's *Oskischumbrische Grammatik* (1892–1897) and R. S. Conway's *The Italic Dialects*.

## Phonology of Umbrian
The *Grammar of Oscan and Umbrian* provides essential supplementation to the study of the Umbrian dialect, particularly regarding the records found in the Tables. The phonology of Umbrian is characterized by several distinct changes and divergences from Oscan:

*   **Consonantal Shifts:** Umbrian exhibits the palatalization of *k* and *g* when followed by *i*, *e*, or a consonant *i* (for example, *tiçit* corresponding to the Latin *decet*). There is also a loss of final *-d* (such as the feminine ablative singular *tota* compared to the Oscan *toutad*). Furthermore, *d* between vowels changed to a sound akin to *r*, and *-s-* between vowels changed to *-r-* (seen in *erom* compared to the Oscan *ezum*).
*   **Final Sound Changes:** The dialect shows a change of final *-ns* to *-f* in the masculine accusative plural (e.g., *vitluf*). In its latest stage, final *-s* changed to *-r* in the ablative plural (e.g., *arver*).
*   **Vocalic Changes:** All diphthongs decayed into monophthongs. Specifically, *ai*, *oi*, and *ei* became *e* or *i* (and rarely *ei*), while *au*, *eu*, and *ou* became *o*.
*   **Initial Consonants:** There was a change of initial *l* to *v*, as seen in *vutu* (Latin *lavito*).

## Morphology and Syntax
According to the archive's records, Umbrian morphology and syntax do not diverge significantly from Oscan, except for the phonetic changes mentioned above and two specific perfect-formations utilizing *-l-* and *-nçi-*. Examples of these include *ampelust* (future perfect) and *combifiançiust*. Full accounts of the syntax and accidence as represented in inscriptions are detailed within Buck's 1904 grammar.

## The Nature of the Umbrian Dialect
The language described in the grammar is primarily based on ancient liturgies. While generally called "Umbrian," the term is strictly applied to the language of a limited area, specifically the eastern half of the Tiber valley from Urvinum Mataurense to Interamna Nahartium. This distinguishes the linguistic "Umbrian" from the wider archaeological use of the term, which some scholars, such as Professor Ridgeway, use to describe the race associated with the Early Iron Age Villanova culture.

The records of the dialect exhibit changes in both language and alphabet over at least four distinct periods. For instance, while Tables I through IV and the first two inscriptions of V use Umbrian characters, the Latin alphabet is employed in the *Claverniur* paragraph and the entirety of Tables VI and VII.
