# Post-Pleiocene Caspian Sea Extension

## Geological Hypotheses of the Caspian Basin
The Caspian Sea, an inland sea situated between Asia and Europe, occupies the deepest portion of a vast depression known as the Aralo-Caspian depression. This area was once the site of a larger inland sea referred to as the Sarmatian Ocean or the Eurasian Mediterranean. In the present era, the surface of the Caspian Sea is positioned 86 feet below ocean level (or 96.7 feet according to Aral-Caspian levelling) and 242.7 feet below the level of the Aral Sea.

Russian scientists have conducted extensive investigations into the depression westward of the Aral and Caspian basins, as well as the original connection between these two seas. These studies have led to the displacement of an older theory which suggested an ancient connection existed between the Caspian and the Oxus. In its place, a more recent hypothesis proposes that during the post-Pleiocene age, the Caspian Sea extended eastwards into Trans-Caspian territory.

## Evidence for Marine Extension
Support for the hypothesis of the Caspian's eastward extension is derived from specific geological and biological findings. Researchers have discovered shells, which are currently found living within the Caspian Sea, located approximately 100 m. inland. These shells were found at altitudes ranging from 140 to 280 ft. above the present level of the Caspian.

Further evidence is found in the composition of the Kara-kum sands. These sands appear to be a purely marine formation. Notably, they exhibit no traces of fluviatile deposits, which would be expected if the area had served as delta deposits for the Oxus.

## Regional Context of the Central Depression
The Caspian is part of a broader geographical context involving the great central depression of the continent. This depression stretches from the Gobi and Lop Nor through the Tarim desert to the foot of the Pamir plateau in the west. Research by Dr. M.A. Stein and Sven Hedin has demonstrated that the Turkestan desert, now characterized by sand-strewn wastes, once hosted highly civilized centers of industry and Buddhist art. Hedin's research also revealed the periodic shifting of the Lop Nor system.
