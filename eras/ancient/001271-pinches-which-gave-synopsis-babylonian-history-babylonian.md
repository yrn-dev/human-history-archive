# Th. G. Pinches and Babylonian Records

Th. G. Pinches was a scholar who contributed significantly to the study of ancient Mesopotamia through the translation and analysis of cuneiform texts. His work focused on various aspects of Babylonian history, including its chronological records, religious beliefs, and cosmogony.

## The Babylonian Chronicle and Political History
Pinches analyzed the "Babylonian Chronicle," a source that provides insights into the final years of the Babylonian monarchy. Through this work, Pinches highlighted the role of Belshazzar, the son of King Nabonidos (Nabunaid). While Nabonidos was noted for his religious and antiquarian interests, the Babylonian Chronicle reveals that from 548 B.C. (the 7th year of Nabonidos), Belshazzar remained with the army in Akkad, near Sippara. 

Pinches pointed out that Belshazzar's presence in this region explains the numerous gifts he bestowed upon the temple of the Sun-god at Sippara. Records indicate that as late as the 17th year of Nabonidos, approximately three weeks after the forces of Cyrus entered Babylonia, Belshazzar paid 47 shekels of silver to the temple as a "tithe" on behalf of his sister.

## Babylonian Cosmogony
In 1890, Pinches provided the first translation of a short account of Babylonian creation. This text is characterized by its non-mythical nature, specifically noted for the absence of the "dragon of chaos and darkness." This lack of a dragon-element is a distinguishing feature when comparing Babylonian cosmogony to other traditions, such as the Hebrew cosmogony.

## Contributions to Religion and Chronology
Pinches authored the work *Religion of Babylonia and Assyria* (1906), contributing to the broader academic understanding of the spiritual life of the region. His research exists alongside other major studies of the era that explored Babylonian magic, sorcery, and the reports of astrologers and magicians from Nineveh and Babylon.

Regarding the recording of time, the archive notes that Babylonia utilized a different chronological system than Assyria. While Assyria used lists of *limmi* (archons), Babylonia dated years by the chief events that distinguished them. These events were determined by the government and communicated to officials. A record of these dates was maintained and divided into reigns, which later allowed for the compilation of dynastic lists.

## The Framework of Babylonian History
The broader historical context of the region involves a complex succession of empires. The Sumerian civilization, characterized by its cuneiform writing and language, prevailed over the simpler culture of the Semitic newcomers. Eventually, the Sumerian Akkadian Empire was pressured by the Elamites from the east and the Amorites from the west. The Amorites settled in the town of Babylon and, under King Hammurabi around 2100 B.C., established the first Babylonian Empire.

Later historical records, such as the Babylonian List of Kings published in 1884, provided a framework for chronological systems by listing kings from the First Dynasty of Babylon through the Neo-Babylonian period. However, gaps in these texts—specifically regarding the lengths of Dynasties IV and VIII—have left some points of uncertainty for historians.
