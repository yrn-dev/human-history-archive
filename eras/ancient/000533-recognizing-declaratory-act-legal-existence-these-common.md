# Recognizing in a Declaratory Act the Legal Existence of Common Institutions

In the context of the monarchy's administration, a declaratory act was utilized to recognize the legal existence of common institutions. This act established the methods for their administration while strictly adhering to the principle of dualism. This principle created a formal parity between Hungary and the other territories of the king.

## Governance and the Delegation
A primary condition of this arrangement was the requirement for constitutional government to exist in the rest of the monarchy, as it did in Hungary. This included the establishment of a parliament representing all other territories. 

From both the Austrian and Hungarian parliaments, a "Delegation" of sixty members was elected. The common ministers were responsible to these Delegations, and the estimates for joint services were submitted to them. To ensure that the Delegations did not overshadow the parliaments that appointed them, specific procedural restrictions were implemented:
* **Meeting Structure:** The Delegations did not sit together; instead, each met separately.
* **Communication:** Communication was conducted via writing, with every document accompanied by a translation in either German or Magyar.
* **Conflict Resolution:** A common session was only convened if the parties failed to agree after exchanging notes three times. In such sessions, no discussion was permitted, and voting was conducted in silence via a simple majority.
* **Location:** Annual meetings alternated between Pest and Vienna.

## Common Ministers and Powers
Three ministers were appointed for common purposes:
1. Foreign Affairs
2. War
3. Finance

While these ministers were responsible to the Delegations, the Delegations themselves possessed no real legislative power. For example, the minister of war controlled the common army, but the laws regarding army recruitment had to be voted on separately within each individual parliament. 

Similarly, the minister of finance presented the common budget to the Delegations, but the Delegations could not vote on taxes or raise money. Once the budget was passed, the necessary funds had to be provided by the separate parliaments. The proportion of the contribution from each half of the monarchy was not decided by the Delegations, but was fixed every ten years.

### Common Institutions in Roman and English Law
The concept of the corporation, which allows for the holding of property in common, has roots in Roman law under the terms *collegium* or *universitas*. A *collegium* required at least three persons (*corporati—habere corpus*) and maintained a common chest. These bodies were legally separate from their individual members and were governed by their own by-laws, provided they did not contradict common law. Roman *collegia* included:
* Municipalities (public governing bodies)
* Religious societies (such as the Vestal Virgins and priests)
* Official societies (such as the *scribae*)
* Trade societies (such as *navicularii*, *pictores*, and *fabri*)

In English law, the origin of the corporation is attributed to five sources: charter, act of parliament, implication, prescription, and common law.

### Common Law and Penalties
Historically, the English common law did not recognize ecclesiastical offenses, and church courts lacked the power to condemn individuals to death. While the crown later claimed the right to issue writs *de haeretico comburendo* for the burning of heretics, some jurists argue that the crown had no such right under common law, as early instances of such writs were issued with the assent of the lords temporal.
