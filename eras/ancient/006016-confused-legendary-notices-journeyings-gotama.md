# The Journeyings and Final Days of Gotama

The historical figure known as the Buddha, whose family name was Gotama, was the son of Suddhodaṇa, a chief of the Sakiya tribe, which was one of the republican clans in India. While early accounts of his life are often surrounded by legendary and miraculous tales, it is generally accepted that there is a basis of truth sufficient to construct an intelligent history.

## Mission and Itinerations
Following the start of his mission, Gotama spent forty-five years as an itinerant preacher. His travels were primarily concentrated in the valley of the Ganges, and he generally did not venture further than approximately 250 miles from Benares. 

Gotama established a consistent yearly pattern for his ministry. During the rainy months, he would remain in one location, typically staying at one of the *viharas* (homes) that had been donated to his society. At the conclusion of the rainy season, he would gather his disciples who had committed themselves to a higher life. He instructed them to wander out of compassion for the world and for the benefit of gods and men, urging them to preach a doctrine that was "beauteous in inception, beauteous in continuation, beauteous in its end." He commanded that no two disciples should travel together. In his own travels, he visited locations such as the General's village in the wilds of Uruvela. While he began his mission alone, in later years he was accompanied by some of his most devoted disciples.

## Companions and Adversaries
In the twentieth year of his mission, Gotama's cousin, Ānanda, became a mendicant. From that point forward, Ānanda attended to the Buddha, remaining constantly by his side and providing personal service driven by love and reverence.

Another cousin, Devadatta, the son of the raja of Koli, also joined the society. However, Devadatta became envious of Gotama and influenced Ajatasattu to persecute him. Ajatasattu had become the king of Rajagaha after killing his father, Bimbisara. Although the accounts of how the Buddha overcame the devices of Devadatta and Ajatasattu are considered legendary, historical records accept the general fact of Ajatasattu's initial opposition to the sect and his subsequent conversion.

## Final Journey and Death
The accounts of the last few days of Gotama's life are described as tolerably clear. At eighty years of age, the teacher traveled toward Kusinara, a town located about 80 miles east of Kapilavastu and 120 miles north-north-east of Benares. During this journey, he rested briefly in a grove at Pawa, which had been provided to the society by a local goldsmith named Chunda. After Chunda prepared a mid-day meal for the mendicants, the Buddha continued toward Kusinara.

The journey was marked by several halts. After some distance, Gotama expressed thirst to Ānanda and was given water. He rested again and bathed for the final time at the river Kukushta, which flows halfway between Pawa and Kusinara. 

Sensing his approaching death, Gotama expressed concern that Chunda might be reproached for the meal he had provided. He instructed Ānanda to tell Chunda that he would receive a very great reward in a future birth because the Buddha was about to die after eating the food he gave. Gotama identified Chunda's gift and a previous gift from Sujātā (given before he attained wisdom under the Bo tree) as the two gifts that would be blest above all others.

## Passing and Final Instructions
The party eventually reached the river Hiranyavati, near Kusinara, where the teacher rested for the last time. Lying under Sal trees with his face toward the south, Gotama spoke earnestly with Ānanda regarding his burial and the rules the society was to observe after his death.

As evening fell, Ānanda wept. The Buddha comforted him with the promise of Nirvāna and reminded him of the impermanence of all things, stating, "O Ānanda! do not weep; do not let yourself be troubled. You known what I have said; sooner or later we must part from all we hold most dear." He noted that the human body contains the power to renew its strength.
