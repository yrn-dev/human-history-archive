# Consular District of Barcelona

The Consular District of Barcelona is a region of administrative and commercial significance. Documentation regarding the movement of commerce within this district is provided by the *British Foreign Office Reports for the Consular District of Barcelona*, which are published annually in London.

## Geography and Administration of Barcelona
Barcelona is a maritime province located in north-eastern Spain. Formed in 1833 from districts that previously belonged to the ancient kingdom of Catalonia, the province is bounded by Gerona to the east and north-east, the Mediterranean Sea to the south, Tarragona to the south-west, and Lerida to the west and north-west. As of 1900, the province had an area of 2,968 square miles and a population of 1,054,541.

The terrain is predominantly composed of mountain ranges that are often densely wooded and rich in minerals, characterized by deep ravines. These ranges are outliers of the Pyrenees, which form the northern frontier and include the Sierra del Cadi, featuring the peak of Tosa at 8,317 feet. Other notable elevations include Montserrat and Monseny, which reach heights between 4,000 and 6,000 feet. The province is watered by the river Ter in the east and the Llobregat, which rises at the base of the Sierra del Cadi and flows into the sea near the capital city of Barcelona. Level country is limited to a few tracts near the coast and the areas surrounding Vich, Sabadell, Manresa, and Igualada.

## History of the City of Barcelona
The city, originally known as Barcino, is traditionally said to have been founded in the 3rd century B.C. by the Carthaginian Hamilcar Barca. Following the Roman conquest, Augustus gave the city the name Julia Faventia (later Augusta and Pia) and established it as a Roman colony. By the 2nd century A.D., it had become a leading mart of the western Mediterranean, competing with Massilia (Marseilles) and Tarraco (Tarragona). The Roman city was situated on Monte Taber.

The bishopric of Barcelona was established in 343. During the 5th and 6th centuries, the Visigoths utilized the city as a temporary capital in 415 and 531, and church councils were held there in 540 and 599. The city, then called Barcinona or Bardjaluna, was captured by the Moors in 713 before passing to the dominion of the Franks in 801, along with the rest of Catalonia. Starting in 874, the counts of Barcelona ruled as independent monarchs.

## Commercial Zenith and Maritime Law
The union of Aragon and Catalonia in 1149 brought Barcelona to its peak of wealth and fame. The city's merchant ships traded as far east as Alexandria and as far west as the Baltic and North Seas, rivaling the maritime power of Venice, Ragusa, and Genoa. 

In 1258, James I of Aragon authorized the city to issue the *Consulado del Mar*, a code of maritime law that became authoritative for many European states. Barcelona was also an early adopter of marine insurance and maintained consuls at major commercial centers near or on the Mediterranean.

## Political Decline and Conflict
The commercial supremacy of Barcelona began to wane following the 1479 union of Aragon and Castile, which favored other Spanish cities. This decline was accelerated by the 1492 discovery of America, which shifted commercial focus to the ports of western Spain.

Political tensions rose as citizens blamed the "Castilian" government for their misfortunes, leading some to favor annexation by France. In 1640, Barcelona became the center of a Catalonian rebellion against Philip IV and sought French protection. Although the city returned to its allegiance in 1652, it was captured by the duke of Vendome in 1697.

## Regional Industry and Urban Centers
Within the province, the town of Igualada serves as a central market for a district rich in wine and agriculture. Located in the province of Barcelona on the left bank of the river Noya (a tributary of the Llobregat), Igualada is the northern terminus of the railway connecting it to Martorell and Barcelona. As of 1900, its population was 10,442. 

The town consists of an old section with ramparts, a fortress, and irregular streets, and a new section with spacious streets. Since 1880, local industries have expanded to include the production of soap, chocolate, cloth, wool, linen, cotton, leather, brandies, nails, and ribbons.
