# Early Christian Churches

The study of early Christian churches encompasses the architectural development of their meeting places, the evolution of their literature and dogma, and the intellectual conflicts they encountered during the Roman era.

## Architectural Development and the Basilica
The relationship between the Roman civil basilica and the early Christian church has been a subject of extensive discussion. While some once theorized that Christian churches were simply civil basilicas converted from secular to religious use, there is no evidence for this regarding public basilicas. Secular demands for such buildings would likely have remained constant regardless of the empire's religion.

Early Christian basilicas, particularly in the west, differ from the normal columned civil basilica in significant ways. They rarely featured galleries over the side aisles, and their interiors were consistently dominated by a semi-dome of an apse terminating the central nave. In contrast, vaulted apses are virtually unknown in normal columned civil basilicas, with the possible exception of Silchester.

In the East, notable basilican churches include the church of St. Philip at Athens, Bosra in Arabia, Xanthus in Lycia, the noble church of St. Demetrius at Thessalonica, and the church of Dana between Antioch and Bir. Justinian also built the monastery of St. Catherine on Mt. Sinai. In North Africa, S. Gsell has identified more than 130 examples of early Christian church remains. While basilicas of strictly early Christian date are not found in Germany, Spain, or France, Germany possesses an unaltered basilica from the time of Charles the Great at Michelstadt in the Odenwald.

## Literature and Canonization
The New Testament consists of a series of early Christian writings that the Church eventually regarded as canonical, placing them in the same category as the Old Testament. These writings were viewed as the Word of God and inspired doctrine, used by the Church to prove its dogma. Later, medieval theologians and Schoolmen utilized the New Testament as a storehouse of proof-texts to support the Church's conclusions.

Scholarly research into early Christian literature has involved the study of the Apostolic Fathers. Authorities such as J. B. Lightfoot and T. Zahn have contributed to this field, specifically regarding Ignatius of Antioch. While some scholars date certain works to 150 or later, others, such as van Loon, suggest dates as late as 175.

## Intellectual Conflicts and the New Platonists
During the declining age of learning, the rise of the "new Platonists" in Alexandria—who silenced the schools of Athens—created a complex relationship with Christianity. Masters such as Plotinus, Porphyry, Amelius, and Ammonius focused on metaphysics, the secrets of the invisible world, and the reconciliation of Aristotle and Plato.

These new Platonists claimed to possess the secret of disengaging the soul from its corporal prison and claimed intercourse with spirits and demons, effectively converting philosophy into magic. Because they agreed with Christians on a few mysterious points of faith, they attacked the rest of the Christian theological system with the intensity of a civil war.

## Academic and Historical Study
The history of the early church has been preserved and analyzed through various academic channels. In Oxford, the Bampton lectures—established by the will of John Bampton (c. 1690-1751)—have addressed topics such as the authority of the primitive fathers regarding the faith and practice of the primitive Church. Notable lecturers on these themes include E. Hatch, who spoke on the organization of the early Christian churches in 1880, and C. Bigg, who discussed the Christian Platonists of Alexandria in 1886.

Further scientific criticism of church history was championed by the French scholar Louis Marie Olivier Duchesne. Duchesne's work, including *Origines du culte chretien*, has shed light on Christian origins and the Latin liturgy prior to Charlemagne, as well as the origin of episcopal dioceses in ancient Gaul.
