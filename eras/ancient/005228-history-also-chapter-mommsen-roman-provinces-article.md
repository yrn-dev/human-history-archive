# Roman Provinces and Historical Studies

The study of the Roman Empire and its provincial administration is documented across various historical and archaeological works, with significant contributions from scholars such as Th. Mommsen. These records cover the geographical extent of Roman control, the administration of its territories, and the cultural and religious intersections within the empire.

## Provincial Administration and Economy
The economic nature of the Roman provinces varied. According to Mommsen, some provinces were characterized by a balance where the cost of maintaining them was equal to the revenue they provided. In terms of social organization, Mommsen has analyzed the origins of Roman families, noting that the Quinctia is considered the older gens, while the Quinctilia is viewed as a later introduction from Alba.

## Roman Britain and Scotland
The Roman occupation of Britain and Scotland is a subject of extensive archaeological and historical research. General accounts of the Romanization of Britain have been provided by Prof. F. Haverfield, while Mommsen’s *Roman Provinces* offers further insight into the region. 

Specific areas of study include:
*   **Defenses and Forts:** Research on Hadrian's Wall includes works by John Hodgson and J.C. Bruce, as well as excavation reports by Haverfield and R.C. Bosanquet regarding the Roman camp at Housesteads. Other forts, such as Hardknott and Gellygaer, have been documented by R.S. Ferguson and J. Ward.
*   **Scotland:** The Roman occupation of Scotland is detailed in the *Antonine Wall Report* by Haverfield, J. Macdonald's work on Roman stones in the Hunterian Museum, and Stuart's *Caledonia Romana*.
*   **Urban Centers:** Archaeological records exist for Silchester, Caerwent, and Roman London, the latter of which was documented by Charles Roach Smith.
*   **Rural and Religious Life:** General Pitt-Rivers conducted excavations of villages in Cranborne Chase. The history of Christianity in Roman Britain and the eventual end of Roman rule in the region have also been analyzed in the *English Historical Review* and by Prof. Bury in the *Life of St Patrick*.

## Roman Influence in Gaul and Italy
In 57 B.C., the Roman Republic sought to conquer the remainder of Gaul, including the region of Belgium. At that time, Belgium was occupied by the Belgae, a Celtic people and part of a larger group of Gauls. The Belgae formed a confederation of tribes, the most important of which were the Nervians, who resided in Flanders, Brabant, and Hainaut.

In Italy, the town of Lucera (ancient Luceria) served as a strategic key to its country. It first appears in history on the Roman side during the Samnite Wars in 321 B.C., and a Latin colony was established there in 314 or 315 B.C. While the town once featured an amphitheatre and a temple of Minerva, Th. Mommsen has remarked on the local neglect of antiquities in this area.

## Marcus Aurelius and the Empire
The reign of M. Aurelius Antoninus provides a focal point for studying the intersection of Roman military, philosophical, and religious life. Military history during this period includes the Markomannenkrieg and eastern campaigns, with Th. Mommsen providing analysis in *Provinces of the Roman Empire* and a historical introduction to the Aurelius column.

The era is also noted for the *Meditations*, a philosophical work published by Xylander in 1558. Scholars have examined the relationship between the Stoicism of Marcus Aurelius and the rise of Christianity during the 2nd century.

## Later Roman Influence and Chronology
The influence of the Roman Empire persisted in the consciousness of later figures. Marius of Avenches, a bishop and chronicler who died in 593 or 594, wrote a *Chronicon* covering the period from 455 to 581. Despite the passage of time, Marius regarded himself and his land as remaining under the authority of the Roman Empire, consequently dating his work by the years of the East Roman emperors and Roman consuls.
