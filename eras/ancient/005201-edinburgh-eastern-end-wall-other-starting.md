# Edinburgh

Edinburgh is a city in Scotland that has served as a significant site for political, intellectual, and military history. It has functioned as a center for royal coronations and the publication of major scientific and encyclopedic works.

## Royal and Political History
Edinburgh was the site of the coronation of Margaret in March 1504. Following the death of James IV on September 9, 1513, at the war ending at Flodden, Margaret was appointed the sole guardian of her infant son, King James V. During a period of political instability in Scotland, the country was split between those favoring an alliance with France—led by John Stewart, duke of Albany—and those favoring England, a faction to which Margaret was generally inclined. 

After a conflict that nearly resulted in civil war, Albany blockaded Margaret in Stirling Castle. Following her capitulation, Margaret returned to Edinburgh. No longer responsible for the custody of the king, she fled to England in September 1515.

## Intellectual and Scientific Contributions
The city has a strong history of literary and scientific publishing. In 1799, Brewster began his literary career as a regular contributor and eventually the editor of the *Edinburgh Magazine*. In 1807, he undertook the editorship of the *Edinburgh Encyclopaedia*, which began appearing in 1808 and concluded in 1830; this work was noted for its strength in the scientific department.

Furthering the city's scientific output, Brewster and Robert Jameson established the *Edinburgh Philosophical Journal* in 1819, which replaced the *Edinburgh Magazine*. The first ten volumes were published under joint editorship from 1819 to 1824, while Jameson edited the remaining four volumes between 1825 and 1826. Additionally, Brewster started the *Edinburgh Journal of Science* in 1824, editing sixteen volumes through 1832.

## Roman Military Frontiers
Edinburgh served as a geographic marker for Roman military fortifications in Scotland. The general Agricola fortified a line extending from Dumbarton to Edinburgh. Later, around 142, the emperor Antoninus Pius, acting through his general Lollius Urbicus, constructed a new rampart of earth between Edinburgh and Dumbarton. 

This frontier, known as the Antonine Wall, was situated on a narrow isthmus between the Forth and Clyde, measuring 36 miles across. The construction consisted of a continuous rampart with a ditch in front and a connecting road running from end to end. The rampart was built using regularly laid sods. Approximately a dozen fair-sized forts were built either abutting the wall or close behind it. 

Excavations of these forts have revealed various features:
* **Bar Hill:** Covering three acres, this site featured turf ramparts, wooden barrack-rooms, and stone structures including a bath, storehouse, and headquarters building.
* **Castle Cary:** Covering nearly four acres, this fort featured massive, well-dressed masonry in its ramparts, with its north face aligned with the frontier wall.
* **Rough Castle:** Located near Falkirk, this smaller fort is noted for its defensive pits and the strength of its earthen and turf-built ravelins and ramparts.
