# Fortresses and Fortifications

Throughout human history, the fortress has served as a critical instrument of military strategy, political control, and territorial defense. These structures have evolved from early strongholds and feudal castles into complex modern fortifications, often situated at strategic geographic points such as mountain passes, river valleys, or imperial borders.

## Evolution of the Castle and Fortress
The transition from the medieval stronghold to the modern fortress is marked by a shift in function and design. Historically, the castle served as the fortress for local magnates who were often ill-governing. Over time, these structures merged into the history of modern fortification. A transitional type appeared around 1540 with the coast castles erected by Henry VIII, such as those at Sandown, Deal, and Walmer; Walmer Castle continues to serve as the official residence of the lord warden of the Cinque Ports.

In the valley of the Doubs, the evolution of fortifications included the construction of artillery towers at castle angles by Charles the Bold, as well as the use of demi-boulevards, boulevards, and earthen outworks to protect masonry. By the 17th century, medieval towers and walls often served merely as an *enceinte de surete*. While some castles became *forts d'arret* or block-houses in defiles and mountain passes, others, like Dover, became the nucleus of military places of arms. Many others fell into ruin or were converted into peaceful mansions, such as Hurstmonceaux in Sussex and Wingfield Manor in Derbyshire, both erected in the 15th century. In many walled towns, the feudal castle site was eventually occupied by a citadel.

## Strategic Fortresses in Europe
In Bohemia, the pass of Neumark (also known as the pass of Neugedein) has historically been the primary approach from Germany. This area, located above the town of Taus (Czech *Domazlice*, once called *Tuhost*, meaning "the Fortress"), was the site of several significant conflicts. In the first half of the 7th century, Samo repulsed Avar hordes here. In 1040, Wratislas II defeated German emperor Henry III in a two-day battle on August 22 and 23. The site also saw major victories for the Hussites in 1431 and later for George of Podebrad against German armies.

In Saxony, the town of Königstein features a massive fortress crowning a sandstone rock 750 feet above the Elbe. Likely a Slav stronghold by the 12th century, it was a fief of Bohemia by 1241 before being ceded to Saxony in 1459 via the treaty of Eger. Strengthened around 1540 to serve as a *point d'appui* against Bohemian inroads, it is often called "the key to Bohemia," though its actual strategic influence on operations was limited. The fortress served as a refuge for King Augustus III of Poland during the Prussian invasion of 1756 and for King Frederick Augustus II and his ministers during the Dresden insurrection of May 1849. It was held by Prussians from 1867 until 1871 and currently serves as a state treasure house, a detention center for officers, and a garrison for Saxon infantry regiments.

In Ireland, the town of Caher (or Cahir) derives its name from *cathair*, meaning "stone fortress." The site, located on an island in the river Suir, originally housed a fortress-palace of Munster called *Dun-iasgach*. The existing castle, built in 1142 by O'Connor, lord of Thomond, faced sieges by Cromwell and during the wars of 1647 and 1599.

## Fortifications in Asia and the Middle East
Fortresses have played pivotal roles in the defense of Asian empires and regional conflicts. Kia-yu-kwan is recognized as one of the gates of China and the fortress guarding the empire's extreme north-west entrance. While some associate the name with "Jade Gate," it likely means "barrier of the pleasant Valley."

In Korea, the fortress of Chinju was regarded as the strongest in the country. During a campaign ordered by Hideyoshi, Japanese troops conducted continuous assaults against Chinju for nine days. The fortress eventually fell, resulting in the loss of between sixty and seventy thousand Koreans, with Japanese casualties estimated to be nearly as numerous. In Japan, the fortress of Koromo-gawa was the site of an encounter between Yoshiiye and Sadato. Yoshiiye's decision to spare Sadato after a display of wit through a couplet is regarded as an act of magnanimity worthy of a true *bushi*.

In the Middle East, the fortress of Masada was besieged by a Roman general who led an army against Eleazar and the Sicarii. To ensure no one escaped, the Romans built a wall entirely around the fortress and established garrisons in the surrounding country. The siege was difficult due to the strength of the fortress and the lack of nearby water and food. Additionally, the fortress of Gamala was captured by Alexander during a campaign in which he also took the city of Dios, the city of Essa, and the cities of Seleucia and Golan.

## Modern Siege Warfare
In modern conflict, such as on the western front, warfare has shifted toward the character of a siege. Opposing forces utilize wire entanglements, *trous-de-loup*, and deep field entrenchments that can only be destroyed by heavy artillery. In this environment, attackers must treat every single position, tree, or metre of open country as if it were a separate fortress.
