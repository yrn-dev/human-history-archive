# GRECIAN COLONIES IN AND NEAR EPIRUS

The Grecian colonies situated on the eastern side of the Ionian sea, in and near the region of Epirus, include Korkyra, Leukas, Anaktorium, Ambrakia, Apollonia, and Epidamnus. These settlements were primarily established through the maritime and colonizing efforts of Corinth, though their relationships with their mother-city and one another varied.

## The Role of Corinth and Korkyra
Corinth was a maritime and commercial power that planted several colonies along the coast of Epirus. Most of these settlements acknowledged a hegemony or supremacy over Corinth, maintaining amicable relations. However, Korkyra served as a glaring exception. Despite being founded from Corinth, Korkyra was frequently at variance with its mother-city, sometimes engaging in aggravated hostility and withholding the customary tributes of filial and honorary respect.

Korkyra's own strategic position made it well-situated for traffic with Epirus. Because Korkyra was jealous of neighboring rivals, and because the coast of Epirus was generally discouraging to colonization, many Grecian emigrants bypassed the region entirely, moving instead toward the maritime plains of Illyria or westward to Italy.

## Specific Settlements and Foundations
Among the colonies associated with Corinth were Leukas, Anaktorium, and Ambrakia. Some of these were joint settlements established by both Corinth and Korkyra. 

Epidamnus (known in Roman times as Dyrrachium) was founded by the Korkyræans on the coast of Illyria in the Ionic gulf, north of Korkyra. Despite being a Korkyræan colony, the sanctity of Grecian custom required the Korkyræans to select the *œkist* (founder-in-chief) from Corinth. This founder, a citizen of Herakleid descent named Phalius, arrived with other Corinthian settlers, making Epidamnus a recognized "granddaughter" of Corinth.

Further north, the colony of Apollonia existed. In the time of Thucydidês and Herodotus, there appeared to be no Hellenic settlements between Apollonia and Ambrakia.

## Geography and Colonization Challenges
The coast of Epirus, from the entrance of the Ambrakian gulf northward to the Akrokeraunian promontory, was largely unprofitable for Grecian colonization. Unlike the extensive maritime plains found in the gulf of Tarentum, the Epirus coast is characterized by an abrupt mountain region with little cultivable soil that reaches near the sea. Consequently, level ground was typically possessed by villagers living on hill-sites, which were often inexpugnable and difficult to attack.

The most significant areas of plain and valley in Epirus, next to that of Ambrakia, were located near the junction of the river and lake of Acheron with the sea at the harbor called Glykys Limên. These were possessed by the Thesprotian town of Ephyrê and possibly the ancient Thesprotian town of Pandosia.

## Later Settlements and External Influence
After the time of Thucydidês, some Greek settlers gained admission into Epirotic towns. Dêmosthenês identifies Pandosia, Buchetia, and Elæa as settlements from Elis. These were eventually conquered by Philip of Macedon and handed over to the king of the Molossian Epirots.

External political ambitions also impacted the region. Dionysius of Syracuse, prompted by Alketas—a dispossessed prince of the Molossian Epirots—established the town of Lissus on the Illyrian coast, north of Epidamnus. Dionysius also assisted Parians in planting settlements on the islands of Issa and Pharos. Dionysius and Alketas intended to use warlike Illyrian tribes to invade Epirus to restore Alketas to his Molossian principality. This invasion was successful, resulting in a bloody battle where fifteen thousand Molossians were slain, though further plans to march toward Delphi were stopped by Sparta.

## The Native Epirots
The native population of Epirus comprised various tribes with little to no ethnical kindred. Some tribes shared ethnic connections with the Macedonians, while others were connected to tribes in southern Italy. The territory was distributed into villages rather than considerable cities, and the boundaries between tribes were difficult to mark. Governance varied among the tribes, with some ruled by kings and others not.
