# Illyrians, Macedonians, and Pæonians

The regions north and north-west of Greece were historically occupied by a variety of kindred tribes and clans. While the terms Illyrian, Macedonian, and Pæonian served as general ethnical designations, they did not represent united national masses, but rather groups of separately governed tribes with their own distinct customs.

## The Illyrians
The Illyrians were a numerous and widely extended group of tribes. Their territory was bounded to the west by the Adriatic and to the east by the Skardus mountain range, which is the northern continuation of the Pindus. This area covered the mountains of Bosnia, Herzegovina, and Montenegro, as well as Middle and Upper Albania. Some sources extend their reach as far north as the Julian Alps and the river Save.

The distribution of Illyrian tribes varied by region:
*   **Southern Tribes:** The Amantini were the southernmost, located north of the Epirotic Chaonians. The Taulantii were neighbors to the site where Epidamnus was founded. Other southern groups included the Chelidonii, the Encheleis, and the Abri.
*   **Interior and Northern Tribes:** The Parthini and the Dassaretii (near Lake Lychnidus) occupied the interior, as did the Penestæ, who possessed the fortified town of Uscana. Further east, the Ardiæans, Autariatæ, and Dardanians extended toward Upper Mœsia and the Skardus range.
*   **Coastal Extensions:** While Skylax identifies the Bulini as the northernmost tribe and excludes the Liburnians and Istrians from the Illyrian race, other sources such as Appian and Herodotus include the Liburnians, Istrians, and even the Eneti (or Veneti) under the Illyrian name.

The Illyrians were often characterized as armed plunderers. In the fourth century B.C., a coalition of Illyrian warriors under a chief named Bardylis—a former charcoal burner known for his bravery and just distribution of plunder—seized a significant portion of Western Macedonia west of Mount Bermius. This forced the native Macedonians into barren hills. Additionally, during the middle of the fourth century B.C., Gallic immigrations from the west pushed northerly Illyrian and Pæonian tribes southward, leading to increased invasions of Macedonia and maritime piracy against Grecian commerce in the Adriatic.

## The Macedonians
The early Macedonians occupied a region eastward of the Pindus and Skardus mountains. They shared a common nationality and ethnical name, though they were often distinguished by local epithets of Grecian origin, such as the Lynkestæ (Macedonians of Lynkus) and the Elymiotæ (Macedonians of Elymeia). The Orestæ, located east of Pindus and north of the Cambunian mountains, were also part of this aggregate, though Hekatæus refers to them as a Molossian tribe. The inhabitants of Pelagonia and Deuriopis were also Macedonian, despite their affinity for the Pæonians.

The Macedonian language was distinct from Greek, Illyrian, Thracian, and Pæonian. However, it was not more widely distinct from Greek than the languages of the Epirots, making the acquisition of Greek relatively easy for the Macedonian people.

Politically, the dynasty of Edessa was established by Argeian Greeks, including Perdikkas. This dynasty expanded its power over the interior Macedonians and as far as the Thermaic gulf. The Macedonian military was similar to that of the Thessalians, featuring well-mounted and armed horsemen. Unlike the surrounding Illyrian or Thracian tribes, the Macedonians were viewed more as regular warriors who conquered to maintain tribute and dominion.

## The Pæonians
The Pæonians were a numerous and divided race who claimed descent from the Teukri of Troy. They were distinct from the Macedonians, Illyrians, and Thracians. Their territory spanned both banks of the Strymon river, from its rise at Mount Skomius down to the lake at its mouth. Pæonian tribes occupied the fertile plain of Siris north of Mount Pangæus and parts of the valley of the Axius.

The Pæonians shared borders with several groups:
*   **North-west:** The Macedonian Pelagonia.
*   **North:** The Illyrian Autariatæ and Dardani.
*   **East, South, and South-east:** The Pierians and Thracians.

In the fourth century B.C., Philip of Macedonia marched into Pæonian territory along the upper Axius. Finding them weakened by the death of their king, Agis, Philip defeated their troops and reduced the Pæonians to Macedonian supremacy.
