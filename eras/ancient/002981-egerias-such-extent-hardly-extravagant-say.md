# Egeria

In historical and literary contexts, the term "Egeria" refers to both a figure of ancient Roman mythology and a descriptor for influential women in the lives of prominent men.

## The Mythological Egeria
According to the writings of Plutarch, Egeria was a nymph who was associated with Numa, a figure who frequently left the city to spend time alone in desert places and sacred groves. Stories arose suggesting that Numa's avoidance of human society was not due to a derangement of the intellect, but because he had been admitted to marriage with the gods. It was said that Numa became blessed and acquired heavenly wisdom through his converse with the nymph Egeria, who loved him.

Plutarch notes that this narrative aligns with various other ancient myths, including those of the Bithynians regarding Herodotus, the Phrygians regarding Attis, and the Arcadians regarding Endymion. While Plutarch suggests it is difficult to believe a deity could feel the passion of love for a human form, he posits that a god may feel friendship for a man, which can guide that individual toward a path of virtue.

## Literary and Biographical Usage
In a biographical context, the term "Egerias" has been used to describe women of high rank who provided essential inspiration and support to creative figures. In the case of the writer Balzac, three ladies of a higher rank than his own are characterized as his "Egerias." It is suggested that Balzac would not have been the person he was without them.

One such figure was Madame de Berny, a mother of nine children connected with the court of the *ancien regime*. Introduced to Balzac in 1821, she became known to him as *La dilecta* and served as the original inspiration for the character Mme de Mortsauf in the work *Le Lys dans la vallee*.
