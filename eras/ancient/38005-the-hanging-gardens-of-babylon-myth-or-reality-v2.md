# The Nineveh Hypothesis: Shifting the Geography of the Hanging Gardens

While traditional narratives attribute the Hanging Gardens to King Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon, the archaeological silence at the site of Babylon has led modern historians to reconsider the garden's origin. For decades, the search for the gardens focused on the Euphrates riverbanks of southern Mesopotamia. However, a compelling scholarly shift suggests that the "Hanging Gardens" may not have been in Babylon at all, but rather in Nineveh, the Assyrian capital.

This perspective moves the debate from "did they exist?" to "where were they actually located?" By examining the discrepancies between Greek accounts and archaeological evidence, we can explore the possibility of a geographical misidentification by ancient chroniclers.

## The Silence of Babylon
The primary challenge for historians is the lack of internal evidence. Nebuchadnezzar II left behind extensive records of his building projects in Babylon, detailing every wall and temple with meticulous precision. Yet, he never mentions the Hanging Gardens. Furthermore, excavations at Babylon have failed to uncover the massive irrigation infrastructure required to lift water to elevated terraces in a desert climate.

If the gardens were as monumental as the Greeks described, it is highly improbable that the Babylonian records would omit them entirely.

## The Case for Nineveh
Dr. Stephanie Dalley of Oxford University has proposed that the gardens were actually constructed by the Assyrian King Sennacherib in Nineveh (modern-day Mosul, Iraq) around 700 BCE. Unlike the Babylonian site, Nineveh provides strong evidence of advanced hydraulic engineering. 

Sennacherib’s records describe a "palace without rival" and a vast botanical garden featuring plants brought from all over the known world. Archaeological surveys have revealed a sophisticated system of aqueducts and canals—including the Jerwan aqueduct—capable of transporting water from the mountains to the city's heights. This infrastructure aligns perfectly with the technical requirements of the "hanging" terraces described by later historians.

## A Case of Mistaken Identity
The confusion likely stems from the fact that Nineveh was often referred to as the "New Babylon" or that later Greek writers conflated the two great Mesopotamian capitals. By the time the accounts of Berossus and Diodorus Siculus were written, the distinction between the Assyrian and Babylonian empires had blurred in the Western imagination, leading the world to credit the gardens to the wrong city.

### Key Facts
* **Traditional Attribution:** King Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon (c. 605–562 BCE).
* **Alternative Attribution:** King Sennacherib of Assyria (c. 705–681 BCE).
* **Engineering Requirement:** A sophisticated "Archimedes screw" or chain pump system to lift water.
* **Primary Sources:** Most descriptions come from Greek historians, written centuries after the gardens' supposed peak.
* **Archaeological Status:** No definitive ruins of the gardens have been confirmed in Babylon.

### Did You Know?
* **Botanical Diversity:** The gardens were said to contain exotic flora from across Asia, acting as one of the world's first botanical collections.
* **The Jerwan Aqueduct:** This massive stone structure built by Sennacherib is one of the oldest known aqueducts in history.
* **The "Hanging" Misnomer:** The term "hanging" is a translation of the Greek *kremastos*, which likely meant "overhanging" or "terraced" rather than suspended in the air.
