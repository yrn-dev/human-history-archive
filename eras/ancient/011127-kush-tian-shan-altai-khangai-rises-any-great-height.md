# The Continental Divide of Central Asia

The geography of Asia is defined by a vast central water-parting, or divide, that stretches from the Black Sea through the Elburz and the Hindu Kush, continuing to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, and extending further to the Bering Strait in the extreme north-east. This divide serves as a primary hydrographic feature of the continent, though it is not consistently marked by steep, well-defined ranges. In some areas, the axis of the main ranges is transverse to the water-parting, resulting in intermediate highlands and lacustrine regions with indefinite watersheds.

## High Altitude Ranges
While a considerable portion of this great continental divide remains below 5,000 ft. in altitude, certain ranges rise to great heights. These include the Hindu Kush, the Altai, the Khangai, and the Tian-shan.

The Tian-shan, also known as the Celestial Mountains, are among the most significant of these ranges. Located between 40 and 43 degrees North and 75 and 95 degrees East, they form the northern boundary of the Gobi desert. Some of the summits of the Tian-shan are said to exceed 20,000 ft. in altitude. The Ala-tau, a prolongation of the Tian-shan, flanks the Syr to the north and reaches heights of 14,000 to 15,000 ft., acting as a barrier between the Balkash and Issyk-kul lakes.

The Altai range, situated near the 50th parallel, rises to 10,000 or 12,000 ft. This range separates the waters of the great rivers of western Siberia from those collecting in Kalka, Dzungaria, and the lakes of north-west Mongolia.

## Regional Topography and Drainage
The continental level rises gradually from the low flats of Siberia toward this central divide. South of the divide, the land drops into a central depression—once a vast inland sea—which extends from the east of the Gobi desert westwards through Mongolia to Chinese Turkestan. This depression, which includes the area around Lop Nor, may actually be below sea-level in its central portions, and generally does not exceed 2,000 ft. above sea level.

Between the Tian-shan and the Kuen Lun mountains lies the Tarim Basin (roughly Eastern Turkestan), characterized by rivers that end in intermittent lakes and swamps rather than reaching the sea. To the west of this basin, the mountain barrier is described as high but not forbidding, providing practicable routes from China to Kashgar, and onward to Bokhara, Samarkand, and Kokand.

Further east, the Great Khingan range serves as the eastern border ridge of an immense plateau extending from the Himalaya to the Bering Strait and from the Tian-shan to the Khingan Mountains. The crest of the Great Khingan rises to between 4,800 and 6,500 ft., with a steep eastern slope descending to the plains of Manchuria.

## Geological and Biological Context
Geological evidence suggests that a broad area from the Arctic Ocean to the south of China has been land since the close of the Palaeozoic era. Marine Mesozoic deposits found in Tibet, Japan, and the western Tian-shan help define the limits of this land mass.

The region is also home to specific wildlife, such as the Tian Shan wild sheep (*Ovis Karelini*), which is smaller than the *Ovis Poli* found in the Pamirs.

## Human Geography and History
The foothills of the Tian-shan range support the principal cultivated districts of central Asia and the few towns located in this otherwise barren and thinly peopled region. One such area is the region of Cascar (Kashgar), which was formerly a kingdom but later became subject to the Great Kaan. The inhabitants of Cascar are known for their trade, handicrafts, and the cultivation of vineyards and cotton. The population includes people who worship Mahommet as well as Nestorian Christians with their own churches.

Historically, these mountain systems influenced the movement of peoples. Between 200 B.C. and 200 A.D., the advancing front of the Chinese Empire pushed nomads westward. This drift initially turned south toward Bactria. It is noted that the "singing arrows" used to destroy the army of Crassus likely originated from the Tian Shan and the Altai. Over time, this movement led to the "Mongolization" of Western Turkestan.
