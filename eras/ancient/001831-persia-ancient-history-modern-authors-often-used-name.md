# PERSIA: Ancient History

## Geography and Regional Divisions
Ancient Persia encompassed various distinct regions and provinces. In the east, the country of Bactria (known in Persian inscriptions as *B[=a]khtri*) was situated between the Oxus (Amu Darya) and the range of the Hindu Kush (Paropamisus). This mountainous region, with its capital at Bactra (modern Balkh), is characterized by a moderate climate, abundant water, and fertile land. While some modern authors have used "Bactria" to designate the entire eastern part of Iran, scholars have abandoned this extensive use of the term.

Further east lay the region where the Aryans lived as a single people before separating into Iranians and Indians. Other notable areas include Tún-o-Káin, the eastern Kuhistán or hill country of Persia, where Tún and Káin served as the chief cities.

## Early Peoples and Cultural Origins
Bactria was the home of one of the Iranian tribes. It is believed that the prophet Zoroaster preached and gained his first adherents in these regions, where fertile mountainous soil is bordered by the Turanian desert. From this area, his religion spread to the western parts of Iran. Consequently, the sacred language of the Avesta, the holy book of Zoroastrianism, was often referred to as "old Bactrian," though this terminology is no longer used by scholars.

Regarding the origins of the Indo-European race, it was once widely believed that Bactria was the cradle of this race based on the theory that European nations immigrated from Asia. However, linguists now acknowledge this is incorrect, asserting instead that the Aryans probably originated in Europe.

## Governance and Administration
Historical records indicate a specific structure of power within the Persian empire. According to Herodotus, the Great King employed a system of double appointment and division of power, a practice that appears to have been maintained until the empire's end. 

## Military and External Relations
Persia engaged in various conflicts and alliances with neighboring nations. The Chionites, who may have been the same as the Huns, were known to make incursions into Asia and were frequently at war with the Persians. In contrast, the Segestans, who inhabited a large, level country south of Khorasan and west of Hindostan, remained an independent nation and an ally of Persia more than eighty years after the victory of Bahram.

In later periods, the Mongol regime in Persia included the Ḳaraunahs. Initially a Mongol tribe forming a *Tuman* (a corps of 10,000) in the Mongol army, they later became savage freebooters based on the eastern frontiers of Persia. The Ḳaraunahs were noted for their use of Naphtha Fire in warfare. Rashiduddin identifies them as a branch of the Kunguráts and notes that their *Tuman* formed the *Injú* (peculium) of Arghún Khan.

## Material Culture and Technology
Ancient Persia is noted for its metallurgy and musical instruments. Persian sabres were highly esteemed; while some believed the steel came from mines in Khorasan, other sources indicate that the steel for these blades arrived as disks from Lahore. Persian smiths combined this with Indian steel, which was considered more tractable.

In terms of music, evidence of the bag-pipe has been traced to ancient Persia. Excavations by Jacques de Morgan at a *tell* (mound) in Susa uncovered terracotta figures of musicians dating from the 8th century B.C. Two of these figures appear to be playing bag-pipes, featuring a visible curved chaunter and a bag indicated under the arm.

## Historical Documentation
Much of the knowledge regarding Persia's attempts to subdue Greece was compiled by the historian Herodotus. To provide context for this history, he wrote accounts of the geography and peoples of Persia, as well as Egypt, Assyria, Babylonia, and Scythia. His work was eventually received with great applause in Athens.
