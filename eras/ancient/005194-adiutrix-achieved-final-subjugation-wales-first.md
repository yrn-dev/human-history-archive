# Roman Conquest of Wales and Yorkshire

The subjugation of Wales and Yorkshire represented a significant and protracted phase of Roman military activity in Britain. While other regions were brought under control, the wilder tribes and hills of these areas offered fiercer resistance, leading to thirty years of intermittent hill fighting between A.D. 47 and 79.

## Military Strategy and Fortifications
The precise steps taken during the conquest are not fully known, but the Roman strategy relied heavily on the establishment of legionary fortresses and strategic outposts. Fortresses were positioned to face the Welsh hills at Caerleon, Chester, and Wroxeter, though the site at Wroxeter was used only for a time. In the northeast, a fortress was established at Lincoln.

The broader method of conquest involved placing small detached forts in strategic positions. These forts were garrisoned by either 500 or 1,000 men. This expansion was not without difficulty; the Roman invaders suffered disasters inflicted by the "vigorous barbarians" they sought to conquer.

## Early Progress and Setbacks
Early Roman successes included the overrunning of Flintshire, known for its lead mines, and Monmouthshire. In A.D. 60, Suetonius Paulinus reached Anglesea. However, overall progress was delayed by the great revolt of Boadicea, which affected a large portion of the nominally conquered Lowlands. Although her rising was crushed, the government remained hesitant to move its garrisons forward for a period. Further complications arose around A.D. 67, when the needs of the empire necessitated the withdrawal of the Fourteenth Legion.

## The Decisive Decade (A.D. 70-80)
The period between A.D. 70 and 80 proved decisive for the region. The Roman army was restored to its proper strength through the addition of Legio II Adiutrix. Under the command of a series of three able generals, the Romans achieved the first conquest of Yorkshire and the final subjugation of Wales. During this time, the legionary fortress at York was established as a substitute for the one at Lincoln.

## Further Northern Campaigns
Following these successes, the general Julius Agricola moved toward the farther north in A.D. 80. He established a frontier between the Forth and the Clyde, guarded by a line of forts, including those at Bar Hill and Camelon (near Falkirk). Agricola advanced into Caledonia and achieved a "famous victory" at Mons Graupius, located likely near the confluence of the Isla and the Tay. Evidence of a Roman encampment from this era exists at Inchtuthill.

Despite Agricola's ambitions to invade Ireland, the home government intervened. The government was averse to costly and fruitless campaigns and required Legio II Adiutrix for work in other locations. Consequently, both the legion and the governor were recalled, and the more northerly nominal conquests were abandoned.

## Cultural Context of Wales
Among the peoples of Wales, the bards—ancient Celtic poets—held a position of great influence. They formed an organized society with hereditary privileges and were exempt from military service and taxes. Their primary duties were to sing hymns of praise to God and celebrate the victories of their people. The society of bards was regulated by laws first formulated by Hywell Dha and later revised by Gruffydd ap Conan. Even after the Roman conquest of Wales, the bards continued to hold great festivals known as *Eisteddfodau*, which were summoned by royal commission.
