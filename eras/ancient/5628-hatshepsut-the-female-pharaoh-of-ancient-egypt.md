# Hatshepsut: The Female Pharaoh of Ancient Egypt

## Overview
Hatshepsut remains one of the most fascinating figures of the 18th Dynasty during the New Kingdom of Egypt. While she initially began her tenure as regent for her stepson, Thutmose III, she eventually took the unprecedented step of declaring herself Pharaoh. 

Unlike many of her successors who focused on military conquest, Hatshepsut’s reign is characterized by economic prosperity, architectural innovation, and the restoration of trade networks. She is perhaps most famous for her expedition to the Land of Punt, which brought exotic goods like myrrh, frankincense, and gold back to Egypt. However, her legacy was nearly erased; years after her death, many of her images were chiseled away and her name removed from monuments. Historians debate the motive behind this *damnatio memoriae*—some argue it was a personal vendetta by Thutmose III, while others suggest it was a political move to ensure a smooth succession and maintain the traditional patriarchal image of the kingship.

## Key Facts
*   **Divine Right:** To justify her rule as a woman in a male-dominated role, Hatshepsut claimed she was the daughter of the god Amun, asserting that the deity had visited her father in the guise of a human to conceive her.
*   **Iconography:** Over time, her official portraits shifted. While early depictions showed her as a woman, later statues depicted her with the traditional pharaonic false beard and masculine musculature to emphasize her authority.
*   **Architectural Legacy:** Her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari is considered a masterpiece of ancient architecture, blending seamlessly into the limestone cliffs of the Theban hills.
*   **Economic Focus:** She prioritized the rebuilding of shrines and the establishment of trade routes over the expansion of borders through warfare.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Punt Expedition:** The expedition to Punt was so successful that Hatshepsut had the exotic myrrh trees transplanted directly into the courts of her temple, marking one of the earliest recorded attempts at botanical transplantation.
2. **The Obelisks:** She commissioned massive granite obelisks at the Temple of Karnak, which were among the tallest in the world at the time.
3. **The Hidden Mummy:** For decades, her identity was a mystery until 2007, when researchers used DNA analysis and a molar tooth found in a wooden box to positively identify her mummy.
