# Moulded Bricks and Architectural Projections

Moulded bricks are a specialized category of brickwork used in architecture for the construction of cornices, string courses, plinths, labels, and copings. These bricks are produced in various patterns across different classes of brick. In many architectural applications, moulded bricks are preferred over rubbers because they offer greater durability and eliminate the need for the labor associated with cutting.

## Brick Types and Manufacturing
Bricks are produced from tempered clay using either hand or machine methods. Hand-making involves pressing clay into wooden or metal moulds or four-sided cases, with allowances made for shrinkage during firing. To prevent sticking, moulds are dipped in water or a mixture of water and sand. A "strike" (a strip of wood) is used to level the top, and a "stock-board" with an elevated tongue may be used to create a "frog" (hollow) in the bottom of the brick. Machine-made bricks are categorized as plastic or semi-plastic; the latter are made from ground clay or shale damp enough to adhere under pressure.

Modern brick presses are typically made of iron and consist of a moulding box, a plunger or die on a ram to provide pressure, and a mechanism to eject the finished brick. Some presses, particularly those used for semi-plastic clay, utilize two rams to press the brick simultaneously from the top and bottom.

Other specialized bricks include:
*   **Blue Bricks:** Produced chiefly in North Wales and South Staffordshire, these are vitrified, heavy, and impervious. They are used for engineering work requiring high compressional resistance, as well as for coping, channelling, and paving.
*   **Fire-bricks:** Designed to withstand great heat for use with furnaces; these must be laid with fire-clay instead of cement or lime mortar.
*   **Glazed Bricks:** These may be salt-glazed (brown in colour, created by throwing salt on bricks in the kiln) or enamelled (dipped in a coloured slip before burning). They are used for sanitary purposes, decoration, and areas requiring reflected light.
*   **Voussoirs:** Bricks shaped specifically for arches and sewer work.

## Cornices and Bed-moulds
A cornice is an architectural projection at the top of a wall designed to throw rainwater from the roof beyond the face of the building. In classic architecture, the cornice is the upper part of an order's entablature and is subdivided into the cymatium, corona, and bed-mould. The bed-mould consists of a congeries of mouldings located under the projecting part of the cornice.

Cornices are not limited to rooflines; they are also used to crown features such as windows and doors, both internally and externally, as well as pieces of furniture. In Roman doorways, cornices are a standard feature. For example, the doorways at Baalbec and Palmyra feature cornices and richly carved friezes.

## Plinths, Bases, and Ground Tables
The base is the lowest member of a column or shaft. In primitive Greek work, this consisted of three small steps at Mycenae or a stone plinth, as seen in Tiryns and Crete. In the 15th century, bases were sometimes given an unusual height with two stages, where the lower stage served as a base course or plinth.

The base of a wall, also known as a ground table, refers to the mouldings that run round a building just above the ground and around its buttresses. A flat band located between the upper mouldings and the plinth is sometimes carved with shields or panelled, an example of which is found at the Henry VII Chapel at Westminster.

## String Courses, Labels, and Decorative Elements
A string course is a horizontal architectural feature that can be formed by moulded bricks. A specific variation is the "corbel table," which is a projecting moulded string course supported by a range of corbels. In Lombard work, these arcaded corbel tables were used as decoration to break up wall surfaces and subdivide storeys.

Labels are another application for moulded brickwork. A "Venetian dentil"—a form of ornament copied from the Byzantine dentil in Santa Sophia, Constantinople—was utilized as a string course and as a label round arches. 

The dentil itself is a small, tooth-shaped block used as a repeating ornament in the bed-mould of a cornice. Originating as a stone reproduction of timber construction (representing the end of a rafter), it is found in the Lycian tombs, Persian tombs, and the tomb of Darius (c. 500 B.C.). In the Pantheon at Rome, where the sinkings that divide the dentils were never cut, the feature is referred to as a dentil-band.
