# Britannia Prima, Secunda, Flavia and Maxima Caesariensis

The administrative organization of the Roman province of Britain underwent several transformations over the course of its history, evolving from a single unified territory into a complex system of smaller provinces.

## Geographical and Social Divisions
Geographically, Britain was composed of two distinct regions. The first consisted of the midlands, south, and east, characterized by comparatively flat lowlands. These areas were well-suited for agriculture and allowed for easy intercourse with the rest of the Roman empire and the continent. Consequently, these lowlands were conquered quickly and easily and became the center of civil life, featuring country houses, villages, and towns. Military presence in these regions was minimal, limited primarily to fortresses on the edges of the hills and a 4th-century chain of forts known as the Saxon Shore, designed to defend the south-east coast.

The second region comprised the northern England districts, Wales, and the hills of Cornwall and Devon. These uplands were mountainous, often rising more than 600 ft. above the sea, and were scarred by deep valleys and gorges. Such terrain made the regions difficult for armies to traverse and ill-suited for peaceful agriculture. These areas were not completely subdued until the end of the 2nd century. In contrast to the lowlands, civil life was almost entirely absent here; no country houses or towns have been found west of Monmouthshire or more than 20 m. north of York. Instead, the uplands functioned as an extensive military frontier, consisting of strategic roads and forts.

## Administrative Evolution
Rome did not initially align its administrative partitions with these geographical divisions. At the start, the entire province was governed by a single *legatus Augusti* of consular standing. This arrangement was later altered by Septimius Severus, who divided the territory into two provinces: Superior and Inferior. The boundary between these two likely ran from the Mersey to the Humber, though the duration of this specific arrangement is unknown.

By the 5th century, the administrative structure had shifted again. The territory was divided into five provinces:
*   Britannia Prima
*   Britannia Secunda
*   Flavia Caesariensis
*   Maxima Caesariensis
*   Valentia (which existed for a period of time)

These provinces were ruled by *consulares* and *praesides* under the authority of a *vicartus*. Regarding the specific details of these divisions, the archive's records indicate that Britannia Prima included Cirencester.
