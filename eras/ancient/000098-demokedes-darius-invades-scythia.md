# Demokedes and the Scythian Expedition of Darius

## The Surgeon Demokedes
Demokedes was a surgeon from Kroton whose life was marked by a series of fortunes and misfortunes. He was carried as a slave to Susa, where he was held among the slaves of Oroetes. During this time, King Darius suffered a severe foot injury after leaping from his horse during a chase. The Egyptian surgeons, who were considered the leaders of their profession, failed to cure the king and instead aggravated his pain, leaving Darius without sleep for seven days and nights.

Demokedes was brought before the king in rags and chains. Although he initially pretended to be ignorant of surgery, he admitted his skill after Darius threatened him with a pricking instrument and a scourge. Demokedes successfully treated the foot, restoring it to a sound state and alleviating the king's pain. In gratitude, Darius rewarded him munificently, presenting him with two sets of gold chains to commemorate his initial state of imprisonment. He was also given a splendid house, furniture, and the privilege of being a companion at the king's table. Furthermore, Demokedes was able to save the lives of the unsuccessful Egyptian surgeons and an Eleian prophet who had been his fellow prisoner.

## The Influence of Atossa
Despite his rewards, Demokedes desired to return to Greece, a favor Darius refused to grant. Demokedes eventually secured his liberty through the influence of Atossa, the wife of Darius. Atossa developed a tumor on her breast which she kept secret until it became too severe to conceal. Demokedes agreed to cure her on the condition that she grant him any request he might make, provided it was not indecent.

After the successful cure, Demokedes instructed Atossa in a stratagem to persuade Darius. Atossa suggested to the king that he should expand the power and splendor of the empire by turning his forces against Greece, specifically mentioning her desire to have the maidens of Athens, Sparta, Corinth, and Argos as slaves. She pointed out that Demokedes, the Greek who had cured the king's foot, was the best person to provide information on the region.

## The Survey of Greece
Darius agreed to the request and sent Demokedes along with fifteen confidential Persians to survey the coasts and cities of Greece. The group departed from Sidon in Phoenicia with two armed triremes and a large storeship. They examined principal locations, including the insular and Asiatic Greeks, Euboea, Attica, the Peloponnesus, and Korkyra, eventually reaching Italy.

Upon arriving at Tarentum, Demokedes executed a plan to escape. He persuaded Aristophilides, the king of Tarentum, to seize the fifteen Persians as spies and remove the rudders from their ships. Demokedes then fled to his home in Kroton. Although Aristophilides later released the Persians, they captured Demokedes in the Kroton market-place. However, the citizens of Kroton protected him and robbed the Persians of their storeship. The Persian envoys, unable to proceed, suffered shipwreck near the Iapygian cape and became slaves until they were ransomed by a Tarentine exile named Gillus, who returned them to Susa.

## The Invasion of Scythia
Between 516 and 514 B.C., Darius directed his conquering appetites toward Scythia rather than Greece. His naval force was composed of insular and Asiatic Greeks. Upon reaching the Danube, Darius ordered the Ionians to construct a bridge, which he then used to cross into Scythian territory.

The historical accounts of the march into Scythia are contradictory. Herodotus describes a vast host moving across twelve degrees of longitude, crossing numerous rivers including the Tyras, Hypanis, Borysthenes, Hypakyris, Gerrhos, and Tanais, despite a lack of roads and agriculture. Conversely, Ktesias claims Darius marched for fifteen days before discovering that the Scythian bow was larger than the Persian bow; intimidated by this, Darius allegedly fled back to the Danube, losing one-tenth of his army and breaking the bridge behind him.

While Darius was delayed in his return, the Ionian despots remained in guard of the Danube bridge. They preserved the bridge to ensure Darius could recross, viewing this as a means to support their own dominion at home, thereby losing an opportunity for emancipation from Persian rule.

## Persian Expansion and Consequences
Concurrent with these events, the Persians conquered Thrace as far as the river Strymon. Histiaeus was given Myrkinus, where he founded a prosperous colony before being recalled to Asia by Darius. Megabazus conquered the Paeonians and Macedonians, though Persian envoys in Macedonia were murdered due to their insolence. Additionally, the Persian general Otanes conquered the Pelasgian populations of Imbros and Lemnos, though the latter two were later captured by Miltiades and the Athenians.

Had Darius invaded Greece instead of Scythia during this period, it is suggested that Grecian independence would have likely perished. At the time, Athens was governed by the Peisistratids, and Hippias likely would have accepted Persian dominion to strengthen his own rule. It was only because the Persian invasion of Greece was delayed until 490 B.C. that the Athenian character underwent a revolution in energy and organization, allowing them to offer significant resistance.
