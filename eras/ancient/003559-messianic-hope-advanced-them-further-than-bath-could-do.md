# Messianic Hope

The concept of Messianic hope appears across various historical and religious contexts within the archive, ranging from early baptismal rites and theological commentaries to the analysis of ancient historical texts.

## Baptism and the Messianic Hope
In the context of the origin of Christian baptism, a distinction is made between the baptism of John and the baptism of proselytes. For publicans and soldiers who sought out John in the wilderness due to remorse, his baptism served as a purification from their past, making it identical to the proselyte's bath in that regard. However, the baptism of John advanced these individuals further than the proselyte's bath could by filling them with Messianic hope and raising them up to be children unto Abraham. This process assured them of a place in a kingdom of God that was soon to be established, notably without requiring the circumcision that was mandatory for the ordinary proselyte.

For those who were already Jews, the baptism of John did not carry the significance of a proselyte's baptism. Instead, it aligned with a baptism used by Jews who sought to consecrate their lives through a stricter practice and study of the law. Epictetus noted that a true understanding of Judaism required knowledge of "the baptized Jew." Records from Acts xix. 4 indicate that John baptized in the name of the coming Messiah, though he did not identify this figure with Jesus of Nazareth. This identification was later provided by the apostolic age, with formulas such as "into Christ Jesus" or "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ." Paul described these as being equivalent to being "into the death of Christ Jesus," suggesting the faithful were raised from death into everlasting life.

## Messianic Ideas in Theological Literature
The Messianic idea also served as the foundation for later religious expositions. George Horne (1730-1792), an English divine and former president of Magdalen College, Oxford, published a *Commentary on the Psalms* in 1771. This work consisted of a series of expositions that were based specifically on the Messianic idea.

## National Ideals and the Israelites
In the history of the Israelites, the foundation of the united monarchy under David is cited as the greatest advance in their history. David is described as a national hero who combined the noblest elements of the Hebrew genius to build a monarchy representing the highest possible conception of national life. Although this structure decayed under his successor and eventually fell apart, it continued to exist in the hearts of the people as a proud memory of the past and as a prophetic ideal of future glory.
