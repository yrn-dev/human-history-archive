# Damia

Damia is a deity associated with fertility and the earth, appearing in both Greek and Roman religious contexts. In the Roman tradition, she is linked to the goddess Bona Dea, an ancient deity of fruitfulness concerning both women and the earth.

## Roman Cult and Identification
In Rome, the goddess Bona Dea was identified with Fauna and, through later syncretism, with Maia and Ops. The connection to Maia is attributed to the fact that the dedication day for Bona Dea's temple on the Aventine was May 1st. The cult of Bona Dea was characterized by strict exclusivity; her temple was cared for and the cult attended by women only. A similar restriction applied to a second celebration held in December at the house of a magistrate possessing *imperium*. This specific celebration became well-known due to the political consequences following the profanation of its mysteries by P. Clodius in 62 B.C.

The cult of Bona Dea featured specific taboos against the use of myrtle and wine. The temple housed snakes, which served as symbols of the medicinal art, as well as herbs possessing healing properties. The sacrificial victim for this deity was a *porca*, a common practice in other fertility cults. This victim was referred to as *damium*, and the goddess herself was known as Damia, while her priestess was called a *damiatrix*.

## Greek Origins and Influence
The names Damia and *damiatrix* are considered to be almost certainly Greek. Damia was worshipped in several locations across Greece, as well as at Tarentum, where a festival known as *Dameia* was held. It is highly probable that the Greek cult of Damia was engrafted onto the original Roman goddess, possibly following the Roman conquest of Tarentum in 272 B.C. Due to this blending, it is no longer possible to clearly distinguish the Roman elements from the Greek elements within the cult, though it remains intelligible as the worship of an Earth-goddess with associated mysteries.

## Worship at Ægina
In Greece, specifically at Ægina, Damia was worshipped alongside Auxesia. According to Herodotus, the Eginetans stole the images of Damia and Auxesia from the Epidaurians and established them at a place called Oia, located about twenty furlongs from their city. 

The worship of these deities at Oia involved sacrifices and choruses of women. These rites were characterized by "scurrilous jesting," a form of unrestrained jocularity similar to the rites of Dêmêtêr in Sicily. For each deity, ten men were appointed to provide the choruses. These choruses did not speak evil of any man, but instead directed their comments toward the local women. The Epidaurians practiced these same rites, in addition to other rites that were not to be divulged.
