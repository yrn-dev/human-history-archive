# The Basilica of St Paul Without the Walls

The Basilica of St Paul without the walls is one of the world-famous Roman basilicas, cited alongside St Peter and St John Lateran as the earliest and grandest examples of the basilican type. Dedicated in 324 A.D., the structure underwent a period of rebuilding between 388 and 423. Following this, the building remained substantially unaltered, though it existed in a state of sad neglect until 1823, when a disastrous fire reduced the nave to a calcined ruin.

## Architectural Plan and Dimensions
The plan and dimensions of the Basilica of St Paul were almost identical to those of the old Basilica of St Peter. The interior featured a long and wide nave that terminated in a semicircular apse. The layout included a narthex, a nave, side aisles, an altar, a bema, and an apse. 

Unlike some basilicas that featured a single aisle on each side, St Paul's was characterized by having two aisles on each side of the nave. These aisles were separated from the nave by colonnades consisting of marble pillars that supported arches.

## Structural Features
The Basilica of St Paul incorporated several specific architectural elements common to the grand Roman basilicas of its era:

*   **The Transept:** The colonnades of the church ceased some distance from the end of the building, which allowed for the formation of a transverse aisle, or transept. This transept was divided from the main nave by a wide arch.
*   **Decoration:** The face and soffit of the arch dividing the transept from the nave were richly decorated with mosaics. In such arrangements, the crown of the arch often featured a bust of Christ or the holy lamb resting upon the altar, flanked by the twenty-four elders, the seven candlesticks, and evangelistic symbols.
*   **The Apse:** The church terminated in a semicircular apse, which was spanned by another arch. From the Carolingian period onward, this specific arch was referred to as the "arch of triumph" due to the suspension of a cross from it.
*   **Clerestory:** Above the pillars, the clerestory wall rose to a great height and was pierced in its upper section by a range of plain, round-headed windows. The area between these windows and the colonnade—later known as the triforium-space—was typically decorated with a series of mosaic pictures arranged in panels.
