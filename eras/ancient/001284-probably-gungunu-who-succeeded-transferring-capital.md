# Transfers of Capital Cities in World History

The relocation of a capital city often reflects shifts in political power, dynastic changes, or strategic needs. Historical records indicate such transfers across various civilizations, including Babylonia, Japan, Brazil, and the Mongol Empire.

## Babylonia and the Dynasty of Ur
In Babylonia, the seat of supreme power shifted southwards to Ur and Isin following the sudden fall of Sargon's empire. While it is generally assumed that two dynasties reigned at Ur and claimed suzerainty over other Babylonian states, clear proof of multiple dynasties is lacking. It is probable that Gungunu, dated to approximately 2500 B.C., succeeded in transferring the Babylonian capital from Isin to Ur, although his specific position within the dynasty or dynasties remains uncertain. 

Gungunu was followed by successors such as Ur-Gur, a prolific builder who restored temples for Bel at Nippur, Ishtar at Erech, the Sun-god at Larsa, and the Moon-god at Ur. Ur-Gur was succeeded by his son Dungi, whose reign exceeded 51 years. During Dungi's reign, Gudea served as the *patesi* (high-priest) of Lagash. Gudea is noted for importing materials for his statues and buildings from across western Asia, including gold and precious stones from the desert between Egypt and Palestine, copper from northern Arabia, and cedar from the Amanus mountains.

## Imperial Japan: Nara to Kyoto
In Japan, the transfer of the capital from Nara to Kyoto occurred during the reign of Kwammu. Nara, located in the province of Yamato, had served as the capital for seventy-five years across eight reigns, evolving into a major center of trade and religion. Kyoto is situated in the neighboring province of Yamashiro, approximately twenty miles away.

Prior to the eighth century, it was common custom to change the site of the capital upon the accession of a new sovereign. At that time, the term *miyako* (metropolis) primarily meant "Imperial residence," and such moves did not require national effort. However, by Kwammu's time, the scale of Nara made the transfer a significant sacrifice. Motives for the move included a desire to reduce the influence of priestly fanaticism and foreign creeds that threatened to overshadow the Imperial Court and undermine the Shinto cult. Additionally, the city of Nara may have been too small for the nation's growing prosperity. This transfer placed extravagant demands on the working classes, and despite Kwammu's efforts to correct local abuses and dismiss provincial officials, the systemic oppression of the lower classes by the upper classes persisted.

## The Mongol Empire
The Mongol capital underwent several relocations as the empire expanded. In 1206, Chinghiz chose Karákorum (known to the Chinese as *Ho-lin*) as his capital. In 1235, Okkodai raised a wall around the city and constructed a palace called *Wang an*. Later, in 1256, Mangu Kaan decided to transfer the seat of government to Shangtu (Kaipingfu), located north of Peking. Subsequently, in 1260, Kúblái transferred the capital to *Ta-Tu* (Peking). By 1264, the Mongol capital had been officially transferred from central Asia to Peking (Cambaluc).

## Other Regional Transfers
In Brazil, the capital of the state of Minas Geraes was transferred from Ouro Preto to Bello-Horizonte (or Minas) in 1898. The new city was constructed by the state on an open plateau and equipped with gas, water, tramway services, and public buildings before the seat of government was moved. The cost of this transfer was approximately L1,000,000.

In the British Shan States, the headquarters of both civil and military administration were transferred from Keng Tung to Loi Ngwe Lông. The original site was found to be very unhealthy, leading to the move to a ridge 6,500 feet above sea level, located 12 miles south of the capital.

## Territorial Transfers
Beyond the movement of capitals, historical records note the forced transfer of populations and territories. In Greece, Dionysius transferred the lands of Rhegium, Kaulonia, and Hipponium to the Lokrians. While the free citizens of Rhegium were transported to Syracuse for sale, the Lokrians became masters of the Calabrian peninsula south of the Gulf of Squillace. In the Near East, Tiglath Pileser III directed the transfer of the Israelites to Media, an event involving the "Lost Ten Tribes," which was likely carried out by Sargon II.
