# Status of the Baptizer in the Early Church

The regulations regarding who was authorized to perform baptism and the administration of the Lord's Supper varied across different early Christian traditions and circumstances, with a strong emphasis in several circles on the necessity of ecclesiastical authority.

## Episcopal and Clerical Authority
In the writings of Ignatius, the bishop's authority was central to the administration of the church. Ignatius asserted that it was not lawful to perform baptisms or to hold an *agape* (the Lord's Supper or love-feast) without the presence or authority of the bishop. This insistence on the "three orders"—bishops, presbyters, and deacons—was viewed as essential; Ignatius maintained that without these orders, no church was worthy of the name. He argued that respect should be given to the bishop as to God, to the presbyters as the college of apostles and the council of God, and to the deacons as to Jesus Christ.

Other early writers echoed these restrictions. Tertullian reserved the right to confer baptism and admit candidates to the rite specifically to the bishop or the *summus sacerdos*. Cyprian extended this right to both priests and bishops. While deacons in outlying areas of Christendom claimed the right to baptize, official churches generally accorded this privilege to presbyters alone. Furthermore, the performance of the seal or confirmation was restricted exclusively to bishops.

## Exceptions and Alternative Practices
Despite the official restrictions of the established churches, different practices existed in other communities and emergency situations. In the Montanist churches, women performed baptisms. Evidence of female baptism also exists in the earliest church and within the Caucasus. Notable examples include St. Nino, the female evangelist of Georgia, who baptized King Mirian, and St. Thekla, who is recorded as having baptized herself in her own blood.

Additionally, the church recognized practical exceptions for urgent cases. In instances of imminent death, or when dealing with new-born children, it was often considered necessary for a woman or a layman to perform the baptism.

## The Role of Ignatius of Antioch
Ignatius, the bishop of Antioch and one of the "Apostolic Fathers," is a primary source for these early ecclesiastical requirements. His focus on the authority of the ministry was largely a response to "centrifugal forces" and theological differences that threatened the unity of the church. Following the era of the apostles, their successors did not inherit the same authority, leading to a tendency for churches to split into sections. Ignatius sought to resist this trend by making the ministry the center of church life to ensure doctrinal purity.

It is noted that in the letters of Ignatius, the ministry is not sacerdotal, nor is there any trace of the later doctrine of apostolical succession. His "bishop" is seen as representing what would be considered a modern pastor. Beyond church order, Ignatius fought against heresies such as Docetism—which claimed Christ had a phantom body—by emphasizing that Christ was truly born, ate, drank, was persecuted under Pontius Pilate, and was truly raised from the dead. He also vehemently protested any return to Judaism, stating it was monstrous to practice Judaism while talking of Jesus Christ.

## Historical Context of Ignatius
Very little is known of Ignatius's personal career. He described himself as "the last of the Antiochene Christians" and used a term suggesting he may have been converted from paganism abruptly and late in life. He bore the surname Theophorus ("bearing God" or "God-clad"). While later tradition suggested he was the child Christ took in his arms, this is regarded as a romantic theory.

Ignatius was the second successor of Peter in the bishopric of Antioch. He wrote his letters while being sent under guard to Rome to fight beasts in the amphitheatre, a sentence that indicates he was not a Roman citizen. Although it is presumed he was martyred in Rome, early evidence for this is lacking.
