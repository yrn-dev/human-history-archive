# Growth of the Persian Empire

The Persian Empire emerged from internal struggles between the Medes and Persians, culminating in 550 B.C. with the accession of Cyrus "the Persian" to the throne of Cyaxares. At the time of his rise, the native Persians occupied the territory of Iran, situated between the Tigris and the Indus. By 550 B.C., Cyrus ruled an empire extending from Persia to the boundaries of Lydia and potentially as far as India.

## The Conflict with Lydia
The growing power of the Persians was recognized by Crœsus, the Lydian king. After a period of mourning for his son, Crœsus sought to cut short the growth of Persian power before they became too great. To ensure success, Crœsus solicited an alliance with Sparta and consulted the oracle at Delphi. Despite a triumphant reply from the oracle and the munificence Crœsus showed toward it, the conflict ended in his defeat.

Crœsus crossed the Halys to attack the Persians, but Cyrus conducted a rapid march to Sardis. Following the siege and capture of the city, Crœsus became a prisoner of Cyrus. The fate of Crœsus served as an impressive lesson to the Greek mind regarding the Mœræ, or Fates.

## Expansion into Asia Minor
Following the conquest of Lydia, the Asiatic Greeks attempted to seek aid from Sparta, but these efforts were in vain. After Cyrus suppressed a Lydian revolt and departed Sardis, the Persians began the conquest of Ionia. This operation was initially led by the general Mazarês and later by Harpagus.

During this expansion, the Persians mastered the southern and western portions of Asia Minor without considerable resistance, including the territory within the Halys previously ruled by Crœsus. In the town of Xanthus, the population perished entirely, with the exception of eighty families who were accidentally absent. The Persians eventually achieved the entire conquest of Asia Minor. Consequently, the tributes from the conquered Greeks were redirected from Sardis to Ekbatana. While Harpagus managed these regions, Cyrus expanded his conquests further into Assyria and Upper Asia.

## The Empire of Darius I
The empire reached a peak of territorial extent under Darius I, becoming larger than any preceding empire. Its boundaries included:
* All of Asia Minor and Syria (incorporating the former Hittite and Lydian empires).
* All the old Babylonian and Assyrian empires.
* Egypt, Media, and Persia.
* The Caucasus and Caspian regions.
* Potential extensions into India to the Indus.

Of all the peoples in the Near East, only the nomadic Arabians did not pay tribute to the satraps, who served as provincial governors.

## Administration and Society
The organization of the empire under Darius I was characterized by a high level of efficiency. The state established a system of royal posts and great arterial roads that connected the various provinces. Government messengers and travelers with government permits could utilize post horses stationed at regular intervals to facilitate travel.

While the empire required the payment of tribute and the recognition of imperial right-of-way, local governments maintained a considerable degree of local freedom. This structure prevented internecine conflict, a period of stability that the Greek cities of the mainland of Asia shared as the "Persian Peace."

Regarding the character of the old Zoroastrian Persians, historical accounts from Herodotus suggest they were highly receptive to foreign customs and luxuries. This openness distinguished them from later Orientals, whose tenacity of customs is associated with the growth of Mohammedanism. In terms of military skill, Persians are noted for their proficiency in using the bow and firelock while on horseback.
