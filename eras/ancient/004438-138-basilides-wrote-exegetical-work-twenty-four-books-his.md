# Basilides

Basilides was a prominent exponent of Gnosticism who lived in Alexandria, likely during the first decades of the 2nd century. While Eusebius's *Chronicle* dates his first appearance to A.D. 133, other evidence suggests his activity may have begun earlier; Agrippa Castor, who lived under the reign of Hadrian (117-138), wrote a polemic against him.

## Literary Works
Basilides authored an exegetical work consisting of twenty-four books based on "his" gospel, although the identity of this specific gospel is unknown. Fragments of his writings, including a portion of the 13th book of the *Exegetica*, have been preserved and published by scholars such as Hilgenfeld and L. Traube.

Additional writings associated with his school include works by his son, Isidorus, titled *Peri prosphuous psuches*, *Ethika*, and *Exegetika* concerning the prophet Parchor.

## Theological System and Salvation
The system of Basilides presents a vision of salvation centered on the restoration of all things. According to the accounts, the world reached a state of confusion so severe that the supreme God dispatched his *Nous*—also referred to as Christ—into the world. Clemens describes this Saviour as a *diakonos* or *pneuma diakonoumenon*.

Basilides' conception of the relationship between this Saviour and Jesus of Nazareth is a subject of varying accounts:
* Basilides himself acknowledged an earthly Jesus but denied the principle of his sinlessness.
* Irenaeus suggests the Saviour appeared only as a phantasm.
* The *Excerpta ex Theodoto* claims the *Diakonos* descended upon Jesus during his baptism in the form of a dove.

Because of these beliefs, the followers of Basilides observed the day of the baptism of Jesus, known as the day of the *epiphaneia*, as a high festival. Salvation is defined as the separation of that which was combined for evil, which Clemens associates with a great separation of the sexes.

## Gnostic Classification and Influence
Basilides' doctrine is characterized as a form of Gnosticism closely related to that of Satornil and Persian dualism. He is distinguished from most other Gnostic sects—with the possible exception of Jewish-Christian Gnosticism—by the total absence of the idea of a fall within the godhead or the figure of the fallen female god, Sophia Achamoth. His work is viewed as a development of Iranian dualism, which later influenced the religious system of Mani.

There is scholarly controversy regarding which accounts of his system are original. Some sources rely on the *Syntagma* of Hippolytus (preserved in Philaster, Epiphanius, and Pseudo-Tertullian) and the writings of Irenaeus and Clemens Alexandrinus. Others point to the *Philosophumena* of Hippolytus, which presents a different, more monistic account, though recent opinion tends to favor the former sources.

## Later Followers and Magic
Among the later adherents of Basilides' school, magic became a determining factor. These followers believed that knowing the secret names of the rulers of the various heavens would allow a soul to pass through those heavens after death to reach the supreme God. 

In this framework, redemption was conceived as the revelation of mystic names. These followers believed Christ possessed a mystic name (*Caulacau* or *QAW LAQAW*) that enabled his descent to earth and subsequent ascent to the Father. This magical tendency is associated with the name Abraxas and Abraxas gems, though it remains undecided if Basilides himself introduced these elements into Gnosticism.

## Historical Reception
By the end of the 2nd century, as the Christian Bible's structure of the Old and New Testaments became established, writings associated with Basilides, along with those of Valentinus, Marcion, and Montanus, were condemned. This is evidenced in the Muratorian Fragment on the Canon, a list of New Testament books roughly dated to the end of that century.
