# Ancient Languages and Epigraphy of Spain

## Early Tribal Geography
From the 5th century B.C., the earliest notices regarding the geography of Spain describe the region as being occupied by a collection of tribes. These were primarily distinguished as Celts, Celtiberi, and Iberi. These groups lacked cohesion and were frequently at war with one another, moving constantly unless they were temporarily united to face a foreign enemy. As Mediterranean civilization advanced, the ruder of these tribes were driven toward the north.

## Ancient Literature and Laws
In the southern region of Baetica, the writers Strabo and Trogus noted that the tribes possessed a literature, poems of ancient date, and written laws. However, none of these literary works have survived to the present day.

## Epigraphic Evidence and Language
The available evidence of these early cultures is limited to marks on pottery, legends on coins, inscriptions, and megalithic monuments. These records utilize alphabets that differ slightly and are distributed across six geographical districts. While these inscriptions still await an interpreter, they indicate that a general language was once spoken throughout the entirety of Spain, as well as across a short distance on the northern slope of the Pyrenees.

The letters used in these inscriptions are clearly of Levant origin. However, the specific alphabets to which they belong, and any potential connection they may have to the Basque language, remain undetermined.

## Basque Linguistic Connections
Following the Renaissance, classical scholars observed that certain ancient Spanish toponyms—though transcribed by foreigners such as Latin and Greek writers who were ignorant of the local language—strongly resemble actual place-names in Basque. Examples include Iriberry and Iliberis, as well as Oloron and Iluro (corresponding to Mendigorry and Mondiculeia), where the physical site explains the name.

These observations were noted by figures such as Larramendi (1760), Juan B. Erro (1806), and Andres de Poza (1587). However, it was W. von Humboldt in 1821 who first brought these facts to the attention of Europe. 

The theory that a people speaking a language akin to Basque were extended throughout Spain, and possibly into Sardinia and Sicily, has been accepted by the majority of students. Nevertheless, some competent Basque scholars deny this extension. Furthermore, a conclusive proof of a connection between the Basques and the Celtiberians or Iberians, whether in terms of language or race, has not been established.
