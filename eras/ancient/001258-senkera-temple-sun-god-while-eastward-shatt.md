# Senkera

Senkera, known in antiquity as Larsa, was a city located in the region eastward of the Euphrates and southward of Sippara. It is situated in a geographical area that included other notable cities such as Kutha, Babylon, Nippur (the site of the great sanctuary of El-lil), and Uruk.

## Religious Significance
Senkera was the site of a temple dedicated to the sun-god. In the broader context of Babylonia, temples were often "oriented," meaning the shrine and entrance were built to face a specific direction. In Babylonian temples, this orientation was most frequently due east, facing the sunrise during the equinoxes of March 21st and September 21st. This timing coincided with the period when the Tigris and Euphrates rivers experienced their floods.

## Scientific and Mathematical Achievements
The archive contains evidence of significant mathematical and mechanical knowledge associated with Senkera. Tablets have been discovered at the site containing calculations of squares and cubes ranging from 1 to 60. 

This mathematical proficiency was part of a wider Babylonian tradition of scientific inquiry. The Babylonians developed a system of ciphering and utilized a duodecimal system, employing units such as the *soss* (60), the *ner* (600), and the *sar* (3600). The unit of 60 corresponded with a degree of the equator.

## Astronomy and Observation
Astronomy held a conspicuous place in Babylonian culture and was often approached from an astrological perspective. The Babylonians invented the zodiac and possessed the ability to foretell eclipses of both the moon and the sun. To facilitate these studies, observatories were attached to temples, and astronomers provided regular reports to the king.

Early Babylonian astronomers numbered and named the stars, and they maintained observations of the phases of Venus as well as tables of lunar longitudes. The people of this region were also acquainted with mechanical tools for measurement and observation, including the clepsydra and the sun-dial.

## Regional Geography
Senkera was part of a landscape defined by major waterways. To its east lay the Shatt el-Hai, which is believed to be the ancient channel of the Tigris. Further east of the Shatt el-Hai was Lagash (Tello), a city that played an important role in early Babylonian history. To the south lay Eridu, the seat of worship for the culture-god Ea, located near Ur.

The combined flow of the Tigris and Euphrates through the southern marshes was referred to by the Babylonians as the *nar marrati*, or "the salt river," a term that was originally used to describe the Persian Gulf.
