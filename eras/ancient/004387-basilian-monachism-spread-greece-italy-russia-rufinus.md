# Basilian Monachism

Basilian monachism refers to the monastic tradition following the rule of Basil the Great. St Basil's reconstruction of monastic life serves as the foundation for the monasticism of the Slavonic and Greek Churches, although the monks themselves do not use the term "Basilians."

## Origins and Principles
Around 360, St Basil established his monastery near Neocaesarea in Pontus. Having visited monasteries in Palestine and Egypt, Basil sought to oppose the trend toward eremitical life and extreme bodily austerities. He asserted that the cenobitical life—characterized by community living—was superior to the eremitical. 

The system Basil established was strictly cenobitical, featuring common meals, common work, and common prayer conducted seven times a day. He emphasized that work should be an integral part of monastic life, both for its own sake and to benefit others; consequently, he advocated for monasteries to be located near towns. He further taught that austerities and fasting should not interfere with work or prayer. While the "Longer" and "Shorter" Rules are viewed more as catechisms of spiritual life than corporate regulations, they emphasized virtues such as chastity, personal poverty, self-denial, and obedience.

## Spread to Italy
Basilian monachism spread from Greece to Italy, aided by the translation of St Basil's Rules into Latin by Rufinus around 400. These Rules became the standard of life for several Italian monasteries and significantly influenced St Benedict, who referred his monks to the "Rule of our holy Father Basil."

Greek monasteries were present in south Italy, Sicily, and Rome during the 6th and 7th centuries. Between the 8th and 10th centuries, invasions by Arabs, Saracens, and others drove crowds of fugitives from Sicily and Greece into southern Italy. From the middle of the 9th century, Greek-speaking monks established numerous Basilian monasteries in Calabria, which eventually spread north to Rome. While many of these houses existed into the 18th century, the only surviving monastery is the one founded by St Nilus around 1000 at Grottaferrata in the Alban Hills. The libraries and scriptoria of these Italian Basilian houses were later dispersed, with some of the chief collections of Greek manuscripts in western Europe—including those at the Vatican and the Bessarion at Venice—originating from these spoils.

## Expansion into Russia and Slavonic Lands
The importation of Basilian monachism into Russia was of great importance, as it became the norm for all Slavonic lands. Greek monks were instrumental in the evangelization of the Slavs. The first Russian monastery was founded at Kiev around 1050 by a monk from Mount Athos.

In the Russian empire, the monastic institute saw significant development, with approximately 400 monasteries for men and 100 for women. Many of these institutions support schools, almshouses, and hospitals. In the East, Basilian nuns have existed since the beginning, starting with a nunnery established by St Basil's sister, St Macrina, under his direction. In Russia, nuns are not permitted to take final vows until they reach the age of sixty.

## Organization and Evolution
Throughout the Greek-speaking lands, the Basilian cenobitic form prevailed over eremitical forms due to civil legislation (the Justinian Code) and ecclesiastical legislation (the council of Chalcedon). In the 9th century, Theodore, the abbot of the Studium monastery in Constantinople, worked to restore the primitive vigor of St Basil's spirit. He created a practical code of laws, known as the Constitutions, to supplement the Rules of Basil. Together with the canons of the Councils, these constitute the primary monastic law for Russian and Greek monks.

Monasteries are categorized into three types:
*   **Cenobia proper**: Where personal poverty and full monastic common life are observed.
*   **Idiorrhythmic**: Where monks lead a freer, mitigated life and are permitted the use of private means.
*   **Lauras**: Where the life is semi-eremitical.

In contemporary Greek monachism, such as on Mount Athos, the life is primarily dedicated to contemplative devotional exercises and lengthy church services. Manual labor has almost disappeared, and intellectual study is rarely cultivated.

## Ecclesiastical Role and Distribution
Within the Orthodox Church, monks hold significant ecclesiastical importance because bishops must be celibate, while parochial clergy must be married; therefore, all bishops are recruited from the monks. Outside the Orthodox Church, there are small congregations of Uniat Basilians, with Catholic Basilian monasteries located in Rumania, Galicia, Hungary, Poland, and among the Uniat Syrians or Melchites.

Currently, there are a considerable number of monasteries in Greece and other Slavonic lands, and no fewer than 100 Greek monasteries exist within Turkish dominions.
