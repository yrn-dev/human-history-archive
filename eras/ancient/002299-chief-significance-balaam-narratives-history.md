# The Balaam Narratives

The narratives concerning Balaam, a Gentile seer and the son of Beor, appear in the biblical history of the Israelites during their sojourn in the plains of Moab. This period occurred at the conclusion of the forty years of wandering, shortly before the crossing of the Jordan and the death of Moses. The primary accounts are found in Numbers xxii-xxv, though references also appear in Deuteronomy xxiii. 3-6 and the farewell speech of Joshua.

## Historical Context and Narrative Framework
The events are precipitated by the Israelite conquest of two eastern Palestinian kings: Og, king of Bashan, and Sihon, king of the Amorites. These victories alarmed Balak, the king of Moab, who sought the services of Balaam to curse Israel. 

The narratives are viewed by scholars as a composite of ancient traditions. The account in Numbers is held to be compiled from two ancient documents, denoted as J and E (dating from 900-700 B.C.), which serve as a framework for seven oracular poems. In the J account, Balak sends elders of Moab and Midian to Balaam in the land of Ammon. In the E account, the princes of Moab are sent to Balaam at Pethor, located on the Euphrates (though some scholars suggest the "River" refers to a ravine in Egypt or Musri, and Deuteronomy xxiii. 4 places Pethor in Mesopotamia).

## The Divine Mandate and the Speaking Ass
The narratives describe Balaam's internal and external struggle to fulfill his mission. In the E account, God initially forbids Balaam from going in a dream, though he later permits it on the condition that Balaam speaks only what God tells him. 

A central episode involves the Angel of Yahweh intercepting Balaam. According to the text, the Angel is initially visible only to Balaam's ass. The animal attempts to avoid the Angel, provoking Balaam's anger and resulting in the animal being striped. Miraculously, the ass is enabled to speak with the voice of a man, complaining of the unjust treatment. Yahweh then enables Balaam to see the Angel, who informs him that he would have slain him if not for the ass. While Balaam offers to return, he is instructed to proceed.

## The Oracles and the Priestly Code
Despite Balak's efforts to secure a curse through sacrifices and by placing Balaam on the top of Pisgah to view the Israelites, Yahweh compels Balaam to bless Israel. Balak remonstrates, but Balaam explains that he can only speak the message provided by God.

A contrasting tradition is found in the Priestly Code. In this version, Balaam does not bless Israel but instead advises the Midianites on how to bring disaster upon the Israelites by seducing them and undermining their loyalty to Yahweh. This account concludes with Balaam being slain in battle while fighting in the ranks of Midian.

## Religious and Historical Significance
The chief significance of these narratives for the history of the religion of Israel is the recognition by the J and E documents of the genuine inspiration of a non-Hebrew prophet. The texts present Yahweh as the God of Balaam as well as the God of Moses. This suggests an original tradition where Yahweh was recognized as the deity of a circle of connected tribes, including the Israelite tribes, and a continuous recognition that Yahweh revealed his will to nations other than Israel. This feature, however, was apparently ignored by the Priestly Code.

## Interpretations and Traditions
Balaam has been a figure of interest across various traditions:
* **Josephus:** Describes him as the best prophet of his time but one with a disposition poorly adapted to resist temptation.
* **Philo:** Characterizes him as a great magician or a "sophist" symbolizing warring opinions.
* **Rabbinical Traditions:** Often represent him as a typical sinner, with various fables claiming he was blind in one eye or identifying him as the Elihu of Job or Laban.
* **Islamic Traditions:** Some accounts describe him as one of the Anakim (giants of Palestine) who read the books of Abraham to learn the name Yahweh, enabling him to predict the future. Some conjecture that the Arabic wise man Luqman is identical to Balaam.
