## Lloyd Kenyon, 1st Baron
Lloyd Kenyon (1732–1802) served as the Lord Chief Justice of England. Born on October 5, 1732, in Gredington, Flintshire, he was the son of a man from an old Lancashire family and a mother who was the daughter of a small proprietor in Wales. His early education took place at Ruthin grammar school, and by his fifteenth year, he was articled to an attorney in Nantwich, Cheshire.

Kenyon entered Lincoln's Inn in London in 1750 and was called to the bar in 1756. During a period of limited employment, he spent his leisure time taking notes on cases argued in the court of King's Bench, which he later published. He gained professional recognition by answering cases for his friend John Dunning (later Lord Ashburton). His career progressed rapidly, and he was made king's counsel in 1780. 

While Kenyon demonstrated significant ability in the cross-examination of witnesses—notably during the trial of Lord George Gordon—he was described as lacking tact in his speeches and deficient in his ability of statement as an advocate. His professional ascent is attributed to hard work, a strong knowledge of the law, and several lucky friendships. Through the influence of Lord Thurlow, Kenyon entered the House of Commons as a member for Hindon in 1780. In 1782, he was appointed attorney-general in the administration of Lord Buckingham, a position he maintained under Pitt. He became a baronet and received the mastership of the rolls in 1784.

In 1788, Kenyon succeeded Lord Mansfield as lord chief justice and was raised to the peerage as Baron Kenyon of Gredington. Despite a capricious and choleric temper that made his elevation unpopular with the bar, he was regarded on the bench as an able lawyer and a judge of rare and inflexible impartiality. He died in Bath on April 4, 1802. He was succeeded as 2nd baron by his son George (1776–1855).

## Legal Proceedings under Lord Kenyon
In 1800, Lord Kenyon presided as judge in the case of John Rusby, who was indicted for buying ninety quarters of oats at 41s. per quarter and selling thirty of them on the same day at 43s. Lord Kenyon addressed the jury strongly against the accused and spoke out strongly against the repealing act of 1773. Although Rusby was heavily fined, a subsequent appeal left the court equally divided on whether regrating, forestalling, and engrossing remained common law offences.

## Scholarship and Textual Criticism
The name Kenyon is associated with several works of classical and biblical scholarship:

*   **Bacchylides Manuscript:** Kenyon observed that no manuscript of equal antiquity is as well supplied with accents as the Bacchylides MS. For comparison, the Alcman fragment in the Louvre, dating perhaps to the early 1st century A.D., also contains frequent accents, likely intended to assist readers unfamiliar with Laconian Doric. Kenyon published the *Ed. princeps* of Bacchylides in 1897.
*   **Attic Law:** Kenyon produced an edition of Aristotle's *Constitution of Athens*, published in London in 1891 and again in Berlin in 1903 (4th edition).
*   **New Testament Studies:** Kenyon authored a handbook on the *Textual Criticism of the New Testament*, which includes a section on the history of the Vulgate described as particularly full and clear.

## Naval History
In a different context, a Lieutenant Commander Kenyon commanded the U.S. destroyer *Warrington*. He and Lieutenant Commander Klein (of the U.S. destroyer *Smith*) received commendation for locating a position in the middle of the night after traveling approximately 250 miles. They successfully located boats and rafts from the *President Lincoln* by estimating the probable drift of the boats based on the direction and force of the wind.
