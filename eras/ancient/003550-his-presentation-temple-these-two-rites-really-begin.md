# Presentation in the Temple and Early Church Rites

In the early traditions of the church, specific rites were prescribed for children following their birth. One such rite, known as "churching," occurred on the fortieth day after birth. During this ceremony, the child was taken into the church accompanied by the mother. Both the mother and the child received blessings from the clergy.

## Purpose and Symbolism of the Rite
The clergy's petition during the churching rite was for God to preserve the child, allowing him to grow through the "unseen grace of His power" and eventually becoming worthy of the washing of baptism in due season. This second rite is viewed as corresponding to the presentation of Jesus in the temple, just as the first rite of the child corresponds to the naming and circumcision of Jesus.

## The Catechumenate
The rite of churching and the preceding initial rite served as the beginning of the catechumenate. This was a designated period of instruction in the discipline and faith of the church. The duration of this period varied depending on the individual. A person would provide their name as a candidate for initiation whenever they felt inclined, a process that typically took place at the start of Lent.

## Admission to Baptism
Following the expression of interest, the bishop and clergy conducted individual examinations of the candidates. To ensure the candidates were worthy of admission, the clergy ascertained from neighbors whether the individuals had led exemplary lives. In instances where candidates were strangers from different churches, they were required to produce certificates of character.

If a candidate was deemed unworthy, the bishop would dismiss them until a later occasion when they might be more worthy. If the examination was satisfactory, the candidate was admitted. In the East, such a person was called a *photizomenos* (one in course of being illumined), while in the West, they were referred to as an *asker* or *competens*.

## Requirements for Initiation
The process of initiation typically involved two sponsors who took responsibility for the sincerity of the candidate's repentance and faith, as well as their past life. A fundamental requirement for baptism was that the individual must come to the rite of their own free will, without compulsion or the hope of gain.

Regarding the necessity of physical elements, Macarius of Jerusalem stated that the grace of the spirit is granted in response to entreaties and prayers, suggesting that even a font is not strictly necessary, only the desire for grace. However, Tertullian argued in his work *On Baptism* that desire alone is not always sufficient. He recounted an instance in Carthage where boys and girls requested baptism, citing the words of Christ: "To each that asketh thee give" and "Forbid them not to come unto Me." Tertullian responded by cautioning against "casting pearls" or giving "the holy thing to dogs."
