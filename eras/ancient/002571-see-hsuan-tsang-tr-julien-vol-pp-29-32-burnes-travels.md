# Hsuan Tsang

Hsuan Tsang (also referred to as Hsüan Tsang or Hiuen Tsang) was a Chinese pilgrim and traveler who visited India and Central Asia during the 7th century A.D. His narratives serve as a primary historical source for the geography, religion, and political state of the regions he traversed, providing details that have been used to verify archaeological sites and historical figures.

## Observations in India and Nepal
During his travels in India, Hsuan Tsang visited several sites of religious and historical significance. In the extreme south of Nepal, near the frontier of the Basti district in British territory, he visited a grove located approximately 3 miles north of Bhagwanpur. This site, now known as Rummin-dei (derived from the ancient name Lumbini), contained four small stupas which were noted by the pilgrim. At this location, Hsuan Tsang also observed an Asoka pillar topped with a horse. He recorded that the pillar had been split by lightning at the time of his visit. This pillar remained half-buried until it was excavated for examination by the Nepalese government at the request of the Indian government, following the 1895 discovery of another inscribed pillar at Nigliva.

In other parts of India, Hsuan Tsang's accounts provide geographical identifications for ancient cities. For example, the ancient city of Broach (or Bharuch), located on the right bank of the Nerbudda in the northern division of Bombay, is identified as the Barakacheva mentioned in his writings.

## Accounts of Central Asia and Bamian
Hsuan Tsang visited Bamian in A.D. 630, describing the city when it was apparently past its meridian. At that time, Bamian served as the head of one of the small states that had emerged after the breakup of the empire of the White Huns. 

His narratives are essential for the identification of the idols of Bamian. Hsuan Tsang saw these figures in their splendor, and his accounts ascertain that they were Buddhist figures. This was later verified by officers who discovered further Buddhist excavations and caves in the Sarikol and Balkhab valleys. Additionally, Hsuan Tsang described a massive recumbent figure located 2 miles east of Bamian, measuring approximately 1,000 feet in length. This figure represented Sakya Buddha entering Nirvana (the act of death).

## Historical and Ethnic Records
The writings of Hsuan Tsang provide significant data regarding the Indian Hunas. He visited the country approximately one century after the death of Mihiragula, and his account is one of the chief sources of knowledge regarding this people, alongside inscriptions and coins found from central India to the Punjab.

## Cartographic and Literary References
The travels of the Chinese pilgrim have been the subject of various scholarly mappings and translations. M. Vivien de St. Martin produced a map of Hsuan Tsang's travels, though some discrepancies exist in the placement of certain locations; for instance, St. Martin placed Pima to the west of Khotan, a placement not supported by the versions of the pilgrim's journey. His works have been translated by Julien and edited by Watters.
