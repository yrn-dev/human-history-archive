# Second Congress at Lacedaemon—Preparations for War and Diplomatic Skirmishes

The period leading up to the conflict between Athens and the Peloponnesians was marked by escalating grievances and strategic diplomatic maneuvers. While a truce remained in place for a time, underlying tensions created a volatile environment that necessitated preparations for inevitable warfare.

## Antecedent Grounds of Complaint
The friction between the Athenians and the Peloponnesians was driven by specific mutual complaints. Corinth expressed grievance over the siege of its colony, Potidæa, which contained both Corinthian and Peloponnesian citizens. Conversely, Athens accused the Peloponnesians of inciting a member of the Athenian alliance—a town that contributed to Athenian revenue—to revolt. Athens further alleged that the Peloponnesians had openly fought against her on the side of the Potidæans. Despite these conflicts, war had not yet officially broken out because the actions at Potidæa were considered a private enterprise by Corinth.

## Preparations for War
Following the affair at Plataea, the existing treaty was broken by an overt act, prompting both Athens and Lacedaemon and her allies to prepare for war. Both parties sought external assistance, resolving to send embassies to the King and other barbarian powers, while simultaneously attempting to secure alliances with independent domestic states.

Lacedaemon focused on expanding its naval capabilities. In addition to its existing marine, Lacedaemon ordered states in Sicily and Italy that had declared for her to build vessels. The goal was to reach a grand total of five hundred ships, with each city's quota determined by its size. These states were also required to provide a specified sum of money. Until these naval preparations were complete, these cities were instructed to remain neutral and allow single Athenian ships to enter their harbours.

Athens focused on the stability of its own confederacy and the strategic geography of the region. The Athenians sent embassies to locations immediately surrounding the Peloponnese, specifically Zacynthus, Acarnania, Cephallenia, and Corcyra. Athens perceived that if these states could be relied upon, she would be capable of carrying the war all around the Peloponnese.

## The Role of Argos
During this period of preparation, Argos emerged as a significant diplomatic actor. The Argives passed a decree to negotiate an alliance with any Hellenic state that desired it, with the specific exception of Athens and Lacedaemon; neither of these powers could join the alliance without the reference and consent of the Argive people.

Argos was inclined toward this plan for two primary reasons. First, the Argives viewed war with Lacedaemon as inevitable since the truce was nearing its expiration. Second, Argos hoped to achieve supremacy in the Peloponnese. This ambition was supported by the contemporary public estimation of the powers: Lacedaemon had sunk very low due to its disasters, while Argos was in a flourishing condition. The Argives had profited largely from their neutrality, having taken no part in the Attic war.
