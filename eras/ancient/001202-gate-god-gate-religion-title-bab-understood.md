# "Gate of God" and "Gate of Religion"

The phrases "Gate of God" and "Gate of Religion" appear in historical and linguistic contexts across different cultures and eras, most notably in the etymology of the ancient city of Babylon and the terminology associated with the 19th-century Persian religion of Babiism.

## The Etymology of Babylon
The name "Babylon" is the Greek form of *Babel* or *Bab-ili*, which translates from Semitic as "the gate of the god." While sometimes incorrectly written as "of the gods," the singular form refers to the divine patron of the city, likely Merodach or Marduk. This Semitic name is a translation of the original Sumerian name, *Ka-dimirra*.

Historically, *Ka-dimirra* (and thus *Bab-ili*) likely referred to the E-Saggila, the temple dedicated to Bel-Merodach, and the immediate surroundings of the sanctuary. The city of Babylon grew around this temple, which had been founded in pre-Semitic times. Other titles for the city included *Din-tir* (Sumerian for "the life of the forest" or "seat of life") and *Uru-azagga*, meaning "the holy city."

## The Title Bab in Babiism
In the context of Babiism—a religion founded in Persia between A.D. 1844 and 1845 by Mirza 'Ali Muhammad of Shiraz—the title *Bab* (meaning "gate") carries a specific theological meaning distinct from the phrases "Gate of God" or "Gate of Religion."

Mirza 'Ali Muhammad, a young Sayyid and former disciple of Sayyid Kazim of Rasht, assumed the title *Bab*. He did so not in the sense of being a "Gate of God" or "Gate of Religion," but rather as an intermediary. This concept is rooted in the doctrine of the Shaykhis (a sect of extreme Shi'ites) known as *Rukn-i-rabi'* ("the fourth support"), which posits that an intermediary must always exist between the faithful followers and the twelfth Imam. 

The prototype for this role was found in the four successive *Babs* (gates) who facilitated communication between the twelfth Imam and his partisans during the period of "minor occultation" (*Ghaybat-i-sughra*) from A.D. 874 to 940. Mirza 'Ali Muhammad eventually assumed the higher title of *Nuqta* ("Point"), and the title of *Bab* was subsequently conferred upon his disciple, Mulla Husayn of Bushrawayh.

## Other Historical "Gates"
The concept of a "gate" as a significant title or landmark appears in various other historical contexts:

*   **Derbent:** This Russian town in Caucasia, identified as the capital of ancient Albania, is associated with the "Iron Gate" or "Caspian Gates." The Arabs, who took possession of the city in 728, referred to it as *Bab-el-Abwab* ("the principal gate") or *Bab-el-Khadid* ("the iron gate"). Arab geographers also used the term *Báb-ul-abwáb* ("Gate of Gates") to describe the Pass of Derbend.
*   **Bab-el-Mandeb:** This strait connecting the Red Sea with the Indian Ocean is known in Arabic as "The Gate of Tears," a name attributed either to the dangers of navigation or to a legend regarding those drowned during an earthquake that separated Africa and Asia.
*   **Babylonian Fortifications:** The city of Babylon featured a great square with twenty-five gates on every side, totaling one hundred gates made of solid brass.
