# The Sumerian Sign MU and the Verb "Give"

In the study of Babylonian and Assyrian proper names, the sign MU presents a significant point of linguistic complexity due to its multiple meanings and the "ideographic" method of writing used in cuneiform. In the Sumerian language, the sign MU serves as the word for "name," but it is also the Sumerian word for "give."

## Linguistic Interpretations of MU
Because the sign MU can signify both "name" and "give," scholars encounter difficulties when determining the intended reading of a verb within a proper name. When MU is read as the Sumerian word for "give," it may be interpreted in the following ways:
* **Iddin**: Meaning "he gave," derived from the root *nadānu*.
* **Nādin**: Meaning "giver."

This ambiguity is exemplified in names where a deity's name is followed by the sequence MU-MU. In such instances, there is uncertainty as to whether the first MU represents a form of the verb *nadānu* ("give") and the second MU represents the noun *shumu* ("name"), or if the order of these meanings is reversed.

## The Cuneiform Writing System
The challenges associated with the sign MU stem from the nature of the cuneiform syllabary, which originated with the non-Semitic settlers of the Euphrates valley, known as the Sumerians (or Sumero-Akkadians). This script was later adopted by the Babylonians and adapted to their Semitic language.

The system employed a double method of writing:
1. **Ideographic**: A sign or combination of signs expressed an entire word. For example, the sign MU could represent the Sumerian word for "name." In a Babylonian or Assyrian text, this ideogram would be read as *shumu*, the Semitic equivalent.
2. **Phonetic**: Words were spelled out in syllables. In this approach, Sumerian signs were treated merely as syllables without reference to their original meaning to spell Babylonian words.

## Context of Sumerian Influence
The Sumerian language and its writing system maintained a profound influence over the region long after the Semites conquered the Sumerian cities. The newcomers adopted Sumerian writing and language, which became the medium of knowledge and power. 

Even during Semitic periods, knowledge of the extinct Sumerian language remained necessary for the educated class. The language of law and religion continued to be the old agglutinative language of Chaldaea for a long duration. To support students, scholars compiled grammars, vocabularies, and interlinear translations, as well as commentaries to explain obscure phrases and words.

## Challenges in Decipherment
The reading of names from the oldest historical periods remains difficult due to the multiplicity of phonetic values attached to signs. Scholars often differ on the correct readings of Sumerian names, and changes in these readings are frequent. 

A primary source of doubt is whether the bearer of a name was a Sumerian or a Semite. If the bearer was a Sumerian, the name is read as Sumerian; if a Semite, the signs are typically read according to their Semitic equivalents. Additionally, scholars must determine if the signs in a Semite's name represent a phonetic reading or an ideographic compound. For example, the name of a ruler of Kish written as Uru-mu-ush was initially mistaken for an ideographic form meaning "he founded a city" (*Alu-usharshid*), though it was eventually accepted as Urumush.
