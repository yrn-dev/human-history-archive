# Nineveh and the Transition to the Neo-Babylonian Period

## The Fall of Nineveh
Nineveh served as the capital of the Assyrian Empire, a military power that had established itself as the chief authority within its sphere and the suzerain of Babylonia during the 9th and 8th centuries B.C. Under monarchs such as Salmaneser and Sennacherib, the Assyrians of Nineveh were powerful conquerors in Phoenicia, Syria, and Judaea. Records indicate that Salmaneser subjected Media, relocating the Israelites from their own country into Persis and Media, while moving the Cuthæans from those regions into the lands of the Israelites.

Despite this period of dominance, Assyrian power eventually waned. The empire struggled against internal and external pressures, including the loss of Egypt under Pharaoh Psammetichus I. By 606 B.C., the Assyrian Empire succumbed to a combined force of Aryan and Semitic peoples. The Medes and Persians from the north-east formed an alliance with the nomadic Chaldeans of the south-east to undo Assyria. This coalition took the city of Nineveh in 606 B.C.

Historical accounts of the city's fall vary slightly. Some sources, including Josephus, attribute the destruction to the Medes and Babylonians united. Other perspectives, such as those of Herodotus, suggest a series of revolts by dependent territories beginning with the Medes, with Nineveh eventually being taken by the Mede Kyaxarês around 600 B.C. However, other testimonies from Scripture and Herodotus maintain the date of 606 B.C. for the city's destruction by the Medes and Babylonians. Because Assyria was essentially a military power, the empire disappeared following the destruction of its capital.

## The Median Empire
Following the division of the spoils of Assyria, a Median Empire was established in the north under Cyaxares. This empire included Nineveh, though its capital was located at Ecbatana. The Median territory extended eastward to the borders of India. For several decades, the Median Empire remained the stronger and hardier of the regional powers, with the Second Babylonian Empire existing "like a lamb within the embrace of the Median lion." Diplomatic ties between the two powers were maintained through marriage; for instance, Nebuchadnezzar the Great married a daughter of Cyaxares.

## The Neo-Babylonian Period
The destruction of Nineveh in 606 B.C. released Babylon from the Assyrian yoke and paved the way for the Neo-Babylonian period. This era began with Nabopolassar (625-604 B.C.), the first independent ruler to inaugurate a new period of prosperity and power for Babylonia. This "Second Babylonian Empire," established under Chaldean rule, reached a peak of wealth and power under Nebuchadnezzar the Great.

During this time, the renascent Babylonia encountered the Egyptians. In a conflict involving Necho II, who had previously defeated King Josiah of Judah at the battle of Megiddo in 608 B.C., the Chaldeans routed the Egyptian forces and pushed the Babylonian frontier down to ancient Egyptian boundaries. From 606 until 589 B.C., the Second Babylonian Empire flourished, fostering both life and learning in the city of Babylon.

## Decline and Legacy
The political independence of the Neo-Babylonian period ended in 539 B.C. with the conquest of Babylon and Babylonia by Cyrus. Despite the end of political autonomy, the religion of the region continued undisturbed for several centuries. This cultural and religious continuity lasted until the Greek conquest of the Euphrates valley in 331 B.C., at which point new influences gradually led to the extinction of the old cults.

While Assyria vanished with Nineveh, Babylon continued to exert a significant influence on religion and culture long after the Persian conquest. The transition from Assyrian to Babylonian dominance is also reflected in the development of regional religion, which is categorized into four periods: the oldest period (c. 3500 B.C. to c. 2250 B.C.), the post-Khammurabic period, the Assyrian period (c. 2000 B.C. to 606 B.C.), and the Neo-Babylonian period.
