# Israeliten

## Territorial and Tribal Origins
The district located to the west of Galilee was known to the Egyptians as Aser(u) during the latter half of the 14th century B.C., specifically in the time of Rameses II and Seti I. This suggests two possibilities regarding the tribe of Asher: either Asher was an Israelite tribe that migrated north after separating from other brethren in Egypt—a pattern potentially followed by other tribes—or the area was a district regarded as part of the national kingdom and categorized as Israelite through the genealogical device of being named a "son" of Jacob.

It is possible that some Israelite populations in this region moved from an earlier home in the south, similar to the movement of Dan. Some associations have been made between the clans of Asher, specifically Malchiel and Heber, and the Habiri people and a hostile chief named Milk-ili found in the Amarna Tablets. However, it is considered improbable that events from approximately 1400 B.C. would survive only in this manner. Similarly, the theory that the name Asher derives from a figure named Abd-ashirta from the same period is viewed with skepticism, though a connection to the goddess Ashir(t)a is considered possible.

## Genealogical Records and Settlements
Genealogical lists provide data on the early seats of Caleb, placing them in the south of Judah, including Maon and Hebron. A separate list includes more northerly towns such as Bethlehem and Kirjath-jearim, while also noting the Kenites and the "families of the scribes." 

These movements are reflected in genealogical statements regarding Caleb's wives. His first wife is identified as Azubah (meaning "abandoned" or "desert region"), and another is Jerioth (meaning "tent curtains"). Following the death of Hezron, Caleb united with Ephrath.

## The Northern Kingdom and National Identity
The term "Israel" is often used to refer to the northern kingdom, though this application can be ambiguous. As a general national name with religious significance, "Israel" can suggest or include the inclusion of Judah. 

## Literary and Prophetical Traditions
There is a close relationship between the book of Deuteronomy and "Deuteronomic" passages, a connection that impacts the traditional dating of the book. This linguistic influence extends to certain prophetical stories, which exhibit the features of Deuteronomic writers and differ in style and expression from the prophecies of others, such as Hosea and Amos, prior to Jeremiah.

In the records of the northern kingdom, certain passages (specifically xiv. 22 and xv. 5) presuppose the existence of fuller records. Later and less trustworthy versions of these records may be represented in 2 Chronicles xxvi. 6-7 and 16-20.
