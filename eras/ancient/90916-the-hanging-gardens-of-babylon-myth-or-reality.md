# The Hanging Gardens of Babylon: Myth or Reality?

Of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, the Hanging Gardens of Babylon remain the most enigmatic. Described by ancient chroniclers as a breathtaking ascent of tiered terraces overflowing with exotic flora and irrigated by complex machinery, the gardens were said to be a marvel of engineering and a testament to royal love. However, unlike the Great Pyramid of Giza, the gardens have left no definitive physical footprint in the soil of Iraq.

For centuries, historians have grappled with a fundamental question: did these gardens actually exist in Babylon, or were they a poetic invention of the Greco-Roman imagination?

## The Traditional Narrative
According to classical accounts, the gardens were commissioned by King Nebuchadnezzar II (r. 605–562 BCE) for his wife, Amytis of Media. Amytis, hailing from the mountainous, green landscapes of Persia, reportedly longed for the greenery of her homeland amidst the flat, arid plains of Mesopotamia. To cure her homesickness, Nebuchadnezzar allegedly constructed a series of ascending stone terraces supported by massive vaults, creating an artificial mountain of lush vegetation.

## The Archaeological Dilemma
The primary challenge for historians is the total absence of Babylonian records mentioning the gardens. While Nebuchadnezzar II left extensive inscriptions detailing his architectural achievements—including the Ishtar Gate and the Etemenanki ziggurat—none mention a botanical paradise. Furthermore, archaeological excavations of Babylon have failed to uncover the massive irrigation systems or the structural foundations required to support such a weight of soil and water.

## The Nineveh Hypothesis
In recent decades, a compelling scholarly debate has emerged, led by Dr. Stephanie Dalley of Oxford University. Dalley proposes that the gardens were not in Babylon at all, but in Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire. She argues that the gardens were actually built by the Assyrian King Sennacherib (r. 704–681 BCE). 

Assyrian texts and reliefs explicitly describe a magnificent palace and a sophisticated system of aqueducts used to bring water from the mountains to the city's gardens. Dalley suggests that later historians simply confused the two great Mesopotamian cities, attributing the Assyrian achievement to the more famous Babylonian king.

## Key Facts
* **Attributed Builder:** Traditionally King Nebuchadnezzar II; alternatively King Sennacherib.
* **Location:** Historically placed in Babylon (modern-day Hillah, Iraq) or Nineveh (modern-day Mosul).
* **Engineering:** Described as using a "chain pump" or Archimedes' screw to lift water to upper levels.
* **Status:** The only one of the Seven Wonders whose exact location has never been established by archaeology.
* **Purpose:** Allegedly built as a gesture of love to alleviate a queen's homesickness.

## Did You Know?
* The term "hanging" is a mistranslation of the Greek word *kremastos*, which more accurately means "overhanging" or "tiered."
* If the gardens existed, they would have required an immense amount of waterproofing—likely using layers of reeds, bitumen, and lead—to prevent water from rotting the stone structures.
* The gardens are the only "Wonder" that is not mentioned in the writings of the contemporary Babylonian scribes of the era.
