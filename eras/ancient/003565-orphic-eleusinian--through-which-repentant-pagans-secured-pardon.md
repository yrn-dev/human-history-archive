# Orphic and Eleusinian Mysteries

The Orphic and Eleusinian mysteries were among the principal mystical rites of ancient Greece. These solemn initiations, along with the Mythraic mysteries, were characterized by a central focus on the idea of re-birth. Through these processes, repentant pagans sought to secure eternal bliss and pardon.

## The Eleusinian Mysteries
The Eleusinian mysteries were centered at the Temple of Eleusis, a structure built by order of the goddess Dêmêtêr to serve as her residence. Dêmêtêr is credited with prescribing the mystic ritual of Eleusis. The details of this divine service are explained in the Homeric Hymn to Dêmêtêr, which serves as a sacred record of the rites. These mysteries held significant importance for the town of Eleusis and maintained a strong hold on the feelings of its inhabitants, eventually expanding in Hellenic importance.

## The Orphic Sect and Theogony
The Orphic sect was a variety of the Dionysiac mystics. It was associated with the Orphic Theogony, a body of work that enlarged and mystically disguised the ideas and persons found in the Hesiodic Theogony. Unlike the more popular Hesiodic accounts, the Orphic vein of invention was adapted for the contemplation of a specially prepared sect rather than a casual audience, gaining currency primarily among speculative men.

While some literary Greeks and later New-Platonists of the third and fourth centuries after Christ viewed Orpheus as an author older than Homer or Hesiod, modern scholarship indicates that these compositions largely emanated from poets of the Alexandrine age or periods subsequent to the Christian era. The Orphic Theogony includes figures such as Zeus, Phanês, and Zagreus.

## Nature of the Mysteries and Initiation
The mysteries were distinct from the Homeric Hymns and were often connected to the worship of Dionysos and Dêmêtêr, influenced by Thracian, Egyptian, and Asiatic elements. In the case of the Dionysiac rites, Asiatic frenzy was grafted onto the existing joviality of the Grecian Dionysia.

Some investigators suggest that these mysteries preserved a secret doctrine of old theological and philosophical mythes. According to this hypothesis, an ancient body of priests from the East or Egypt communicated knowledge of nature, humanity, and a refined monotheism to the Greeks using symbols and allegories. This secret doctrine was treasured within religious fraternities, including the Bacchic, Orphic, Eleusinian, and Samothracian mysteries. Access to this ancient cosmogonic and religious doctrine was granted only to those who had completed preliminary ceremonies of initiation and were under a strict obligation of secrecy.

## Historical Context and Influence
In the first centuries, thousands of *mystae* in Italy, Asia Minor, Egypt, and other regions practiced these pious ejaculations. While these cults shared themes of pardon and re-birth with early church believers, there is limited evidence that the church directly borrowed its interpretations or ceremonies from these outside sources; it is suggested that the outside cults may have borrowed as much from the church as the church did from them.

In later Athenian history, the site of Eleusis also became a strategic point of conflict. During a period of political instability in Attica, the Thirty—a ruling group in Athens—sought to secure Eleusis as a place of safety. They conducted a seizure of Eleusinian citizens, ordering all those qualified for armed service to present themselves, after which they were fettered and conveyed to Athens to be held in the custody of the Eleven.
