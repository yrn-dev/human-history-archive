# Revolutionary Ideas and Their Impacts

The history of human development is marked by the emergence of "revolutionary" ideas—conceptual, political, and technological shifts that challenge established institutions and habits. These ideas have manifested as spiritual doctrines, political movements for democratic governance, and the importation of transformative technologies.

## Spiritual and Moral Revolutions
One of the most revolutionary doctrines to influence human thought was the teaching of the Kingdom of Heaven, the primary message of Jesus. This doctrine presented a bold and uncompromising demand for a complete cleansing of the human race, both internally and externally. Because it challenged the established habits and institutions of mankind, the world of that time often failed to grasp its full significance and recoiled in dismay. 

Many disciples and converts, struggling with the impact of these ideas on established norms, reverted to familiar traditional practices. These included the use of altars and temples, the reliance on consecrated priests and magic blessings, and a return to habitual lives characterized by pride, competition, profits, and hates.

## Political Revolutions and Democratic Thought
The transition toward modern democratic republics in America and France was fueled by a ferment of political ideas. A central point of departure for these movements was the work of the English writer John Locke (1632-1704), specifically his *Two Treatises on Civil Government*. Locke’s writings served as a bridge between the political thinking of earlier republican days and the later revolutionary movements in France and America.

These revolutionary political ideas included the propositions that:
* Kings were created for the benefit of the people, rather than the people for the kings.
* No government possesses the right to enslave its subjects.
* While many governments are arbitrary *de facto*, none are arbitrary *de jure*.

In the 19th century, this fermentation of ideas continued, leading to the development of the idea of Socialism and the influence of Darwinism on political and religious thought. In Russia, revolutionary movements among the common people also emerged as a response to state failure, such as the "remote and reasonless slaughter" of the Russian Baltic Fleet, which pressured the Tsar to end the war in 1905 and evacuate Manchuria.

## Technological and Material Revolutions
Revolutionary changes have also arrived through intercultural exchange. Intercourse between Europe and the East brought several revolutionary items and ideas to the Western world. These included the mariner’s compass, which allowed European shipping to move beyond coasting, and the use of gunpowder in warfare. Additionally, the extended use of printing from blocks and paper represented a significant shift. While popular imagination often attributes these importations to Marco Polo, there is no evidence he played a part in them; rather, they were brought by various unnamed individuals.

## State Resistance to Revolutionary Ideas
Governments have historically employed various methods to suppress the infiltration of revolutionary ideas and maintain the status quo. In the Austrian dominions, the government attempted to block "revolutionary" ideas from the outside by erecting a "Chinese wall" consisting of censors and tariffs. This measure, however, saw little success.

To guard against the internal growth of Liberalism, the state implemented rigid supervision of the press and restored clerical control over education. While the government encouraged the revival of national languages and literatures in places like Croatia and Bohemia—believing this would divert interest from politics toward scientific pursuits—it maintained a stagnant administration. This system relied on non-representative provincial diets where only nobles and clergy were eligible, and the ancient right of petition was rarely used and typically met with imperial disfavour.
