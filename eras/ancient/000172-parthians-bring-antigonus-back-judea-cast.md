# The Parthian Restoration of Antigonus in Judea

The restoration of Antigonus, the son of Aristobulus, to the government of Judea was the result of a strategic alliance between Antigonus and the Parthian Empire, facilitated by regional political interests. This period was marked by the displacement of Hyrcanus and Phasaelus, the flight of Herod, and a series of violent conflicts within Jerusalem.

## The Parthian Intervention
The return of Antigonus was orchestrated through the influence of Lysanias, the son of Ptolemy (son of Menneus), who had succeeded his father in the government of Chalcis. Lysanias formed a league of friendship with Antigonus and used his influence with a Parthian commander to secure military support. To induce the Parthians to act, a promise of one thousand talents and five hundred women was made to the Parthian leadership.

In the second year of this period, Pacorus, the son of the king of Parthia, and a commander named Barzapharnes took possession of Syria. Acting on the agreements made, Pacorus marched along the sea-coast while Barzapharnes advanced through the midland regions to remove Hyrcanus from power and bestow the government upon Antigonus. Additionally, Antigonus had promised that the Parthians would kill Herod as part of the arrangement.

During the maritime advance, the people of Ptolemais and Sidon received Pacorus, although the Tyrians refused him entry. To support the operation, Pacorus dispatched a troop of horsemen into Judea, led by a royal cup-bearer (also named Pacorus), to assess the state of the country and provide assistance to Antigonus.

## The Fall of Jerusalem
Antigonus initially gathered support from the Jews dwelling near Mount Carmel at a place called Drymi. With these forces, he launched a private attack on Jerusalem. As their numbers grew, they besieged the king's palace. A battle subsequently broke out in the market-place between the forces of Antigonus and the parties of Herod and Phasaelus. 

The young men of Antigonus's faction defeated their enemies and pursued them into the temple. Armed men were sent into adjoining houses to trap the remaining opposition; these individuals and the houses they occupied were then burned. Although Herod later fought and slew a great number of these adversaries in revenge, the city eventually fell.

## The Captivity of Hyrcanus and Phasaelus
Upon his return to Judea via the Parthians, Antigonus took Hyrcanus and Phasaelus as prisoners. Antigonus expressed disappointment that the women promised to the Parthians had escaped, as he had intended to give them to the enemy along with the money as a reward.

Fearing that the multitude might restore the kingdom to Hyrcanus while he was under Parthian guard, Antigonus ordered that Hyrcanus's ears be cut off. This maiming was intended to permanently disqualify Hyrcanus from the high priesthood, as the law required that the holder of this dignity possess all their members entire.

Phasaelus, perceiving that he was to be executed, sought to avoid a death delivered by his enemy, which he considered dishonorable. Because his bonds prevented him from killing himself with his hands, he dashed his head against a great stone. Some reports indicate that Antigonus sent physicians to treat the wound, but ordered them to infuse poison into it to ensure his death. Before dying, Phasaelus learned from a woman that his brother Herod had escaped, allowing him to face death cheerfully in the hope that Herod would avenge him.

## Roman Involvement and the Return of Herod
Following the Parthian incursions, the Roman general Ventidius was sent from Syria to restrain the Parthians. Ventidius entered Judea under the pretense of assisting Joseph (Herod's brother) and his party, though his actual objective was to extract money from Antigonus. After stripping Antigonus of a significant amount of wealth, Ventidius retired with most of his forces, leaving a subordinate named Silo in Judea. Antigonus maintained a good relationship with Silo, hoping the Parthians would return to defend him.

Meanwhile, Herod sailed from Italy to Ptolemais, where he assembled an army of foreigners and countrymen. He was assisted by Ventidius and Silo, who had been persuaded by a representative of Antony named Dellius to support Herod's return. As Herod marched through Galilee, most of the region joined him. 

Herod's primary objective was to deliver his relations from a siege at Masada, where they were suffering from a lack of water. Before reaching Masada, Herod first captured the city of Joppa to ensure no enemy stronghold remained behind him. After taking Joppa, he set free his family members at Masada. His support grew as people joined him due to their friendship with his father, his own splendid appearance, or the hope of future rewards once he was settled in the kingdom.
