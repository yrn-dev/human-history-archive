# Babylon: Agriculture and Flora

The region of Babylon, historically associated with the land of the Assyrians, is characterized by a specific climate and highly productive agricultural land. According to the accounts of Herodotus, the land receives very little rain. This limited rainfall provides nourishment for the roots of the corn, but the ripening of the crop and the development of the ear are achieved through manual watering using swing-buckets or irrigation from the river. This differs from the Egyptian system, where the river itself overflows the fields.

## Irrigation and Land Productivity
The Babylonian territory is divided into channels, similar to the layout of Egypt. The largest of these channels are navigable by ships and run from the Euphrates toward the Tigris in the direction of the sunrise during the winter; the city of Nineveh was located along the banks of the Tigris. 

The land is described as being among the best known for the production of corn. Herodotus notes that the average yield is two-hundred-fold, reaching three-hundred-fold at its best, with wheat and barley leaves growing up to four fingers broad. However, Theophrastus provides a more conservative estimate of the soil's fertility, stating the produce of the grain to be fifty-fold or one hundred-fold.

## Flora and Date-Palms
While the land is exceptional for corn, it is noted that it does not bear trees such as the olive, vine, or fig. Instead, date-palms grow across the entire plain. These palms are used to produce honey, wine, and solid food. The inhabitants attend to these palms in a manner similar to how fig-trees are tended. Specifically, they take the fruit of "male-palms" and tie them to date-bearing palms; this process allows the gall-fly from the male-palm to enter the date, which prevents the fruit from falling off and helps it ripen.

Some date-palms in ancient Babylonia bore no fruit but were valued for providing high-quality wood used in the construction of houses and for making furniture.

## The Gardens of Bagoas
The botanical quality of Babylon is further highlighted by the estates of Bagoas, a powerful Persian eunuch and minister to Artaxerxes III. Bagoas rose to significant power, eventually acting as the real master of the kingdom and manipulating the succession of the throne, raising Arses and later Darius III as kings. Bagoas was known for his wealth, which he increased in part by confiscating sacred writings from Egyptian temples and selling them back to priests for bribes.

Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, Bagoas's house in Susa and its treasures were given to Parmenio. Bagoas also maintained gardens in Babylon which were noted by Theophrastus and Pliny for containing the best species of palms.

## Other Regional Products
In addition to corn and dates, the region produced sesame seed. The inhabitants of Babylon did not use olive oil, relying instead on oil produced from sesame seed. The region also grew millet, which produced trees of a size that Herodotus suggests might be viewed with disbelief by those who have not visited the land.
