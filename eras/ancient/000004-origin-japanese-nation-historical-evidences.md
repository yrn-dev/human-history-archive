# Origin of the Japanese Nation: Historical Evidences

The determination of the origins of the Japanese nation relies upon four primary guides: linguistic affinities, physical features, archaeological relics, and written annals. While archaeological and geographical relics provide essential data, the written records—specifically the Chronicles and the Records—offer a foundational, though sometimes disjointed, narrative of the people who inhabited the islands.

## The Written Annals and Identified Peoples

The written annals identify six distinct groups of people involved in the early history of the nation. These include:
*   **Izanagi and his fellow Kami:** These figures are reasonably identified as the original immigrants represented in the narratives concerning the "birth" of the islands.
*   **Jimmu and his followers:** This group is credited with the re-conquest of the islands.
*   **The Yemishi:** Identified as being identical to the modern Ainu.
*   **The Kumaso:** One of the early groups noted in the records.
*   **The Sushen:** A group mentioned in the annals, though historical evidence does not conclusively indicate that they ever immigrated in appreciable numbers.
*   **The Tsuchi-gumo:** Referred to as "earth-spiders."

It is noted that the separation of these six groups in the annals does not necessarily imply that they represented different races. Based on historical evidence, the conclusion is suggested that the Japanese nation is composed of four primary elements: the Yamato, the Yemishi, the Kumaso, and the Sushen.

## The Yemishi and Northern Conflict

The Yemishi were not among the peoples who observed the custom of visiting the Chinese capital and were apparently not part of the family of Far Eastern nations. From the second half of the seventh century, they are recorded as receiving presents and being entertained by the Japanese Court in exchange for tribute. However, this relationship was not universally peaceful.

In the northeastern section of the main island, the Yemishi continued to cause trouble until the beginning of the ninth century. Between A.D. 647 and 800, the Japanese were forced to construct ten barrier posts and six castles along the west coast from the thirty-eighth parallel northward and throughout the northeast region.

During the reign of Emperor Temmu in 682, the Yemishi of Koshi, who had become docile, received seven thousand families of captives to establish a district. While one Japanese writing claims these captives were subjects of the Crown enslaved by savages, this is considered improbable. It is more likely that the Yamato provided prisoners taken from the Sushen, the Koreans, or another hostile nation, as the Yamato would not have condemned their own race to such a position among the Yemishi.

## Theories on Foreign Influence and Insurgency

There are two conjectural explanations regarding the Yemishi of Mutsu. One suggests they were led by chieftains of Yamato origin who migrated northeast due to disaffection or a search for fortune; however, this is considered unlikely as it is only mentioned in one not fully credible book.

The second theory posits that the Yemishi were allied with Tatar hordes who traveled south via the islands of Yezo and Saghalien or crossed from the Asian mainland. Evidence for this includes the names of insurgent leaders: "Akagashira" (red head) and "Akahige" (red beard), which are common Japanese terms for foreigners. Additionally, the name "Akuro-o" may be read as "Oro-o," a term for "Russian." Physical evidence cited for this theory includes a box of foreign construction found at a shrine in Suzuka-yama in Ise and a fortress in the Tsugaru district of modern Mutsu province that possesses non-Japanese features. These inferences, however, remain hypothetical.

## Documentation of National History

The formal project of compiling Japanese history took shape during the reign of Empress Suiko (593-628). At the suggestion of Prince Shotoku, the Empress instructed the Prince and her chief minister, Soga no Umako, to compile historical documents starting in the year 620. This resulted in the *Original Records (Hongi)* of the Free People (the Japanese proper, as opposed to aborigines, captives, and aliens), the *Record of the Country (Koki)*, a *Record of the Emperors (Tennoki)*, and records of the great families and 180 Hereditary Corporations (*Be*).

Most of these documents were destroyed by fire in 645 by Soga chiefs on the eve of their execution for treason. Only the *Record of the Country* was saved and is believed to have been incorporated into the *Kojiki* (Records of Ancient Things). Thirty-seven years later, Emperor Temmu initiated a new effort to correct the history, as he found that existing chronicles and family records were amplified with "empty falsehoods" and deviated from the truth.
