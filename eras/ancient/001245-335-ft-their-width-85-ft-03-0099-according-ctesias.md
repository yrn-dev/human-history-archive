# The City of Babylon

The city of Babylon was constructed on both sides of the river in the form of a square. According to the accounts of Herodotus and Ctesias, the city was enclosed within a double row of lofty walls, though Ctesias adds that there was a third wall.

## Wall Dimensions and Area
Historical estimates regarding the circumference of the outermost wall vary among ancient sources. Ctesias recorded the circumference as 360 stades (42 m.), while Herodotus estimated it at 480 stades (56 m.), an area that would encompass approximately 200 sq. m. Other estimates include 365 stades from Clitarchus, 368 stades from Q. Curtius, and 385 stades from Strabo. Even the smaller estimate provided by Ctesias would imply an area of about 100 sq. m.

Regarding the height and width of the walls, Herodotus stated that the walls were approximately 335 ft. high and 85 ft. wide. Ctesias provided a different estimate for the height, placing it at about 300 ft. While these measurements may seem exaggerated, records from the time of Xenophon note that the ruined wall of Nineveh remained 150 ft. high.

## Construction and Features
The walls of Babylon featured 250 towers. The spaces between these towers were wide enough to allow a four-horse chariot to turn. The bricks used for the walls were made from clay dug from the moat. 

The city was equipped with 100 gates, all constructed of bronze, including the posts and lintels. Two inner enclosures were decorated with enamelled tiles depicting hunting scenes. Additionally, two walls ran along the quays and banks of the Euphrates; these walls contained 25 gates each, corresponding to the number of streets they led into.

## Infrastructure and Connectivity
To facilitate movement between the two parts of the city, ferry-boats operated between the landing-places of the gates. The two sections were also joined by a movable drawbridge that was 30 ft. broad and supported by stone piers.

## Historical Perspectives
The archive notes that the account of Babylon provided by Herodotus was not based on eye-witness testimony and is considered meagre and untrustworthy. Herodotus was controverted by Ctesias, though Ctesias is noted to have mistaken mythology for history, contributing to Greek romances involving figures such as Ninus, Semiramis, Ninyas, and Sardanapalus.
