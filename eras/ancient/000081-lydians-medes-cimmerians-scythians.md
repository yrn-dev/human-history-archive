# Lydians, Medes, Cimmerians, and Scythians

The history of Asia Minor and Upper Asia during the first millennium B.C. is characterized by the rise of indigenous kingdoms and the disruptive migrations of nomadic Aryan tribes. These interactions shaped the political landscape of the region prior to the expansion of the Persian Empire.

## The Lydians
The Lydians inhabited Asia Minor, with their capital at Sardis. Early Lydian history is marked by the transition from the Herakleid dynasty to the Mermnad dynasty, beginning with Gygês. Legends regarding Gygês appear in the writings of Plato, and the broader legends of Asia Minor frequently exhibit a feminine influence. Gygês divided Lydia into two parts: Lydia and Torrhêbia. He was succeeded by his son, Ardys.

Later Lydian kings included Sadyattês and Alyattês. Alyattês engaged in war against Milêtus; although he committed a sacrilege, he eventually made peace with the city following an oracle. Alyattês' reign was long, and his successor, Crœsus, expanded Lydian power by conquering the Asiatic Greeks and capturing Ephesus. Crœsus became the king of all Asia westward of the Halys River. During this period, the philosopher Thalês suggested merging the twelve Ionic cities into a single Pan-Ionic city at Teôs to foster cooperation, though this suggestion was unavailing. The conquests of Crœsus established a new era for the Hellenic world, which was later continued on a larger scale by the Persians.

## The Medes
The Medes, an Aryan people closely related to the Persians, appeared in Assyrian inscriptions around the ninth century B.C., where they were described as the "dangerous Medes." Initially a tribal people not united under a single king, they were forced to pay tribute to Sargon II and Tiglath Pileser III. The first Median king was Dêïokês, though his history is composed of Grecian rather than Oriental materials. Subsequent leaders included Phraortês and Kyaxarês.

Kyaxarês became the king of all the Medes and formed an alliance with the Babylonians; Nebuchadnezzar the Great married a daughter of Kyaxarês. In 606 B.C., Kyaxarês took the Assyrian capital of Nineveh, releasing Babylon from Assyrian rule and facilitating the establishment of the Second Babylonian Empire. For approximately seventy years, the Second Babylonian Empire existed within the influence of the Median power.

## The Cimmerians and Scythians
The Cimmerians and Scythians were nomadic peoples whose migrations caused widespread instability. The Scythians, a formidable and courageous Aryan people from the neighborhood of the Palus Mæotis, drove the Cimmerians out of Europe and into Asia Minor. The Scythians were led by King Madyes.

The Scythian invasion of Upper Asia was more protracted and ruinous than the Cimmerian movement. They overspread nearly all of Asia, reaching as far as Palestine and the borders of Egypt. In Egypt, King Psammetichus diverted their advance through prayers and costly presents. During their return, a detachment of Scythians plundered the temple of Venus (Aphroditê) at Askalon. Some Scythians settled at Bethshan in the tribe of Manasseh, renaming the city Scythopolis.

The Medes initially struggled against the Scythians; Kyaxarês was totally defeated after being called away from the siege of Nineveh. The Scythians dominated Upper Asia for twenty-eight years through cruelty and oppression. Eventually, Kyaxarês and the Medes entrapped the Scythian chiefs at a banquet and slew them while they were intoxicated. Upon their expulsion, the Scythians returned to the Tauric Chersonese (Crimea), where they reportedly fought a battle against their own offspring and rebellious slaves to regain control.

## Related Tribes and Geography
Other related groups included the Sarmatians and the Massagetæ, the latter of whom dwelt on the plains eastward of the Caspian and were considered by many to be of the same race as the Scythians. In the southern Crimea, the Tauri dwelt; they are distinguished from the Scythians by Herodotus, though some sources identify them as remnants of the expelled Cimmerians. The Tauri were known for immolating human sacrifices to a native virgin goddess identified by Greeks as Artemis.

The River Halys served as an ethnographical boundary, with Syro-Arabians located to the east. To the north of Asia Minor lived the Thracian race. Additionally, the Phrygians influenced early Greek colonists, particularly in the realm of music, from which the Greeks borrowed parts of their musical scale. While legendary figures like Midas son of Gordius are mentioned—Midas being the first foreign sovereign to send offerings to the Delphian temple—there is little evidence of a great united Phrygian monarchy.

## Lydian-Median Relations
A conflict arose between the Lydians and the Medes when Alyattês refused to surrender the Scythians to Kyaxarês. This war lasted five years, with both sides achieving intermittent victories. In the sixth year, a battle occurred during which a sudden eclipse turned day into night—an event foretold by Thales the Milesian. This phenomenon prompted both nations to seek peace. The peace was brokered by Labynetos the Babylonian and Syennesis the Kilikian. To secure the agreement, an interchange of marriages was arranged, and Alyattês gave his daughter Aryenis to Astyages, the son of Kyaxarês. The peace treaty was sealed with oaths involving incisions in the skin of the arms and the licking of each other's blood.
