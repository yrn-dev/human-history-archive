# The Spirit of Greek Monachism and the Reforms of Theodore

The spirit of Greek monachism, as regenerated by Theodore, is most clearly understood through his *Letters, Discourses and Testament*. Theodore became the abbot of the monastery of the Studium in Constantinople around the year 800, a period characterized by general decadence in Greek monasticism, which had otherwise seen little development or change for four centuries. Theodore is regarded as the only other creative name in Greek monachism besides St Basil.

## The Studite Reformation
Theodore's primary objective was to reform his monastery and restore the spirit of St Basil in its primitive vigour. To ensure the permanence of this reformation, Theodore recognized the need for a practical code of laws to regulate the details of daily life, serving as a supplement to St Basil's Rules. He drew up constitutions that were later codified and became the norm for life at the Studium. These constitutions gradually spread to other monasteries throughout the Greek empire. To this day, the Constitutions of Theodore the Studite, the Rules of Basil, and the canons of the Councils form the chief part of Russian and Greek monastic law.

## Organization and Daily Life
Under Theodore's rule, the monastery grew from ten monks to over a thousand. The administration included several officials under the abbot who superintended various departments. The daily schedule was divided between liturgical services in the church—which occupied a considerable portion of the day—and a combination of reading and work. While Theodore did not attempt to revive the early Studium practice regarding liturgical services, he enforced a life that was simple, hard, and self-denying, though not one of extreme austerity.

The monks were divided into two distinct classes:
*   **Choir-monks:** Their life was predominantly contemplative, focused on private prayer, study, and church services.
*   **Lay-brothers:** This group was responsible for external works and various trades.

Strict personal poverty was enforced for all members, and monks were encouraged to frequently approach communion and confession. Additionally, vows had been imposed on monks by the council of Chalcedon in 451. There is little to no evidence that Studite monks undertook works of charity outside the monastery.

## Intellectual and Manual Labor
The Studite monks pursued theological and literary studies, including the cultivation of the mysticism of pseudo-Dionysius. They were particularly zealous and successful in the copying of manuscripts. This effort preserved many ecclesiastical and classical works of antiquity and resulted in a great number of existing Greek manuscripts. Manual labor was also central to the community, encompassing the chief handicrafts.

## Influence and Legacy
The picture of life at the Studium represents the normal form of Slavonic and Greek monachism to the present day. The importation of Basilian monachism into Russia was of significant importance, as it became the norm for all Slavonic lands. Greek monks were instrumental in the evangelization of the Slavs; for example, a monk from Mount Athos founded the first Russian monastery at Kiev around 1050.

In the Russian empire, the monastic institute developed extensively, with some 100 monasteries for women and 400 for men, many of which support schools, almshouses, and hospitals. In the Turkish dominions, there are no fewer than 100 Greek monasteries.

## Types of Monasteries
Greek and Slavonic monks typically wear a black habit. Monasteries are categorized into three types:
1.  **Cenobia proper:** These observe full monastic common life and personal poverty.
2.  **Idiorrhythmic:** In these, monks lead a more free and mitigated life and are permitted the use of private means.
3.  **Lauras:** These are characterized by a semi-eremitical life.

While the Basilian cenobitic form eventually prevailed throughout Greek-speaking lands due to civil legislation (Justinian Code) and ecclesiastical legislation (council of Chalcedon), eremitical forms have always maintained themselves. Over time, the center of Greek monachism shifted from Constantinople to Mount Athos. The first monastery there, that of St Athanasius, was founded around 960, followed by numerous Greek, Slavonic, and one Latin monastery.
