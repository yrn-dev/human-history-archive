# The Self-Description of the Englishman by Andrew Boorde

## The Works of Andrew Boorde
Andrew Boorde was a learned physician who authored several works concerning medicine and domestic hygiene. Among his authentic writings are *The Brevyary of Health* (dated approximately 1547), *The Princyples of Astronamy* (approximately 1547), and a work titled *Here foloweth a Compendyous Regimente or Dyetary of health, made in Mountpyllor*, which was printed by Thomas Colwell in 1562, though earlier undated editions exist. Other attributed works include "The Peregrination of Doctor Board," printed in 1735 in *Benedictus Abbas Petroburgensis*, and *A Pronostycacyon or an Almanacke for the yere of our lorde MCCCCCXLV*. While he wrote an *Itinerary of Europe* and a *Treatyse upon Berdes*, these texts are now lost. Additionally, several jest-books are attributed to him without authority, including *Scogin's Jests* (1626), *The Merie Tales of the Mad Men of Gotam* (earliest extant edition 1630), a Latin poem titled *Nos Vagabunduli*, and *A mery jest of the Mylner of Abyngton, with his wyfe, and his daughter, and of two poore scholers of Cambridge*.

## The Introduction of Knowledge
Boorde's most entertaining work is titled *The Fyrst Boke of the Introduction of Knowledge. The whyche dothe teache a man to speake parte of all maner of languages, and to know the usage and fashion of all maner of countreys. And for to know the moste parte of all maner of coynes of money, the whych is currant in every region*. This work, written around 1547, was dedicated to Lady Mary, the daughter of King Henry VIII. 

The book is structured to provide information on various countries, utilizing a consistent format for each. For example, after the initial sections, the text provides a geographical description of the country and includes a model dialogue in the Cornish language.

## Observations on the Englishman
Within *The Fyrst Boke of the Introduction of Knowledge*, Boorde provides a description of the Englishman. Writing in lively verse, he describes himself and the foibles associated with his countrymen. Specifically, Boorde identifies the Englishman's fickleness, his obstinacy, and his fondness for new fashions.
