# The Efficient Cause of Summer Rains

The phenomenon of summer rains varies significantly by region, with different historical and scientific explanations provided for the causes of precipitation in South Asia, North Africa, and Eastern Europe.

## Summer Rains in South Asia

In South Asia, the principal efficient cause of summer rains is linked to the interaction between vapour-bearing winds and geographical obstacles. Analysis of observed phenomena shows that the heaviest rainfall occurs where mountain ranges directly face these winds. Examples include the west coast of the Malay peninsula and the Western Ghats of India. 

The most extreme rainfall recorded anywhere in the world—at least 600 inches annually—occurs on the Khasi range, located approximately 100 miles north-east of Calcutta. This high volume is attributed to the range presenting an abrupt front to moist winds flowing upward from the Bay of Bengal. 

Conversely, geographical features can limit rainfall. The projection of the south-east coast of Arabia restricts the length of the coastline exposed to the south-west monsoon air current and limits the breadth of the current itself, causing the rains to cease on the southern border of Baluchistan, west of Karachi. Similarly, the irregular and small amount of rain in Sind and along the Indus is caused by a lack of obstacles in the path of vapour-bearing winds. These winds carry uncondensed rain further inland to the Punjab, where it eventually falls on the outer ranges of Afghanistan and the western Himalaya.

## The Role of the Sun and Winds in Libya and the Nile

Regarding the summer increase of the Nile, Herodotus proposes that the Sun is the primary cause. He explains that when the Sun passes through the upper parts of Libya—a region characterized by warm weather, clear air, and an absence of cold winds—it draws water to itself. The Sun then drives this water to the upper parts of the country, where winds take it up, scatter it, and melt it into rain. Consequently, the South and South-west winds are the most rainy.

Herodotus suggests the Sun does not release all the water of the Nile each year, retaining some. During the milder winter, the Sun returns to the midst of the heaven and draws equally from all rivers. While other rivers flow in large volumes during winter due to mingling rain and torrent streams, they become weak in summer because rain fails and the Sun draws their water. The Nile is unique in that it lacks rain and is drawn by the Sun, causing it to flow in much less than its proper volume during the winter, while increasing in the summer.

Other accounts attribute the Nile's inundation to rains falling in Ethiopia (Abyssinia). These rains begin in April and continue for five months until the beginning of September or the end of August. This causes the Nile to begin rising in May, though it does not overflow its banks until the end of June (the summer solstice), continuing to rise until the end of September before gradually decreasing in October and November.

## Precipitation in the Ister and Scythia

In the region of the Ister (Danube), the river maintains a relatively equal volume in both summer and winter. In winter, the land receives very little rain and instead experiences constant snow. In the summer, the abundant snow that fell during winter melts and runs into the Ister. This meltwater, combined with many violent summer rain showers, swells the river's volume. This increase in summer is balanced by the amount of water the Sun draws away during the summer months, resulting in a consistent river level.

In Scythia, the climate is exceptionally severe, with intolerable frost for eight months of the year. In this region, the usual seasonal patterns of rain are inverted; there is almost no rain during the winter, but it rains continually during the summer. Thunder is also very frequent in the summer, whereas its occurrence in winter is regarded as a prodigy.
