# Greek Jews of the Day

The history of the Israelites during the period of Greek influence is characterized by a divergence in thought and practice, particularly between those who adhered strictly to traditional law and those who sought to assimilate with the surrounding Hellenized cultures.

## Hellenization and Social Division
Within Jewish affairs, two distinct lines of thought emerged. On one side were the "high and narrow" Jews, represented by the Pharisees. This group was characterized by intense patriotism, exclusivity, and a punctilious adherence to the minutest details of the law. Their commitment to religious observance was such that Jerusalem fell to the Seleucid monarch Antiochus IV and later to Pompey the Great because the Israelites refused to work or destroy siege trains on the Sabbath day.

Opposing this orthodox group were the "broad Jews," known as Hellenizers. This group included the Sadducees, who did not believe in immortality. The Hellenizers were disposed to mingle and assimilate with the Greeks and other Hellenized peoples. While they were more generous in their willingness to accept proselytes and share God's promise with all mankind, they were regarded as the worldlings of Judea. A notable consequence of this cultural shift occurred in Egypt, where Hellenized Jews lost their Hebrew language, necessitating the translation of their Bible into Greek.

## Philo and the Influence of Cynicism
Philo (20 B.C.–A.D. 40) provided detailed descriptions of a life that he believed embodied the highest wisdom. His views were heavily inspired by the more respectable forms of cynicism, a philosophy that had taken deep root among the Greek Jews of that era. 

In his tract *On Change of Names*, Philo describes a company of the "good and wise" who voluntarily divested themselves of copious wealth and rejected things dear to the flesh. He contrasts these disciples of wisdom with athletes; while athletes are "lusty" and "of good habit" because they fortify the body to serve the soul, the disciples of wisdom are described as "pale and wasted," appearing almost as skeletons. This physical state resulted from their decision to sacrifice all bodily strength to the faculties of the soul.

## The Therapeutae and Asceticism
Philo’s favorite ascetics were the Therapeutae, whose primary center was located in Egypt. This group practiced a rigorous form of renunciation, abandoning all property and the temptations associated with it. Their commitment to this lifestyle involved the irrevocable abandonment of:
* Parents and children
* Wives and brothers
* Throngs of kinsmen
* Intimacy of friends
* The fatherlands of their birth and upbringing

This ideal of renunciation operated independently of the influence of Jesus, though a similar ideal is found in the writings of Epictetus.
