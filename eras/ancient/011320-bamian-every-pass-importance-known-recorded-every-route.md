# Bamian

Bamian is a once renowned city of Afghanistan, located approximately 80 miles northwest of Kabul. It is situated in a valley within the Hazara country, positioned at the northern foot of the Koh-i-Baba, which is a prolongation of the Indian Caucasus. The city lies on the primary road leading from Kabul toward Turkestan.

## Geography and Topography
The valley of Bamian is situated at a height of approximately 8,500 feet. The surrounding terrain was carefully surveyed in November 1885 by the Hon. M. G. Talbot and Major Pelham J. Maitland during the work of the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission. The region is characterized by significant mountain barriers; the passes on the Kabul side reach absolute heights of 11,000 and 12,000 feet, while the passes to the north are only slightly lower. 

The valley is drained by a river that serves as one of the primary sources of the Aksarai (also known as the Surkhab), which is an important tributary of the Upper Oxus. The cliffs lining the valley are topped with the remains of numerous massive towers. For a distance of six or seven miles, the precipitous faces of these cliffs are pierced by an infinity of ancient cave-dwellings, some of which remain occupied. These excavations are possible because the hills are composed of pebbles and indurated clay.

## Historical Overview
The history of Bamian is described as very obscure, and it cannot be identified with any city in classical geography, specifically not *Alexandria ad Caucasum*. The first known mention of the city is by Hsuan-Tsang. At that time, Bamian appeared to have passed its meridian and served as the head of one of the small states resulting from the breakup of the empire of the White Huns.

Later, for a period of half a century ending in A.D. 1214, Bamian was the seat of a branch of the Ghori dynasty, which ruled over the basin of the Upper Oxus, or Tokharistan. In 1222, the city was besieged and annihilated by Jenghiz Khan. This destruction was prompted by the death of a favorite grandson of the Khan, who was killed by an arrow fired from the city walls. While there is no further record of Bamian as a city after this event, traditions and the character of ruins at Ghulgulah suggest the city was rebuilt after the Mongol period and subsequently perished again.

In 1840, during the British occupation of Kabul, the site was the location of a military action where Colonel William H. Dennie, leading a small force, routed Dost Mahommed Khan and several Uzbeg chiefs.

## Archaeological Remains
The site of the old city is currently marked by walls and mounds. An isolated rock in the center of the valley contains considerable ruins identified as the acropolis, known locally as Ghulgulah.

The most famous remains are two colossal standing idols carved into the cliffs on the north side of the valley, measuring 173 feet and 120 feet in height. These images are set in niches coated with stucco, as are the images themselves. Both have suffered significant damage, apparently from cannon-shot. The larger idol features an uninterpreted inscription above it and staircases on either side of its niche. These staircases lead to a chamber near the head of the idol, which shows traces of gilding and azure ornamentation; these chambers are currently used by the amir as grain store-houses. Figures have also been painted on the surfaces of the niches.

Additionally, a similar but slightly smaller colossus exists in one of the branch valleys, and there are indications of further idols and niches. Records also mention a recumbent figure located 2 miles east of Bamian, approximately 1,000 feet in length, representing Sakya Buddha entering *Nirv[=a]na* (the act of death). This figure was likely made of plastered rubble and lacked durability, as modern travelers have not alluded to its traces.

## Regional Significance
Bamian is part of a broader strategic mountain system that connects the southern abutment of the Hindu Kush with the water-divide north of Herat. This system represents a definite factor in practical politics. In this region, every route of significance has been explored and mapped, and every important pass has been recorded. On the road from Kabul to Bamian, near Sir-i-Chashma, there is a sacred pond where fish were traditionally protected from being touched, except on the day of the vernal equinox when the fish would move into a rivulet.
