# Mons Graupius

Mons Graupius is the site of a "famous victory" won by the Roman general Julius Agricola during the Roman invasion of Caledonia. The location is sometimes incorrectly spelled as Grampius.

## Historical Context and Campaign
Julius Agricola, described as the third and best-known of the generals of his era, moved toward the conquest of the farther north in A.D. 80. As part of his strategic operations, he established a frontier between the Forth and the Clyde intended to be permanent. This frontier was guarded by a line of forts, including those at Bar Hill and Camelon (near Falkirk), both of which have been identified and excavated.

Following the establishment of this frontier, Agricola advanced into Caledonia—the Roman name for North Britain, a term that appears in Roman literature and the poet Lucan (A.D. 64). The Romans first invaded Caledonia under Agricola around A.D. 83.

## The Battle of Mons Graupius
According to Tacitus, the decisive battle of the war was fought on the slopes of Mons Graupius. Tacitus represents the outcome of this engagement as a victory for the Roman forces. 

The exact site of the battle remains a subject of hot contest among antiquaries, as well as between Romans and Caledonians. One proposed location is near the confluence of the Isla and the Tay. This theory is supported by the presence of Inchtuthill, a Roman encampment that has been partly examined. Inchtuthill is the most northerly ascertained Roman encampment in Scotland and appears to date from the age of Agricola.

## Aftermath and Roman Withdrawal
Despite the victory at Mons Graupius, the Roman presence in the region was short-lived. Agricola had envisioned invading Ireland, believing it to be an easy task, but the home government disagreed. The government recalled both the legion (Legio II Adiutrix) and the governor. This decision was driven by a need for the legion elsewhere, an aversion to costly and fruitless campaigns in barren hills, and possibly jealousy of a general who had become too brilliant.

As a result, the nominal conquests in the north—all areas beyond Cheviot or the Tweed—were abandoned. While the district up to the Firth of Forth was definitely annexed around A.D. 140 (marked by the Wall of Antoninus Pius and forts at Carpow and Ardoch), the conquest was stubbornly disputed. The land north of Cheviot appears to have been lost again around A.D. 180-185.

## Legacy and Nomenclature
The most enduring result of Agricola's campaigns is linguistic rather than territorial. The misspelling of the battlefield as "Grampius" provided antiquaries with the basis for the modern name of the Grampian Hills.
