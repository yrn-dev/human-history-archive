# Sargon I

## Identification and Nomenclature
Sargon I is described as a famous figure with a "Semitic" name. In the study of ancient names, his full name is generally read as *Sharru-kenu-sha-ali*. This phonetic reading is typically interpreted to mean "the legitimate king of the city."

## Scholarly Debate
The reading of Sargon I's name is an example of the ongoing vacillation and difficulty found in the research of ancient phonetic readings. Recently, questions have been raised regarding the established reading, with some scholars suggesting that the name should instead be read as *Sharru-kenu-shar-ri*. Under this alternative reading, the name would be interpreted as "the legitimate king rules."

## Context of Ancient Name Research
The uncertainty surrounding the name of Sargon I exists within a broader complex of research into Babylonian, Assyrian, and Sumerian nomenclature. In these fields, scholars often rely on Aramaic endorsements on business documents—which repeat the names of parties in Aramaic transliteration—to help fix phonetic readings.

Research into these names reveals various patterns of formation. Some full names contain three or more elements, while others were reduced to two elements early on, either by combining a verbal form with the name of a deity or by omitting the deity's name entirely. Additionally, names of a single element often feature "hypocoristic affixes" (similar to modern pet-names), such as *-tum* (feminine), *sha*, *um*, *an*, *a*, *atum*, or *atija*.

Further complications arise with genuine or pseudo-Sumerian names. Because multiple phonetic values can attach to the signs used, and because knowledge of the ancient Sumerian language remains imperfect, scholars frequently differ on readings. This is exemplified by other historical figures and locations, such as a king of Ur whose name was read as Ur-Bau but is now read as Ur-Engur, or a king of Erech read by some as Lugal-zaggisi and by others as Ungal-zaggisi. 

In some instances, doubt persists as to whether the bearer of a name was a Sumerian or a Semite. If the bearer was a Semite, the signs might be read according to Semitic equivalents, or they might represent an ideographic compound rather than a phonetic reading.
