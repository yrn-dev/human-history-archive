# Identification in Historical and Scientific Analysis

The process of identification—whether applied to ancient geography, ethnography, linguistics, or biology—is a recurring challenge in historical and scientific research. Across various disciplines, scholars attempt to link ancient names, artifacts, or species to known entities, though these efforts are often complicated by a lack of tangible evidence or the limitations of nomenclature.

## Geographical and Urban Identifications
In the study of the cities of Bashan, the identification of specific sites remains a subject of debate. For example, Edrei has been identified as *Ed-Dera'a*, a comparison considered perhaps the most satisfactory due to the presence of Greco-Roman remains and an extensive subterranean city of unknown date. However, this remains *sub judice*. 

Other identifications are viewed with more skepticism. Ashteroth Karnaim (also known as Ashtaroth or Be-eshterah) has been linked to *Busrah* (Bostra) because of important Herodian ruins, yet there is no tangible evidence to prove the site's remote antiquity. Due to name similarities, researchers have also looked at *Tell 'Ashtera* and *Tell Ashari*. Similarly, Kenath in eastern Manasseh has been identified by Porter as Kanawat. Salecah is considered less doubtful, as a ruin called *Salkhat* exists in the Hauran and is inhabited by Druses. These examples highlight a broader scholarly warning: identifications based solely on the outward similarity of names are often fruitful sources of error and can only be truly determined through excavation.

## Ethnographic and Tribal Identifications
The identification of peoples and tribes often involves analyzing migrations and nomenclature. In the Indian borderlands, the identification of existing peoples with Arab, Persian, and Scythic races who entered from High Asia has created a vast field of ethnographical inquiry. This includes the study of "reflex waves" that flowed from India back into the border highlands.

In the records of Marco Polo, the identification of the *Mekrit* (or *Mescript*/*Mecri*) is discussed. While some, such as D’Avezac, believe the *Kerait* are intended, Polo's assigned position for the *Mekrit* agrees with authorities regarding the Merkit. However, it is noted that when Polo rehearses hearsay information, the identification of a name does not guarantee the correctness of the associated circumstances. For instance, under the name *Mekrit*, Polo includes characteristics that actually belong to the Tunguses (described by Rashiduddin as *Uriangkut* of the Woods), who lived beyond the frontier of Barguchin and used birch bark tents and snow-shoes.

## Biographical and Figure Identifications
Historical figures are often subject to varying identifications across different traditions. Balaam is a primary example:
* **Rabbinical (Yalkut) and Modern Critics:** Some identify Balaam with Laban, the father-in-law of Jacob.
* **Islamic Tradition:** Mahommedan fables describe him as one of the Anakim (giants of Palestine) who read the books of Abraham and used the name Yahweh to predict the future.
* **Arabic Tradition:** It has been conjectured that the wise man Luqman is identical to Balaam, based on the similarity of their fathers' names and the fact that "Luqman" means "devourer" or "swallower," a meaning reachable via popular etymology of "Balaam."

Depending on the source (such as the Priestly Code, E, or J), Balaam is variously identified as a Midianite, an Aramaean, a native of south Judah, an Ammonite, or an Edomite.

## Scientific and Material Identifications
Identification is also central to archaeology and biology. In musicology, the construction of the lyre and cithara provides "irrefutable evidence of identification." Both instruments feature a sound-chest—the lyre with a vaulted back imitating a tortoise and the cithara with a shallower chest connected by ribs—characteristics that persisted into the middle ages.

In paleontology, identifications are often more tentative. The hypothesis that Vertebrates derived from Arachnids or Crustaceans is based on the resemblance between Silurian mailed fishes (*Ostracodermi*) and Arthropods, though morphologists often dismiss this as superficial "mimetic resemblance." Similarly, conodonts from the Ordovician were once identified as the teeth of Cyclostomes, but histological structure has since invalidated this identification.
