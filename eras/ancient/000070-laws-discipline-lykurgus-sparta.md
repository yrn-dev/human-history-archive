# Laws and Discipline of Lykurgus at Sparta

The figure of Lykurgus is central to the historical identity of Sparta, though ancient sources provide conflicting accounts of his life. Plutarch notes that absolutely nothing can be asserted about Lykurgus that is not controverted, including his birth, travels, death, and the timing of his legislative activities. Some accounts suggest he was sent by the Delphian oracle to reform the state following a long absence from Sparta.

## Political Constitution
The constitution ascribed to Lykurgus is described as a close oligarchy that remained formally unchanged for a long duration, a fact that fostered pride among Spartans and respect throughout Greece. This system was not typical of the Dorians in general but was peculiar to Sparta.

The government featured a pair of kings whose constant dissensions served as a security for the state against despotism. Alongside the kings were the ephors, a board of popular origin. An oath was interchanged between the kings and the ephors; during historical times, the ephors held supremacy and the kings were subordinated. The ephors are viewed ambiguously in historical records: some see them as a democratic element because any Spartan citizen could become one, while others view them as a despotical element due to their lack of responsibility and restraint in exercising power. Other components of the constitution included a Senate and a public assembly.

## Social Structure and Population
The population of Laconia was divided into three distinct groups:
1. **Spartans**: The citizen class.
2. **Periœki**: A group whose origin is debated by Isokratês and Ephorus, though no racial distinction between them and the Spartans was known in historical times.
3. **Helots**: Essentially villagers and serfs (*adscripti glebæ*). The Spartans treated the Helots with cruelty and fear, utilizing the Krypteia to maintain control.

The Spartan citizens themselves were divided into three Dorian tribes: the Hylleis, Pamphyli, and Dymanes.

## The Lykurgean Discipline
Lykurgus is characterized less as a political lawgiver and more as the founder of a "warlike brotherhood." His system aimed to create a society where citizens lived like "bees in a hive," with their feelings entirely implicated in the commonwealth and divorced from home and family. 

A primary feature of this system was the interdiction of all written laws through one of the three primitive Rhetræ. Instead of formal enactments, magistrates decided disputes based on their own sense of equity, guided by the personal discipline they had undergone. This discipline was a universal system of schooling, training, and drilling imposed on all—rich and poor, men and women, youths and virgins. It focused on bodily efficiency, endurance, and single-minded obedience to ensure the state was always ready for conquest, defense, and dominion.

This rigorous training resulted in a character that some sources describe as savage and fierce, lacking in letters and narrow in scope. To maintain this environment, Sparta restricted communication with strangers. No Spartan could go abroad without leave, and strangers were not permitted to stay; they could be removed via a process called *xenêlasy*.

## Economic and Social Regulations
Various economic measures are attributed to Lykurgus, including the establishment of the *syssitia* (public mess) and the partition of lands. 

There is significant historical debate regarding the "absolute equality of property." While later reformers like Agis and Kleomenês attempted to redistribute land and cancel debts to revive the state, claiming these were original Lykurgean institutions, earlier evidence contradicts this. Thucydidês observed that rich men simply assimilated their clothing and habits to the simplicity of the poor. Xenophon noted that wealth provided little superior comfort. Furthermore, the poet Alkæus recorded complaints as early as 600-580 B.C. that wealth provided the only means of being honored in Sparta. Historians such as Hellanikus and Herodotus make no mention of an original equal redivision of land.
