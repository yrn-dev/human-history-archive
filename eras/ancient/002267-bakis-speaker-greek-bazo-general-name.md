# BAKIS

The Bakis (derived from the Greek *bazo*, meaning "speaker") were a general class of inspired prophets and dispensers of oracles who were active in Greece between the 8th and 6th centuries B.C.

## Classification and Notable Figures
According to Suidas, there were three primary figures associated with this name: one from Athens, one from Arcadia, and one from Boeotia. The Boeotian Bakis was the most famous of the three and was said to have received his inspiration from the nymphs of the Corycian cave. 

The Arcadian Bakis is credited with curing the women of Sparta during a fit of madness. However, it is noted that many of the oracles attributed to the Arcadian Bakis may have actually been authored by Onomacritus.

## Characteristics of the Oracles
The oracles of the Boeotian Bakis were composed in hexameter verse. These prophecies were regarded as having been strikingly fulfilled. Specimens of these writings are preserved in the works of Pausanias and Herodotus.

## Historical Examples and Impact
The oracles of Bakis played a role in the actions and misfortunes of various Greek populations during conflicts with the Persians:

*   **The Euboeans:** Bakis issued a warning to the Euboeans, stating: "Mark, when a man, a Barbarian, shall yoke the Sea with papyrus, Then do thou plan to remove the loud-bleating goats from Euboea." The Euboeans neglected this oracle, treating it as if it had no meaning or was unworthy of credit. Consequently, they failed to store provisions for war or remove their possessions from their land, which brought trouble upon them and resulted in severe punishment for their presumption.
*   **The Battle of Asopos:** Bakis composed an oracle regarding a battle by the banks of the grassy Asopos, where the river Thermodon flows between Glisas and Tanagra. The prophecy described a gathering of Hellenes and the cries of Barbarian voices, predicting that many "Medes bow-bearing" would fall before their destiny when the fatal day approached.

## Legacy and Attribution
Because the collections of Bakis (and others such as Musæus) were respected, they were sometimes used as a cover for fabricated prophecies. For instance, fabricators like Onomacritus would circulate new prophecies by claiming they were part of old, established collections, such as those of Bakis, to give them more credibility.
