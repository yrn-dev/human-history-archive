# The State of Greece from the Earliest Times to the Commencement of the Peloponnesian War

The history of Greece is characterized by a transition from a legendary, heroic age of personal ascendency to a historical era of organized city-states and collective political power. This evolution culminated in the great conflict between the Athenians and the Peloponnesians, an event Thucydides described as the greatest movement known in history, involving not only the Hellenes but a large portion of the barbarian world.

## Legendary and Heroic Greece
The earliest state of Grecian society is preserved in legendary poems, which provide pictures of real manners rather than historical facts. In this legendary period, society was dominated by the king, who possessed an overruling personal ascendency. While the *boulê* (council) and the *agora* (assembly) existed, they were subordinate to the king; the *agora* served primarily as a medium for the king to promulgate his intentions. Justice was administered by the king or chiefs in the *agora*, though some, such as Hesiod, complained of unjust judgments.

Social life in the heroic age was driven by personal feelings toward individuals, the king, or the gods. Strong family ties existed, and respect was paid to wives. Hospitality toward strangers and suppliants was a core value. However, this era was also marked by unrestrained aggressive passions and ferocious behavior, including the mutilation of dead bodies. Homicide was typically appeased through *poinè* (valuable compensation) paid to the kinsman of the deceased, rather than being treated as a crime against society.

Economically, the Homeric Greeks engaged in limited commerce and navigation, interacting with the Phoenicians, Taphians, and Kretans. Their society included various classes, such as slaves and *thêtes*.

## Religious and Cultural Integration
Early Greek sociality was fostered through common sacrifice between near neighbors. The sentiment of fraternity between villages or tribes was manifested by sending a *Theôria* (sacred legation) to offer sacrifices at festivals and participate in recreations. These arrangements established truces with solemn guarantees.

Over time, these small village festivals expanded into town festivals, eventually evolving into the massive confluence of the Pythian and Olympic games. The city administering these ceremonies, such as Elis for the Olympic games, enjoyed inviolability of territory during the event. Elis was known to impose heavy fines on other towns—including the powerful Lacedæmon—for violating the Olympic truce.

Cultural life also included a passion for horse-racing. The four-horse chariot-race was introduced to the Olympic games by the 23rd Olympiad, followed by mounted horse races in the 33rd. Similar contests occurred at the Nemean, Isthmian, and Pythian games, as well as the Athenian Panathenaea.

## Political Evolution and the Rise of Despots
As Greece entered the historical age (beginning in 776 B.C.), the Council and Assembly replaced the king as the paramount depositaries of power, though Spartan kings remained an exception with limited powers. The use of public speaking became an engine of government, stimulating intellectual development.

The political landscape shifted from monarchies to oligarchies, reflecting an advance in the Greek mind and a growing anti-monarchical sentiment. However, dissatisfaction with oligarchies often led to the rise of despots. These demagogue-despots differed from the early heroic kings; they often acquired power through the dissatisfaction of the people. In cities like Corinth, Sikyôn, and Megara, dynasties of despots emerged, such as the Kypselids in Corinth (including Kypselus and Periander) and the Orthagoridæ in Sikyôn.

The military structure also evolved. While early oligarchies relied on cavalry, the rise of free military marines and heavy-armed infantry proved unfavorable to oligarchical rule.

## Regional Developments and Colonies
In the Æolic settlements, Lesbos was the earliest colony, originally consisting of six independent governments: Mitylêne, Mêthymna, Eresus, Pyrrha, Antissa, and Arisbê. After the Methymnæans destroyed Arisbê, five towns remained, with Mitylêne being the most prominent. Other Æolic towns on the continent, such as Kymê, were generally small and unimportant.

In Attica, early history was recorded by prose logographers in works called *Atthides*. These writers interwove legends, such as those of Theseus—who is noted in the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* for carrying Ariadnê from Krête—and the religious legends of Eleusis, into a single chronological sequence.

## The Prelude to the Peloponnesian War
By the time of the Peloponnesian War, Greece was divided by the competing influences of Athens and Sparta. For over thirty years, Sparta had claimed to interfere in Greek affairs only to liberate Greece from the "usurped ascendency" of Athens. Sparta positioned itself as the "Liberator of Greece," while Athens was branded as the "despot city" that had absorbed the independence of many Greeks.

In 432 B.C., the Corinthians urged Sparta to fulfill its function as liberator. In the winter preceding the war, Lacedæmonian envoys demanded that Athens restore autonomy to the Greeks to maintain peace. When King Archidamus of Sparta besieged Platæa, he affirmed that the sole purpose of the Spartan effort was to secure autonomy for all Greeks enslaved by Athens. This banner of general enfranchisement garnered widespread sympathy across Greece.
