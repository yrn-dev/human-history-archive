# Babylonian Civilization: Chronology, Law, and Culture

The estimation of the age of Babylonian civilization has been subject to significant revision due to reductions in the dates assigned to its earlier dynasties. Historically, many writers assigned dates as high as 5000 or 6000 B.C. to the earliest remains of the Babylonian Semites and Sumerians. These high estimates relied heavily on a statement by Nabonidus, who claimed that 3200 years separated his own era from that of Nar-am-Sin, the son of Sargon of Agade. Based on this single statement, Sargon was typically assigned a date of 3800 B.C.

## Chronological Debates and Evidence
The accuracy of Nabonidus's statement has been contested. Winckler viewed the claim as an uncritical exaggeration, while Lehmann-Haupt suggested the text be emended to reduce the figure by a thousand years. It is suggested that the scribes of Nabonidus may have mistakenly reckoned several early dynasties as consecutive when they were actually contemporaneous, or they may have been motivated by a desire not to diminish the antiquity of the foundation-inscription of Nar-am-Sin.

Other authorities vary on these dates; some place Sargon I at 3750 B.C. based on traditional Babylonian records. While a definite date for the early kings of Agade remains elusive, archaeological and epigraphic evidence suggests that the empire of the Semitic kings of Agade was not separated by a very long interval from the kings of Sumer and Akkad, whose rule began with the Dynasty of Ur. Furthermore, excavations at Eridu conducted by Capt. R. Campbell Thompson revealed a Neolithic agricultural stage beneath the earliest Sumerian foundations, where crops were cut with earthenware sickles. Sayce estimates that Eridu was on the seacoast in 6500 B.C.

## Law and Social Structure
The legal framework of Babylonia is centered on "the Code," which served as a backbone for the region's legal history. Recovered fragments from Assur-bani-pal's library at Nineveh and later Babylonian copies indicate the Code was divided into chapters titled *Ninu ilu [s.]irum* and was recopied for over fifteen hundred years. This legal system remained largely in force through the conquests of the Greeks, Persians, and Parthians, eventually influencing later Mahommedan law in Mesopotamia and Syro-Roman law.

The evolution of law in the region is divided into "early" law (the customs preceding the Code) and "late" law (that of the New Babylonian empire and subsequent conquests). When Semitic tribes settled in Babylonian cities, their tribal customs transitioned into city law. Early history was characterized by struggles for supremacy between cities; while a dominant metropolis would demand military support and tribute, it generally left local customs and cults unaffected.

## Contrast with Assyria
Babylonia and Assyria differed fundamentally in character. Babylonia was primarily a land of agriculturists and merchants, whereas Assyria functioned as an organized camp. This distinction extended to their leadership: Assyrian dynasties were founded by successful generals, while in Babylonia, revolutions raised priests to the throne. The Babylonian king remained a priest under the control of a powerful hierarchy. In contrast, the Assyrian king was an autocratic general supported by a feudal nobility and, from the time of Tiglath-pileser III, an elaborate bureaucracy. While the Assyrian people were predominantly soldiers, the role of the sailor belonged to Babylonia.

## Religion and Astrology
The development of Babylonian-Assyrian religion is categorized into four periods:
1. The oldest period (c. 3500 B.C. to c. 2250 B.C., the time of Khammurabi).
2. The post-Khammurabic period in Babylonia.
3. The Assyrian period (c. 2000 B.C. to the destruction of Nineveh in 606 B.C.).
4. The neo-Babylonian period (beginning with Nabopolassar, 625-604 B.C., and ending with Cyrus's conquest in 539 B.C., though the cults persisted until the Greek conquest in 331 B.C.).

Early Babylonian astrology and "liver" divination were empirical and focused almost exclusively on public welfare and the person of the king, rather than individual horoscopes. The fortunes of the country were believed to depend on the king's favor with the gods. Individuals utilized purification rituals and incantations to ward off evil spirits or demons, but the primary goal of the priesthood was to secure divine favor for the general benefit.

## Influence on Other Cultures
Babylonian culture exerted a significant influence on the Israelites. During the exile in Babylon, the influence of Babylonian historical inquiry and the antiquarian research of Nabonidus encouraged the exiled Israelites to inquire into their own history. Under the leadership of the prophet Ezekiel, they used genealogies and traditions to amplify their own story. It is noted that the stories of the Flood, the Creation, and portions of the stories of Moses and Samson were likely incorporated from Babylonian sources. While the formal aspect of Genesis I is considerably Babylonian, it adapted these mythic elements to fit a virtually monotheistic community.
