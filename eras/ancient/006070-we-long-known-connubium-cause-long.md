# Connubium

*Connubium* refers to the legal right or capacity to contract a valid marriage. In various ancient societies, this right was not universal but was often restricted based on social class, legal status, or geographic and political boundaries.

## Connubium in Roman Law
In the Roman legal system, no legal marriage could be contracted unless *connubium* existed between the parties. Historically, this privilege was a source of significant social conflict; originally, there was no *connubium* between the patricians and the plebeians. This restriction led to a long and determined struggle, which eventually resulted in the privilege being conceded via the Lex Canuleia.

In later periods, the right was further restricted. Latini and Peregrini were excluded from *connubium* unless the right had been expressly granted to them. During the early empire, the Lex Julia et Papia Poppaea introduced additional restrictions based on the condition of the parties. For example, senators were forbidden from marrying women of inferior rank or freedwomen; if a man who was the husband of a freedwoman became a senator, he was set free from that marriage.

The concept of *connubium* also extended to the granting of citizenship rights. For instance, the town of Arpinum received limited Roman *civitas* in B.C. 303, which likely included *commercium* and *connubium*, though the right of suffrage (*suffragii latio*) was not granted to its people until B.C. 188.

## Political and Strategic Use of Connubium
The restriction of *connubium* was sometimes used as a political tool to isolate populations. When the Romans divided Macedonia into four provinces, they did so with the intent of cutting off connections between the regions. As part of this strategy, all *connubium* and *commercium* were forbidden between these provinces.

## Connubium in Other Ancient Cultures
The concept of restricted intermarriage is not exclusive to Rome. Evidence suggests that restrictions on *connubium* and commensality (the right of eating together) existed among other Aryan tribes, including the Germans, Russians, and Greeks. Such rules of endogamy and exogamy, as well as class-restricted privileges regarding eating together, are described as world-wide phenomena. These ancient customs are viewed as the key to the origin of the modern Indian caste system, although the archive notes that "caste" in the modern sense did not exist in these earlier contexts.

In the history of the Lokrians in Greece, the emigration to Italy is said to have been caused, in part, by disorderly intercourse between noble Lokrian women and their slaves. This may have involved intermarriage with persons of inferior station in instances where no recognized *connubium* existed.

## Later Developments in Marriage Restrictions
Following the Roman period, new restrictions were developed within canon law. The early church recognized "disparity of faith" as a bar to matrimony, specifically between pagans and Christians, or between heretics and the orthodox. Additionally, the clergy were forbidden from marrying, and those who bound themselves to a vow of celibacy were deemed incapable of marrying.
