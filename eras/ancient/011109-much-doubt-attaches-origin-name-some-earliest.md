# Etymology and Origins of Names

The origin of various geographical, national, and professional names is frequently subject to scholarly doubt and historical debate. Records indicate that the naming of regions, peoples, and roles often evolved from restricted local applications to broader meanings, or were influenced by the linguistic shifts of the cultures that used them.

## Geographical Designations: Europe and Asia

The origins of the names "Europe" and "Asia" are characterized by significant uncertainty. Some of the earliest Greek geographers viewed the known world as consisting of only two portions: Asia and Europe, with Libya (the Greek term for Africa) included within Asia. Herodotus, however, categorized Libya as one of the world's chief divisions, separate from Asia. While Herodotus dismissed common explanations for the names Europe and Asia as fables, he admitted he could not determine their actual origin.

One probable theory suggests these words were derived from Hebrew or Assyrian roots. In this view, the root for Europe signifies the west or the setting sun, while the root for Asia signifies the east or the rising sun. It is suggested that these terms were initially used to imply the east and west generally, or perhaps had a restricted local application before acquiring a more extended meaning. Even after "Asia" took on a larger geographical sense, the Greeks continued to use the term specifically to designate the region around Ephesus. The final boundaries of Asia were largely determined by arbitrary and imprecise conceptions rather than natural differences between regions.

## National and Ethnic Identifiers

The term "Aryan" has been widely popularized through the works of Max Muller on comparative philology. Evidence from early Sanskrit writings shows the word was used as a national name in Persia, Bactria, and India (appearing as *arya* in Sanskrit, *airya* in Zend, and *ariya* in Old Persian). 

There is scholarly disagreement regarding the specific root of the word. While Max Muller asserts a connection to the Sanskrit word for earth, *ira*, other scholars, such as Spiegel, argue that while the word connects to a root *ar-*, there are several such roots with different meanings and no certain criterion to decide between them. Furthermore, while some have attempted to link the term to *Erin* (Ireland), this connection is considered impossible. However, it may share an origin with the *Ario-* found in Celtic and Germanic names, such as *Ariovistus*. Within Sanskrit, a distinction may exist between *arya* (the origin of Aryan) and *arya*, an epithet for deities found in the *Rig Veda*. In some instances, *aryas* may be the genitive of *ari*, meaning "pious, devoted, active."

## Professional and Social Titles

The designation of "sophists" in ancient Greece illustrates how names are often assigned by the public or critics rather than the individuals themselves. Protagoras claimed that sophists had existed since the earliest times in Greece, but that these early men deliberately avoided the name due to the odium attached to it. Protagoras asserted that he was the first to openly call himself a sophist. 

The application of the term varied among contemporaries. Diogenês of Apollonia used "sophists" to describe Ionic physiologists or philosophers. The comic poet Kratinus applied the term to all poets, including Hesiod and Homer. Much of the specific attachment of the title to certain teachers is attributed to the "unfriendly spirit" of Plato.

## Other Historical and Cultural Names

### The "Country-Dance"
The English "country-dance" was not an adaptation of the French *contre-danse*; rather, the latter is a corruption of the English term. English country-dances were introduced to France in the early 18th century. Later, French modifications of these dances were brought back to England under the French name, leading to confused derivations.

### Royal and Animal Pedigrees
In old heathen faith, the pedigrees of kings from Sweden, Norway, Denmark, Anglian, and Saxon origins were traced to Odin or his sons. After the decline of Odin's worship, these lines were extended to Noah or Japhet, and Odin was relegated to a simple human member of the lineage.

In the history of the British race-horse, the *Stud-Book* (first published in 1808) was created to correct inaccurate pedigrees. While most dates in the book begin in the 18th century, two exceptions exist: the Byerly Turk (1689) and a horse called Counsellor (1694).

### City Names
The name Constantinople is preserved by the modern Greeks, the nations of Europe, the emperor in public mandates, and the Arabs. However, the name is also known by the "vulgar appellation" of Istambol, which is a Turkish corruption of *εἰς τήν πόλιω*.
