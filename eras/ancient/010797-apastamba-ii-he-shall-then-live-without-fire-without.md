# The Asceticism of the Apastamba

## The Tradition of the Wandering Beggar
In India, the figure of the hermit or wandering beggar existed long before the advent of Buddhism. For those seeking this path, no formal initiation was required, with the exception of young men, who were tasked with living in the house of their teacher initially. Once a man had completed the duties associated with the student order, he was expected to "go forth remaining chaste," according to the *Apastamba*, ii. 9. 8.

## Requirements of the Ascetic Life
The *Apastamba* outlines a rigorous set of conditions for the ascetic's existence. The practitioner is commanded to live without a house, without protection, without pleasures, and without a fire. His life is characterized by silence, and he is to utter speech only when performing the daily recitation of the Veda.

Regarding sustenance and attire, the ascetic must wander and beg for only as much food in the village as is necessary to sustain his life. He is to wear clothes that have been thrown away by others, and some interpretations of the tradition suggest that he may go entirely naked.

## Spiritual Goals and Detachment
The vision of life presented in the *Apastamba* is one of total detachment. The ascetic is instructed to care for neither the heavens nor the world. He is to abandon the concepts of truth and falsehood, as well as pleasure and pain. Furthermore, he is to abandon the Vedas, the current world, and the world to come.

The ultimate purpose of this detachment is to seek the Universal Soul. According to the text, eternal salvation is found through the knowledge of this Universal Soul.
