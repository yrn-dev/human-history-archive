# The Books of Baruch

The literary corpus associated with Baruch consists of several distinct works, including the *Apocalypse of Baruch* and *The Rest of the Words of Baruch*. These texts are characterized by complex questions regarding their authorship, chronological origins, and structural integrity.

## The Apocalypse of Baruch
The *Apocalypse of Baruch* is a composite work containing conflicting elements, leading scholars to debate whether it was produced by a diversity of sources or a diversity of authors. If the epistle to the tribes in captivity (sections lxxviii.-lxxxvi.) is excluded, the book is divided into seven sections separated by fasts (with a probable textual defect after section xxxv). These sections address a variety of theological and historical themes, including:
* The Messiah and the Messianic kingdom.
* The destruction of Jerusalem and the past woes of the sons of Israel.
* Theological inquiries regarding free will, original sin, the nature of the resurrection body, works, and the number of the saved.

The text exhibits a sharp contrast in tone. Certain passages (such as xxix., xxxvi.-xl., and liii.-lxxiv.) are marked by vigorous optimism regarding the ultimate well-being of the sons of Israel on earth, depicting the blessedness of the chosen people in glowing colors. Conversely, other passages are hopelessly pessimistic, describing the world as a scene of irremediable corruption and the future of the sons of Israel as one of "lamentation, and mourning, and woe."

## Integrity and Structure
Regarding the integrity of the book, section lxxvii. 19 mentions that Baruch wrote two epistles: one addressed to the two and a half tribes at Babylon and another to the nine and a half tribes. The epistle to the nine and a half tribes is found in sections lxxviii.-lxxxvi. The epistle to the two and a half tribes is lost, though it may be partially preserved in the Book of Baruch, iii. 9-iv. 29.

## Chronology and Dating
The dating of the various constituents of the work is uncertain, with several competing theories:
* **Pre-70 A.D.:** Some assign the earliest sections (i.-iii. 8) to the period following Ptolemy I's conquest of Jerusalem in 320 B.C., while others place them in the time of the Maccabees (early 2nd century B.C.). The Maccabean theory is supported by the idea that the Twelve tribes were believed to be in Palestine during the Maccabean dynasty's expansion into Galilee, Samaria, and beyond the Jordan.
* **Post-70 A.D.:** Other scholars argue the book was written after the fall of Jerusalem in A.D. 70. This is supported by the view that sections iv. 31-v. 9 depend on the Greek version of Psalm xi.
* **2nd Century A.D.:** A reference in section ii. 17 regarding spirits taken from bodies not giving glory to the Lord suggests a date reaching into the beginnings of the 2nd century A.D.

## The Rest of the Words of Baruch
Distinct from the Apocalypse is *The Rest of the Words of Baruch* (also known in the Abyssinian Church as *The Rest of the Words of Jeremiah*). This work was originally written by a Jew but later revised by a Christian. It has been preserved in Slavonic, Armenian, Ethiopic, and Greek.

The narrative begins with the removal of the Temple's sacred vessels before its capture by the Chaldees. In this account, Baruch remains in Jerusalem while Jeremiah accompanies the exiles to Babylon. After 66 years of exile, Jeremiah returns the sons of Israel to Jerusalem, though he refuses entry to those who brought heathen wives. The work concludes with a vision of Jeremiah that is Christian in nature.

## Relation to Other Works and Historical Context
There are numerous affinities between the works of Baruch and 4 Ezra, leading some to assume a single author, though significant divergences exist. Scholars remain divided on which work holds priority.

Historically, Baruch is identified as the friend and amanuensis of the prophet Jeremiah. In 604 B.C., Baruch wrote down the prophecies of Jeremiah's first twenty-three years of ministry. According to historical accounts, when these predictions regarding the city and the temple were read before King Jehoiakim in the fifth year of his reign, the king tore the book and cast it into the fire. While Baruch authored the initial rolls and likely some biographical narratives, the final compilation of the Book of Jeremiah occurred in stages long after the return of the sons of Israel from Babylon.
