# Concerning John Of Gichala

The conflict between Josephus and John of Gichala was characterized by a series of plots, betrayals, and strategic maneuvers. John, a native of the city of Gischala, emerged as a primary adversary to Josephus, utilizing both open revolt and secret treachery to undermine Josephus's authority.

## Early Plots and the Flight to Gischala
John engaged in plotting that eventually drew the opposition of the people in the neighboring country. When these populations learned of John's schemes, they gathered in great multitudes to oppose him. To avoid this confrontation, John fled to his native city, Gischala. 

In response, tens of thousands of armed Galileans converged upon Josephus, expressing their desire to burn both John and the city that had sheltered him. Josephus, however, sought to restrain their fury. Rather than slaying his enemies, he opted for a policy of prudent conduct. He identified by name those in every city who had joined John's revolt and issued a public proclamation: anyone who did not forsake John within five days would have their effects seized and their houses and families burned. This stratagem proved effective, as three thousand of John's followers immediately abandoned him and surrendered their arms to Josephus.

## Secret Treachery and Political Intrigue
Following the loss of his followers, John shifted from open attempts to "secret ways of treachery," supported by two thousand Syrian runagates. He sent private messengers to Jerusalem to accuse Josephus of possessing too much power, warning the metropolis that Josephus would soon arrive as a tyrant unless he was prevented. While the general population ignored these accusations, some rulers and grandees, motivated by envy, sent money to John privately.

## The Plot at Tiberias
John later framed a new plot by pretending to be sick. He wrote to Josephus requesting permission to use the hot baths at Tiberias for his recovery. Suspecting nothing, Josephus granted the request and instructed the city governors to provide John with lodging and necessities. Within two days, John used these favors to persuade the inhabitants of Tiberias to revolt against Josephus, employing a combination of money and "delusive frauds" to corrupt them.

Josephus was alerted to the plot via a letter from Silas, the guardian of the city. Josephus marched through the night and arrived at Tiberias the following morning. John, sensing the danger, remained in bed and sent a friend to claim he was too ill to pay his respects. While Josephus was addressing the people in the stadium from an elevation of six cubits, John sent armed men to slay him. As the assassins drew their swords, the crowd cried out. Josephus, seeing the swords at his throat, fled in haste to the seashore and escaped into the lake on a ship with two of his guards.

## The Revolt of Tiberias and Josephus's Stratagem
Following these events, John remained within the walls of Gischala due to fear of Josephus. However, Tiberias revolted again, and its people invited King Agrippa to return to authority. When Agrippa did not arrive and Roman horsemen appeared, Josephus was expelled from the city.

While stationed at Taricheae, Josephus faced a dilemma: he was unaware of how to march against the revolters because his soldiers had been sent out to gather corn. Furthermore, he did not intend to proceed the following day as it was the sabbath. To circumvent the revolters, Josephus employed a stratagem. He first ordered the gates of Taricheae shut to prevent information from leaking. He then gathered 230 ships on the lake, placing only four mariners in each. 

Josephus sailed to Tiberias, keeping the vessels at a distance so they were not easily seen. He ordered the empty ships to float up and down while he approached the city walls with only seven unarmed guards. The adversaries on the walls, seeing the fleet, mistakenly believed the ships were full of armed men. Overcome by astonishment, they threw down their arms and begged Josephus to spare the city.

## Other Adversaries and Unrest
Beyond the conflict with John, Josephus faced instability from other sources. In one instance, young men from the village of Dabaritta stole costly garments, silver cups, and six hundred pieces of gold from Ptolemy, the steward of Agrippa and Bernice. When these men brought the loot to Josephus at Taricheae, he blamed them for their violence and deposited the items with Eneas, the most potent man in the city, intending to return them. This act incited the thieves, who spread rumors that Josephus was a traitor. This led to a massive uprising where one hundred thousand armed men gathered in the hippodrome at Taricheae to demand his deposition or death. During this unrest, John and a man named Jesus, the son of Sapphias (the governor of Tiberias), worked to irritate the multitude against Josephus.
