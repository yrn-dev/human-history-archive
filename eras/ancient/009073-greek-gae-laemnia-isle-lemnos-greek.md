# Lemnian Earth (gae Laemnia)

Lemnian Earth, known in Greek as *gae Laemnia*, is a specific type of bole. Bole is a clay-like substance that typically appears in red, brown, or yellow colors and consists essentially of hydrous aluminium silicate with varying amounts of iron. Unlike ordinary clay, most bole is not plastic; instead, it drops to pieces when placed in water, a characteristic similar to that of fuller's-earth.

## Properties and Production
The Lemnian Earth, sourced from the Isle of Lemnos in the Greek Archipelago, was the most famous variety of bole and was held in great repute for medicinal purposes. The extraction and preparation of this earth were governed by strict ritual and ceremony. It was dug only once a year. Following extraction, the earth was mixed with the blood of goats and formed into small balls or cakes. These cakes were then stamped by priests, leading to the substance becoming known as *Terra sigillata*, which translates to "sealed earth."

## Geography and Administration
The Isle of Lemnos is located within the Greek Archipelago. In later administrative contexts, it served as the center of a sanjak that included the island of Samothrace, which is located approximately 17 miles from the island of Imbros. Imbros, a Turkish island in the Aegean situated at the southern end of the Thracian Chersonese peninsula, was part of the sanjak of Lemnos and the province of the Archipelago Isles.

## History and Conquest
The island of Lemnos has a complex history of occupation and conquest. It was once inhabited by the Pelasgians, a population that also occupied Imbros and certain settlements on the mountainous promontory ending in Athos. In these Pelasgic settlements, the inhabitants spoke both Hellenic and Pelasgic languages.

During the Persian expansion, the Persian general Otanês, operating on the Hellespont, conquered the Pelasgian population of Lemnos and Imbros. Subsequently, the Athenians and Miltiadês captured Lemnos and Imbros. Miltiadês' fame was significantly enhanced by his role in subduing Lemnos; this reputation helped neutralize the enmity he had faced as governor of the Chersonese and contributed to his election as one of the ten generals for the year of the Marathonian combat.

## Religious and Cultural Significance
Lemnos was a primary seat of worship for the Cabeiri, a group of minor deities in Greek mythology. The nature of the Cabeiri is not certain, but they are often viewed as deities of the sea who protected navigation and sailors, sometimes identified with the Dioscuri. In Lemnos, the Cabeiri were associated with the promotion of the fruitfulness of cattle, as well as the fostering of fruits of the field and the vine.

The worship of the Cabeiri on Lemnos included an annual festival lasting nine days. During this period, all fires on the island were extinguished, and new fire was brought from Delos. Because of this tradition, and statements by Strabo that the father of the Cabeiri was Camillus (a son of Hephaestus), some have theorized that the Cabeiri were demons of volcanic fire, though this view is not generally held.

Additionally, Lemnos appears in Greek tradition as the location from which Philoctetes was fetched by the Greeks to join the Trojan War, a move suggested by the soothsayer Calchas of Mycenae or Megara.
