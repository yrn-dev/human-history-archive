# ASCUS

The term ascus (derived from the Greek *askos*, meaning "bag") is a botanical term used to describe the membranous sacs that contain reproductive spores within certain fungi and lichens. The ascus is considered one of the most sharply characterized structures among fungi and serves as the defining sporangium for the group known as Ascomycetes.

## Development and Structure
In the development of an ascus, two nuclei at the base fuse together to create a single nucleus within the young ascus. This single nucleus then undergoes three successive divisions, resulting in eight nuclei that lie free within the protoplasm. Through a process of free-cell formation—first described by Harper—a mass of protoplasm is cut out around each nucleus, forming eight uninucleate ascospores. The remaining protoplasm is referred to as epiplasm, which frequently contains glycogen.

While eight spores are typical, the number can vary. In some instances, the number of spores is reduced to fewer than eight due to the abortion of some nuclei. Conversely, in other cases, nuclear division continues further before the spores are formed, resulting in 16, 32, 64, or more spores.

## Classification and Variations
The Ascomycetes are divided into different groups based on how the asci are borne:

*   **General Ascomycetes:** In simpler forms, the asci may be borne free on the mycelium.
*   **Carpoascomycetes:** In these divisions, the asci are not free but are enclosed within definite fruit bodies known as ascocarps. An ascocarp consists of two primary portions: a main mass of vegetative or sterile hyphae and fertile ascogenous hyphae, which bear the asci at their ends.

In Carpoascomycetes, the asci are usually derived from the penultimate cell of the branches of the ascogenous hyphae, with the tip curving over to form a structure called a crozier. This positioning brings the ascus cell to the top, where it develops enormously following the fusion of two nuclei to produce ascospores. These spores are released from the asci in various ways, including a specialized ejaculation-mechanism.

## Sexual Processes and Life Cycle
The life history of the ascus involves complex nuclear fusions. In some forms, such as *Sphaerotheca* and *Pyronema*, there are definite male and female sexual organs. The ascogenous hyphae arise from the ascogonium (the female organ), sometimes following its fusion with an antheridium. In other forms, such as *Lachnea stercorea* or *Humaria granulata*, the antheridium is absent or abortive, and female nuclei fuse in pairs. In still other cases, such as *Humaria rutilans*, both the ascogonium and antheridium are absent, and fusion occurs between vegetative nuclei. Some forms, like *Exoascus*, apparently lack sexual fusion altogether.

Regardless of whether the initial sexual process is normal or reduced, all forms exhibit the fusion of nuclei within the ascus. This results in two nuclear fusions during the life history. Harper suggested that the division into eight nuclei (rather than the four spores typical of most reduction processes) is associated with a double reduction process. In *Humaria rutilans*, Miss Fraser identified two reductions: a normal synaptic reduction during the first nuclear division and a second, peculiar reduction division.

The Carpoascomycetes exhibit a marked alternation of generations. The ordinary mycelium serves as the gametophyte, bearing the antheridia and ascogonia. The ascogenous hyphae and their asci represent the sporophyte, as they are derived from the fertilized ascogonium.

## Related Terminology and Associations
Several botanical compounds are derived from the word ascus:
*   **Ascophorous:** Producing asci.
*   **Ascospore:** The spore or sporule developed within the ascus.
*   **Ascogonium:** The organ that produces the ascus.

Additionally, the *Saccharomycetaceae* (which include yeasts of the genus *Saccharomyces*) are sometimes placed among the Ascomycetes. This association is based on the fact that their sporangium and endogenous spores have been compared to an ascus. Under certain conditions, the nucleus in a yeast cell divides, and each daughter-nucleus divides again to form four spores in the mother cell, a process comparable to the development of ascospores. However, the archive notes that this association is considered very doubtful.
