# Legal Provisions Regarding Property and Sacrifice

The history of legal systems across various civilizations demonstrates a wide array of approaches to the management, confiscation, and protection of property, ranging from maritime contributions and debt relief to state-mandated seizures.

## Maritime Law and General Average
The law of General Average (G.A.) is designed to ensure that an individual whose property is sacrificed for the collective safety is placed on an equal footing with others, rather than a superior one. For example, if goods valued at L100 are thrown overboard to ensure the general safety of a voyage, the owner does not receive a full L100 contribution. Because the goods were part of the adventure that was partially saved, the owner must bear a portion of the loss and contribute alongside other saved interests.

In calculating the amount to be "made good" to the owner, the value of the sacrificed property is estimated based on its probable condition upon arrival, rather than its value at the time of sacrifice. This prevents the owner from being placed in a better position than those whose goods remained on board and were subject to the subsequent risks of the voyage.

## Debt and Property in Ancient Rome
Primitive Roman law maintained a distinction between debts arising from money lent (*pecunia certa credita*) and those arising from contracts, sales, or delicts. Creditors for money lent possessed significant power over the debtor's person and property. If a debt was proved or confessed and not paid within thirty days, the creditor could seize the debtor (*manûs injectio*). 

If the debtor failed to pay or provide a surety (*vindex*) after being held in chains for two months, the creditor was empowered to sell the debtor as a slave, put them to death, or subject them to forced work with unrestricted ill-usage. Additionally, the magistrate authorized the creditor to seize any of the debtor's property found within limits sufficient to cover the payment.

## Property Laws in Ancient Greece
Greek legal history shows a progression toward written codes and specific property protections. Zaleucus, who provided the earliest written code for the Epizephyrian Locrians in 663 B.C., established legislation that strictly protected property rights. Later, Philolaus of Corinth introduced "laws of adoption" for the Thebans specifically to prevent changes in the number of land allotments.

In Athens, property was often subject to state confiscation following legal decrees. For instance, after eight generals were found guilty by a condemnatory vote, six of them—including Periklês (son of the statesman), Diomedon, Erasinidês, Thrasyllus, Lysias, and Aristokratês—were executed by hemlock and their property was confiscated as prescribed by the senate. Furthermore, Athenian law allowed citizens to inform officials about articles of property that belonged to estates already confiscated by the city.

## Japanese Legal Codes and Debt Relief
In Japan, the *Daiho* code's effectiveness varied based on the centralization of administrative authority, often losing binding force during periods of feudalism. Under this system, slaves were viewed as the proprietor's property, similar to other chattels, and were not recognized by the law.

The Ashikaga shogunate utilized a financial device called *tokusei* ("virtuous administration"). While originally a temporary tax remission, it evolved into the *tokusei-rei* in 1297 to relieve landowners who had mortgaged property by prohibiting eviction for debt. Under the Muromachi shogun, this expanded to include all pecuniary obligations. However, implementation varied; in Nara, debtors had to pay one-third of their obligation before receiving exemption. The frequent issuance of these edicts—thirteen times under Yoshimasa—disturbed the economic basis of society and angered the creditor class.

## State Confiscation and Political Law
Property has frequently been used as a tool for political control or state revenue. In Rome, proscriptions were used to eliminate enemies and raise funds; Appian records that approximately 300 senators and 2,000 equites were proscribed and their property confiscated. This process included the seizure of deposits held by depositaries and even those protected by the sanctity of the Vestals.

In other contexts, laws were designed to ensure citizens remained engaged in the state. One law disfranchised citizens who remained neutral during party conflicts to prevent them from listlessly securing their own personal property while avoiding the misfortunes of their country. Additionally, laws regarding heiresses were established to ensure estates remained within the family; for example, if a husband was impotent, an heiress could use the services of the husband's next of kin to obtain an heir.
