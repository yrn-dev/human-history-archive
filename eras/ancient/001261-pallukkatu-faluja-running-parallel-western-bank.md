# Pallukkatu and Faluja

## Overview of the Babylonian Irrigation System
The Babylonian plain was once a pestiferous and uninhabitable swamp, but it was reclaimed through elaborate irrigation and engineering, eventually becoming the most fertile country in the world. This transformation was achieved through a skillfully planned and regulated network of canals. The region was thickly populated, with numerous towns whose sites are still represented by mounds. Recent American excavations have identified some of these sites, such as Bismya (the site of Udab, also called Usab or Adab) and the neighboring Fara, which is the site of ancient Kisurra.

## The Major Canals of Babylonia
Three chief canals were responsible for carrying the waters of the Euphrates to the Tigris above the city of Babylon:

*   **The Zabzallat Canal:** Also known as *Nahr Sarsar*, this canal ran from Faluja to Ctesiphon.
*   **The Kutha Canal:** This canal ran from Sippara to Madain, passing through Kutha (or Tell Ibrahim) along the way.
*   **The King's Canal:** Known as Ar-Malcha, this canal was situated between the Zabzallat and Kutha canals. It was conducted from the Euphrates toward Opis (or Upi). Research by H. Winckler indicates that Opis was located on the western side of the Tigris, close to Seleucia. The canal may have owed its name to Khammurabi.

## The Pallukkatu Canal
The Pallacopas, referred to as Pallukkatu in Neo-Babylonian texts, originated at Pallukkatu or Faluja. This canal ran parallel to the western bank of the Euphrates, extending as far as Teredon (or Iddaratu). The Pallukkatu served two primary functions: it watered an immense tract of land and supplied a large lake located near Borsippa. B. Meissner has suggested that this canal may be the same as "the Canal of the Sun-god" mentioned in early texts.

## Agricultural Impact
The advanced system of irrigation led to highly developed soil cultivation in Babylonia. Historical accounts highlight the extreme productivity of the land:
*   **Herodotus** noted that wheat commonly returned two hundred-fold to the sower, and occasionally three hundred-fold.
*   **Pliny** stated that wheat was cut twice, and the remaining growth served as keep for sheep.
*   **Berossus** observed that various crops grew wild, including wheat (which still grew wild near Anah), sesame, barley, palms, apples, ochrys, and many kinds of shelled fruit.

## Regional Geography and Terminology
The alluvial plain of Babylonia was known as Edin (the Eden of Genesis ii.), though this name specifically referred to "the plain" on the western bank of the river where Babylonian masters pastured the flocks of Bedouins. This western bank, or *kisad*, and the corresponding western bank of the Tigris (identified by Hommel as the modern Shatt el-Hai), gave the name to the land of Chesed, the source of the *Kasdim* of the Old Testament.

In early Lagash inscriptions, the district was called Gu-Edinna (the Sumerian equivalent of *Kisad Edini*), while the coast-land was known as Gu-bba (*Kisad tamtim*), meaning the "bank of the sea." Southern Babylonia was more broadly called Kengi ("the land") or Kengi Sumer ("the land of Sumer"). Northern Babylonia was known as Urra (Uri) or Akkad. The term *Urra* likely originally meant "clayey soil" but came to signify "highlands," whereas *kengi* referred to "lowlands."

Following the rise of Sargon's empire, the name Akkad—derived from the capital Agade—was extended to denote the whole of Babylonia in the imperial title "Sumer and Akkad." After the Kassite conquest, northern Babylonia was called Kar-Duniyas ("the wall of the god Duniyas"), referring to a line of fortification. A similar fortification was built by Nebuchadrezzar between Opis and Sippara, which Strabo called "the Wall of Semiramis."
