## Biological and Agricultural Restrictions
Restrictions are also observed in the distribution of flora and fauna. In the British Isles, the cultivation of hops is restricted to England, where it is practically confined to two counties in the west-midland districts and four in the south-eastern districts. As of 1901, the Board of Agriculture reported the English crop occupied 51,127 acres. Historical data from 1868 to 1907 shows the acreage fluctuated between a high of 71,789 acres (1878) and a low of 44,938 acres (1907), with Kent generally accounting for over 60% of the area.

In the Neotropical region, certain bird groups—including *Meleagris*, *Icteridae*, *Vireonidae*, *Mniotiltidae*, and various *Tetraoninae*—are considered comparatively recent immigrants originally peculiar to the Nearctic area. ## Historical and Social Restrictions
Historically, the movement of "ordinary people" as a class with common ideas was comparatively restricted before the nineteenth century. While propagandist religions such as Islam and Christianity encouraged massive movements, earlier equalitarian peasant insurrections from the Wycliffe period onward remained confined to definite localities and spread slowly. Similarly, town artisan riots were typically local. The Parisian crowd of the First Revolution is characterized as primitive-thinking and savage when compared to Western European crowds after 1830.

## Geological and Cultural Subsidence
The physical area of certain landmasses has been restricted over time due to geological changes. In Polynesia, borings from Funafuti in 1897 indicate the area is one of comparatively recent subsidence. This suggests that land connections were once more continuous and easier than they are now. Massive stone structures found in Kusaie and Ponape, as well as those extending to Easter Island, appear out of proportion with the present restricted areas on which they stand. These ruins, which include a citadel with cyclopean ramparts of basaltic blocks and artificial harbours in the lagoons of the islet of Lele, are attributed to a prehistoric race of Neolithic man.

Similarly, the Baltic region experienced shifts in area and communication. A subsequent upheaval after glacial times restricted direct communication with the open sea to the Danish channels. Later, the area was transformed into the *Ancylus* lake, covering approximately 220,000 square miles, including Lake Ladoga, a large part of Finland, and the present Baltic area.
