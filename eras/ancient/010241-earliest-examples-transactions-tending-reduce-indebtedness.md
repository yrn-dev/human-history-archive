# Transactions Tending to Reduce Indebtedness

In the history of book-keeping, the methods used to record transactions that reduce indebtedness have evolved from simple chronological lists to the structured systems of debit and credit used in modern ledgers.

## Early Recording Methods
In the earliest examples of book-keeping, transactions intended to reduce indebtedness were recorded according to the date they occurred. These entries were placed directly underneath the transactions that had originally created the indebtedness. To determine the current state of the debt, the amount of the reduction was subtracted from the total sum of the indebtedness recorded up to that date.

## Evolution of the Contra Account
The chronological method of recording reductions was eventually found to be inconvenient. This led to the development of a system where two separate accounts were maintained: one account to record transactions that created indebtedness and a second account, known as the "contra account," to record transactions that either extinguished or reduced that indebtedness.

For the sake of convenience, these two accounts were placed on opposite sides of the ledger. This evolution resulted in the "Dr." (debit) and "Cr." (credit) account system. In this format, all transactions that create indebtedness due from a person to a business—representing benefits the person received from the business—are recorded on the left-hand or Dr. side. Conversely, all transactions representing benefits the person imparted to the business, which create a liability for the business, are recorded on the right-hand or Cr. side.

To keep the state of the account apparent, the account is typically ruled off at periodical intervals or each time all indebtedness is extinguished.

## The Role of the Ledger and Subsidiary Books
The ledger serves as the center of all book-keeping systems. In early practice, the ledger was not a complete record of every transaction but contained only those accounts deemed necessary to keep accessible, such as "personal accounts," which recorded transactions with other business houses.

Other books are used for practical convenience and are termed "subsidiary books," "books of entry," or "books of first entry." Transactions are first recorded in these subsidiary books before being classified into the ledger. Additionally, "statistical" or "auxiliary books" may be used for statistical purposes and do not form the basis for entries in the ledger.

## Double-Entry and Tabular Systems
The principle of double-entry book-keeping ensures a complete record of every business transaction. Because transactions are viewed as cross-dealings—where one party receives a benefit and another imparts one—an entry of an equal amount must be made on both the debit and credit sides of the ledger. This allows for the creation of a "trial balance," a list of all ledger balances where total debits must equal total credits. While this provides a mechanical test of the ledger, it does not safeguard against fraud, omitted transactions, or incorrect monetary values.

More recent modifications include "tabular book-keeping," which replaces standard forms with books ruled with numerous columns. This system reduces clerical work and increases the speed of results. When applied to ledgers, it allows a large number of personal accounts to be recorded on a single opening, provided the number of entries for each individual is small.

## Legal and Religious Frameworks for Obligations
Beyond accounting methods, various instruments and guidelines govern the recording of indebtedness and obligations:

*   **Legal Instruments:** Railway companies used "Lloyd's bonds" to increase indebtedness without violating their charters; these are instruments under seal admitting a specified debt for goods or work. "Debenture bonds" are secured only by a company's covenant, while a "recognizance" is an obligation entered before a court of record. In Scots law, a "heritable bond" involves a bond for money joined with a conveyance of land as security.
*   **Religious Guidance:** The Qur'an instructs believers that when dealing in transactions involving future obligations over a fixed period, they should reduce them to writing. A scribe should faithfully record the transaction, and the party incurring the liability should dictate the terms, fearing Allah and not diminishing what is owed. If the liable party is unable to dictate due to weakness or mental deficiency, a guardian should dictate faithfully. The text emphasizes that writing contracts—regardless of whether the amount is small or big—is more suitable as evidence and prevents doubt. For transactions carried out on the spot, writing is not required, though witnesses should be taken for commercial contracts. If a scribe is unavailable during a journey, a pledge with possession may be used.
