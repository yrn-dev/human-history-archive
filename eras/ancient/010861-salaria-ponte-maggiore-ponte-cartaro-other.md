# Via Salaria and Roman Bridges

## The Via Salaria and the Bridge of Narses
The Via Salaria was served by the bridge of Narses, a structure constructed in the 6th century. This bridge carried the Via Salaria over the Anio. In accordance with the design of later bridges, the bridge of Narses featured a fortification used for the purposes of defense or for the enforcement of tolls. The structure was destroyed in 1867 during the approach of Garibaldi to Rome.

## Roman Bridges of the Tiber
The first known bridge constructed in Rome over the Tiber was the timber Pons Sublicius, which was the bridge defended by Horatius. Other significant Roman bridges over the Tiber include:

*   **Pons Milvius:** Now known as Ponte Molle, this bridge was reconstructed in stone in 109 B.C. by M. Aemilius Scaurus. The present structure is believed to contain portions of the old bridge, with arches spanning between 51 and 79 ft.
*   **Pons Fabricius:** Also known as the modern Ponte dei Quattro Capi, this bridge dates to approximately 62 B.C. and remains practically intact.
*   **Pons Cestius:** Built probably in 46 B.C., this bridge retains much of its original masonry.
*   **Pons Aelius:** Built by Hadrian in A.D. 134 and later repaired by Clement IX and Pope Nicholas II, it is currently the bridge of St Angelo. It featured eight arches, the largest of which had a span of 62 ft.

## Other Roman Engineering Works
Roman engineers constructed bridges beyond the city of Rome and the Tiber. Trajan built a bridge over the Danube in A.D. 104, which Dio Cassius mentions as possibly being 3,000 to 4,000 ft. in length. While some piers are said to still exist, a bas-relief on the Trajan column depicts the bridge with timber arches and masonry piers, though this representation is likely conventional. Trajan also constructed the bridge of Alcantara in Spain, which was 670 ft. long and situated 210 ft. above the stream. This stone bridge consisted of six arches built without the use of cement.

Additionally, Roman engineers built great lines of aqueducts from 300 B.C. onwards. Where these aqueducts were carried above ground, they took the form of arched bridge structures of remarkable magnitude, generally constructed from concrete and brick.
