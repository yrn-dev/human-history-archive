# Bagoas and the Persian Administration

Bagoas was a confidential minister to the Persian king Artaxerxes III. His name, a shortened form of names such as Bagadata (meaning "given by God"), was frequently used for eunuchs. During his tenure, Bagoas rose to a position of immense power, eventually becoming the real master of the kingdom.

## The Egyptian Campaign and Administrative Division
Bagoas distinguished himself as one of the most able servants of the Persian king, specifically during the campaign to subject Egypt to the Persian empire, likely around 342 B.C. He operated in tandem with Mentor, a Rhodian condottiere. The Persian force sent against Egypt was one of the largest in many years, consisting of ten thousand Greeks: six thousand from Greek cities in Asia Minor, three thousand from Argos, and one thousand from Thebes. This army was split into three divisions, with one division entrusted to the joint command of Bagoas and Mentor.

The Egyptian prince Nektanebus attempted to defend the territory with a force of twenty thousand mercenary Greeks and a larger body of Libyans and Egyptians, focusing defenses on the fortress of Pelusium at the mouth of the eastern branch of the Nile. However, the cunning and ability of Bagoas and Mentor overcame these defenses, forcing Nektanebus to retire into Ethiopia. Following the Persian victory, Egypt became a province under the satrap Pherendates.

Following the reconquest, a division of labor was established between the two officers. Mentor was rewarded with 100 talents and the satrapy of the Asiatic seaboard, where he managed Greek mercenaries to bring independent chiefs, such as Hermeias of Atarneus, under Persian dominion. While Mentor served as general of the maritime provinces and sent Greek mercenaries to the king, Bagoas administered the upper satrapies.

## Wealth and Political Influence
Bagoas acquired great wealth through the pillaging of Egyptian temples. He confiscated sacred writings and archives from the temples and subsequently sold them back to the priests in exchange for large bribes.

His influence extended into religious and civil matters of the empire. When the high priest of Jerusalem, Jesus, murdered his brother Johannes in the temple, Bagoas—who had been a supporter of Johannes—imposed a new tax on the Israelites. Bagoas entered the temple, asserting that he was purer than the murderer who held the priestly office.

## Regicide and the Succession of Kings
Bagoas utilized his power to manipulate the Persian throne through a series of murders. In 338 B.C., he killed King Artaxerxes III and all of the king's sons except for the youngest, Arses, whom Bagoas raised to the throne. Two years later, Bagoas murdered Arses and installed Darius III as king.

When Darius III attempted to establish independence from Bagoas, who served as the powerful vizier (or *chiliarchos*), Bagoas attempted to poison the king. However, Darius was warned of the plot and forced Bagoas to drink the poison himself.

## Legacy and Later References
The wealth of Bagoas was noted in the records of later conquerors. His house in Susa, containing rich treasures, was presented by Alexander the Great to Parmenio. Additionally, Theophrastus and Pliny mention Bagoas' gardens in Babylon, which contained the best species of palms.

The archive notes that a later story claiming Bagoas was an Egyptian who killed Artaxerxes III due to the death of the sacred Apis is considered to be without historical value. Furthermore, records indicate that another eunuch also named Bagoas was a favorite of Alexander the Great.
