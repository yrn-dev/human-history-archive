# Dating of Historical and Sacred Texts

The determination of dates for ancient books and manuscripts is often a complex process involving the analysis of internal evidence, linguistic shifts, and external historical synchronisms. In many cases, the dates of various constituents within a single work remain uncertain or are subject to scholarly debate.

## Dating of the Book of Baruch
The dating of the constituents of the Book of Baruch is a subject of significant disagreement among scholars. Ewald, Marshall, and Gifford assign sections i.-iii. 8 to the period following Ptolemy I's conquest of Jerusalem in 320 B.C., while Reuss suggests a date several decades later. Other scholars, including Toy, Keil, Schrade, and Fritzsche, attribute the work to the time of the Maccabees. Specifically, the view held by Fritzsche—placing the work in the early decades of the 2nd century B.C.—is considered probable. This period aligns with the belief that the Jewish Kingdom once again embraced the entire nation as the Maccabees extended their dominion eastwards beyond the Jordan and northwards over Galilee and Samaria, displacing the older belief that nine and a half tribes remained in captivity.

Conversely, Kneucker, Hitzig, and Schuerer assume the book was written after A.D. 70. Similarly, James and Ryle argue that Baruch was reduced to its present form after A.D. 70, citing a dependency of sections iv. 31-v. 9 on the Greek version of Ps. xi. Further evidence for a later date is found in ii. 17, which refers to spirits taken from bodies, suggesting a date in the beginnings of the 2nd century A.D. However, the statement regarding the desolate condition of the Temple in ii. 26^a is rejected by Kneucker as an interpolation.

## Chronology of the Old Testament and Related Texts
The Hebrew text of the Old Testament is presented in the Massoretic text, which is generally dated between the 6th and 8th centuries of the Christian era, though it may have become fixed as early as the 2nd century A.D. 

The Septuagint, the earliest and most important version for textual criticism, likely arose in the 3rd century B.C. to serve Greek-speaking Jews in Alexandria. Tradition states the law was translated during the reign of Ptolemy Philadelphus (284-247 B.C.). By 132 B.C., the prologue to Sirach indicates that "the rest of the books," the Prophets, and the Law were already current in translation. While the exact date the various books were combined into a single work is unknown, the Septuagint as a whole is assumed to have existed by the 1st century A.D.

In the books of Samuel, the record is not of a single date but is extracted from various sources. This is evident in the history of David's early adventures, which is often disordered and repetitive, leading critics to conclude that the First Book of Samuel is drawn from at least two sources. Regarding the chronology of David, king of Judah and Israel, the usual date of 1055-1015 B.C. is likely too early by thirty to fifty years.

## Analysis of Other Ancient Works
Different methodologies are applied to other texts based on their content:

*   **The Book of Daniel:** This work is bilingual, featuring both Hebrew and Aramaic. One theory suggests the book was originally written entirely in Hebrew and later translated into Aramaic for the general reader, with missing Hebrew sections subsequently supplied from the Aramaic translation.
*   **The Book of Jubilees:** A section of another analyzed work (chapters lxxii.-lxxxii.), which focuses on the laws of heavenly bodies, was known to the author of Jubilees. Consequently, this section must have been written before the last third of the 2nd century B.C.
*   **Buddhist Texts:** The *Saddharma Pundarika* (a Sanskrit exposition of Mahayana doctrine) and the *Netti Pakarana* (a Pali work) are both dated to the 2nd or 3rd century of the Christian era. The *Prajnaparamita* and the *Divyavadana*—a collection of legends about the Buddha's disciples—belong to approximately the same age, though the *Divyavadana* contains some very ancient fragments.

## Chronological Errors and Sources
In some chronological notices, the lengths of reigns were derived from state annals, such as the "book of the chronicles of Judah" or "Israel." However, "synchronisms"—the corresponding dates of contemporary reigns in different kingdoms—were derived by computation. Errors in these computations or in the recorded lengths of reigns have vitiated the chronology of the period. These errors are demonstrated by inconsistencies within the chronology of the two kingdoms and disagreements with contemporary Assyrian chronology as fixed by Assyrian inscriptions.
