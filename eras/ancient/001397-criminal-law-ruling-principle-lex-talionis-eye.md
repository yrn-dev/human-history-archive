# Lex Talionis

The *lex talionis* is a principle of criminal law characterized by retaliation in kind, often summarized as "eye for eye." This legal concept appears across various ancient legal systems and primitive customs, serving as a basis for penalties regarding personal injury, homicide, and social transgressions.

## Application in the Code of Khammurabi
In the laws of Khammurabi, the *lex talionis* served as the ruling principle of criminal law. For assaults committed upon an *amelu*, the penalties were strictly reciprocal: eye for eye, tooth for tooth, and limb for limb. This principle extended to symbolic retaliation, where the specific body part used to commit a crime was punished:
* **Hands:** The hand was cut off if it was used to steal a trust or strike a father. Similarly, a surgeon lost his hand if his actions caused the loss of a limb or life, and a brander lost his hand for obliterating a slave's identification mark.
* **Tongues and Eyes:** The tongue was removed for denying a mother or father (a penalty also applied to perjury in Elamite contracts), and the eye was lost if it "pried into forbidden secrets."
* **Other Organs:** A wet-nurse who substituted a changeling for an entrusted child had her breast cut off. Slaves who denied their master or struck a freeman lost an ear, which served as a symbol of obedience and the organ of hearing.

While the *lex talionis* was a remaining primitive feature of this state-law, the system also included other elements such as ordeal, district responsibility, and family solidarity. Under this legal framework, the king acted as a benevolent autocrat who protected the weak, though royal pardons could only be granted once private resentment had been appeased.

## Homicide and Primitive Law
The application of *lex talionis* varied significantly regarding homicide. The laws of Khammurabi did not provide a sweeping general provision for the punishment of homicide; instead, penalties were often based on social status. For example, if a man caused the death of a gentleman's daughter, his own daughter was put to death, whereas if a poor man was the slayer, he paid 1/2 mina.

In contrast, primitive law generally subjected a slayer to the "lawful vengeance" of the slain person's kindred, regardless of whether the homicide was innocent, unless a composition could be reached. This specific form of *lex talionis*—a life for a life—led to several historical developments:
1. The rise of private justice, seen in blood feuds and vendettas.
2. The establishment of cities of refuge and sanctuaries where an innocent manslayer could seek protection from the avenger of blood.
3. The creation of a system of *wer*, *bote*, and *wite*, which assigned an assessed price to a man's life, payable to his next of kin and chief.

This differs from the Mosaic law, where the Decalogue provided an absolute command: "Thou shalt not kill."

## Other Historical Instances
The principle of *lex talionis* is also noted in other ancient contexts:
* **Ancient Greece:** The earliest written Grecian laws were characterized by extraordinary rigor. The laws of Zaleukus, who was considered a counterpart to Drako, appear to have enjoined the application of *lex talionis* as punishment for personal injuries.
* **Ancient Governance:** Historical records indicate that Adonibezek was punished according to the *lex talionis* after having tyrannized seventy-two *reguli* (small kings).
