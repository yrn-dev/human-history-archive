# LYRIC POETRY AND THE SEVEN WISE MEN

## Nature and Development of Lyric Poetry
Lyric poetry is defined as poetry that is, or can be supposed to be, sung with the accompaniment of a musical instrument. While primeval oracles were chanted in verse and the Bacchic and Orphic Mysteries combined music with metre, a distinction is traditionally made between the epical (what was said) and the lyrical (what was sung). While the archaic hexameters of the *Iliad* and *Erga* are styled as epic, they were still recited with stringed instrument accompaniment.

The period of Greek lyric poetry is roughly defined from 670 to 440 B.C. It followed the epical age and offered a wider range of subjects, new metres, and an enlarged musical scale. The lyric expression is characterized as being deeper and more impassioned than the incomplete self-expression found in the iambic and elegiac forms. Its ideal character is enhanced by its intimate union with the rhythmical movement of the dance and music.

## Musical Evolution and Form
The effectiveness of lyric poetry depended upon progress in the art of music. The Greeks initially used a four-stringed lyre. A significant epoch occurred around 660 B.C. when Terpander of Lesbos added three strings, giving the lyre the compass of the octave. Further improvements to the flute were made by Olympus and others, and additional musical advancements are ascribed to Thaletas.

In terms of poetic structure, new metres were added to the Hexameter, including the Trochaic, Iambic, and Elegiac. In the development of the chorus, Stêsichorus introduced a specific distribution consisting of the Strophê, Antistrophê, and Epôdus. Over time, the popular chorus was substituted by professional performers.

## Notable Lyric Poets
Between 600 and 670 B.C., several prominent poets emerged, including Archilochus, Kallinus, Tyrtæus, and Alkman. In Sparta, musical and poetical tendencies were marked by choric training involving Thalêtas and Alkman, who employed the Doric dialect in their compositions. Other significant figures include Arion, Stêsichorus, Alkæus, and Sappho. Additionally, Solon and Theognis are categorized as gnomic or moralizing poets, where the words and meaning took precedence over the orchestrical and musical accompaniment.

## The Seven Wise Men
The Seven Wise Men represent an early manifestation of philosophy, appearing in the form of maxims or aphorisms. They were the first individuals to achieve an Hellenic reputation without possessing poetical genius, though most or all of them were composers in verse or poets. In their era, the mark of an accomplished man was the ability to recite poetry, sing, and provide smart, ready answers.

These men were not philosophers in the later sense of the word, but were instead persons of practical discernment regarding society and man, similar in mind to the fabulist Æsop. Their legacy consists of pithy repartees and a single distinctive motto or maxim peculiar to each.

## Composition and Identity of the Seven
Historical accounts of the Seven Wise Men are often confused or contradictory, with different authors providing different numbers and names. Dikæarchus listed ten, while Hermippus listed seventeen. However, four names appear on all lists:
* Solon the Athenian
* Thalês the Milesian
* Pittakus the Mitylenean
* Bias the Prienean

Plato's list included the above four, along with Cheilon of Sparta, Myson of Chênæ, and Kleobulus of Lindus in Rhodes. 

The individuals are remembered for various attributes: Bias was praised as an excellent judge, and the Delphian oracle declared Myson to be the most discreet man among the Greeks. Conversely, the poet Simonidês described Kleobulus of Lindus as a fool. Some of the mottoes attributed to them and inscribed in the Delphian temple include "Know thyself," "Nothing too much," "Know thy opportunity," and "Suretyship is the precursor of ruin."

## Intellectual Transition
The era of the Seven Wise Men and their authoritative aphorisms marked the limit of the preceding century's intellectual reach. This stage of "unexpanded results" and "single-minded sentiment" eventually gave way to the wider intellectual range of tragedy. Through tragedy, which allowed for dissent, debate, and shifting points of view, Grecian literature transitioned into the ethical speculation, dialectics, and rhetoric of the fifth century B.C. This period also saw the growth of discussion, the increase of writing habits, and the commencement of prose compositions.
