# Asia: Geological History, Geography, and Ancient Divisions

## Geological History
The early geological history of Asia is characterized by significant periods of marine submersion. During the Ordovician and Cambrian periods, a large portion of the continent was covered by the sea. However, evidence indicates that much of eastern and northern Asia has remained land since the Palaeozoic era. Specifically, the peninsula of India has been conclusively proven to have remained above the sea since at least the Carboniferous period.

Between these ancient land masses existed an area with well-developed Mesozoic marine deposits, indicating it was beneath the sea for the majority of the Mesozoic era. These regions have been identified by various scholars: E. Suess named the northern land mass Angaraland and the intervening sea the Tethys, while Neumayr and others referred to the southern land mass (of which the Indian peninsula is a fragment) as Gondwanaland and the intervening sea as the central Mediterranean sea. 

In the later stages of the Cretaceous period, the sea covered much of western Asia, including Arabia, Persia, Baluchistan, Afghanistan, the drainage area of the Aral Sea, and the basin of the Obi. It is probable, however, that a considerable portion of this specific region was dry land during Jurassic times.

## Rainfall and Climate
Data regarding rainfall across the continent varies significantly by region. In northern Asia, between the Lena and the Volga, rainfall is generally equal, ranging from 19 to 29 inches. This includes Manchuria and northern China, with a more considerable increase observed in Japan, Siam, and Korea. In south-western Siberia, rainfall is 12 to 14 inches, decreasing eastward to 6 or 7 inches at Barnaul and 5 or 6 inches at Urga in northern Mongolia. Eastern Siberia records approximately 15 to 20 inches.

In China, the fall at Peking is about 23 inches, but this increases to 78 inches at Canton, where the south-west monsoon region is entered. Similar levels are found at Batavia in Java (about 78 inches) and Singapore (nearly 100 inches). Higher quantities are recorded on the Malay peninsula coast not sheltered by Sumatra. The Burmese and Tenasserim coasts register falls of more than 200 inches, with no area falling below 75 or 80 inches.

In the east of the Ganges delta, the average is about 64 inches at Calcutta. The hills flanking Bengal to the east experience very great rainfall; on the Khasi hills (elevation approx. 4500 ft.), the ten-year average exceeds 550 inches, with a single year recording 610 inches and one month measuring 150 inches.

On the Indian peninsula, the west coast sea-level rainfall varies from 75 to 100 inches, while mountain elevations commonly register more than 250 inches. The east coast is far drier, never rising to 50 inches and reducing to 25 or 30 inches toward the southern apex. Ceylon records 60 to 80 inches. Rainfall diminishes as one moves inland, reaching 25 or 30 inches at the head of the Gangetic plain. The tract along the Indus, up to within 60 or 80 miles of the Himalaya, is almost rainless. Other specific measurements include 22 inches at Tiflis, 7 or 8 inches on the Caspian, and 5 or 6 inches on the Sea of Aral.

## Ancient Divisions and History
In ancient history, the northern and eastern parts of Asia are less known. The northern regions include Asiatic Scythia and Asiatic Sarmatia, which correspond to Tartary. Sarmatia is located between the river Rha (Volga) and the river Tanais, the latter of which separates Asia from Europe. Scythia is divided into two parts by mount Imaus; its best-known nations are the Massagetæ and the Sacæ.

The most eastern regions consist of India, Sinarum Regio (China), and Serica (Cathay). Anciently, India was better known than the other two. It was divided by the Ganges into two parts: one on the other side of the river, and one between the Ganges and the Indus (the latter composing the dominions of the Great Mogul).

Regarding the naming of the continent, the Lydians claim Asia was named after Asias, the son of Cotys and grandson of Manes, and that the Asian tribe in Sardis derives its name from him. Other accounts suggest the name comes from Asia, the wife of Prometheus.

## Political and Strategic Context
Historically, Asia has been a primary scene of geopolitical interest. In one instance, Napoleon and Alexander of Moscow discussed the division of Turkey and arranged for the conquest of most of Asia and India during their meeting at Tilsit. However, these visions were complicated by the fact that the British fleets sailed unchallenged on the seas.
