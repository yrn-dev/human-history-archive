# CLOSING EVENTS OF LEGENDARY GREECE.—PERIOD OF INTERMEDIATE DARKNESS

The transition from the mythical world of legendary Greece to the era of historical record is marked by a series of migrations and a subsequent period of chronological obscurity. This phase serves as the bridge between the heroic races of antiquity and the historical races that later competed in the Pythian and Olympic games.

## The Return of the Herakleids and Colonial Establishments
The concluding events of the legendary age are defined by the "Return of the Herakleids," also referred to as the Dorian conquest of the Peloponnêsus. This event, along with three major emigrations—the Æolic, Ionic, and Doric—provides the legendary explanation for how the Hellenic aggregate was distributed across its geography.

As a result of these movements, the Peloponnêsus was shared unequally among the Dorians, Achæans, Arcadians, and Ætolo-Eleians. Simultaneously, the coast of Asia Minor and the islands of the Ægean were settled by Ionians, Dorians, and Æolians. While chronologists may refer these events to specific dates—such as the Ionic migration being placed 140 years after the Trojan war—these massive groupings are characteristic of legend, presenting the foundations of the Cyclades and Asiatic Ionian cities as the result of a single great impulse.

## The Period of Intermediate Darkness
Following these legendary migrations, there exists a period described as an "intermediate darkness" or a "blank" in the historical record. According to received chronology, this gap comprises nearly three centuries, lasting until the first recorded Olympiad.

The existence of this blank has been a point of scholarly discussion. Some, such as M. Rochette, viewed the adventures of Grecian heroes as historical realities embellished by poets, which would make a three-century gap in tradition unaccountable. However, other perspectives suggest that this blank is conformable to the nature of legend. Mythical narratives typically thrive in a "supposed remote past" rather than the immediate past. Because the gods and heroes were conceived as being separated from the listener by many generations, the legendary matter became more imposing when placed at a respectful distance. Consequently, a society might maintain accredited narratives about ancient ancestors while remaining indifferent to the "inglorious names" and proceedings of their own fathers and grandfathers.

## Literary Traditions of the Legendary Age
The legendary period was preserved through various forms of epical composition, though many have been lost. These are categorized into different types:

*   **Heroic Epics:** Exemplified by the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, these focus on great personages or events (such as the legend of Troy) with a limited number of contemporaneous characters, achieving a degree of poetical unity.
*   **Genealogical Poems:** Such as the *Catalogue of Women*, the *Eoiai*, and the *Naupaktia*, these are tamer in spirit and string together distinct events without a specific view toward concentration of interest.
*   **Biographical Poems:** Works like the *Herakleia* or *Theseïs* occupy an intermediate position, recounting the principal exploits of a single hero.
*   **Hymns:** These are viewed as epical fragments narrating the adventures of specific gods.

Later Athenian playwrights, including Sophoklês and Æschylus, maintained faith in these legendary antiquities but exercised license with the details to suit the critical ethical and political feelings of their audience. They often exalted the mythical world, presenting it as divine and heroic rather than human.

## Historical Interpretations of Migration
The historical value of the Dorian invasion has been debated. Some scholars, such as J. Beloch, questioned these traditions, suggesting they were attempts to reconcile the political geography of 8th-century Ionians (as seen in Homer) with that of historic Greece. In this view, "migrations" were legendary precedents used to justify political ambitions or rivalries, particularly between Dorian Sparta and non-Dorian Thebes and Athens. 

Conversely, as the probable date for Homer has moved earlier, some argue that the poems accurately represent a specific "Achaean" or "Homeric" phase of prehistoric culture that was ended by a general catastrophe, as suggested by the legends.
