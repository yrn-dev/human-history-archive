# Codex Alexandrinus

The Codex Alexandrinus (G. M. reg. ID v.-viii., Greg. A. von Soden 84) is an uncial manuscript dating from the 5th century. It is currently held in the British Museum.

## Physical Characteristics and Format
The Codex Alexandrinus represents a stage in the evolution of the book format. During the period when papyrus was the predominant material, books typically took the form of the *volumen* (a roll wound around sticks). The *codex*—a term originally referring to a block of wood or the tablets sawn from such a block—gained popularity as parchment began to supersede papyrus. 

The influence of the earlier *volumen* is evident in the column arrangements of early codices. While the 4th-century Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus utilized four and three columns per page respectively, the Codex Alexandrinus features only two columns. By the 6th century, as seen in the Codex Bezae, the format shifted further to a single column.

## History and Provenance
The manuscript was given to Charles I in 1621 by Cyril Lucar, the patriarch of Constantinople. It is probable that Lucar brought the volume with him from Alexandria, where he had previously served as patriarch. Palaeographical arguments and historical notes support an Alexandrian provenance. 

The archive contains conflicting notes regarding the manuscript's origin:
* A note by Cyril Lucar claims the manuscript was written by a noble Egyptian lady named Thecla.
* An Arabic note from the 14th century attributes the writing to Thecla the martyr, a claim described as an obviously absurd legend.
* Another Arabic note by Athanasius (likely Athanasius III, patriarch c. 1308) states the manuscript was given to the patriarchate of Alexandria.
* A later Latin note dates this presentation to the year 1098.

## Content and Textual Analysis
Originally, the Codex Alexandrinus contained the entirety of the Old and New Testaments. The Old Testament portion included the Psalms of Solomon, while the New Testament portion included 1 and 2 Clement. However, the manuscript has suffered mutilation in several places.

Scholars have analyzed the text of the codex as follows:
* **Old Testament:** Some scholars believe the text represents the Hesychian recension, a view that aligns with the manuscript's palaeographic indications and Alexandrian origin, though this perspective has recently lost favor among students of the Septuagint.
* **New Testament:** In the gospels, the manuscript contains a late text of the "Syrian" type (as categorized by Westcott and Hort). In the epistles, however, there is a strongly marked "Alexandrian" element.

## Relation to Apocryphal Works
The Codex Alexandrinus is noted for its inclusion of certain writings that functioned as an appendix to the New Testament. Specifically, the "Epistle of Barnabas" is followed by two writings that form an appendix within the Codex Alexandrinus. 

The presence of the Epistle of Barnabas in this and other early manuscripts, such as the 4th-century Codex Sinaiticus, indicates that the work once enjoyed quasi-canonical authority. This standing was particularly strong in Alexandria, where Origen cited it in the same spirit as scripture and Clement commented on it as he did the canonical scriptures. While Clement ascribed the work to "the prophet" or "the apostle" Barnabas (referring to Paul's fellow-apostle), internal evidence suggests this ascription is impossible. It is suggested that the Alexandrian Church attributed the work to the famous Barnabas because the writing was so highly favored.
