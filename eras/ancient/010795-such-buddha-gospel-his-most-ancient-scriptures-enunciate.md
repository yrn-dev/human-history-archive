# The Gospel of the Buddha

The gospel of the Buddha, as enunciated in his most ancient scriptures, presents a philosophy centered on the attainment of Nirvana, which these texts constantly define as supreme happiness. While the strict interpretation of this philosophy leaves it unclear whether a self remains to experience this happiness, its practical application is manifested in the life of the monk who separates himself from the world.

## Ethical Code and the Path to Nirvana
For those seeking to approach the higher life of an ascetic or saint, the ancient scriptures mandate the observation of five commandments:
*   To kill no living thing.
*   Not to lay hands on the property of another.
*   Not to touch the wife of another.
*   Not to speak what is untrue.
*   Not to drink intoxicating drinks.

The Buddhist technical term for entering the "eightfold path to Nirvana" is the *Sambodhi*, or "setting out for the Great Wisdom." This path is further characterized as a purely ethical system, independent of ritual and theology, serving as a code of morals for laymen.

## The Personality and Mission of the Buddha
Early accounts of the Buddha describe a man of deep earnestness and thorough conviction in the truth of his gospel. He is noted for having a commanding presence and a deep, rich, and thrilling voice. 

Upon reaching the age of 35, the Buddha intended to proclaim his gospel to his former teachers, Alara and Udraka, but discovered they had died. He subsequently traveled to the Deer-forest near Benares to address five former disciples. During this journey, he encountered a wandering sophist named Upaka. When asked whose religion made him so calm and glad, the Buddha replied that he was on his way to Benares to "beat the drum of the Ambrosia" (set up the light of the doctrine of Nirvana) in the darkness of the world. He identified himself as a conqueror because he had conquered "the intoxications"—the mental intoxication resulting from craving after future life, sensuality, or ignorance—and because evil dispositions had ceased within him.

## Conversion of the First Disciples
The Buddha's success in persuading his five old disciples was aided by their previous shared culture and their long-standing relationship with him. While later books claim they were converted immediately, the most ancient Pali record indicates a more gradual process. Although their reverence for the Buddha was rekindled upon his arrival, leading them to vie in providing personal attention to their teacher, they only accepted his plan of salvation in its entirety after the Buddha spent five days talking to them, both separately and together.

## Historical Propagation and Influence
The propagation of the Buddhist gospel was significantly advanced by Asoka, a famous Buddhist emperor of India who reigned from 264 to 228 or 227 B.C. Asoka, the grandson of Chandragupta and son of Bindusara, was deeply affected by the horrors of warfare during his invasion of Kalinga in the ninth year of his reign. Consequently, he abandoned the desire for territorial conquest and devoted himself to conquest by "religion."

Asoka's Buddhist commitment progressed in stages: he formally joined the Buddhist community as a layman in the ninth year of his reign, became a member of the order in the eleventh year, and set out for the *Sambodhi* in the thirteenth year. He left behind thirty-five inscriptions on caves, pillars, and rocks that are of religious import, urging both the laity and the order to rehearse and learn passages from the Buddhist scriptures. His records also commemorate visits to the birthplace of Gotama (the Buddha) and the dome erected over the ashes of Konagamana (the Buddha).

The influence of the Buddha's life extended beyond India into other cultures. In ancient Bulgarian literature, the biography of Buddha served as the foundation for the story of *Barlaam and Josaphat*.
