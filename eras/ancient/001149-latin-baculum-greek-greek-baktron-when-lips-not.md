# Latin and Greek Linguistic and Cultural Relations

## Linguistic Characteristics and Phonetics
The development of European alphabets shows a strong connection between Phoenician, Greek, and Latin forms. The symbol for the voiced stop produced by closing the lips and vibrating the vocal chords—differing from *p* by the presence of vibration and from *m* because the nasal passage is also closed—corresponds to the second symbol in the Phoenician alphabet. In the Phoenician system, this appeared in rounded or early forms. 

While Greek did not borrow this symbol in its original form, early rock inscriptions from Thera (700-600 B.C.) show a form resembling the ordinary Greek lambda, likely resulting from writing the Semitic symbol upside down. Other adaptations appeared in the 6th and 5th centuries in Syracuse, Selinus, and Melos, suggesting Aramaic influence where the head of the letter was opened. Further variations occurred in the alphabets of Delos and Naxos, as well as on Byzantine coins.

## Comparative Prosody and Metre
In the study of prosody, the caesura (Latin for "cutting," Greek *tomê*) is a pause that separates a verse into two parts, known in Greek as *kôla*. Both Greek and Latin hexameters frequently employ the penthemimeral caesura, occurring after the 5th half-foot. However, certain types of caesura are more characteristic of one language than the other:
* **Greek-specific:** The "bucolic" caesura, found primarily in writers such as Theocritus, occurs after the 4th dactyl. A caesura after the 2nd syllable of the 3rd dactyl is common in Homer but rare in Latin.
* **Latin-specific:** The hephthemimeral caesura (after the 7th half-foot) is common in Latin but rare in Greek.

In iambic trimeters, both languages most commonly use the penthemimeral caesura, followed by the hephthemimeral.

## Cultural and Political Antagonisms
Historically, a deep rivalry existed between the Latin and Greek worlds. This was evident in the divide between the Latin Empire and the Greek Empire, as well as the subsequent rupture between the Latin Church and the Greek Church in 1054. This split was consummated by a dispute regarding a fine point of the doctrine of the Holy Trinity.

During the Crusades, this hostility persisted. The Crusaders served the Latin Church, and the Greek patriarch of Jerusalem faced a more difficult situation under the Latins than under the Turks. Latin princes in Syria, including those in the principality of Edessa, acted as a buffer between the Greeks and the Turks.

## Latinization and Education
The Roman Empire "Latinized" regions such as Britain, Gaul, Pannonia, and Dacia, creating cities where Latin was the dominant speech and Roman customs and gods were adopted. In contrast, Greece and the eastern empire remained Greek in spirit and culture and were never Latinized. Even within Rome, educated men viewed Greek as the language of a gentleman, preferring Greek learning and literature over Latin.

In later periods, such as the 4th century A.D., Greek grammarians like Dositheus Magister worked in Rome to assist Greek-speaking inhabitants of the empire in learning Latin through translations of Latin grammars. During the Renaissance, scholars like Jean Antoine de Baif were bred in both languages, with Baif studying Latin under Charles Estienne and Greek under the Cretan scholar Ange Vergece.

## Influence on the English Language
The revival of classical Greek and Roman writers during the Renaissance led to a massive influx of loanwords into English. Scholars often found it more natural to use borrowed Latin terms than native English equivalents. Many of these words were introduced via French, which had already altered Latin forms. This process continued into the 19th century with the formation of words like *telephone*, *geology*, and *photograph*, which maintain forms that would have been consistent had they been used by the original Greeks and Latins.
