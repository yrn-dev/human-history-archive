# Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus

The Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus are two of the most critically important uncial manuscripts of the Bible, both dating from the 4th century. They represent a pivotal era in the history of the written book, marking the transition from the *volumen* (the roll wound around sticks) to the *codex*. The *codex* form, originally derived from a word for a block of wood or tree stump, gained popularity as parchment began to supersede papyrus. The influence of the earlier *volumen* is evident in these early codices through their use of multiple columns per page.

## Codex Sinaiticus

The Codex Sinaiticus (identified as Greg. $\aleph$ and von Soden $\delta^2$) is an uncial manuscript written on thin vellum. It was discovered in 1844 by C. Tischendorf at the monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai and was later acquired by the tsar in 1869; it is housed in the Imperial Library in St Petersburg. 

The manuscript is characterized by a layout of four columns per page, with each column containing forty-eight lines. Originally, the codex contained the entire Bible, and its New Testament remains complete. Additionally, it contains the *Shepherd of Hermas* and the *Epistle of Barnabas* (an apocryphal book of the New Testament) at the end, though these are incomplete. Evidence suggests another document may have originally existed between these two texts.

Tischendorf identified four scribes as the authors of the text, noting that one of these scribes was also responsible for the Codex Vaticanus. The manuscript underwent several corrections, most notably in the 6th century by a scribe known as $\aleph^a$ and in the 7th century by $\aleph^c$. The corrections made by $\aleph^c$ are considered important because they were based on an early copy corrected by Pamphilus, a friend of Eusebius, a disciple of Origen, and the founder of a library at Caesarea. In terms of textual character, the Codex Sinaiticus generally possesses a Neutral text, which is less mixed in the Epistles than that of Codex Vaticanus, though it is not as pure in the Gospels.

## Codex Vaticanus

The Codex Vaticanus (Vat. Gr, 1209; Greg. B; von Soden $\delta^1$) is also a 4th-century uncial manuscript. Unlike the Sinaiticus, it is written in three columns with forty-two lines per column. While it originally contained the whole Bible, several sections of the New Testament are now missing, including the Apocalypse, 1 and 2 Timothy, Titus, Philemon, and portions of Hebrews (ix. 14 and xiii. 25).

The manuscript was written by three scribes. Tischendorf identified the scribe of the New Testament in this codex as the same individual (Scribe D) who worked on the Codex Sinaiticus. The text was corrected by two different scribes: one who was contemporary with the original writer (the *diorthotaes*) and another from the 10th or 11th century. This later scribe likely re-inked the entire manuscript and introduced some textual changes. Some critics, however, suggest these changes and the filling of the lacunae in the Apocalypse and Hebrews were performed by a 15th-century monk using a manuscript belonging to Bessarion.

The Codex Vaticanus is regarded as the best example of the Neutral Text, except within the Pauline epistles, where it exhibits a strong "Western" element. The exact history of how the manuscript arrived at the Vatican is unknown, but it appears in the catalogue of 1481 and was absent from the catalogue of 1475, suggesting it was acquired between those years.

## Textual Comparisons and Variations

Both the Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus are used by scholars to determine the original nature of biblical texts. For example, in the address of the epistle "To the Ephesians," both the 4th-century Codex B (Vaticanus) and $\aleph$ (Sinaiticus), along with the 11th-century Codex 67, omit the words "in Ephesus" ([Greek: en Epheso]). This omission supports the theory that the address was originally general or left blank, and that the specific location was later inserted based on the title of the letter.
