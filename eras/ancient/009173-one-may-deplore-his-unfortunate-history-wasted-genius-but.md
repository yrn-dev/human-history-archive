# Analysis of Genius and Deplorable History

The concepts of genius and the "deplorable" nature of historical events appear across various records of human history, ranging from the intellectual achievements of antiquity to the political failures of later eras. These records often contrast the innate talent of individuals or nations with the unfortunate circumstances or poor judgments that led to their decline or wasted potential.

## Genius in Antiquity and Literature

The archive records the "expansion of Grecian genius" between 776 and 560 B.C., a period marked by the creation of gnomic, lyric, choric, iambic, and elegiac poetry. This movement was improved by various masters, including Archilochus, Kallinus, and Stesichorus. In the following century, Pindar and Simonidês—described as "high-crested bards" and "proud"—further elaborated lyric and choric poetry. This progression culminated in the fifth century B.C. with Grecian drama, which integrated iambic dialogue with choric and lyric song, representing the final ascending movement of the race's poetical genius.

Beyond factual history, the archive suggests that fiction, such as the legends of Theseus, Œdipus, Hercules, and the fables of the Odyssey and Iliad, can be used to characterize the genius of the people who admired and rehearsed them, even when such stories are not authorities on factual history. Similarly, the "romans" of the Middle Ages are cited as essential for understanding the customs that make historical facts plausible.

In later literary history, the Danish poet Jens Baggesen (1764-1826) is identified as a man of great genius and the greatest comic poet produced by Denmark. Despite his ease of style and unrivaled skill as a witty lyrist and satirist, his talents were wasted on fruitless resistance to the modern literary spirit and trifling themes. Conversely, the figure of Davenant is noted for a prominence in literary history that his writings do not justify; his poems are described as unnatural and stilted, and his plays as utterly unreadable. His influence on English drama is characterized as wholly deplorable.

## Deplorable Effects and Wasted Potential

The term "deplorable" is frequently applied to the consequences of political and military failures. In the history of the Netherlands, the Belgic wars are described as having produced deplorable effects across Christendom, both by land and sea, despite being rich in policy and stratagems. In the context of the Syracusan conflict, Demosthenês recognized the deplorable effects resulting from the mistake of Nikias, who had wasted precious time and diminished the impact of his armament.

Individual lives also reflect a tension between genius and misfortune. One figure's history is described as unfortunate and his genius as wasted, though the archive notes it is impossible to regret his exclusion from the government of England. This individual was succeeded in title by his nephew Frederick, the 3rd Viscount St John.

In the case of Burke, the archive notes his passion for justice and order was struck by the "mischievous and fatal system" of penal laws against Catholics, corruption in administration, and the absenteeism of landlords in Ireland. It is suggested that a modern historian might reflect with remorse that the absenteeism of Ireland's men of genius was a greater curse to the country than the absenteeism of its landlords.

## Loss of Knowledge and Intellectual Integrity

The archive contains accounts of the potential loss of ancient learning. Some scholars have deplored the "irreparable shipwreck" of the genius, arts, and learning of antiquity, citing a narrative where the caliph Omar ordered the destruction of Greek writings that disagreed with the book of God. According to this account, volumes of parchment and paper were used as fuel for four thousand baths over six months. However, this account is contested by other sources. The archive notes that Mahometan casuists declare that religious books of Christians and the sons of Israel acquired through war should not be burned, and that works of poets, philosophers, physicians, and historians may be lawfully used by the faithful.

Finally, the archive records the intellectual vigor of Julian, who was assisted by his own genius and the integrity of Sallust. Julian later wrote a pedantic discourse in which he deplored the loss of Sallust, a valuable friend to whom he owed his reputation.
