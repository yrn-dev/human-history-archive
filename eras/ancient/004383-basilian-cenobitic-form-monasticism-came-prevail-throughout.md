# Basilian Cenobitic Monasticism

Basilian monasticism refers to the monastic rule and institute established by St Basil the Great. This system of monastic life serves as the foundation for the monasticism of the Slavonic and Greek Churches, although the monks within these traditions do not typically refer to themselves as Basilians.

## Origins and Principles
Around 360 AD, St Basil formed his monastery in Pontus, in the neighborhood of Neocaesarea. This establishment was a deliberate departure from the monastic trends Basil had observed during his visits to Palestine, Syria, and Egypt. In those regions, he found that monastic life was becoming increasingly eremitical (hermit-based) and characterized by extreme bodily austerities.

Basil declared the cenobitical life—community-based living—to be superior to the eremitical life. He established a system where fasting and austerities were not permitted to interfere with work or prayer. A central tenet of his system was that work should be an integral part of monastic life, pursued both for its own sake and to provide benefit to others. Consequently, Basil maintained that monasteries should be located near towns.

The life established by St Basil was strictly cenobitical, featuring common meals, common work, and common prayer performed seven times a day. Despite these new organizational ideas, it remained an austere, contemplative life dedicated to heavy field-work, the reading of the Scriptures, and prayer.

## The Basilian Rules and Virtues
The "Rules" associated with St Basil (categorized as the Longer and Shorter Rules) are described as catechisms of the spiritual life rather than corporate regulations for community operation. While some have questioned Basil's authorship of these and other ascetical writings, the current tendency is to recognize the two sets of Rules as his. A more accurate understanding of his system may be found in his correspondence with St Gregory Nazianzen.

In the Basilian system, monastic virtues such as self-denial, chastity, personal poverty, and obedience were strongly emphasized, although apparently no formal vows were taken.

## Propagation and Prevalence
The propagation of Basilian monachism was ensured by the influence of St Basil and the fact that his institute was well-suited to European ideas. In the provinces neighboring Cappadocia, Sozomen notes that there were no hermits, only cenobites. 

Despite this, a preference for the eremitical life persisted in the east. The Basilian cenobitic form of monasticism only came to prevail throughout the Greek-speaking lands through the application of civil legislation (the Justinian Code) and ecclesiastical legislation (the council of Chalcedon), although eremitical forms continued to maintain themselves.

## Geographic Spread and Influence
Basilian monachism spread from Greece into Russia and Italy. Around 400 AD, Rufinus translated the Rules into Latin, leading to their adoption in certain Italian monasteries. St Benedict was influenced by these texts, referring his own monks to the "Rule of our holy Father Basil"; it is noted that Benedict owed more of his Rule's ground-ideas to St Basil than to any other monastic legislator.

In Italy, Greek monasteries existed in the 6th and 7th centuries in south Italy, Sicily, and Rome. Between the 8th and 10th centuries, invasions by Arabs, Saracens, and others drove crowds of fugitives from Sicily and Greece into southern Italy. From the mid-9th century, Greek-speaking monks established numerous Basilian monasteries in Calabria, which eventually spread north to Rome. While many of these disappeared by the 18th century, the monastery founded by St Nilus around 1000 AD at Grottaferrata in the Alban Hills survives.

## Ecclesiastical and Social Role
Within the Orthodox Church, monks hold significant ecclesiastical importance because bishops, who must be celibate, are recruited from the monks (as parochial clergy must be married). Basilian monasticism also extended to women; St Macrina, the sister of St Basil, established a nunnery under his direction. These nuns pursued a purely contemplative life. In Russia, where approximately one hundred such nunneries exist, nuns are not permitted to take final vows until age sixty.

Beyond the Orthodox Church, there are small congregations of Uniat Basilians, including Catholic Basilian monasteries in Rumania, Galicia, Hungary, and Poland, as well as among the Uniat Syrians or Melchites.
