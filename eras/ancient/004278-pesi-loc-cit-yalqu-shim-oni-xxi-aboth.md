# Rabbinical Literature and the Fall of Jerusalem

## Relation to Rabbinical Literature
There exists a very close relationship between certain historical texts and rabbinical literature, with some parallels appearing almost word for word. This connection is particularly evident in descriptions of the destruction of Jerusalem. For example, the account of the destruction of Jerusalem by angels, found in sections vi. through viii. of certain texts, is also recorded in the *Pesikta Rabbati* 26 (ed. Friedmann 131a). 

The utility of these rabbinical parallels extends to the correction of textual errors. As Ginzberg has demonstrated, the passage in *Pesikta Rabbati* allows for the correction of a corrupt reading in section vi. 7; the phrase "the holy Ephod" ([Hebrew: 'PWD HQWDSH]) is corrected to "the holy Ark" ([Hebrew: 'RWN HQWDSH]).

## Accounts of the Fall of Jerusalem
Certain elements that may appear to be poetic fancies in some texts are presented as historical facts within rabbinical literature. A primary example is the account of the priests during the fall of Jerusalem. In this narrative, the priests are instructed to take the keys of the sanctuary and cast them into the height of heaven. They are to give the keys to the Lord, stating, "Guard Thine own house; for lo we are found unfaithful stewards."

This specific event is documented across several rabbinical sources, including:
* *Ta'anith*, 29a
* *Pesikta Rabbati*
* *Yalqu[t.] Shim'oni* on Is. xxi
* *Aboth* of Rabbi Nathan vii.

## Other Rabbinical Parallels
Beyond the fall of Jerusalem, other supernatural events find parallels in rabbinical records. One such instance involves the soldiers of Sennacherib. It is recorded that the bodies of these soldiers were burned, yet their armour and garments remained unconsumed. This account is mirrored in *Sanh.* 94a.

## Traditions on Teaching and Age
Rabbinical tradition also provides insight into the customs regarding the age of teachers. While some interpretations of the Gospel of Luke suggest a minimum age of thirty for public teaching, the *Bab. Talm.* (ed. 1715, fol. 19 b) indicates that if there was a Jewish custom or tradition regarding a minimum age for a teacher, that age was forty, not thirty.
