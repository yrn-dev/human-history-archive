# Historical Perspectives on Poetry and Verse

The archive's records on poetry span various cultures and eras, detailing the evolution of literary forms from the early epics of Greece and the religious verses of India and England to the structured satires of France and the sketchy stanzas of Japan.

## Early Greek Poetry and the Epic Tradition
The earliest literary monuments of Greece are epical in character, utilizing hexameter verse. These compositions were not limited to narratives of warlike adventures, but also served didactic purposes or celebrated religious mysteries. This early movement is associated with the mythical names of Hesiod, Orpheus, and Homer. While Homer and Hesiod are viewed as records of primitive thought, they were not the first embodiments of this form; unwritten epics circulated by word of mouth long before historical poets emerged.

A class of nameless and impersonal bards known as Rhapsodists or Homeridae recited this poetry at solemn occasions and feasts. Over time, saga-character works were attributed to Homer, a blind inhabitant of Chios. Aristotle later treated Homer as a specific person, attributing to him the *Iliad*, the *Odyssey*, and the now-lost *Margites*. Other early epic poets, such as Linus, Lesches, Arctinus, and Eugammon, remain largely symbolic shadows.

Other Greek poetic works include the tragedies of Lycophron, a poet and grammarian from Chalcis who flourished in Alexandria under Ptolemy Philadelphus. Lycophron is known for the *Alexandra* (or *Cassandra*), a 1,474-line iambic prophecy regarding the fortunes of Troy and the eventual empire of Alexander the Great. The work is noted for its enigmatical style and use of uncommon myths and obscure words. Additionally, the poems of Solon, as discussed by Plutarch and Diogenês, are considered lost treasures; remaining fragments suggest they blended personal feelings with observations of social and public phenomena.

## Old English and Religious Verse
Old English religious poetry was primarily written in cloisters by men familiar with Latin devotional literature and the Bible. This poetry utilized the alliterative long line as its sole metre, remaining faithful to native models in diction and imagery, often presenting saints and Christ as Germanic warriors. Most of this work was produced in Northumbria during the 8th century, though the poem *Judith* is later and *Exodus* is likely of southern origin.

Within the Junius MS., the poem *Daniel* is noted for its lucid narration, borrowing approximately 70 lines from a poetical rendering of the Song of the Three Children and the Prayer of Azarias. Other poems in this collection exhibit imaginative power and religious exhortation but lack literary finish and are characterized by repetitions. Some of these pieces are possibly compositions of the poet Cædmon.

Secular Old English heroic poetry is scarce, as professional reciters often resisted committing oral traditions to writing. Extant portions of narrative poems include *Beowulf*, *Widsith*, *Finnesburh*, and *Waldere*. *Widsith* serves as an archaeological record of kings in German tradition, while *Finnesburh* is a battle piece. *Waldere* concerns 5th-century Frankish and Burgundian traditions.

## French Satires and Literary Reform
In the 17th century, Boileau transformed French versification. His early preserved poems showed little promise of his ultimate impact, but his first satire (1660), imitating Juvenal, displayed his "peculiar powers." Boileau used his satires to attack high-ranking writers such as Philippe Quinault, Jean Chapelain, the abbe Charles Cotin, and Georges de Scudery. 

Boileau introduced terseness, vigour, and regularity to French verse, demonstrating the capabilities of the language in a way that had not been attempted since Malherbe. In 1664, he wrote the prose *Dialogue des heros de roman*, which effectively abolished the elaborate romances of Mlle de Scudery and La Calprenede. Boileau was a constant friend to Racine and Moliere, and the three, along with Antoine Furetiere and Chapelle, met at the *Pomme du Pin* and *Moulon Blanc* to discuss literary matters.

## Eastern Poetic Traditions
In India, the *Jataka* book consists of 550 verses identifying the Buddha with heroes of various folk-tales in his previous births. Another significant work is the *Sutta Nipata*, which contains fifty-five poems, mostly short lyrics. These works serve as authorities on the ethics and philosophy of early Buddhists.

Japanese poetry is characterized by a "sketchy nature," particularly in the five-line stanza and the three-line *hokku* (containing 17 syllables). These forms are often pictorial and conventional; for example, poems may focus on the moon in the early dawn or wild-geese in autumn clouds. Western critics have sometimes viewed this verse as an exercise in ingenuity or an "Oriental puzzle" rather than a literary form, a perception reinforced by the prevalence of couplet-composing contests.
