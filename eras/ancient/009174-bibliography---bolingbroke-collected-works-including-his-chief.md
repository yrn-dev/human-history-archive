# Bibliography and Bibliology

## Definition and Evolution
The term bibliography originates from the post-classical Greek word *bibliographia*, which was used to describe the writing of books. As late as 1761, Fenning's *English Dictionary* defined a bibliographer as an individual who copies or writes books. During the 18th century in France, the meaning shifted from "a writing of books" to "a writing about books," a transition evidenced by the 1763 publication of de Bure's *Bibliographie instructive*. 

In England, this updated meaning was popularized in the early 19th century by the Rev. Thomas Frognall Dibdin. During this period, Southey utilized the alternative term *bibliology*, though it is now rarely used. Modern custom generally restricts the province of bibliography to printed books rather than manuscripts. However, its scope is broad, encompassing interests of the book-loving antiquary, such as book-collecting, book-binding, book-illustration, and the history of printing. Technically, bibliography is the art of describing, collating, and examining books, as well as their arrangement and enumeration in lists for informational purposes.

## The Bibliography of Bibliography
The study of bibliography itself—the bibliography of bibliography—includes various specialized and universal efforts. Students of early printing have created nearly exhaustive lists of extant books from the 15th century. For books printed between 1501 and 1536, a tentative enumeration exists in the continuation of Panzer's *Annales Typographici* (1803).

While projects for a universal bibliography have declined as press output increased, the closest approximations are the printed catalogues of the Bibliotheque Nationale at Paris and the library of the British Museum. For rare books not found in these catalogues, records exist in the *Bibliographer's Manual* by Lowndes, the bibliographical collections of Mr. W.C. Hazlitt, and Brunet's *Manuel du libraire*.

National bibliographies emerged as universal efforts were deemed impossible. Notable examples include:
* **Great Britain:** Robert Watt's *Bibliotheca Britannica* (Edinburgh, 1824).
* **France:** Querard's *La France litteraire* (Paris, 1827-1839), which focuses primarily on "gens de lettres, historiens, et savants" from the 18th and early 19th centuries.
* **Germany:** Records of book output from the last two centuries are found in the works of Kayser (*Buecherlexikon, 1750*, etc., Leipzig, 1834, etc.) and Heinsius (*Allgemeines Buecherlexikon, 1700-1815*, Leipzig, 1812-1817).

## Bibliographical Reference Works
Several significant "bibliographies of bibliographies" have been produced, including:
* *Bibliotheca bibliographica* by Dr. Julius Petzholdt (Leipzig, 1866).
* *Manuel de bibliographie generale* by Henri Stein (Paris, 1898).
* *Manuel de bibliographie historique* by Ch. V. Langlois (Paris, 1901).
* *A Register of National Bibliography* by W.P. Courtney (London, 1905).

Additionally, Professor Ferguson's *Some Aspects of Bibliography* (Edinburgh, 1900) provides an eclectic list and introduction. The British Museum also provides the *List of Books of Reference in the Reading-Room* (first published 1889) and a *Subject-index of the Modern Works added to the Library of the British Museum in the years 1881-1900*, edited by G.K. Fortescue.

## Subject-Specific Bibliographies
The archive contains various bibliographies dedicated to specific figures or subjects:

**Bolingbroke:** His collected works, including philosophical essays such as *On Authority in Matters of Religion*, *On the Rise and Progress of Monotheism*, *On the Folly and Presumption of Philosophers*, and *Concerning the Nature, Extent and Reality of Human Knowledge*, were first published in a 1754 edition by Mallet. This edition was denounced by Johnson as "the blunderbuss charged against religion and morality." Subsequent publications occurred in 1778, 1809, and 1841. Other works include *A Collection of Political Tracts* (1748) and *Letters* published by G. Parke (1798) and Grimoard (1808).

**Dante:** The first attempt at a bibliography of Dante's editions was in Pasquali's collected works (Venice, 1739), followed by a more adequate work by the viscount Colomb de Batines (1846-1848). Further contributions include a supplement by Dr. Guido Biagi (1888), Julius Petzholdt's *Bibliographia Dantea* (1865-1880), and Passerini and Mazzi's *Un Decennio di bibliografia Dantesca* (1905).

**Thomas Hood:** His works include *Odes and Addresses to Great People* (1825), *Whims and Oddities* (1826, 1827), *Tylney Hall* (1834), and *National Tales* (1837). Biographical sources include *Memorials of Thomas Hood* (1860) and *Hood in Scotland* (1885) by Alexander Elliot.

**Other Subjects:** The laws of Athens have been collected in Telfy's *Corpus juris Attici* (1867). Biographies of Andrea Doria include works by V. Sigonius (1576), L. Cappelloni (1863), F. D. Guerrazzi (1874), and E. Petit (1887).
