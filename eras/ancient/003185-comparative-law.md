# COMPARATIVE LAW

Comparative law, also referred to as comparative jurisprudence, is the study and analysis of legal institutions and principles across different nations and historical eras. This field encompasses the examination of ancient legal systems, the evolution of legal terminology, and the academic study of jurisprudence in modern university settings.

## Ancient Greek Law and Comparative Jurisprudence
Ancient Greek law serves as a significant branch of comparative jurisprudence, although its importance has historically been overlooked by jurists, who often left the subject to scholars. These scholars generally avoided comparing Greek institutions with those of other nations, though some comparisons have been made between Greek and Roman law, as well as with the primitive institutions of Germanic nations.

Greek law can be studied through various sources, including the laws of Gortyn in its earlier stages and legal documents found in Egyptian papyri. In its final stages, it is recognized as a consistent whole in its relationship to Roman law within the eastern provinces of the Roman empire. The existence of panhellenic legal principles is suggested by the practice of using external arbitration to settle differences between members of a single state or between two different Greek states. General unity in Greek law is most evident in the laws of contract, commerce, adoption, and inheritance, as well as in the uniform publicity given to legal agreements.

## Legal Concepts and Terminology in Greece
In ancient Greece, a distinction existed between different types of laws and the nature of governance. The concept of the *τύραννος* (tyrant) was viewed as the antithesis of law; as Euripides stated, in a city ruled by a tyrant, there are no common laws, and one man possesses the law within himself. This stands in contrast to concepts such as *ἰσονομίη* (equal laws) and *ἰσηγορίη* (equal speech), which were opposed to monarchy. Aristotle further discussed the distinction between the government of laws and the rule of a king, noting that a person described as a "king according to law" is not a king in the true sense.

Terminology also evolved over time. Aristotle and Dêmosthenês referred to the ordinances of Drako as *νόμοι*, whereas Andokidês distinguished between the *νόμοι* of Solon and the *θεσμοί* of Drako. Solon himself used the term *θεσμοί* for his laws. The term *θεσμοί* originally carried a religious sanction, as the word *ἱδρύεσθαι* (used in relation to *θεσμοί*) referred to the establishment of protecting gods; the expression *θέσθαι νόμους* is a later term for the making of laws.

## Cross-Cultural Legal Influences
Comparative analysis reveals potential influences between different ancient civilizations. Diodorus mentions a law attributed to the Egyptian king Bocchoris which released debtors from personal liability, making only their properties liable. This law is said to have served as an example for Solon to copy, whereas other Greek lawgivers continued to use severe laws that enslaved the person of the debtor.

Regarding the Roman Twelve Tables, there has been scholarly debate over whether they included laws imported from Greece. While some, such as Dr. Arnold, incline toward the opposite view, the negative opinion—maintained by Niebuhr and Hugo—is now almost universally adopted.

## Roman and Lombard Law
Roman legal thought included the principle announced by Ulpian that all men are equal before the law, using legal rights as the measure of equality. In the later history of Italy, the Lombard laws provide insight into the manners of the Lombards. For example, the Laws of Rotharis, promulgated in A.D. 643, preserve various circumstances of the state of Italy, although they do not contain evidence of the "payment of thirds." Additionally, the Lombard laws use the Teutonic name *Faras* to describe families or generations.

## Academic Study of Law
In the modern era, the study of law has been promoted at several English universities. Since 1850, Oxford and Cambridge have offered curricula designed to provide students with fundamental knowledge of general principles and an elementary acquaintance with modern English law. Subjects taught include:
* Jurisprudence, Roman law, International law, and Constitutional law.
* Criminal law, Procedure, and Evidence.
* The Law of Contract and Tort.
* The law of Personal and Real Property.

While these schools are noted for their academic character and remoteness from law courts, law is also taught at Manchester and Liverpool. London University further encourages the study of law through degree examinations requiring a high standard of knowledge, with teaching provided at King's College, London, and University College, London.
