# Birs Nimrūd and the Sipparas

## Birs Nimrūd
Birs Nimrūd is identified as the suburb of Babylon. It is located within the rich alluvial plain of Chaldaea, a fertile region formed by the deposits of two great rivers. This area is characterized by its industrious population and is bordered to the east by the mountains of Elam and to the south by sea-marshes inhabited by other Aramaic tribes and the Chaldaeans (or Kald). To the west, the civilization of Babylonia extended beyond the Euphrates into the territory of the Suti, who were Semitic nomads.

The geography surrounding Birs Nimrūd includes the Arakhtu, known as the "river of Babylon," which flowed past the southern side of the city. To the south-west of the city, on the Arabian bank, lies the inland freshwater sea of Nejef. This sea is surrounded by red sandstone cliffs reaching 40 meters in length and 35 meters in breadth at its widest point. Extending from Birs Nimrūd to Kufa are the Chaldaean marshes; these marshes are dependent upon the state of the Hindiya canal and disappear if the canal is closed.

## The Two Sipparas
The archive identifies two locations known as Sippara, which correspond to the Sepharvaim of Scripture. These sites occupied both the Chaldaean and Arabian sides of the river.

One Sippara is located at Abu Habba, south-west of Bagdad and midway between the Tigris and Euphrates. It sits on the south side of a canal that may have once been the main stream of the Euphrates. This site is the location of the temple of the sun-god. The second Sippara, dedicated to the goddess Anunit, is located at Der, situated on the opposite bank of the canal from Abu Habba.

## Historical and Political Significance of Sippara
Sippara held significant military and religious importance during the Babylonian monarchy. The city was located in the region of Akkad (northern Babylonia). The capital of the elder Sargon, Agadē, was situated close to Sippara, and may have been a quarter of the city itself. To defend the kingdom from northern attacks, Nebuchadrezzar built a line of fortification between Sippara and Opis, referred to by Strabo as the "Wall of Semiramis."

During the reign of Nabonidus (Nabunaid), Sippara served as a strategic military hub. Nabonidus established a camp there near the northern frontier of his kingdom. His son, Belshazzar, took command of the army and lived with the troops in the camp near Sippara. Belshazzar is noted for his political and military energy, organizing the defense against the invasion of Cyrus. 

The Babylonian Chronicle indicates that from 548 B.C. (the 7th year of Nabonidus), the king's son remained with the army in the neighborhood of Sippara. During this period, Belshazzar bestowed numerous gifts upon the temple of the sun-god at Sippara. Records show that as late as the 17th year of Nabonidus—approximately three weeks after Cyrus's forces entered Babylonia—Belshazzar paid a tithe of 47 shekels of silver to the temple on behalf of his sister.

## The Fall of Sippara
The strategic importance of Sippara was evident during the Persian conquest. In 538 B.C., Cyrus invaded Babylonia. Following a battle at Opis in June, the Babylonians were defeated, and Sippara surrendered to the invader. Two days after the capture of Sippara, the soldiers of Cyrus entered Babylon without fighting, leading to the capture of Nabonidus and the end of the Babylonian monarchy.
