# Semitic Peoples of Mesopotamia and Beyond

The Semitic peoples were a group of nomadic and settler populations who entered the Euphrates-Tigris valley at a remote period, eventually establishing dominant empires and assimilating with the existing non-Semitic cultures of the region. While their arrival date is uncertain, their influence is evident in the earliest Sumerian texts, suggesting they were present on the Babylonian border early on, possibly originating from Arabia.

## Interaction with Sumerian Civilization
The early civilization of Babylonia was developed by the Sumerians, a non-Semitic race who spoke an agglutinative language and invented cuneiform writing. They established the region's chief cities and created the engineering works used to regulate river overflows and drain marshes. When the Semites entered the plain of Babylonia, they borrowed and adapted the cuneiform system. 

Although the Semites eventually conquered the Sumerian cities, the Sumerian civilization prevailed over the simpler Semitic culture. The newcomers adopted the Sumerian language as the speech of officials and the learned—a role similar to that of Latin in the European Middle Ages. Consequently, Sumerian remained the language of law and religion long after the Semites became the ruling race. Education in Babylonia was general, and both men and women learned to read and write, which required knowledge of the extinct Sumerian language and a complex syllabary.

## The Rise of Semitic Empires
The first major Semitic empire was founded by Sargon (approximately 2750 B.C.), a leader of the Akkadians. Described as an illiterate barbarian, Sargon united the Semitic tribes and conquered the Sumerian land, extending his rule from the Persian Gulf to the Mediterranean Sea. This Sumerian Akkadian Empire endured for over two hundred years.

Following the decay of Sargon's empire and incursions by the Elamites from the east, another Semitic group, the Amorites, settled in the up-river town of Babylon. After a century of warfare, they became masters of Mesopotamia. This first Babylonian Empire was consolidated by King Hammurabi (circa 2100 B.C.), who is credited with creating the earliest known code of laws. Later, a Semitic dynasty of Ur—including rulers such as Dungi, Bur-Sin, Gimil-Sin, and Ibi-Sin—extended its power to the Mediterranean.

## Linguistic Adaptation and Cuneiform
The transfer of the non-Semitic cuneiform script to the Semitic Babylonian language created a double method of writing. Words could be written "ideographically," where a sign represented an entire word, or phonetically in syllables. For example, the Sumerian sign MU for "name" was used in Babylonian-Assyrian texts but read as the Semitic equivalent, *shumu*. Similarly, the Sumerian SIG-BA for "clothing" was read as the Semitic *lubushtu*.

Over time, the Babylonians began using Sumerian signs merely as phonetic syllables to spell Babylonian words, regardless of the signs' original meanings. This system evolved into the Babylonian Assyrian syllabary as the culture spread north to Assyria.

## Expansion into Assyria and the Levant
In the north, the Semites founded an empire in Akkad, with settlers spreading from Susa to Sippara. From Babylonia "went forth the Assyrian." In these states, the *patesi* (high-priest) originally preceded the king, as the state grew around a sanctuary where the god was the nominal ruler. Eventually, Semitic princes unified Babylonia, and the earthly king became an incarnate god, though at Babylon, the king still required adoption by Bel-Merodach to rule.

Later, the Arameans, another Semitic group whose chief city was Damascus, settled in the north and restrained Assyrian expansion toward Egypt for four centuries. The descendants of the Arameans are the modern Syrians. In 745 B.C., Tiglath Pileser III founded the New Assyrian Empire by conquering Babylon and directing the transfer of the Israelites to Media. He was succeeded by Shalmaneser IV and later by Sargon II, a usurper who took an ancient Akkadian Sumerian name and is believed to have been the first to arm Assyrian forces with iron weapons.

## Semitic Influence in Egypt
Around the time of Hammurabi, a successful Semitic invasion of Egypt occurred. This led to the establishment of a line of Pharaohs known as the Hyksos, or "shepherd kings," who ruled for several centuries. Unlike the assimilation seen in Mesopotamia, the Hyksos remained foreigners and barbarians in the eyes of the Egyptians and were expelled by a popular uprising around 1600 B.C.
