# A Great Slaughter About Ascalon and the Command of Vespasian

## The Appointment of Vespasian
Nero appointed Vespasian to take command of the Roman armies in Syria. Nero viewed Vespasian as a man of sure experience and great skill, and considered the fact that Vespasian's sons were hostages for his fidelity to be a favorable circumstance. Nero believed the flourishing age of the sons would make them fit instruments under their father's prudence. To facilitate this command, Vespasian sent his son Titus from Achaia to Alexandria to retrieve the tenth and fifth legions. Vespasian himself traveled by land into Syria after crossing the Hellespont, where he assembled Roman forces and a significant number of auxiliaries provided by neighboring kings.

## The City of Ascalon
Ascalon was an ancient, well-fortified city located on the Mediterranean coast, 12 miles north of Gaza and 520 furlongs from Jerusalem. Historically, it was one of the five chief cities of the Philistines and remained an enemy to the Jews. The city served as a seat of worship for the fish-goddess Derketo; additionally, it is noted that the Philistines likely venerated Astarte there. In ancient times, the city housed the temple of Aphrodite Urania, described as the most ancient of all temples to that goddess, from which the temple in Cyprus was founded.

## The Jewish Campaign Against Ascalon
Following their victory over Cestius, the Jews were elevated by their success and extended the war to remoter areas. A great multitude of hardy soldiers marched toward Ascalon, led by three men noted for their sagacity and strength: John the Essene, Silas of Babylon, and Niger, known as the Persite. At the time of the attack, Ascalon's garrison consisted of one troop of horsemen and one cohort of footmen under the captain Antonius.

## Vespasian’s Rise to Empire and Military Movements
While Vespasian was in Alexandria, news arrived from Rome regarding his advancement to the entire government. This occurred during a period of heavy calamities in Rome, where Vitellius had arrived from Germany with a multitude of soldiers. These soldiers filled the houses of Rome and, driven by covetous desires upon seeing the city's gold and silver, were prepared to engage in plunder and slaughter.

Upon the confirmation of his government, Vespasian turned his attention to the unsubdued regions of Judea. He eventually traveled to Antioch, where he decided to proceed to Rome rather than Alexandria, as he viewed the affairs at Rome as being in disorder due to Vitellius. He committed a considerable army of footmen and horsemen to Mucianus, who led them on foot through Phrygia and Cappadocia to avoid the winter seas.

## The March of Titus
Vespasian sent his son Titus, accompanied by a select part of the army, to destroy Jerusalem. Titus's route began with a march on foot to Nicopolis, 20 furlongs from Alexandria, where his army boarded long ships. They sailed along the river through the Mendesian Nomus to the city of Tumuis. From there, Titus moved through several stations:
*   Tanis
*   Heracleopolis
*   Pelusium (where the army refreshed for two days before crossing the mouths of the Nile)
*   The temple of the Casian Jupiter
*   Ostracine (a location lacking its own water)
*   Rhinocolura
*   Raphia (marked as the beginning of Syria)
*   Gaza
*   Ascalon
*   Jamnia
*   Joppa
*   Cesarea, where Titus resolved to gather all other forces.

## Military Strategies in Judea
In other engagements, Vespasian dealt with the citizens of Jotapata. Irritated by the boldness of the citizens and their use of robber-like contrivances—including plundering and setting fire to works—Vespasian shifted his strategy. He ordered his army to cease active fighting and instead lie around the city to starve the inhabitants into surrender, believing that famine would weaken them and make the eventual conquest easier.
