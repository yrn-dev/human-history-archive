# Autographs and the Figure of Daniel

The study of autographs and the historical accounts of the figure Daniel represent distinct areas of archival research, ranging from the collection of signatures of eminent persons to the analysis of biblical and historical narratives.

## Autograph Collections and Definitions
In a restricted sense, autographs refer to documents or signatures written by individuals of notoriety or eminence across various professions and ranks of life. Because private documents are less likely to survive, early autographs typically consist of signatures and subscriptions of great officials and royal personages attached to important public deeds, which were more carefully preserved.

From the 16th century onward, autograph documents became more plentiful. However, some figures remain notably absent from the record; for example, the handwriting of Shakespeare is known only through two signatures on property conveyances in Blackfriars and three signatures on the sheets of his will. Other purported signatures found in books are dismissed as imitations or forgeries, such as those created by W. H. Ireland.

In England, several published collections of autograph facsimiles exist. These include:
* *British Autography* by J. Thane (1788-1793), which included a supplement by Daniell in 1854.
* *Autographs of Royal, Noble, Learned and Remarkable Personages in English History* by J. G. Nichols (1829).
* *Autographs of the Kings and Queens and Eminent Men of Great Britain* by J. Netherclift (1835).
* *The Handbook of Autographs* (1862) and *The Autograph Miscellany* (1855) by F. G. Netherclift.
* *The Autograph Album* by L. B. Phillips (1866).
* *Facsimiles of Autographs* published by the British Museum in five series between 1896 and 1900.

Official publications also contain facsimiles of national manuscripts, including those from Ireland (1874-1884), Scotland (1867-1871), and those from William the Conqueror to Queen Anne (1865-1868). Additionally, the Arabian historian Maqrizi (1364-1442) wrote a cyclopaedia of Egyptian biography called the *Muqaffa*; while intended to be 80 volumes, only 16 were completed, and three autograph volumes of this work exist in manuscript form in Leiden, with one in Paris.

## The Biblical Figure of Daniel
Daniel is the central figure of the biblical Book of Daniel, a work generally regarded as dating from the time of Antiochus Epiphanes (175-164 B.C.). The origins of the hero are obscure. According to Daniel 1:3, the Babylonian chief eunuch was ordered to bring nobles and "certain of the children of Israel, and of the king's seed" to serve in the court. 

Josephus records that Nebuchadnezzar, King of Babylon, took children from the most noble of the Israelites and kinsmen of their king, Zedekiah, who were remarkable for their beauty and comeliness. Among these were four from the family of Zedekiah: Daniel, Ananias, Misael, and Azarias. Nebuchadnezzar changed their names to Baltasar, Shadrach, Meshach, and Abednego, respectively. These four were esteemed by the king for their application to learning, wisdom, and excellent temper.

Other traditions regarding Daniel's origins vary. The true Epiphanius states Daniel was born at Upper Beth-Horon, a village near Jerusalem, and names his father as Sabaan. Pseudo-Epiphanius viewed him as an Israelite noble.

## Life and Legacy of Daniel
The biblical account provides little information regarding Daniel's later life and death. Various traditions exist:
* Some rabbis suggest Daniel returned to Jerusalem following the captivity and helped found the mythical Great Synagogue.
* Other traditions claim he died in Babylonia and was buried in the royal vault.
* The 12th-century traveller Benjamin of Tudela and the Arab Abulfaragius (Bar-hebraeus) mention a tomb in Susa.

In the narratives, Daniel is associated with the reign of Nebuchadnezzar. Josephus explains that Nebuchadnezzar experienced a seven-year banishment from men, during which he lived among beasts before returning to his reason and royal authority.

## Other References to Daniel
The name Daniel also appears in other historical and literary contexts:
* **Literature:** A poet named Daniel is noted for his sonnets, which differ from the Italian form by closing with a couplet. His works include *The Complaint of Rosamond*, *Epistles to Distinguished Persons* (including an epistle to the countess of Bedford in *terza rima*), and *Hymen's Triumph*. He also wrote *Musophilus*, a dialogue defending learning and poetic education addressed to Fulke Greville.
* **Theology:** In 1854, Samuel Hopkins published a *Treatise on the Millennium*, in which he used prophecies from *Revelation* and *Daniel* to deduce that the millennium would arrive near the end of the twentieth century.
