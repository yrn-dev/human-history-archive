# Gospels

The Gospels are written accounts of the life and teachings of Christ, originating from the needs of early Christian missions. These texts evolved from early collections of sayings into more structured historical narratives as the priorities of the early Church shifted.

## Origins and the Logia
The development of written manuals was driven by the nature of early Christian missionary work, particularly in regions outside Palestine where knowledge of Christ and Christian ideals was limited. Because early teachers and apostles rarely remained in one location for long, they left behind congregations that required guidance during the intervals between visits.

The diverse levels of preparation among converts necessitated a standardized tool for instruction. While some individuals, described in the Gospel as "waiting for the kingdom of God," were already well-trained in the Old Testament, other Gentile converts required basic instruction in monotheistic and ethical religion. To address these varying needs, the first Christian Gospel was likely created as an outline of Christ's characteristic teaching and a clear statement of His moral demands. This early work is identified as the *Logia* of St Matthew, often designated by the symbol Q. The *Logia* was not intended to be a complete history, but rather a collection of discourses and sayings designed to provide a rule of life.

## Development of Historical Gospels
A more strictly historical interest in the past arose as the eschatological outlook of the early Church weakened. This shift in focus from the future to the past became active around the decade A.D. 60-70. The first prominent result of this historical interest was the present Gospel of St Mark, which was likely composed in Rome between A.D. 64 and 70. There is no sufficient reason to presuppose an older form of this text, known as an *Ur-Marcus*.

The *Logia* (also referred to as the Non-Marcan document common to Matthew and Luke) and the Mark-Gospel served as the primary factors in the composition of subsequent Gospels. The Gospels of Matthew and Luke are constructed as combinations of these two documents, supplemented by additional material collected by their editors. Other fragmented works, such as the Gospel of Peter and the Gospel according to the Hebrews, are believed to have been built from these same materials.

## Versions and Translations
The New Testament was translated into various languages to facilitate its spread. Among the secondary versions, the Armenian version is noted for its importance. Its early history suggests two distinct translations occurred in the 4th century: one by Sahak based on Syriac, and another by Mesrop, assisted by Hrofanos (possibly Rufinus), based on a Greek text. Following the council of Ephesus in A.D. 430, Mesrop and Sahak revised and compared their work using manuscripts from Constantinople. While the general character of the Armenian version is considered late, it contains valuable evidence in the Epistles and Acts where the Old Syriac basis is still recognizable, as the Old Syriac is no longer extant in those sections.

## Theological Treatises on the Gospel
In later centuries, the concept of the Gospel continued to be a subject of theological debate. Andrew Fuller (1754-1815), an English Baptist divine, wrote *The Gospel worthy of all Acceptation* to oppose the prevailing Baptist hyper-Calvinism. This system had previously suggested that nothing spiritually good was the duty of the unregenerate and that exhortations to them should only concern external obedience. Fuller's work, along with his treatise *The Gospel its own Witness* (which argued against Deism), helped modify the views of English dissenters.
