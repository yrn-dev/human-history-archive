# IONIC PORTION OF HELLAS: ATHENS BEFORE SOLON

The Ionic portion of Hellas comprises Athens and Eubœa, the Cyclades Islands, and the Ionic cities located on the coast of Asia Minor along with their various colonies. The islands of the Cyclades and Sporades are categorized by their geological origins: some are prolongations of the mountain systems of Attica (such as Keôs, Kythnus, Serīphus, Pholegandrus, Sikinus, Gyarus, Syra, Paros, and Antiparos) or Eubœa (including Andros, Tênos, Mykonos, Dêlos, Naxos, and Amorgos), while others, such as Kimôlus, Mêlos, and Thêra, are of volcanic origin.

## The Ionic Cities and Colonization
Legends regarding the foundation of the Ionic cities vary. One account credits Ion, the son of Kreusa and Apollo, as the planter of these cities. A more common legend attributes the honor to the sons of Kodrus; specifically, Androklus is named as the founder of Ephesus and Neileus as the founder of Milêtus, both cities described as being founded directly from Athens.

Other Ionic cities appear to have been separate settlements that adopted Ionic tribes and the characteristic festival of the Apaturia. These include Kolophôn (settled by emigrants from Pylus under Andræmôn), Teôs (settled by Minyæ of Orchomenus under Athamas), Klazomenæ (settled by those from Kleônæ and Phlius), Phôkæa (settled by Phocians), and Priênê (settled largely by Kadmeians from Thebes). Herodotus notes that the population of the twelve Ionic cities was supplied by various races, including Dryopians, Molossians, Arkadian Pelasgians, and Dorians from Epidaurus. While the Milesians claimed the truest Ionic blood and a starting point at the prytaneium in Athens, the majority of other settlers likely did not depart from the same hearth. Similarly, the native authors of Chios and Samos did not ascribe their populations to an Athenian origin.

## Early Athenian Social and Political Structure
Historical facts regarding Athens prior to the legislation of Solon are very few. The period between 776 B.C. and 624 B.C. (the epoch of Drako’s legislation) is largely reduced to a list of archons. Early Attic chronology begins with the archonship of Kreôn in 683 B.C. The progression of leadership moved from kings—with no king following Kodrus—to life archons, then decennial archons, and finally to nine annual archons.

Early Attica was allegedly divided duodecimally and organized into four Ionic tribes: the Geleontes, Hoplêtes, Ægikoreis, and Argadeis. These were not based on professions or castes. The social structure included the Trittys, the Naukrary, the Phratry, and the Gens. The gens, or gentile communion, was an artificial enlargement of primitive family associations based on the belief in a common divine ancestor. These gentile and phratric associations remained political until the revolution of Kleisthenês.

Politically, Athens was originally composed of many distinct communities, and a cantonal feeling persisted for a long time; for example, Eleusis was originally independent. Political power was initially held by the Eupatridæ, who presided over the Senate of Areopagus and the nine archons. Other social classes included the Geômori and Demiurgi.

## Legal and Civil Developments
Before the reforms of Solon, Athens was governed by the laws of Drako. Drako's legislation established regulations for the Ephetæ and created different tribunals for homicide, some of which were influenced by local superstitions. 

The city also experienced periods of instability, including an attempted usurpation by Kylôn. This effort failed, leading to the massacre of his partisans by order of the Alkmæônids, who were subsequently tried and condemned. Following this, Athens suffered from pestilence, which was eventually addressed when Epimenidês of Krete visited the city to purify it.

## Economic Systems and the Euboic Scale
In the seventh and eighth centuries B.C., the Euboic scale of weight and money prevailed in Athens, Eubœa, and the Ionic cities generally. This scale was of Eastern origin and was used for gold tribute in the Persian empire. It consisted of a talent equal to sixty minæ, a mina equal to one hundred drachms, and a drachm equal to six obols. 

The Euboic scale differed from the Æginæan scale (introduced by Pheidôn of Argos); specifically, the Euboic drachm contained silver equal to only five Æginæan obols. The prevalence of the Euboic scale indicates a period when the Eubœan cities of Chalkis and Eretria surpassed Athens in maritime power and commercial relations. This system remained in place until Solon introduced a debasement of the coinage (amounting to approximately twenty-seven per cent), which created a distinct third scale known as the Attic scale.
