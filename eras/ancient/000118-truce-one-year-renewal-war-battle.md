# TRUCE FOR ONE YEAR: RENEWAL OF WAR AND BATTLE OF AMPHIPOLIS

The period following the capture of Sphakteria was marked by a shifting cycle of Athenian confidence, diplomatic truces, and a subsequent return to hostilities that culminated in the battle of Amphipolis.

## Athenian Prospects and the One Year Truce
The eighth year of the war began with sanguine hopes for Athens and dark promises for Sparta, largely due to the capture of Sphakteria. During this time, Athens launched two significant enterprises: one against Megara, which was partially successful, and another against Bœotia, which resulted in a ruinous defeat. Following the defeat at Delium and subsequent losses in Thrace—compounded by the expectations surrounding the career of Brasidas—the perception of Athenian power was seriously lowered.

While the Athenian public had been warlike following the success at Sphakteria, the unfavorable turn of events over the ensuing year made Athens more disposed to peace. Consequently, a truce for one year was concluded. This general truce, which the Epidaurians swore to in conjunction with Sparta in the beginning of B.C. 423, served as a temporary cessation of hostilities.

## The Expiration of the Truce and the Pythian Games
The expiration of the one-year truce did not immediately result in the renewal of active warfare. Although either party was permitted to resume hostilities once the truce dissolved, neither did so for several months. 

The Athenians had a specific motive for this delay: the Pythian festival at Delphi, celebrated in July or the beginning of August. Because the Athenians had been excluded from Delphi and the Pythian festival from the start of the war until the conclusion of the one-year truce, they felt a peculiar longing for the pilgrimages and festivals associated with the holy spot. Consequently, no actual warfare took place until the Pythian games had concluded.

## Renewal of War and the Battle of Amphipolis
Despite the weariness of a large portion of the Athenian public, Kleon remained eager to renew the war in Thrace. His personal influence and sanguine character were primary drivers in the resumption of warlike operations. This renewal of conflict led directly to the battle of Amphipolis, an engagement that proved fatal to both Kleon and Brasidas.

## Aftermath and the Move Toward Peace
The battle of Amphipolis, combined with the retreat of Ramphias from Thessaly, prompted both Athens and Lacedaemon to cease prosecution of the war and seek peace.

Athens was driven toward peace by several factors:
* Severe losses at Delium and Amphipolis.
* A loss of confidence in her own strength and the belief in ultimate victory.
* Fear that her allies might be tempted to rebel due to her reverses.
* Regret over missing the earlier opportunity for peace offered during the affair of Pylos.

Lacedaemon similarly found its position untenable:
* The devastation of Athenian land had failed to overthrow Athenian power within a few years.
* Sparta had suffered an unknown disaster on the island and saw its country plundered from Cythera and Pylos.
* Helots were deserting, creating a constant fear of revolution within the Peloponnese.
* A thirty-year truce with the Argives was expiring; the Argives refused renewal unless Cynuria was restored, making it impossible for Sparta to fight both Athens and Argos simultaneously.
* There were suspicions, and in some cases confirmation, that certain Peloponnesian cities intended to defect to the enemy.
