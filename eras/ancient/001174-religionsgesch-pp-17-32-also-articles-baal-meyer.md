# Baal

## Etymology and General Meaning
The term "Baal" is a Semitic word that primarily signifies an inhabitant, owner, or lord. Within the context of Semitic religious and family relations, the term was specifically used to describe the relationship between a husband and his wife, as well as the relationship between a deity and a worshipper. In a religious sense, the term did not necessarily imply that the god was the lord of the worshipper, but rather that the deity was the ruler in, or possessor of, a specific district or place.

Because "Baal" functioned as a title rather than a proper name, it was frequently applied to distinct deities (*numina*) across different communities. These various baals were often distinguished by adding a special attribute or the name of a location to the title. While allied communities might share a cult and the attributes of these deities were often similar—which later facilitated syncretism—the various baals were not necessarily conceived as identical.

## Usage in the Old Testament and Israelite Culture
In the Old Testament, the term is regularly written with the article as "the Baal." The existence of multiple distinct deities is reflected in the use of the plural form, "the Baalim," referring to the baals of different sanctuaries or tribes.

Evidence indicates that the Israelites applied the title of Baal to Yahweh. This is seen in proper names such as Be'aliah ("Yahweh is baal or lord"), Jerubbaal (Gideon), Eshbaal (a son of Saul), and Beeliada (a son of David). The name Beeliada appears as Eliada in 2 Samuel v. 16, suggesting that El (God) was regarded as equivalent to Baal. However, during periods when the name Baal was exclusively associated with idolatrous worship, a marked abhorrence for the word developed. In compound proper names, the word *bosheth* (meaning "shameful thing") was substituted for *baal*, resulting in names such as Mephibosheth and Ishbosheth.

Beyond its use as a divine title, the word *ba'al* also functioned as a noun of relation in common Hebrew usage. Examples include "a ba'al of hair" (a hairy man), "a ba'al of wings" (a winged creature), "ba'als of arrows" (archers), and "ba'als of oath" (conspirators).

## The Tyrian Baal and Phoenician Influence
The Baal of Tyre, also known as Melkart ("king of the city"), was widely worshipped throughout Phoenician colonies. Ancient writers, including Herodotus, provide accounts of this deity, who was frequently identified with the Greek Heracles and occasionally with the Olympian Zeus. 

The Tyrian Baal was honored in a magnificent temple in insular Tyre founded by Hiram. This temple received gifts from many countries, particularly during great feasts. One such annual feast, occurring shortly after the winter solstice, celebrated the "awakening" of the deity, highlighting his solar character. Symbolically, the Tyrian Baal was associated with two pillars—one of gold and one of *smaragdus*—which are linked by fantasy to the Pillars of Hercules. The influence of this deity is also evident in Carthaginian proper names, such as Hannibal and Hasdrubal. In Marseilles, a surviving tablet records the charges priests made for offering sacrifices at a temple of Baal.

## Baalzebub and Related Deities
The name Baalzebub (or Beelzebub) is often identified with the Hebrew common noun *zebub*, meaning "flies." Under this interpretation, Baalzebub is the "Baal to whom flies belong or are holy." Because flies appear in summer, they are viewed as symbols of the sun's warmth, with which Baal is closely related. Other interpretations suggest the "Fly-god" may have been an "averter of insects," similar to the Greek *Zeus apomouios* or *muiagros*. Some have suggested the deity was the Egyptian dung-beetle (*Scarabaeus pillularius*). However, Cheyne posits that Baalzebub is a contemptuous, uneuphonic Jewish modification of the name Baalzebul.

The name Baalzebul is linked to the names *Zebulun* and *Izebel* (Jezebel), suggesting that *Zebul* may have been an ancient name for a deity. Punic and Phoenician inscriptions contain the names *baal ezebel* and *shemzebel*. In the New Testament, Beelzebub is identified as an arch-fiend, a substitution that may have been influenced by the Aramaic *B'el-debaba*, meaning "adversary."

Other compounded names include Baalgad (the Baal of Fortune) and Baal-Shamem (Baal of the heavens). In Egyptian records, Rameses III is described as "strong-horned" like Baal, and the deity is associated with the horns of other gods such as Hadad and Samas.
