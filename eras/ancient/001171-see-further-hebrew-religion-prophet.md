# HEBREW RELIGION AND THE PROPHETS

## Evolution of Hebrew Religious Practice
The religious history of the Israelites is characterized by a complex transition from temple-based worship to a prophetic and creative religion. Early Israelite worship shows striking similarities to the native Canaanite cult, and some historians suggest that the germs of specific Yahweh worship existed from the time of Moses. However, the Old Testament depicts the history of the people as a cycle of apostasy and subsequent penitence and return to Yahweh.

A significant point of contention in this history is the role of "Baalism." It remains unclear whether the false worship stigmatized by the prophets was a form of Yahweh worship treated as a local nature god, or if Baalism was recognized as a distinct entity from the beginning. During the reign of Ahab, the introduction of a rival deity was accelerated when Ahab, following the example of Solomon, erected a temple to the Tyrian Baal in honor of his wife, Jezebel. While Ahab continued to consult the prophets of Yahweh and name his sons Ahaziah and Jehoram ("Yah[weh] holds" and "Y. is high"), the prophet Elijah strongly opposed the introduction of this rival deity. By the time of the prophet Hosea, the term "Baal" was considered dangerous to true religion and was often replaced by the contemptuous term *boshe*.

## The Nature and Role of the Prophets
The Hebrew prophets represented an overflow of moral ideas into the general community, marking a shift away from a religion limited to the priesthood and temple precincts. This movement is viewed as a parallel to the development of the free conscience of mankind, expanding the concept of one God over all the world. This prophetic tradition introduced the idea of a single rule in the world and the possibility of an active, splendid peace and happiness in human affairs.

The prophets did not have particular cities allotted to them, though it is noted that a school of the prophets may have existed in the city of Nob, where the tabernacle was located. The influence of the prophetic style was not limited to the Israelites; for instance, the Balaam narratives indicate a recognition of the genuine inspiration of a non-Hebrew prophet, suggesting that Yahweh revealed his will to nations other than the sons of Israel.

## Influence on Later Universal Religions
The prophetic teaching of the Hebrew prophets served as a precursor to later universal religions. Jesus of Nazareth is described as a prophet of unprecedented power whose teachings were of the "new type" established by the Hebrew prophets. His original teaching was prophetic rather than priestly; it lacked a consecrated temple, altar, rites, or ceremonies, focusing instead on the sermon and an organization of preachers, with the "broken and contrite heart" as its sacrifice. This differs from the later fourth-century Christianity of Nicæa, which became a priestly religion centered on the mass and a hierarchy of bishops, priests, and deacons.

Similarly, Muhammad, appearing in Arabia twelve hundred years after the Hebrew prophets, developed prophetic characteristics similar to theirs. His teachings, which were strongly influenced by Jewish and Christian ideas, emphasized the One True God and the rewards and punishments associated with virtue and wickedness. Muhammad declared himself the last chosen prophet of God, sent to perfect religion, and identified Abraham and Jesus Christ as his forerunners.

## Education and Literature
Because the religion of the Israelites was sustained by literature, it led to early efforts to provide elementary education for all children within the community. This literary foundation allowed for the preservation of traditions, although the texts underwent long-continued processes of redaction by successive editors and copyists. For example, the Greek Septuagint version of the book of Kings represents a different recension of the text than the Hebrew *receptus*, providing insight into how these religious narratives were transmitted and modified over time.
