# Ancient Critical Essays and Nero Caesar

The titles *Ancient Critical Essays* and *Nero Caesar, or Monarchic Depraved* are associated with the works of Edmund Bolton, an English poet and historian.

## Edmund Bolton
Edmund Bolton (born 1575; died after 1633) was an English historian and poet. He was educated at Trinity Hall, Cambridge, and later resided at the Inner Temple in London. Brought up as a Roman Catholic, Bolton served as a retainer to the duke of Buckingham, which allowed him to secure a small position at the court of James I. 

During his career, Bolton proposed a scheme to establish an English academy; however, this project failed following the death of King James I, despite the king's favorable view of the plan. Bolton also authored a *Life of King Henry II* for Speed's *Chronicle*, but this work was replaced by a life written by Dr. John Barcham because Bolton's Catholic sympathies were evident in his treatment of Thomas Becket. In 1600, he contributed to *England's Helicon*.

## Hypercritica and Ancient Critical Essays
Among Bolton's numerous writings, *Hypercritica* (published circa 1618) is considered one of his most important works. It is a short critical treatise that is valued for its notices of authors contemporary to Bolton. This treatise was later reprinted in 1815 in Joseph Haslewood's *Ancient Critical Essays* (vol. ii.).

## Nero Caesar, or Monarchic Depraved
Another significant work by Bolton is *Nero Caesar, or Monarchic Depraved*, published in 1624. This text includes special notes regarding British affairs.

## Historical Context of Nero
While Bolton's work focuses on the "monarchic depraved" nature of Nero, other archival records provide broader context on the Roman emperor Nero and the nature of monarchical government. 

In terms of physical legacy, Nero built a huge villa in Baiae, an ancient city of Campania in Italy, likely on the site now occupied by a 16th-century castle. Ancient writers frequently commented on the immorality and luxury of life in Baiae during the empire.

In broader historical analysis, Nero is cited alongside Tiberius, Caligula, and Domitian as examples of extreme Roman emperors. In a comparison of governments, it is noted that while modern civilized monarchies are viewed as governments of laws rather than men—where property is secure and the arts flourish—the Roman emperors Nero, Caligula, Tiberius, and Domitian represent a level of bad governance not matched by later absolute princes in Europe, such as Philip the Second of Spain.
