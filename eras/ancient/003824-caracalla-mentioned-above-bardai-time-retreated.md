# Bardaisan

Bardaisan was an early teacher of Christianity based in Mesopotamia and the founder of a school of thought that was eventually branded as heretical. According to the *Chronicle of Edessa*, he was born in the city of Edessa on July 11 (11th Tammuz), A.D. 154. His parents were of high rank and were likely pagans. One tradition suggests that in his youth, Bardaisan served as a priest in a heathen temple located at Mabbog.

## Education and Influence
It is probable that Bardaisan shared an education with a royal prince who later became the king of Edessa, possibly Abgar bar Manu, who reigned from 202 to 217. Bardaisan is credited with converting this prince to Christianity and may have played a significant role in the christianization of the city of Edessa.

## Literary Works and Hymnology
Bardaisan authored numerous works in Syriac, though almost all have perished. Among the few remaining possibilities is the *Hymn of the Soul* found in the *Acts of Thomas*, which is considered one of the only pieces of real Syriac poetry to survive. He is regarded as the father of Syriac hymnology; St Ephrem notes that Bardaisan composed 150 hymns modeled after the Davidic psalter.

His other writings included polemical dialogues directed against Marcionism and various other heresies, which were later translated into Greek. Additionally, during a period of persecution under the emperor Caracalla, Bardaisan wrote an apology for the Christian religion. Due to this persecution, he is said to have retreated into Armenia for a time, where he preached Christianity.

## Theological Doctrines and Controversies
The nature of Bardaisan's teachings is a subject of historical debate, as primary sources are scarce and often hostile. St Ephrem, a primary source of information on his doctrine, acknowledged that Bardaisan was in principle a monotheist. However, his teachings shared affinities with gnosticism; he denied the resurrection of the body and described the origin of the world as a process of emanation from the supreme God, whom he called "the Father of the living."

There are conflicting accounts regarding his religious trajectory. Epiphanius and Barhebraeus claim he began as an orthodox Christian and later became an adherent of Valentinus. Conversely, Eusebius and the Armenian Moses of Chorene state that he began with errors and largely, though not completely, purged himself of them in his later days.

Some of his teachings are preserved in the *Book of the Laws of the Countries*. Although written by a disciple, this work is cited by Eusebius as a genuine exposition of Bardaisan's views. This text upholds the freedom of the human will and recognizes the delegated authority and influence of celestial bodies over the material sphere and the human body. While some scholars, such as M. Nau, argue that these astrological speculations were misunderstood as heresy, others maintain that the connection to Valentinianism is strong and that the *Book of the Laws of the Countries* represents a shift toward orthodoxy. Later followers of his school reportedly moved toward a Manichean dualism.
