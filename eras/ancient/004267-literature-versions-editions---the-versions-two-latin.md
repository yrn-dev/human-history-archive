# Literature: Versions and Editions

The study of historical literature involves the analysis of various versions—translations of a text from one language to another—and editions, which are the specific published forms of these texts. These processes often involve complex chains of translation, textual revisions, and the identification of different recensions.

## Biblical Versions and Textual Criticism

In the study of biblical texts, versions are generally categorized as either primary or secondary. Primary versions are those translated at an early period directly from Greek originals, including the Latin, Syriac, and Egyptian versions. Secondary versions are those derived from other versions or from late, unimportant Greek texts.

### Latin Versions
The Old Latin version is of significant importance as it represents a Greek original prior to the *Hexapla*. While some authorities believe it originated in Carthage, recent writers suggest Antioch, citing its agreement with the Lucianic recension and early Syriac manuscripts. The Old Latin version exists primarily in fragmentary form through manuscripts and quotations from the Fathers. It was eventually superseded after the 6th century by the Vulgate.

The Vulgate was a Latin translation of the Old Testament produced by Jerome during the last quarter of the 4th century. Jerome translated primarily from Hebrew but also utilized Greek versions, particularly those of Symmachus. Jerome's original goal was to revise the Old Latin; his two revisions of the Psalter (the Roman and the Gallican) and his revision of the book of Job survive.

### Other Biblical Versions
The Book of Baruch possesses two Latin versions, one Syriac, and one Arabic. The Latin version found in the Vulgate is tolerably literal and dates to a time prior to Jerome. A second, less literal Latin version was published by Jos. Maria Caro in 1688 and later reprinted by Sabatier. The Syriac and Arabic versions found in the London Polyglot are literal. Additionally, Paul, the bishop of Tella, produced a Hexaplar-Syriac version in the early 7th century.

## Recensions and Editions of Early Christian Works

The term "recension" refers to different versions of a text based on different manuscript traditions. A notable example is the "Ignatian problem" regarding the Epistles of Ignatius, which exist in three recensions:
*   **The Short (Vossian) Recension:** Contains letters to the Ephesians, Magnesians, Trallians, Romans, Philadelphians, Smyrnaeans, and Polycarp. It was derived from the Medicean MS at Florence and published by Vossius in 1646.
*   **The Long Recension:** Contains the seven letters mentioned above in expanded form, plus additional letters to the Tarsians, Philippians, Antiochians, Hero, and Mary of Cassobola, as well as a letter from Mary to Ignatius.
*   **The Syriac (Curetonian) Recension:** Produced by William Cureton in 1845, this version contains abbreviated versions of only three epistles: those to Polycarp, the Romans, and the Ephesians.

Other early works, such as those of Ephraim, have seen numerous editions. A six-volume folio edition (1732-1746) provides texts in Greek and Syriac with Latin versions, though Burkitt noted the unsatisfactory nature of the Latin translation and the inclusion of non-Ephraim works. Other significant editions include the *Carmina Nisibena* (G. Bickell, 1866) and *Hymni et sermones* (T.J. Lamy, 1882-1902).

## Translation Chains and Literary Defects

The history of translation can sometimes result in "vicious circles" of retranslation, as seen in the works of Marco Polo. The Latin version published by Grynæus in 1532 was a retranslation from another version (possibly Portuguese) of Pipino's Latin version. Grynæus introduced modifications, generalized descriptions of animals, and omitted specific numbers. French editions from the mid-16th century were then translated from Grynæus, creating a chain: French—Italian—Pipino’s Latin—Portuguese?—Grynæus’s Latin—French.

In English biblical translation, the "Earlier Version" of the Old Testament (up to Baruch iii. 19) is described as stiff, awkward, and sometimes nonsensical due to an overly close adherence to the Latin text. This led to the "Later Version," which adopted different principles of work. Later, the Revised Version was produced, though its literary defects were criticized by G. Washington Moon in *The Revisers' English* (1882) and *Ecclesiastical English* (1886).

## Other Literary Versions

The *Pancha Tantra* and related fables have seen various international versions. These include the Persian version *Anvar-i Suheili* (translated by E. Eastwick, 1854), German translations by Philipp Wolff (1839) and Benfey (1859), and the earliest English version, Sir T. North's *Morall Philosophie of Doni* (edited by J. Jacobs, 1888). J.G.N. Keith-Falconer also provided a translation of a later Syriac version of the *Kalilah and Dimnah*.
