# The Symbolism of Christ as a Fish and the Concept of Spiritual Rebirth

In early Christian art and literature, Jesus Christ was occasionally symbolized as a fish. This imagery is closely linked to specific interpretations of baptism and the concept of spiritual rebirth, particularly within the adoptionist school of Christology.

## The "Fish" Symbolism and Baptism
The representation of Christ as a fish appeared in the literature of the 2nd century and in the art of the catacombs. This symbolism is rooted in the idea that Jesus was reborn of the Spirit during his baptism. Tertullian expressed this connection by stating, "We little fishes, after the example of our Fish Jesus Christ, are born in the water." He further noted that salvation is not possible except by abiding in the water.

This theme continued into the 5th century. Around the year 440, the Gaulish poet Orientius described Christ as a "fish born of the waters" and identified him as the originator of baptism.

## Spiritual Rebirth and Adoptionism
The concept of Jesus being "born again" through baptism is associated with the adoptionist school. This view is supported by ancient texts, including the *Gospel of the Hebrews*, regarding Luke iii. 22. In these texts, the words "Thou art my beloved Son, this day have I begotten Thee" were interpreted to mean that Jesus became an adoptive Son of God and the Messiah at the moment of his baptism.

Hilary echoed this sentiment, writing that Jesus was "born again" through baptism and subsequently became the Son of God. He clarified that while Jesus was the son of man, he was constituted the son of God through baptism, describing the event as the begetting of a man who was being reborn to become a perfect son.

## The Feast of Epiphany and the "Birthday"
Historically, the feast of Epiphany was regarded as a commemoration of the spiritual rebirth of Jesus in the Jordan. Because of this, the feast acquired the name *natalis dies* (birthday). This spiritual birthday was distinct from the *natalis in carne*, or the birthday in the flesh.

The association of Epiphany with the Baptism was widespread:
* **Early Traditions:** Some suggest the feast may have been instituted by Ebionite Christians in Palestine or by the disciples of John the Baptist.
* **Gnostic Influence:** Egyptian gnostics celebrated the feast on January 6, coinciding with the blessing of the Nile.
* **Regional Variations:** In the Eastern churches of Greece, Russia, Georgia, Armenia, Egypt, and Syria, the focus on the Baptism remained more conservative. In contrast, in the West, the day became known as the Feast of the Three Kings, and its original significance as a commemoration of the Baptism was obscured.

In Antioch, as late as 386, Epiphany was one of the two great feasts. On the eve of Epiphany, rivers and springs were blessed, and the water was stored for use in baptisms and lustrations throughout the year. Chrysostom emphasized that Christ became manifest to all not at his physical birth, but when he was baptized.

## The Rite of Baptism and Illumination
Baptism was frequently referred to as "illumination" (*photismos*) because those who underwent the process were believed to be illuminated in their understandings. According to Justin Martyr, the rite involved the following:
* **Preparation:** Those who believed and undertook to live according to the teachings prayed and fasted for the remission of past sins.
* **The Process:** Candidates were brought to water and washed in the name of God the Father, the Saviour Jesus Christ, and the Holy Spirit.
* **Purpose:** Justin asserted that Christian baptism alone could purify those who had repented, describing it as the "water of life." He contrasted this with rites that cleansed only the flesh, arguing that baptism should purify the soul from hatred, envy, and wrath.

## Theological Debates on Regeneration
The concept of regeneration is a subject of textual debate. While some associate it with baptism, others note that the New Testament—specifically the conversation with Nicodemus in John iii. 3-8—insists on spiritual rebirth. Some scholars suggest that the mention of "water" in these passages may have been a later addition or was omitted by early writers like Justin Martyr. Additionally, 1 Pet. i. 3 and v. 23 mention being "begotten again" through the word of God and the resurrection of Jesus, though it is not explicitly clear if this refers to the rite of water baptism.
