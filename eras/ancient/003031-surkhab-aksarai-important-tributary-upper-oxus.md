# Surkhab (Aksarai)

The Surkhab, also referred to as the Sarkhab or Aksarai, is a river that serves as an important tributary of the Upper Oxus. 

## Geography and Course
The river drains the valley of Bamian, a once renowned city in Afghanistan located approximately 80 miles northwest of Kabul. The valley of Bamian is situated in the Hazara country on the primary road leading from Kabul toward Turkestan. It lies at the northern foot of the Koh-i-Baba, which is a prolongation of the Indian Caucasus. 

The height of the Bamian valley was established at approximately 8,500 feet. This measurement, along with a survey of the surrounding country, was conducted in November 1885 by the Hon. M. G. Talbot and Major Pelham J. Maitland during the progress of the Russo-Afghan Boundary Commission. The region is characterized by high passes; those on the Kabul side reach absolute heights of 11,000 and 12,000 feet, while the passes immediately to the north are only slightly lower.

## Regional Associations and Nomenclature
The river is associated with several different names and regional identifiers across various historical and geographical records. In addition to Surkhab and Aksarai, it is referred to as the River of Ghori. According to the memoirs of Baber, the river is also called the Dogh-ábah. 

The vicinity of the Aksarai or Ghori River is linked to a district or place called Dehánah, as mentioned in Baber's writings. Records indicate that a village named Dehánah still exists within the Ghori territory.

## The Bamian Valley
The river is the primary drainage for the Bamian valley, a site noted for its significant ancient remains. The valley is lined with cliffs featuring numerous massive towers on their prominences. The precipitous faces of these cliffs are pierced by a vast number of ancient cave-dwellings over a stretch of 6 or 7 miles, some of which remain occupied.

The old city of Bamian is marked by remains of walls and mounds. An isolated rock in the center of the valley contains ruins of what is believed to have been the acropolis, known locally as Ghulgulah. The valley is most famous for two colossal standing idols carved into the northern cliffs, measuring 173 feet and 120 feet in height. These stucco-coated images are set in niches; the larger idol features an uninterpreted inscription and staircases leading to an ornamented chamber near the head, which the amir uses for grain storage. A third, slightly smaller colossus is located in one of the branch valleys, with further evidence of other idols and niches in the area.
