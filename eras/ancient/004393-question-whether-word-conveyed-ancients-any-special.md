# The Question of Special Significance in Ancient Terminology

In the study of ancient history and linguistics, scholars frequently examine whether specific terms used by ancient peoples conveyed a specialized meaning—whether architectural, legal, or divine—or if they were used in a general sense.

## Architectural Significance of the Basilica
A primary example of this inquiry concerns the word "basilica." Historians have debated whether this term conveyed a special architectural significance to the ancients. Some writers argue that the name merely denoted the use of the building, while others suggest it also implied a specific form. 

Knowledge regarding the ancient basilica as a civil structure is primarily derived from the writings of Vitruvius, as well as from inscriptions, incidental notices in classical writers, and existing remains. A review of this evidence suggests that a normal form of the building did exist, although many examples deviated from this standard. The essential character of the building can be understood by analyzing the information provided by Vitruvius.

## Legal and Commercial Terminology
In the realm of jurisprudence and commerce, the term "carrier" serves as a general designation for any person hired to convey the goods of another. While more specifically applied to tradesmen who used wagons or carts on public roads—a role largely superseded by the railway system—the legal application of the term is broader. In jurisprudence, it collectively refers to all conveyers of property, whether by land or water.

The legal responsibility of carriers is rooted in the civil law's praetorian edict, specifically the ninth title of the fourth book of the Pandect. The edict states: "*nautae, caupones, stabularii, quod cujusque salvum fore receperint, nisi restituent, in eos judicium dabo*." This simple rule established the principle that a carrier's engagement to the public is a contract of indemnity, requiring the carrier to deliver the entrusted goods or their value. This framework removed secondary discussions regarding the carrier's culpability in the event of loss or damage, leaving such limitations to be handled via special contract.

## Divine Voices and Omens
In ancient Greece, the concept of $\Phi\acute{\eta}\mu\eta$ (Pheme) illustrates a specialized understanding of communication. $\Phi\acute{\eta}\mu\eta$ was viewed as a vocal goddess or divine voice that could move a crowd with a single, unanimous feeling, effectively turning the *Vox Dei* (Voice of God) into the *Vox Populi* (Voice of the People). This concept is distinct from a human speaker and is reflected in the works of Homer, Hesiod, and Æschines. 

Similarly, the word $\kappa\lambda\eta\delta\dot{\omega}\nu$ (kleidōn), as used by Sophokles and Herodotus, carries a related meaning. Both terms can signify an omen delivered through an undesigned human word or speech, which was believed to be determined by the special intervention of the gods to inform a listener.

## Political Titles in Rome
The transition of power in Rome under Octavian provides another instance of the significance of terminology. After resigning extraordinary powers and handing the republic back to the Senate and people, Octavian was styled as *Princeps*. This was a title of courtesy familiar to republican usage. Rather than inventing a new office or reviving a kingly title—which Octavian expressly refused—the title *Princeps* conveyed only the idea of recognized primacy and precedence over fellow citizens. To further mark his dignity, the Senate granted him the additional cognomen "Augustus."

## Philosophical and Theological Inquiries
Ancient discourse also questioned the "special" nature of virtue. Sokratês and Plato explored whether virtue was truly teachable or if it was vouchsafed according to the special volition and grace of the gods. This was contrasted with the training of horses, for which professional trainers exist; no such master could be found to ensure the acquisition of virtue in one's sons. This led to an analysis of the specific branches of virtue, including prudence, fortitude, temperance, and justice.

In a theological context, the nature of oracles mentioned in profane history has been questioned. Some, such as the Dutch physician Van dale and the young Monsieur Fontenelle, attributed oracles to human imposture. Conversely, Father Baltus, a Jesuit professor, argued that dæmons were the real agents. This view was supported by the Fathers of the Church and ecclesiastical writers, who maintained that the devil was the author of oracles and idolatry in general.
