# Methods and Materials of Ancient Writing Rolls

The history of the written word is closely tied to the materials used for recording information and the physical methods employed to arrange and preserve that text. Before the development of the modern book, various civilizations utilized rolls made of papyrus, vellum, or paper, employing three distinct methods of writing upon them.

## Methods of Arranging Text on Rolls
The arrangement of writing on a roll determined how the document was stored and accessed:

1.  **Short lines at right angles:** Text was written in short lines perpendicular to the length of the roll. These were rolled up and stored in cylinder-shaped containers, sometimes grouped together in a box known as a *scrinium*, with title tags attached to the ends.
2.  **Long lines:** Text was written in lines spanning the entire length of the roll. This form is most notably seen in Buddhist prayer-wheels, where the rolls are kept in circular boxes with center handles to allow them to revolve.
3.  **Short lines parallel to the length:** Text was written in short lines parallel to the roll's length, leaving spaces on either side of each column or page. This method is still utilized in the scrolls of the law in Jewish synagogues, which are kept on two rollers.

This third method led to the development of the *orihon*, a Chinese and Japanese form of book. By folding the roll backwards and forwards in the vacant spaces between columns, the roll became a book with writing on one side and the other side blank. These were sometimes guarded by boards or fastened along one side using a cord or fiber run through holes stabbed through the material. This "stabbed" binding is the earliest method of keeping book leaves together, appearing in an 8th-century Coptic papyrus from Thebes, though it was rarely used for papyrus due to the material's brittleness.

## Writing Materials
Various substances were used for writing depending on the era and region:

*   **Papyrus:** Made from the pith of a reed found chiefly in Egypt, papyrus is believed to have been used as early as 4000 B.C. It was the primary vehicle for writing until the early Christian era, though it was used for pontifical bulls until A.D. 1022 and in Coptic manuscripts until approximately A.D. 1250. The Greek word for papyrus, *bublos* or *biblos*, is the origin of the word *biblion* (book) and subsequently the word "Bible."
*   **Parchment and Vellum:** Parchment is dressed sheepskin. It was introduced by Eumenes II, king of Pergamus, in the 2nd century B.C. as an improvement over papyrus, which had become hard to procure. Vellum is made from calf-skin and is considered finer than common parchment.
*   **Paper:** The manufacture of paper was learned by the Arabs from the Chinese via Central Asia, and subsequently passed from the Arabs to Europeans. Paper was produced from "filthy rags picked up in the streets." The lack of abundant, uniform paper in Europe is cited as a factor in the relative backwardness of Europe during the dark ages and a barrier to the early development of printing and popular education.

## Preservation and Illustration
Protective covers evolved alongside the materials. Early Assyrian tablets from the 8th century B.C. were enclosed in outer shells of the same shape. Egyptian papyrus rolls were typically bound with papyrus tape, often sealed with Nile mud, and stored in rectangular wooden hollows. Other early materials included tree bark (used for rolls of medical receipts or magical formulae) and wooden diptychs filled with blackened wax, which were scratched with a stylus.

Texts were also frequently illustrated. Examples include *The Book of the Dead* from fifteen centuries B.C., which featured scenes in brilliant colors. In early Rome, illustrated books were common, though these paintings were generally illustrative rather than decorative. Early vellum and papyrus manuscripts also featured primitive independent decorations, such as scrolls, dotted patterns, cables, and cross-hatchings at the beginning or end of texts.

## Transition to the Codex
The *volumen* (roll) was eventually superseded by the *codex*, a term originally referring to a block of wood or tree stump. The codex gained popularity as parchment replaced papyrus. The influence of the roll's narrow columns persisted in early codices; for example, the 4th-century Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus featured three and four columns per page, respectively. By the 6th century, as seen in the Codex Bezae, the arrangement shifted toward a single column.
