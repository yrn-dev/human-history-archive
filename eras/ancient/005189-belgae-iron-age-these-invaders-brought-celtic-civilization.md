# Belgae in the Iron Age

The Belgae were a Celtic people who, during the Iron Age, occupied a significant portion of the European continent and expanded their presence into Britain. They are first mentioned by Julius Caesar, who described them as forming one third of Gaul.

## Geography and Territory
In Gaul, the Belgae were separated from the Celtae by the Matrona (Marne) and the Sequana (Seine) rivers. Their territorial boundaries were defined by the ocean to the west and the lower Rhine to the north and east. The core region of the Belgae proper was located in the west, encompassing the areas occupied by the Atrebates, Ambiani, and Bellovaci; Caesar likely referred to this specific region—corresponding to the old Artois and Picardy provinces—as Belgium.

The Belgae also extended their reach into Britain during the time of Caesar, settling in southern counties, including Somerset, Hants, and Wilts. Among their established towns in this region was *Magnus Portus* (Portsmouth).

## Origins and Tribal Composition
The origins of the Belgae are a subject of historical debate. A deputation from the Remi informed Caesar that the Belgae were of German origin, having crossed the Rhine in early times to drive out the Galli. While Caesar noted that the Belgae differed from the Celtae in their laws, institutions, and language, some sources suggest this distinction was too sweeping, as many names and words are common to both groups. 

Historians have offered various interpretations of these origins. T.R. Holmes suggested that the ancestors of the Belgic conquerors may have simply dwelt in Germany, a fact also true of the ancestors of the Gauls. W. Ridgeway proposed that the Belgic tribes were Cimbri who moved across the Rhine into north-eastern Gaul. While the exact number of tribes is not provided by Caesar, Strabo recorded that there were fifteen in total. Within the confederation, the Nervians—who dwelt in the provinces of Flanders, Brabant, and Hainaut—were the most important.

## Civilization and Society
The Belgae, along with the Brythons, brought Celtic civilization and dialects to Britain during the Iron Age. By the era of Julius Caesar, nearly all inhabitants of Britain (with the possible exception of some far northern tribes) had adopted Celtic customs and speech. 

Politically, these people were organized into separate, often warring tribes, each led by its own princes. Their residential patterns varied:
* **Hill forts:** Structures featuring walls made of rude stone or earth.
* **Villages:** Round huts sunk into the ground, similar to those found in northern Gaul.
* **Other dwellings:** Subterranean chambered houses or pile-dwellings built within marshes.

In southern Britain, town life and market centers began to emerge, accompanied by improved housing. These southern tribes utilized a gold coinage as well as a currency consisting of iron ingots or bars. These bars typically weighed 1-1/3 lb, though some weighed 2-2/3 lb or roughly two-thirds of a pound.

## Art and Religion
The art of these tribes followed a native Late Celtic fashion, which was connected to the La-Tène culture of continental Celts and descended from Mediterranean antecedents. This style was characterized by a flamboyant nature.

Religious life was dominated by the priesthood of the Druids. The Druids wielded significant influence and taught a secret lore. They were known to practice magical arts and "barbarous rites of human sacrifice." Despite their social power, Druids generally did not participate in political affairs.
