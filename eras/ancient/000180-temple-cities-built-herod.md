# Of The [Temple And] Cities That Were Built By Herod

## The Temple and Jerusalem Edifices
In the fifteenth year of his reign, Herod rebuilt the temple, an undertaking that cost a vast sum of money. Herod noted that the Asamoneans, during their one hundred and twenty-five years of government, had been unable to perform a work of such magnitude for the honor of God. As part of this project, he encompassed the temple with a wall, enclosing a piece of land twice as large as the previous enclosure. He also erected great cloisters around the temple and a gate upon which he engraved the name of his friend, Agrippa.

On the north side of the temple, Herod repaired the citadel, which he named Antonia in honor of Antony. This structure, which some sources suggest was first built by John Hyrcanus and called "Baris," was rebuilt by Herod with great improvements. The tower of Antonia was situated on a precipice upon a rock fifty cubits high, which Herod covered with smooth stone to prevent anyone from climbing or descending. The tower itself rose to a height of forty cubits and functioned as a palace with various rooms, courts, and bathing places. It featured four corner towers; three were fifty cubits high, while the southeast tower reached seventy cubits to allow a view of the entire temple. The tower served as a guard for the temple, housing a Roman legion that used passages to enter the temple cloisters during Jewish festivals to prevent innovations.

In the Upper City, Herod built a palace containing two large and beautiful apartments, named Caesareum and Agrippium after his friends. These apartments were described as being larger than the holy house itself. Additionally, Herod dedicated a tower in Jerusalem named after his brother, Phasaelus.

## Cities and Fortifications within the Kingdom
Herod established several cities and strongholds throughout his domain. To honor his father, he built the city of Antipatris in the finest plain of his kingdom, an area abundant with trees and rivers. He also built the city of Phasaelis in the valley leading northward from Jericho. Near Jericho, he constructed a strong and fine citadel surrounded by a wall, which he dedicated to his mother and called Cypros.

On the sea-coast of Palestine, Herod built Caesarea (approximately 25-13 B.C.) on the site of Tunis Stratonis. This city featured a temple dedicated to Caesar, a theatre, a hippodrome, two aqueducts, and a boundary wall. Its most significant feature was a gigantic mole, 200 feet wide and built of 50-foot stones in 20 fathoms of water, protecting a harbor 180 yards across. 

Other urban projects included the rebuilding of the coastal city of Anthedon, which he renamed Agrippeum, and the construction of the city Sebaste in Samaria, which he encompassed with a wall.

## Projects of Herod's Successors
Following the death of Augustus—who reigned for fifty-seven years, six months, and two days—the sons of Herod, Philip and Herod Antipas, continued the administration of their tetrarchies. Philip built the city of Cesarea in the region of Paneas and at the fountains of Jordan, as well as the city of Julias in the lower Gaulonitis. Herod Antipas built the city of Tiberius in Galilee and another city also named Julias in Perea.

## Contributions to Foreign Cities
Herod extended his architectural patronage to numerous foreign cities. In Tripoli, Damascus, and Ptolemais, he built palaces for exercise. He constructed a wall around Byblus and built temples, market-places, cloisters, and large rooms at Tyre and Berytus, as well as theatres at Damascus and Sidon. 

His contributions included:
* **Infrastructure:** He built aqueducts for the Laodiceans by the sea and constructed costly fountains, baths, and cloisters for the people of Ascalon. In Antioch in Syria, he paved a twenty-furlong open place with polished marble and added a cloister of the same length.
* **Philanthropy:** He provided corn to those in need, gave large sums to Rhodes for shipbuilding, and rebuilt the temple of Apollo at his own expense. He also made donations to the Athenians, Lacedemonians, Nicopolitans, and the city of Pergamus in Mysia.
* **Cultural Patronage:** He established "Caesar's Games" every fifth year, featuring an amphitheater, theater, and market-place. During the hundred ninety-second olympiad, he provided royal bounty to the first, second, and third-place victors. He also provided annual revenues to settlements for exercises and to the people of Cos.
