# Indo-Aryans

The Indo-Aryans represent the specific part of the Eastern branch of the Indo-European peoples who settled in India. This designation is used by scholars to distinguish them from the Iranians, who remained in Persia and Bactria. The term *arya-* is described as a word with dignified associations, used by the peoples of the Eastern Indo-European section to refer to themselves.

## Origins and Migration
The prevalent view among authorities is that the Aryans originated in Asia Minor or southern Russia. From this home, a section of the population spread eastwards, eventually dividing into two closely related branches: the Iranians and the Hindus. 

The Aryans are believed to have been settled north of the Hindu Kush before migrating south-eastwards around 1500 B.C. While the Hindus possess no tradition of their invasion of India, they are not an indigenous people; evidence suggests they entered the region from the other side of the Hindu Kush, pushing through the Kabul valley into the open country of the Indus. By approximately 1000 B.C., during the period of their earliest literature, they were settled in the Indus valley.

## Settlement and Early Culture
The *Rig Veda*, the sacred songs of the ancient Aryans, portrays a life that was democratic in many ways and quasi-nomadic. Professor E.W. Hopkins argues that the *Rig Veda* was composed in the district around Umballa. He bases this on the text's description of the dawn, which is addressed as one who comes gleaming over the waters before the wind arises, suggesting the people were then located to the west of the great rivers.

The linguistic connection between the Indo-Aryans and Iranians remained strong following their separation. The vocabulary of the two groups remained largely the same, to the extent that whole sentences can be transliterated from one language to the other through regular phonetic changes without changing a single word.

## Social and Political Development
By the 6th century B.C., the Aryans had transitioned from their nomadic lifestyle to form settled states in the Ganges valley. These states were absolute monarchies, though the king's power was tempered by the significant influence of the Brahmans, a hereditary sacerdotal class. 

The position of the Brahmans is linked to the institution of caste, which divided the population into groups based partly on racial distinctions. This system of Brahmanism eventually faced opposition from the doctrine of the Indian prince Gotama, known as the Buddha.

## Interaction with Other Populations
The population of India is composed of at least three strata. The first consists of uncivilized aborigines, such as the Santhals and Kols. The second stratum is the Dravidians (including the Kanarese and Tamils), who may represent the earliest northern invaders and developed their own culture. Some authorities suggest that the Dravidians and Kolarians represent a single physical type, though they possess different languages and levels of civilization.

In prehistoric times, these groups were spread across India, but the third stratum—the Aryans—drove them toward the south and center of the peninsula. Additionally, invasions of Mongolian races from the north-west may have contributed to this displacement. These Mongolian groups are believed to have profoundly affected the population of Bengal, resulting in a composition described as Mongolo-Dravidian.

The term *Aryo-Indian* is sometimes used specifically to distinguish the Indian people of this stock from the Dravidian and other stocks inhabiting the Indian peninsula.
