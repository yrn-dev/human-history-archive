# Apocalypse of Baruch

The *Apocalypse of Baruch* is a long-lost work that was rediscovered through the efforts of Ceriani, who located a 6th-century manuscript (MS) in the Milan library. This work has survived exclusively in a Syriac version.

## Publication History and Editions
Following the discovery of the 6th-century manuscript, Ceriani published a Latin translation of the text in 1866 in *Monumenta Sacra*. This translation was later reproduced by Fritzsche in 1871 in *Libri Apocryphi Veteris Testamenti Graece*. Ceriani subsequently published the original text in 1871 and provided a photo-lithographic facsimile in 1883.

The work has been translated into several languages. R. H. Charles provided an English translation in 1896, while Ryssel produced a German translation in 1900. Regarding the Greek text, convenient editions are available from Tischendorf (in the second volume of his Septuagint), Swete (in volume iii), and Fritzsche (1871). Other notable editions of the book include those by Kneucker (*Das Buch Baruch*, 1879) and Gifford in the *Speaker's Apoc.* ii.

## Structure and Integrity
The *Apocalypse of Baruch* contains sections that were known long before the discovery of the full manuscript. Specifically, chapters lxxviii through lxxxvi consist of Baruch's epistle to the nine and a half tribes in captivity. These chapters were previously published in Syriac and Latin in the Paris and London Polyglots, and in Syriac from a single MS in Lagarde's *Libri V. T. Apocryphi Syr.* (1861). R. H. Charles later published these chapters from ten MSS in 1896.

There are questions regarding the integrity of the book. In chapter lxxvii. 19, it is mentioned that Baruch wrote two epistles: one to the nine and a half tribes and another to the two and a half tribes at Babylon. While the former is present in chapters lxxviii-lxxxvi, the latter is lost, though it may be preserved partially or wholly in the Book of Baruch, iii. 9-iv. 29.

## Content and Authorship
The book is characterized by conflicting elements, leading some scholars—including R. H. Charles, Beer, Kabisch, and de Faye—to postulate a diversity of authorship. Others, such as Ginzberg and Ryssel, suggest a diversity of sources. 

Excluding the letter to the tribes in captivity, the book is divided into seven sections separated by fasts (with a probable defect in the text after chapter xxxv). These sections address various themes:
* The Messiah and the Messianic kingdom.
* The destruction of Jerusalem and the past woes of Israel.
* Theological inquiries regarding free will, works, original sin, the number of the saved, and the nature of the resurrection body.

The tone of the work varies significantly. Some passages exhibit a vigorous optimism regarding the blessedness of the chosen people in the Messianic kingdom and Israel's ultimate well-being on earth. Conversely, other passages are hopelessly pessimistic, describing the world as a scene of irremediable corruption and the future of Israel as one of "lamentation, and mourning, and woe."

## Related Works
The *Apocalypse of Baruch* is distinct from the *Rest of the Words of Baruch* (also known in the Abyssinian Church as the *Rest of the Words of Jeremiah*). The latter was originally written by a Jew but revised by a Christian. It begins with an account of the removal of the Temple's sacred vessels before the Chaldees captured it. In this narrative, Baruch remains in Jerusalem while Jeremiah accompanies the exiles to Babylon; after 66 years, Jeremiah returns the Israelites to Jerusalem, though he refuses those who brought heathen wives. This work concludes with a Christian vision of Jeremiah.
