# Balaam

Balaam (Hebrew: *Bil`am*), the son of Beor, was a Gentile seer and prophet. He appears in biblical history during the Israelites' sojourn in the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, occurring at the end of the Forty Years' wandering, shortly before the crossing of the Jordan and the death of Moses.

## Narrative Framework and Oracles
The primary account of Balaam is found in Numbers xxii-xxv. This passage consists of a narrative that serves as a framework for seven oracular poems. The first four of these poems are of some length, while the final three are very brief.

The fourth poem (Num. xxiv. 14-19) announces the arrival of a king—possibly David—who will conquer Moab and Edom. The remaining three oracles are often viewed as later additions intended to bring the total number of poems to seven. The fifth (Num. xxiv. 20) concerns the ruin of Amalek; the sixth (Num. xxiv. 21 f.) addresses the destruction of the Kenite state by Assyria; and the seventh (Num. xxiv. 23 f.) speaks of ships from the West attacking "Eber" and Assur, which some interpret as a reference to Alexander the Great's conquest of Persia.

## The Conflict with King Balak
The narrative begins with Balak, the king of Moab, who became alarmed after the Israelites conquered two kings of eastern Palestine: Og, king of Bashan, and Sihon, king of the Amorites. To counter this threat, Balak sought the services of Balaam to curse Israel.

According to the "E" source, Balak sent the princes of Moab to Balaam at Pethor, located on the Euphrates (though some scholars suggest the "River" refers to a ravine in Egypt or Musri). According to the "J" source, Balak sent the elders of Moab and Midian to Balaam in the land of Ammon.

Balaam initially hesitated. In one account, God appeared to him in a dream and forbade him from going. However, after Balak sent the princes back to apply further pressure, God permitted Balaam to go on the condition that he spoke only what God told him.

## Divine Intervention and the Speaking Ass
While Balaam set out with two servants to meet Balak, he was intercepted by the Angel of Yahweh. Initially, only Balaam's ass could see the Angel, and the animal's attempts to avoid the entity aroused Balaam's anger. In a miraculous event, the ass was enabled to speak to Balaam. Eventually, Yahweh allowed Balaam to see the Angel, who revealed that he would have slain Balaam if not for the ass. Although Balaam offered to return, he was instructed to proceed.

## The Blessings of Israel
Upon meeting Balak, Balaam warned the king that he could only deliver the message God provided. Despite Balak's efforts to secure a curse—including offering sacrifices and placing Balaam on the top of Pisgah to view the Israelites—Yahweh compelled Balaam to bless the sons of Israel. After several remonstrances from Balak and subsequent explanations from Balaam, the prophet eventually returned home.

## Literary and Theological Significance
The narratives of Balaam are analyzed as being compiled from two ancient documents, denoted as "J" and "E," dating between 900 and 700 B.C. "E" is characterized by a preference for the name *Elohim* ("God") over "Jehovah" and reflects traditions from northern Israel.

Theologically, the stories are significant for the recognition of a non-Hebrew prophet's genuine inspiration, suggesting that Yahweh revealed his will to nations other than Israel. In this context, Balaam is seen as a companion figure to Jonah; where Jonah attempted to flee his calling, Balaam initially wanted to go where he was not sent.

## Alternative Traditions and Later Interpretations
Different sources provide varying accounts of Balaam's identity and fate:
* **The Priestly Code:** This source presents a different story where Balaam advises the Midianites on how to bring disaster to Israel by seducing the people away from their loyalty to Yahweh. In this version, Balaam is eventually killed in battle while fighting with the Midianites.
* **New Testament:** Balaam is cited as a type of avarice. Revelation ii. 14 mentions false teachers who followed the "teaching of Balaam," who allegedly taught Balak to lead the children of Israel into fornication and the eating of things sacrificed to idols.
* **Other Traditions:** Rabbinical identification (Yalkut) has linked Balaam with Laban, the father-in-law of Jacob. Islamic traditions describe him as one of the Anakim (giants of Palestine) who read the books of Abraham and used the name Yahweh to predict the future. Some conjecture that he is identical to the Arabic wise man Luqman.
