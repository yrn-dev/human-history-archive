# Fibulae (Safety-Pin Brooches)

Fibulae, or brooches of the safety-pin type, were extensively utilized throughout antiquity, though their use was confined to specific time periods and geographical regions. They were unknown to the Egyptians and oriental nations that had not been touched by Greek influence. Similarly, they do not appear in Hissarlik or Crete.

## Origins and Early Development
The exact place of origin for the fibula is not yet determined, but evidence suggests it emerged in central Europe toward the close of the Bronze Age, shortly before 1000 B.C. The earliest iteration was a simple pin bent for security, with the point caught against the head. As the form evolved, it became more similar to the modern safety-pin, utilizing a coiled spring to force the point against a catch. Examples of this early form have been found in the lower city of Mycenae, late Mycenaean Age deposits such as those at Enkomi in Cyprus, central Italy, Hungary, Bosnia, the Swiss lake-dwellings of the later Bronze Age, and rarely in the "terramare" deposits of the Po valley.

Toward the end of the Bronze Age, the safety-pin was arched into a bow to allow for a greater amount of material to be held. During the "Hallstatt period" (the older Iron Age), the bow and its accessories were modified and thickened to provide more rigidity and surfaces for decoration.

## Classification of Early Types
Montelius categorized the chief early types into four main groups based on their characteristic forms:
*   **Type I:** The catch-plate wire is hammered into a flat disk where the pin rests.
*   **Type II:** The bow is thickened in the middle to create a "leech" shape or hollowed underneath to create a "boat" form. The catch-plate is elongated to mask a long pin and is only slightly turned up.
*   **Type III:** The catch-plate is flattened (as in Type I), but the bow features additional convolutions.
*   **Type IV:** The bow is convoluted (sometimes represented by knobs), and the catch-plate develops as seen in Type II.

Additionally, the geometric age of Greece produced variations with an exaggerated vertical portion of the catch-plate.

## The La Tène Period and Technical Evolution
During the La Tène period (300–1 B.C.), the terminal of the catch-plate became more reflexed until it practically merged with the bow. A significant technical improvement introduced within the La Tène culture was the transition from a unilateral spring (coiled on one side of the bow) to a bilateral spring. In the bilateral design, the wire is coiled on one side of the bow's axis, crosses to the other side, and is coiled again before forming the pin. This innovation divides ancient fibulae into older and younger groups.

As the bilateral spring developed, it formed two projections on each side of the axis. To provide protection and strength, these were given a containing tube and a metal core. This change meant the pin no longer had to be a continuation of the bow, leading to the development of the T-shaped or "cross-bow" fibula.

## Later Developments and Regional Variations
The T-shaped fibula reached great importance and size during the first centuries of the Empire, with a high point of development seen around A.D. 400 on the ivory diptych of Stilicho at Monza. Between the 5th and 9th centuries, Frankish and kindred Teutonic tribes evolved the T-shape into a more ornate semicircle with a flattened base and radial knobs. An example from A.D. 481, belonging to King Childeric, features a singularly complicated pin-fastening.

Other brooch forms developed concurrently:
*   **Medallion Brooches:** Circular studs used to fasten cloaks on the shoulder, common in Etruscan deposits and Roman provinces, where they were often decorated with enamel, paste, or inlaid stone.
*   **Animal Brooches:** Early versions used animals as decorative appendages, while later versions used the animal as the brooch body with a pin attached underneath.
*   **Anglo-Saxon Brooches:** Found in England (particularly the medallion-form in Kent), these were often gold with central bosses and mosaics of garnets on gold foil, mother of pearl, and turquoises.
*   **Scandinavian/Viking Brooches:** Between the 5th and 8th centuries, T-shaped brooches became more massive. By the Viking period (A.D. 800–1050), they became oval, convex, and "tortoise" shaped. These evolved from a frog-like animal form into purely decorative lines with perforated upper shells of chased work and massive bosses. Their distribution in Normandy, Iceland, Ireland, England, northern Scotland, and Livonia marks the extent of Northmen conquests.
*   **Celtic Brooches:** Characterized by a penannular ring and a long pin. The ring could be turned to act as a buckle. Often made of silver or bronze with dragonesque or interlaced designs, such as the Hunterston brooch.

## Materials and Usage
Fibulae were crafted from gold, silver, or bronze, and adorned with filigree, precious stones, or enamel. Despite the richness of the body, the pin was nearly always made of iron. In Roman society, fibulae were part of women's ornaments, alongside bracelets, necklaces, and ear-rings. They were also used for specific garments, such as the suffibulum of the vestals. In medieval times, the form simplified into a flat circular disk with an open center, sometimes inscribed with talismanic or religious formulae.
