# THE DAIHO LAWS AND THE YORO LAWS

The Daiho and Yoro laws represent a significant period of legal codification in early Japanese history, characterized by efforts to centralize administrative power and establish the authority of the Throne. These codes, along with their subsequent revisions, formed an elaborate assemblage of laws that governed various aspects of society, from land ownership to penal consequences.

## The Daiho Laws
The Daiho laws (also referred to as the Taiho legislation) were established in the eighth century. A primary objective of the Daiho code was the centralization of administrative power and the assertion of the Throne's title of ownership over all land throughout the realm. This centralized approach was diametrically opposed to the spirit of feudalism.

The Daiho code addressed several specific areas of governance:
*   **Land and Agriculture:** The code included provisions for reclaimed uplands, referred to as *onchi* (or *yenchi*), which were distinguished from irrigated rice land. It also established *kwanden*, which were official or Court lands.
*   **Military Organization:** The laws provided for the organization of military affairs and included the *Gumbo-ryo*, a code of military art.
*   **Criminal Law:** The Daiho code classified crimes and established penal laws. Specifically, it included legislation regarding *Lése Majesté*.

A notable limitation of the Daiho code was its accessibility; it was written exclusively using Chinese ideographs, which rendered the laws unintelligible to the majority of the people they affected.

## The Yoro Laws
The legal framework established by the Daiho laws underwent a series of revisions. Under the instructions of Emperor Mommu, a second revision took place in the years 700 and 701. This process was carried out by a committee of ten members, led by Mahito (Duke) Awada and Fuhito of the Fujiwara family. This revision resulted in the promulgation of six volumes of Penal Law (*ritsu*) and eleven volumes of the Code (*ryo*). To ensure the new legislation was understood, expert jurists were dispatched to various regions to explain the laws.

Further amendments occurred seventeen years later, in 718, by order of the Empress Gensho. This revision was again headed by Fujiwara Fuhito, who had by then become prime minister. The resulting amended volumes—ten of the Law and ten of the Code—became known as the "Code and Law of the Yoro Period," or the "New Statutes."

The Yoro laws were not standalone documents but were part of a broader legal system. They were supplemented by:
*   **Kyaku:** A body of official rules.
*   **Shiki:** Operative regulations.

## Historical Legacy and Transition
The influence of the Daiho and Yoro codes persisted for centuries, though they eventually clashed with the evolving social structure of Japan. By the thirteenth century, the incongruity between the centralized nature of the Daiho code and the rising spirit of feudalism became apparent. This tension had already been noted during the time of Yoritomo, leading to the creation of "Rules for Decisions" (*Hanketsu-rei*).

These contradictions, combined with the difficulty of reading Chinese ideographs, led to the compilation of the Joei Shikimoku (or *Kwanto Goseibai Shikimoku*) in 1232-1233 during the regency of Yasutoki. Unlike the earlier codes, the Joei legislation was written mostly in the *kana* syllabary to ensure the laws were intelligible to all.
