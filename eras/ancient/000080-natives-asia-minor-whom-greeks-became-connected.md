# NATIVES OF ASIA MINOR WITH WHOM THE GREEKS BECAME CONNECTED

The history of Asia Minor is characterized by its geographical position as a bridge between Asia and Europe, serving as a battleground between the East and the West. This region was home to various non-Hellenic kingdoms and peoples who came into contact with Greek settlements established on the adjacent islands and the coast of Asia Minor.

## Early Populations and Racial Origins
At a very early period, Asia Minor was occupied by non-Aryan tribes or races that shared similar social systems, languages, and religions. Among these were the "Hittites," also known as "Syro-Cappadocians." After their rule ended, Herodotus referred to them as "White Syrians," and their descendants remained recognizable in the villages of Cappadocia. The center of Hittite power is believed to have been Boghaz Keui, east of the Halys, with roads extending to the Cilician plain, northern Syria, Sinope, and Aegean harbors. Evidence of their culture, including inscriptions and sculptures, has been found at sites such as Euyuk, Pteria, Fraktin, Bulgar, Muden, Ivriz, and Kiz Hissar (Tyana).

The original population of much of Asia Minor may have been of a "Mediterranean" race, potentially akin to the original populations of Crete and Greece. This is supported by the presence of art similar to that found in Mycenæ and Cnossos scattered across the region.

## Aryan Migrations and the Phrygians
Beginning in an unknown period and dying out by the 10th and 11th centuries B.C., Nordic or Aryan tribes migrated from Europe across the Bosphorus into Asia Minor. In some areas, these peoples became the dominant inhabitants and maintained their Aryan speech. The Phrygians were such a people; their language was nearly as close to Greek as that of the Macedonian. The Phrygians established a kingdom evidenced by forts, towns, and rock tombs, as well as Greek legends. This power was broken in the 8th or 9th century B.C. by the Cimmerii, who entered the region via Armenia.

## The Lydians
Following the decline of Phrygian power, the kingdom of Lydia rose with its center at Sardis. Unlike the Phrygians, the Lydians were a non-Aryan people who spoke a non-Aryan language, of which only a few words are known today. While the rising kingdom of Lydia was almost destroyed by a second Cimmerian invasion, the original race and language managed to hold their own.

## Other Native Groups and Regional Peoples
In the region of Mount Ida, the Greeks encountered ante-Hellenic inhabitants known as the Teukrians and Mysians. Specifically, Teukrians were noted in Gergis. In other parts of the peninsula, native princes ruled over Cappadocia and Paphlagonia as tributary provinces. In the south, the region of Lycia continued as an autonomous League for a period.

Later, in the 3rd century B.C. (specifically 278-277 B.C.), Gallic tribes crossed the Hellespont and Bosphorus to establish a Celtic power in central Asia Minor. Following victories by Attalus I. of Pergamum around 232 B.C., these tribes were confined to a district on the Halys and Sangarius, an area that became known as Galatia. They were later subjected to the suzerainty of Pergamum after their defeat by Manlius in 189 B.C.

## Cultural and Political Interactions
Greek contact with these natives involved both political and cultural exchanges. In the south, the Seleucids founded cities such as Attalia, Apamea, Antioch, the Seleuceias, and the Laodiceas to serve as centers of commerce, which later contributed to the Hellenization of the country. 

Cultural exchange is also evident in musical instruments. The barbiton (or barbitos), a stringed instrument derived from Persia, was used in Asia Minor. While it was used by some, such as the poet Anacreon (a native of Teos in Asia Minor), it was generally regarded by the Greeks of Hellas as a barbarian instrument favored only by those with unorthodox artistic tastes.

Politically, the region saw various shifts in power. The defeat of Antiochus the Great at Magnesia in 190 B.C. left Asia Minor vulnerable to Rome. Eventually, the region was divided into Roman provinces, including Bithynia-Pontus and Cilicia. Over time, the introduction of Christianity—which spread via the trade route to Ephesus and roads from the Cilician Gates—along with the advance of Greek culture, led to the disappearance of old religions and native languages. This process resulted in the thorough Hellenization of the people.
