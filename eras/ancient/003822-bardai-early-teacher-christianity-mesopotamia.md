# BARDAI[S.][=A]N

Bardai[s.][=a]n was an early teacher of Christianity in Mesopotamia and the founder of a school that was soon branded as heretical. He is recognized as the father of Syriac hymnology, having composed poems that enjoyed significant favor for a century and a half until the time of St Ephrem.

## Life and Early Career
According to the *Chronicle of Edessa*, Bardai[s.][=a]n was born in the city of Edessa on July 11 (11th Tammuz), A.D. 154. His parents were of rank and were likely pagan. Barhebraeus records that in his youth, Bardai[s.][=a]n served as a priest in a heathen temple located at Mabb[=o]g. 

Tradition suggests that Bardai[s.][=a]n shared an education with a royal prince who later became the king of Edessa, possibly Abgar bar Manu (who reigned from 202 to 217). Bardai[s.][=a]n is said to have converted this prince to Christianity and may have played a significant role in the christianization of Edessa. During the persecutions under Caracalla, Bardai[s.][=a]n is said to have retreated into Armenia for a period, where he preached Christianity.

## Literary Works
While the numerous Syriac works written by Bardai[s.][=a]n have entirely perished, there are a few notable exceptions and attributions. His most famous contributions were his hymns; St Ephrem states that Bardai[s.][=a]n wrote 150 hymns composed in imitation of the Davidic psalter. The *Hymn of the Soul*, found in the apocryphal *Acts of Thomas*, is considered by some to be his work and represents one of the only pieces of real Syriac poetry to survive.

Earlier works attributed to him by Eusebius and others include polemical dialogues directed against other heresies and Marcionism, which were subsequently translated into Greek. Additionally, he wrote an apology for the Christian religion during the period of persecution under Caracalla.

## Doctrines and Theological Controversy
The nature of Bardai[s.][=a]n's teachings is a subject of historical debate, as the available evidence relies on Greek writers and the hostile witness of St Ephrem. His teachings shared certain affinities with gnosticism, specifically in his denial of the resurrection of the body. Based on obscure quotations from his hymns, he described the origin of the world as a process of emanation from the supreme God, whom he called "the Father of the living." Despite these views, St Ephrem acknowledges that Bardai[s.][=a]n was, in principle, a monotheist.

Conversely, the *Book of the Laws of the Countries*—a dialogue written by a disciple and cited by Eusebius as a genuine exposition of Bardai[s.][=a]n's teaching—presents a different perspective. This text upholds the freedom of the human will and is largely reconcilable with orthodox Christian teaching, though it attributes a certain delegated authority to celestial bodies and recognizes their influence over the material sphere and the human body. 

Some scholars, such as M. Nau, have attempted to clear Bardai[s.][=a]n of the charge of gnosticism, arguing that the accusations of heresy stemmed from a misunderstanding of his astrological speculations. However, ancient testimony linking him to Valentinianism remains strong. There are conflicting accounts regarding his spiritual trajectory: Epiphanius and Barhebraeus claim he was first an orthodox Christian and later an adherent of Valentinus, while Eusebius and the Armenian Moses of Chorene state that he spent his later days purging himself of earlier errors.

## Legacy and School
Bardai[s.][=a]n's school continued after him, though its direction shifted over time. While the *Book of the Laws of the Countries* may represent a modification of his teaching toward orthodoxy, later adherents of the school appeared to move toward a Manichean dualism. Because so few fragments of his own original work remain, it is impossible to fully reconstruct his system or reach a definitive verdict on his theology.
