# Second Year of the War: The Plague of Athens

## Outbreak and Nature of the Plague
In the first days of summer during the second year of the war, the Lacedaemonians and their allies invaded Attica. This force was commanded by Archidamus, son of Zeuxidamus, King of Lacedaemon, and consisted of two-thirds of their forces. Shortly after their arrival, a plague began to manifest among the Athenians. While it was reported that the disease had previously appeared in other locations, including the neighborhood of Lemnos, no pestilence of such mortality or extent had been remembered.

The disaster proved overwhelming, rendering human art and medical knowledge ineffective. Physicians, ignorant of the proper treatment, suffered the highest mortality rates because they visited the sick most frequently. Similarly, divinations and supplications in temples were found to be futile. While Thucydides affirms that the infection could only be contracted once, other sources, such as Evagrius and Fabius Paullinus, suggest that some individuals who survived the first attack succumbed to a second.

## Progression and Impact
The plague devastated Athens for a total of three years, occurring continuously during the second and third years of the war. Following this period, there was a notable abatement for a year and a half before the disease revived with its original fury for another year.

The human cost was immense. The epidemic claimed the lives of 4,400 heavy infantry (hoplites) and 300 cavalrymen, the latter of whom were among the rich men of the state. The number of deaths among the general population was so great that it defied computation. This loss of manpower and the resulting private misery reduced Athenian power more than any other factor. During the second visit of the plague, which lasted no less than a year, Athens was also afflicted by numerous earthquakes, specifically in Athens, Euboea, and Boeotia (particularly at Orchomenus).

The plague's impact extended to the military movements of the enemy. The Lacedaemonian army withdrew from Attica earlier than intended due to the fear of contracting the contagion. While the disease spread from Athens to some populous islands, it did not spread into the Peloponnesus.

## Position and Policy of Pericles
The prosperity of Athens during this era was characterized by a "brilliant epoch," though it had a darker side. Pericles introduced payment for public service, a move that may have raised the general level of citizen culture but was censured by notable Greek thinkers for creating a dangerous precedent. Furthermore, Athenian prosperity was achieved by exploiting confederates in an illiberal and selfish manner. This led to Athens being labeled a "tyrant city," a sentiment that helped rouse Greek public opinion and contribute to the Peloponnesian War.

Following Pericles, Athens was served by advisers described as short-sighted or selfish. The city struggled to maintain a consistent foreign policy on its own initiative, committing blunders that contributed to the eventual ruin of the Athenian empire. Despite these failures, the discipline and patriotic devotion of the Athenian commons allowed the city to withstand prolonged attacks from external enemies and internal traitors, even while weakened by military disasters and the plague.
