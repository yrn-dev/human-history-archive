# Early Christian Baptism and the Baptistery

## The Rite of Baptism
In the early Christian Church, the process of baptism involved specific spiritual and physical preparations. Candidates for the sacrament, known as neophytes, prepared themselves through the keeping of vigils, fasting, and prayer. Before entering the baptismal font, neophytes of both sexes did so quite naked and were required to renounce the angels and pomps of the devil.

The ritual was designed to link physical actions to spiritual outcomes:
*   The washing of the flesh was intended to free the soul from stain.
*   The anointing of the flesh served to consecrate the soul.
*   The sealing of the flesh with the cross was meant to protect the soul.
*   The imposition of hands overshadowed the flesh so the soul could be illuminated by the Spirit.
*   Feeding on the blood and body of Christ was intended to sate and fill the soul with God.

Following the baptism, neophytes were given a draught of honey and milk. These rites were typically conferred by a bishop, or by deacons and priests commissioned by the bishop, during the season of Pentecost and at Easter.

## The Baptistery Structure
The baptistery (*Baptisterium*, or *photisterion* in the Greek Church) was a separate chapel or hall connected to the early Christian Church. It served as the location where the sacrament of baptism was administered and where catechumens received instruction. While some were circular, others were constructed with twelve or eight sides.

The building generally consisted of two main areas:
1.  **The Ante-room (*proaulios oikos*):** A space where catechumens were instructed and where they made their confession of faith prior to the baptism.
2.  **The Inner Apartment:** The area where the sacrament was performed. The central feature of this room was the baptismal font (*piscina* or *kolumbethra*).

The font was originally always made of stone, though metals were used in later periods. Three steps led down into the font, where those being baptized were immersed three times. A dove made of silver or gold was suspended over the font, and the walls were frequently decorated with pictures depicting scenes from the life of John the Baptist.

## Administration and Evolution
Baptisteries were necessary because the early Church practiced immersion and saw great numbers of adult catechumens. It was customary for the bishop to baptize all catechumens within his diocese, which is why these structures are commonly attached to cathedrals rather than parish churches. Because the rite was performed only three times a year, the bishop's seal was used to seal the baptistery doors during the months when no baptisms occurred. Some of these buildings were large enough to host synods and councils.

The use of a font was not enjoined by early sources such as the *Didache*, Justin, Tertullian, or the New Testament, which generally contemplated the use of living or running water. This reflected a Jewish rule that water should run away from the body during ablutions. Consequently, early baptisms occurred in the sea, lakes, or rivers. In the case of martyrs, baptism in these waters was common, though in exceptional cases, being sprinkled with one's own blood sufficed.

Historically, there is little trace of the separate baptistery before the 4th century, when Constantine made Christianity the state religion. By the 6th century, fonts were being built within the church itself or in the porch. After the 9th century, the construction of separate baptisteries became rare, with notable later examples found in Parma, Lucca, Padua, Florence, and Pisa.
