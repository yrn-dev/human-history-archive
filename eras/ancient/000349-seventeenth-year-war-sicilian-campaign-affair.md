# Seventeenth Year of the War—The Sicilian Campaign

The seventeenth year of the Peloponnesian War was characterized by strategic shifts in the Mediterranean, the intensification of the Athenian expedition against Syracuse, and the resulting interventions by the Lacedaemonians. This period marked a critical phase in the broader conflict, as the fortunes of Athens became inextricably linked to its ambitious projects in Sicily.

## The Sicilian Expedition and Athenian Ambitions
The Athenian involvement in Sicily began as a response to appeals from the Leontines and their allies, including the Chalcidian cities and Camarina, who sought aid against the Syracusans. While Athens officially justified the deployment of a fleet based on common Ionian descent and ancient alliances, the underlying strategic goals were to test the possibility of bringing Sicily into subjection and to prevent the exportation of Sicilian corn to the Peloponnese. 

Initially, Athens sent twenty ships under the command of Charoeades, son of Euphiletus, and Laches, son of Melanopus. The Athenians established a base at Rhegium in Italy to conduct operations in concert with their allies. Over time, these efforts evolved into a massive expedition against Syracuse. This campaign is later described as the single greatest cause of the downfall of the Athenian empire, representing a "death-wound" and a catastrophe of unprecedented proportions in Grecian experience.

## Lacedaemonian Response and Strategic Shifts
The Lacedaemonians, who had previously intended to march against Athens, shifted their focus upon receiving specific information from Alcibiades. This intelligence prompted them to prioritize the fortification of Decelea and the provision of immediate aid to the Sicilians. 

To manage the relief effort, the Lacedaemonians appointed Gylippus, son of Cleandridas, to lead the Syracusans. Gylippus was tasked with consulting the Syracusans and the Corinthians to arrange the speediest possible delivery of succours. As part of these arrangements, Gylippus requested that the Corinthians immediately send two ships to Asine and prepare further vessels for sailing.

Beyond the immediate Sicilian crisis, the Lacedaemonians sought to bolster their overall naval strength to an aggregate of five hundred triremes. They relied heavily on friendly Dorian cities along the Sicilian and Italian coasts, imposing specific contributions and contingents. These preparations were ordered to be carried out silently, without immediate declarations of hostility toward Athens, and without barring Athenian ships from their harbors. Furthermore, the Lacedaemonians planned to send envoys to other barbaric powers and the Persian king to bring the Phoenician fleet back into the Aegean to crush Athens.

## Concurrent Events of the Seventeenth Year
While the Sicilian campaign progressed, other conflicts persisted. In the winter following the summer season, the Lacedaemonians attempted to invade Argive territory. However, they retreated after finding the sacrifices for crossing to be unfavourable. This attempted invasion led the Argives to arrest several fellow citizens suspected of treachery, though some managed to escape.

Simultaneously, the Athenians continued their siege of Melos. After the Melians captured a feebly garrisoned part of the Athenian lines, Athens sent reinforcements under the command of Philocrates, son of Demeas. Following internal treachery, the Melians surrendered. The Athenians executed all grown men, sold the women and children into slavery, and settled the area with five hundred colonists.

## Conclusion of the Year
As the seventeenth year of the war drew to a close, the Athenian generals in Sicily sent a galley back to Athens to request cavalry and money. The Athenian assembly voted to provide the necessary supplies for the armament and the cavalry. With the end of winter, the seventeenth year of the war concluded.
