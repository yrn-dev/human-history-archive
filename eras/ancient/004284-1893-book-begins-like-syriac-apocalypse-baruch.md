# The Books of Baruch

The archive contains records of several distinct works associated with the name Baruch, primarily the "Rest of the Words of Baruch" and the "Apocalypse of Baruch." These texts vary significantly in their origins, contents, and historical reception.

## The Rest of the Words of Baruch
Also known in the Abyssinian Church as "the Rest of the Words of Jeremiah," this work is titled in Greek as *ta paraleipomena Hieremiou prophetou*. Evidence suggests the book was originally written by a person of the Israelite people but was later revised by a Christian.

The narrative begins with an account of the removal of the Temple's sacred vessels prior to the capture of the city by the Chaldees. In this account, Baruch remains in Jerusalem while Jeremiah accompanies the exiles to Babylon. After 66 years of exile, Jeremiah leads the Israelites back to Jerusalem; however, he refuses entry to those who had married heathen wives. The text concludes with a vision of Jeremiah that is described as Christian in nature.

The work has been preserved in several languages, including Ethiopic, Armenian, Slavonic, and Greek. The Greek text was first printed in Venice in 1609, with subsequent editions by Ceriani (1868), Harris (1889), and Bassiliev (1893).

## The Apocalypse of Baruch
The Apocalypse of Baruch was a long-lost work rediscovered by Ceriani, who found a 6th-century manuscript in the Milan library. This apocalypse has survived only in a Syriac version. Ceriani published a Latin translation in 1866 and the original text in 1871. A full English translation was later provided by R. H. Charles in 1896, and a German translation by Ryssel in 1900.

### Structure and Integrity
The text indicates in chapter lxxvii. 19 that Baruch wrote two epistles: one addressed to the two and a half tribes at Babylon and another to the nine and a half tribes in captivity. The epistle to the nine and a half tribes is found in chapters lxxviii. through lxxxvi. The epistle to the two and a half tribes is lost, though it is believed to be preserved in part or whole within the Book of Baruch, iii. 9-iv. 29.

### Authorship and Date
The book was likely written between 30 B.C. and A.D. 70. This dating is based on the fact that the text utilizes the Book of Wisdom, the (Ethiopic) Book of Enoch, and Sirach, but describes the temple as still standing in chapter lix. 2.

## Themes and Internal Conflicts
Scholars have noted conflicting elements within the Apocalypse of Baruch, leading to theories of either diverse sources or multiple authors. Excluding the letter to the tribes in captivity, the book is divided into seven sections separated by fasts. These sections address:
* The Messiah and the Messianic kingdom.
* The past woes of the Israelites and the present destruction of Jerusalem.
* Theological inquiries regarding free will, works, original sin, the nature of the resurrection body, and the number of the saved.

The text exhibits a sharp contrast in tone. Some passages are characterized by vigorous optimism regarding the ultimate well-being of the Israelites on earth, depicting the Messianic kingdom in sensuous and glowing colors. Conversely, other passages are hopelessly pessimistic, describing the world as a scene of irremediable corruption and the destiny of the Israelites as one of "mourning, and woe," signaling that the end of the world is near and a spiritual world is imminent.

## Literary Relations and Canonicity
The Apocalypse of Baruch shows a close connection to the New Testament in both diction and matter. For example, the phrase "Blessed is he who establishes peace" (lii. 11) parallels Matthew v. 9 ("Blessed are the peacemakers"). Similarly, the text's stance against swearing by any creature God made (xlix. 1) mirrors the instructions in Matthew v. 34, 35, 37.

Regarding the Book of Baruch's canonicity:
* **Israelite Reception:** It was never accepted as canonical by the Palestinian Israelites.
* **Christian Reception:** It was in general use in the church until the Protestant churches rejected its canonicity. It was formally accepted by the Roman church at the council of Trent. While the *Apostolic Constitutions* claim it was read in public worship on the 10th day of the month Gorpiaeus, this is considered unlikely.

The versions of the Book of Baruch include two Latin versions (one in the Vulgate and a later, less literal one), a Syriac version, and an Arabic version. A Hexaplar-Syriac version was produced by Paul, bishop of Tella, in the early 7th century.
