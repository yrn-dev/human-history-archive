# GRECIAN AFFAIRS DURING THE GOVERNMENT OF PEISISTRATUS AND HIS SONS AT ATHENS

The period of Grecian history beginning with the rule of Peisistratus at Athens and Crœsus in Lydia marks a significant era of political transition. Peisistratus established himself as the despot of Athens in 560 B.C. His dynasty governed the city for a total of fifty years, ending with the final expulsion of his son, Hippias, in 510 B.C.

## Rise to Power and Political Factions
Prior to his ascent, Athens was characterized by strife between three primary factions. These were the Pediaei, led by Lykurgus; the Parali, led by Megakles (the son of Alkmaeon); and the Diakrii, led by Peisistratus. The Diakrii were supported by the mass of the poorer classes who harbored hatred for the rich. 

During the return of Solon to Athens, Peisistratus presented himself as a man of moderation, a protector of the poor, and a law-abiding citizen who opposed revolutionary proceedings. While he deceived the general public with this persona, Solon detected his true plans to make himself master of the city, suggesting that if Peisistratus could abandon his desire to be first, he would be an excellent and virtuous citizen.

## The Reign of Peisistratus
The thirty-three years of Peisistratus's reign were not continuous, as they were interrupted by two periods of exile—one lasting five years and another lasting at least ten years. 

The longer exile was precipitated by a quarrel with the Alkmæônids. Peisistratus had married the daughter of Megaklês, but she bore him no children. Because Peisistratus already had adult sons from a previous marriage and believed the Kylonian curse affected the entire Alkmæônid family, he did not intend for his wife to become a mother. This incensed Megaklês, who renounced the alliance and joined forces with the adherents of Lykurgus, forcing Peisistratus to evacuate Attica for Eretria in Eubœa.

During his ten-year exile, Peisistratus remained influential, assisting Lygdamis of Naxos in becoming a despot and providing services to cities such as Thebes. In return, he received large monetary contributions. He eventually returned to Attica by landing at Marathon with mercenaries from Argos and troops and money provided by Lygdamis. Through a series of events that appeared to be a concerted betrayal by the Athenian government, Peisistratus routed the opposing forces and marched on Athens to be reinstated.

Peisistratus is described as a mild despot. His government was characterized by the use of mercenaries and the purification of Delos.

## The Rule of Hippias and Hipparchus
Peisistratus was succeeded by his sons, Hippias and Hipparchus. The stability of the dynasty was shaken in 514 B.C. when Harmodius and Aristogeitôn conspired and killed Hipparchus. This event left a lasting sentiment in the Athenian public, despite being based on a great historical mistake.

Following the death of Hipparchus, Hippias became the sole despot from 514 to 510 B.C. Unlike his father, Hippias's rule was marked by cruelty and a sense of conscious insecurity. During this time, Athens maintained connections with the Asiatic coast of the Hellespont and the Thracian Chersonesus. The Peisistratids sent the second Miltiadês to the Chersonese, where the first Miltiadês had previously served as œkist.

## The Fall of the Peisistratids
The expulsion of Hippias was orchestrated by the exiled Alkmæônids. After the Delphian temple suffered a conflagration, the Alkmæônids rebuilt it with magnificence. In gratitude, the Delphians helped them obtain directions from the oracle to seek assistance from Sparta. 

Following Spartan expeditions into Attica, Hippias was expelled, leading to the liberation of Athens. This event paved the way for the subsequent revolution of Kleisthenes and the establishment of democracy.

## Cultural and Literary Context
The age of Peisistratus is often discussed in relation to the Homeric epics. Some authorities, including Cicero and Pausanias, claim that Peisistratus collected and arranged the rhapsodies of the Iliad and Odyssey. Similarly, a Pseudo-Platonic dialogue attributes the introduction of Homeric poetry into Attica to Hipparchus, who allegedly prescribed the sequence of recitations for the Panathenaic festival.

However, internal evidence suggests the Iliad and Odyssey belong to an age two or three centuries prior to Peisistratus. The poems lack "modernism" relative to the sixth century B.C., showing no trace of the coined money, improved ship construction, or the specific despotisms and republican governments familiar to the epoch of Peisistratus.
