# Sumer and Akkad

The terms "Sumer and Akkad" historically denoted the entirety of Babylonia. This designation emerged following the rise of the empire of Sargon, whose capital, Akkad (also written as Agade or Akkattim in Elamite inscriptions), was located near or within Sippara. The expansion of Sargon's empire led to the adoption of "Sumer and Akkad" as an imperial title to represent the whole region.

## Regional Geography and Terminology
The alluvial plain of Babylonia was known as Edin, a name specifically restricted to the plain on the western bank of the river used by Bedouins for pasturing flocks. This western bank, or *kisad*, and the corresponding western bank of the Tigris gave the land of Chesed its name, from which the *Kasdim* of the Old Testament originated. In early Lagash inscriptions, this district was called Gu-Edinna, the Sumerian equivalent of the Semitic *Kisad Edini*. The coast-land was referred to as Gu-bba (Semitic *Kisad tamtim*), meaning the "bank of the sea."

Southern Babylonia was more comprehensively known as Kengi ("the land") or Kengi Sumer ("the land of Sumer"), with "Sumer" eventually being used alone. While some suggest Sumer is the origin of the Biblical Shinar, Shinar likely represented northern Babylonia and may correspond to the Sankhar mentioned in the Tell el-Amarna tablets. In contrast to Kengi and Sumer were the highlands or "upper country" known as Urra (or Uri), and northern Babylonia, known as Akkad.

## The Empire of Sargon and Early Dynasties
The Semitic empire founded by Sargon of Akkad marked a period of significant expansion. According to legend, Sargon, the son of Itti-Bel, was born in concealment and sent adrift in a bulrush ark on the Euphrates before being rescued by a husbandman named Akki. Upon ascending the crown of Babylonia, Sargon invaded Syria and Palestine four times, spending three years subduing "the west" to unite these regions with Babylonia into a single empire. He also subjugated Elam and northern Mesopotamia, suppressing rebellions in Babylonia and Kazalla.

Sargon's reign is associated with early administrative and technological advancements. A cadastral survey for taxation was in operation during his age, and the Babylonians utilized well-burnt bricks extensively. While Nabonidus claimed that 3,200 years separated his time from that of Nar-Sin (Sargon's son), leading to a traditional date of 3800 B.C. for Sargon, other scholars like Lehmann-Haupt and Winckler have questioned this figure as an exaggeration or a result of counting contemporaneous dynasties as consecutive.

## The Kassite Period and Subsequent Rule
The Kassite dynasty was founded by Kandis, Gandis, or Gaddas around 1780 B.C. and lasted for 576-3/4 years. The Kassites originally conquered Babylon after it had been weakened by raids from Hittites of Cappadocia during the reign of Samsu-ditana, the last king of the First Dynasty of Babylon. The Kassites eventually captured southern Babylonia, which had been held by Dynasty II of the Kings' List, thereby occupying the entire Babylonian plain.

Under Kassite dominion, Babylonia lost its empire over western Asia, and Syria and Palestine became independent. A notable shift occurred in royal ideology: the title of "god," previously used by Semitic kings, was never granted to a Kassite sovereign. However, Babylon remained the capital and the holy city of western Asia. Following the Kassite conquest, northern Babylonia became known as Kar-Duniyas ("the wall of the god Duniyas"), named for a line of fortifications designed to defend the kingdom from northern attacks.

## Assyrian Influence and Conflict
Assyria grew in power as Babylonia declined. Initially, under Khammurabi, a vassal-prince named Samsi-Hadad (or Samsi-Raman) ruled at Assur. Later, the Kassite king of Babylonia married the daughter of the Assyrian king Assur-yuballidh. This alliance ended in disaster when a Kassite court faction murdered the king; Assur-yuballidh subsequently marched into Babylonia to install Burna-buryas on the throne.

By 1300 B.C., Shalmaneser I openly claimed supremacy in western Asia. His son, Tukulti-In-aristi, eventually conquered Babylon and executed its king, Bitilyasu, effectively replacing Babylonia as the dominant power in the oriental world. Tukulti-In-aristi ruled Babylon for seven years using the old imperial title "king of Sumer and Akkad" before a Babylonian revolt led to his murder by his son, Assur-nazir-pal I.

Later conflicts continued between the two powers. In 1107 B.C., the Assyrian conqueror Tiglath-pileser I suffered a temporary defeat at the hands of Merodach-nadin-akhi of Babylonia, a period during which the Kassite dynasty had finally fallen to Elamite attacks, giving way to a new line of kings.
