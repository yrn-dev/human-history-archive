# The Style and Works of Bacchylides

Bacchylides was a lyric poet whose work was highly regarded by the Alexandrian scholars, who included him in their "canon" of the nine lyric poets alongside figures such as Pindar, Sappho, and Alcaeus. His poetry continued to be popular through at least the first four centuries of the common era, with the emperor Julian noted as an admirer of his writing.

## Stylistic Characteristics
The most general quality of Bacchylides' style is characterized by perfect simplicity and clearness. His sentences are typically simple in structure and lucid in meaning, a quality attributed to his native grace of mind, delicate skill, and a tendency to avoid complex thoughts or bold imagery. This pellucid style likely contributed to his wide popularity among cultivated readers.

A prominent trait of his style is a love of picturesque detail. Unlike poets such as Pindar, who may use a magic phrase to flash an image upon the mind, Bacchylides employs a quieter method, described as painting "cabinet pictures." He relies on elegance and observation rather than piercing insight. This tendency is particularly evident in his mythical narratives and his treatment of similes. While his full development of similes is Homeric in manner, his motive differs from Homer; where Homer sought vividness, Bacchylides focused on the decorative value of the details.

Examples of his imagery include:
* The forms of the Nereids streaming with a radiance "as of fire."
* An athlete compared to "the bright moon of the mid-month night" among stars.
* The hope of the Trojans appearing like a ray of sunshine "from beneath the edge of a storm-cloud."
* The shades of the departed on the banks of the Cocytus compared to countless leaves fluttering on "the gleaming headlands of Ida."

## Narrative and Moral Approach
In his epinikian odes, Bacchylides utilizes mythical narratives—such as those of Croesus, the Proetides, or Heracles and Meleager—to occupy the central part of the poem. His approach to these stories is a gentle flow of simple epic narrative, relying on the interest of the story as a whole rather than the forceful presentation of specific scenes.

His "gnomic" elements, or moral sentiments, differ from the didactic and sometimes obscure maxims of Pindar. Bacchylides' moralizing is instead an utterance of quiet meditation, reminiscent of Ionian gnomic elegy.

## Range of Compositions
The works of Bacchylides encompass several classes of composition:
* **Epinikia:** Odes of victory.
* **Dithyrambs:** Six compositions classed by Alexandrian scholars as dithyrambs, which they defined broadly as lyric poems featuring mythical narratives. These often include epic narrative, short speeches, and picturesque detail. Examples include poems on Io, Idas, and Theseus.
* **Hymns and Choral Songs:** These include *apopemptikoi* (hymns of pious farewell), *paianes* (such as a well-known passage on the blessings of peace), *prosodia* (choral odes for temple processions), and *huporchemata* (dance-songs for religious festivals).
* **Erotika:** Drinking-songs (*skolia*), including humorous verses on the power of wine.
* **Elegiacs:** Two *epigrammata anathematika* found in the Palatine Anthology, one being an inscription for a shrine to Zephyrus and another commemorating a victory gained by a chorus.

## Historical Context and Reception
Bacchylides visited the court of Hiero I of Syracuse between 478 and 467 B.C. Evidence suggests a professional rivalry between Bacchylides and Pindar. Some traditions and scholia indicate that Hiero preferred the Odes of Bacchylides to those of Pindar. In 468 B.C., Bacchylides alone was commissioned to celebrate Hiero's victories, though Pindar composed an ode for a different Syracusan victor that same year.

Ancient critics offered varying assessments of his merit. In the treatise "On the Sources of Elevation in Style" (ascribed to Cassius Longinus), the author contrasts Bacchylides with Pindar. While the author prefers the "greatness" of Pindar, he describes Bacchylides as a "faultless" poet of the second class, possessing a style of perfect elegance and finish but never reaching the loftiest heights.

His influence extended to later poets; Horace is noted as having studied and imitated Bacchylides' work, specifically in *Odes* i. 15 and iii. 21.
