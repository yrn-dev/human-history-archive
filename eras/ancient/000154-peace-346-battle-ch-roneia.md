# From the Peace of 346 B.C. to the Battle of Chæroneia and the Death of Philip

## The Peace of 346 B.C.
In early 346 B.C., Athens faced a shifting political landscape. While some factions had become warlike, Demosthenes adopted a pacific stance, recognizing that Athens required time to gather strength and that continuing the war was unproductive. This sentiment was shared by Macedonian sympathizers, most notably Philocrates. In February 346 B.C., eleven envoys—including Demosthenes, Aeschines, and Philocrates—were dispatched to Philip. Following debates in Athens, a peace treaty was concluded in April.

The primary terms of the agreement dictated that Philip, Athens, and her allies would each retain the territories they held at the time of ratification. However, a critical point of contention arose regarding the inclusion of the people of Halus in Thessaly and the Phocians. Thebes had been at war with Phocis for ten years, and Philip was engaged with the Halians. Demosthenes argued for their inclusion in the peace to prevent Philip from using these conflicts as a pretext for armed intervention in Greece. Philip insisted they be excluded; ultimately, they were not included.

## Ratification and Consequences
The envoys returned to Philip at the end of April 346 B.C. to receive his ratification oaths. Philip did not return from Thrace to Pella until late June, a delay that allowed him to acquire all towns he captured in the interim. Subsequently, Philip traveled through Thessaly to Thermopylae. Invited by the Thebans and Thessalians, he intervened in the Phocian War, resulting in the surrender of Phalaecus and the crushing of Phocis. Consequently, Philip assumed Phocis's place in the Amphictyonic Council.

The peace was further undermined by "false promises" used by Philip to induce Athenian consent. These promises are noted in the writings of Demosthenes, specifically in the second Philippic and the oration *de Falsâ Legatione*.

## Territorial Disputes and the Peninsula
Following the peace, disputes emerged regarding a specific peninsula. Athens had sent Diopeithes and a body of unpaid mercenaries to protect settlers there, leaving the soldiers to levy their own contributions. Meanwhile, Philip garrisoned and protected Kardia, a city near the peninsula's isthmus that asserted its independence and was ill-disposed toward Athens. During the peace-oaths of 346 B.C., Aeschines and the Athenian envoys admitted Kardia as an independent ally of Philip.

Philip subsequently distributed lands that Athenian settlers claimed as their own. When the settlers complained, Philip insisted they resolve the matter through arbitration with Kardia as an independent city. The settlers refused this, despite Aeschines' prior recognition of Kardia's status.

## Diplomatic and Political Conflict in Athens
The period was marked by intense rivalry between the orators Demosthenes and Aeschines. In 343-342 B.C., Demosthenes impeached Aeschines, accusing him of corruptly betraying Athenian interests during his second embassy to Philip in 346 B.C. This conflict is documented in the *De Falsâ Legatione* and Aeschines' defense. Later, in 330 B.C., the conduct of Demosthenes regarding the peace of 346 B.C. was again contested during the prosecution of Ktesiphon by Aeschines and the subsequent defense by Demosthenes.

Historical reconstruction of these events is complicated by the contradictory nature of these speeches. The accounts often exhibit a spirit of perversion and hostile feeling, making them unreliable except where collateral facts can provide confirmation.

## Philip's Influence and the Persian Empire
After the peace of 346 B.C., Philip's name carried an intimidating influence in Greece. During this time, the Persian king, Ochus, faced significant internal instability, with revolts in Cyprus, Phenicia, and Egypt. Isokrates used this weakness as a primary argument to encourage Philip to attack the Persian empire, noting that Ochus had been ignominiously repulsed and held in contempt after failing to reconquer Egypt.

While Diodorus suggests Ochus's expeditions against Phenicia and Egypt occurred between 351-348 B.C., other evidence suggests they took place after the March 346 B.C. peace, likely concluding in 346-345 B.C. To regain control of Cyprus, Ochus utilized the force of the Karian prince Idrieus, who commanded the islands of Rhodes, Kos, and Chios. Idrieus provided forty triremes and eight thousand mercenaries under the command of Evagoras and the Athenian Phokion. After a long siege of Salamis, held by Protagoras, Cyprus was brought back under Persian authority.
