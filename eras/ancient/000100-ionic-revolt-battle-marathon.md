# From the Ionic Revolt to the Battle of Marathon

The period between the Ionic revolt and the Battle of Marathon represents a critical confluence in Greek history, where the separate trajectories of the European and Asiatic Greeks merged into a direct and often violent relationship with the Persian Empire. This era was defined by a growing Persian intention to conquer Attica, evolving from a threat by the satrap Artaphernês—who demanded the Athenians reinstate Hippias as a condition for safety—into a passion for Darius following the burning of Sardis.

## Chronology of the Ionic Revolt
Determining the precise dates of the events leading from the Ionic revolt to the Battle of Marathon is a subject of significant debate among chronological critics. While the Battle of Marathon is fixed in August or September of 490 B.C., other dates are less certain. Some scholars, such as Mr. Clinton, suggest a ten-year interval between the start of the revolt and the battle, though other estimates range from fourteen to sixteen years.

Probable dates suggest the Ionic revolt may have begun in 502–501 B.C., coinciding with the Naxian expedition led by Aristagoras. Herodotus records that the city of Milêtus was captured in the sixth year after the revolt began, which would place the end of the siege of Milêtus around 496–495 B.C.

## The Role of Miltiadês
Miltiadês played a complex role during this interval. Having served as governor of the Chersonese around 515 B.C. under Hippias, he eventually incurred the hostility of the Persians. There is scholarly debate regarding his movements; while Herodotus suggests Miltiadês fled the Chersonese to avoid the Scythians, other views suggest he left to avoid the Persians, particularly after the Scythian expedition of Darius.

Between 502 and 494 B.C., while the Persians were occupied with the Ionic revolt, Miltiadês achieved the conquest of Lemnos. This achievement was distinct from the operations of the revolted Ionians. His fame from the conquest of Lemnos helped neutralize the enmity he had earned as governor of the Chersonese and contributed to his later election as one of the ten generals for the Athenian army at Marathon. He returned to Athens approximately two or three years before the battle, eventually serving as the acting commander.

## Greek Perspectives on Persian Power
During the onset of the Ionic revolt around 502 B.C., the Persians were viewed with extreme terror by the Greeks. While Aristagoras may have suggested that the Spartans were a match for the Persians in the field, the idea of marching to Susa to claim the empire of Asia would have been viewed as insanity at the time. 

In Sparta, the king, such as Kleomenês, maintained active management of foreign affairs, though he remained subject to trial and punishment by the ephors. Despite the later historical perspective of contempt for the Persians, the Greeks of this era lived under the shadow of uninterrupted Persian success. The "high-handed and cruel" suppression of the Ionic revolt over a ten-year period intensified this alarm.

## The Battle of Marathon
By the time Datis landed in the bay of Marathon, the Athenians faced a situation that seemed like a "sentence of death," compounded by the recent calamities of Eretria and the successes of Datis. The extreme courage required to face the invaders was reflected in the divided opinions of the ten Athenian generals.

The decision to fight was reached through a democratic process; when the polemarch cast the deciding vote, the five dissenting generals cooperated heartily for the success of the policy they had deprecated. This cooperation demonstrated the deep integration of constitutional democracy and the acceptance of majority decisions within the Athenian mind.

The Battle of Marathon resulted in a defeat for the Persians. While not a decisively overwhelming victory, it was historically significant as the first time the Persians had ever been defeated by Greeks in the field.
