## Literary and Pastoral Contexts
The term "bucolics," derived from the Greek *boukolikos* (meaning "pertaining to a herdsman"), refers to rural or pastoral poetry. In English, this expression has been used since the beginning of the 14th century to describe the "Eclogues" of Virgil. The most famous collection of bucolics from antiquity is that of Theocritus, which consists of approximately thirty works written mainly in hexameter verse and the Doric dialect. Virgil is believed to have originally named his own pastoral poems "Bucolics" to challenge the writings of Theocritus.

In modern usage, "bucolics" is often a synonym for pastoral poetry, though it has evolved to carry a more agricultural meaning than a shepherd-based one. Consequently, Virgil's "Georgics" is sometimes viewed as more "bucolic" than his "Eclogues." Other poets, such as Ronsard, have specifically used the title "Les Bucoliques" for collections of eclogues. Additionally, the fifth eclogue or pastoral is described as a perpetual and obscure allegory.

## Geographical Corydon
Corydon is also the name of a town and the county-seat of Harrison County, Indiana, in the United States. Located on Indian Creek, it is situated approximately 21 miles west by south of Louisville, Kentucky. The town's population was recorded as 1,610 in 1900 and 1,703 in 1910. 

Settled around 1805, Corydon served as the capital of the Indiana Territory from 1813 to 1816, and remained the state capital until 1824. The convention that framed the first state constitution met there in June 1816. A two-storey stone building that served as the original state house still stands. 

The town is a health and summer resort known for its sulphur springs, and Wyandotte Cave is located several miles to the west. Corydon is situated in an agricultural region with valuable nearby quarries; local manufactures include building stone, lithographic stone, and wagons. The town is served by the Louisville, New Albany & Corydon railway, which connects to the Southern railway at Corydon Junction, located 8 miles to the north. Historically, the town was captured by Confederates on July 9, 1863, during the raid of General Morgan.
