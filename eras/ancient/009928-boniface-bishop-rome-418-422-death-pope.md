# BONIFACE I.

## Election and Accession
Boniface I. served as the bishop of Rome from 418 to 422. His ascension to the position occurred following the death of Pope Zosimus, an event that left the Roman clergy divided into two opposing factions. One faction elected a priest named Boniface, while the other elected a deacon named Eulalius. 

To maintain public order, the imperial government intervened and commanded both competitors to leave the town. The government reserved the final decision regarding the case to a council. However, Eulalius broke the ban imposed upon him. Consequently, the emperor Honorius decided to recognize Boniface, and the scheduled council was countermanded. Despite this official recognition, the faction supporting Eulalius continued to foment disorders for a significant period, which eventually necessitated further intervention by the secular authority.

## Other Popes Named Boniface
The archive records several subsequent bishops of Rome who bore the name Boniface:

*   **Boniface II (530–532):** A Goth by birth, his election was influenced by the Gothic king and the nomination of his predecessor, Felix IV. He faced opposition from Dioscorus, a priest of Alexandria, but Dioscorus died one month after his election. Boniface II attempted to establish a custom of nominating his own successor, though the senate of Rome and the clergy forced him to cancel this arrangement.
*   **Boniface III (606):** Served from February 15 to November 12, 606. He received recognition from Phocas for the "headship of the church at Rome," which likely meant Phocas forced the patriarch of Constantinople to momentarily abandon his claim to the title of oecumenical patriarch.
*   **Boniface IV (608–615):** Received the Pantheon at Rome from emperor Phocas and converted the building into a Christian church.
*   **Boniface V (619–625):** Contributed to the christianizing of England. He wrote encouraging letters to Justus, bishop of Rochester, and Mellitus, archbishop of Canterbury. He also wrote to Aethelberga and King Eadwin of Northumbria. A letter from 625 established Canterbury as the metropolitan see of Britain permanently.
*   **Boniface VI (896):** Elected in April 896 and died fifteen days later.
*   **Boniface VII (984–985):** Born with the family name Franco, he was initially substituted for the assassinated Benedict VI in 974 by the Roman barons and Crescentius. He was later ejected by Count Sicco, a representative of emperor Otto II, and fled to Constantinople. After Otto's death in 983, he returned, imprisoned Pope John XIV, and installed himself as pope from August 984 to July 985.
*   **Boniface VIII (1294–1303):** Born Benedetto Gaetano in Anagni, he was a student of civil and canon law. He served as a notary at the papal court and was once besieged in the Tower of London in 1267. As pope, he asserted the lordship of the papacy over all world kingdoms and engaged in conflicts with European powers, including Philip IV of France and Edward I of England. He died in 1303 following the outrage of Anagni by Guillaume de Nogaret.

## Saint Boniface (680–754)
Distinct from the bishops of Rome, Saint Boniface (born Wynfrith) was a Saxon from Devonshire known as the apostle of Germany. He studied theology and grammar at Exeter and Nutcell. In 716, he attempted a mission to Frisia but returned due to the hostility of King Radbod. 

In 719, Pope Gregory II commissioned him to evangelize Germany and counteract Irish monks. He was consecrated bishop on November 30, 722, after swearing obedience to the pope. With the protection of Charles Martel, he conducted a systematic crusade in heathen Germany, baptizing converts, founding monasteries and churches, and overturning idols. He was created archbishop in 732 and later became a legate for Pope Zacharias in 741, tasked with reforming the Frankish church.

Boniface organized the church in Bavaria into four bishoprics (Passau, Salzburg, Freising, and Regensburg) and the east Frankish church into four others (Eichstadt, Buraburg, Wurzburg, and Erfurt). He founded the abbey of Fulda and presided over the first German council in 742. His career included a theological dispute with Virgil, the Irish bishop of Salzburg, and a conflict in Neustria with a Frankish bishop named Adalbert, who claimed to receive relics directly from angels.
