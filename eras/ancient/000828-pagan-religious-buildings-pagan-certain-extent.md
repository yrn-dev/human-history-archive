# Paganism and Related Religious Structures

The term paganism refers to various ancient religious systems and beliefs across different cultures and eras, ranging from the classical world of Greece and Rome to the early inhabitants of Arabia and Norway. The transition from these beliefs to newer religious frameworks, such as Christianity and Islam, often involved long periods of overlap, where ancient traditions continued to influence national life and intellectual development.

## Classical Paganism and the Transition to Christianity

In the Greco-Roman world, the transition from pagan antiquity to the Christian middle ages was a gradual process. While the reign of Constantine the Great marked a new period in national life, ancient traditions persisted for centuries. The final remnants of paganism did not disappear until the 6th and 7th centuries. A notable example of this continuity was the university of Athens, the last great educational establishment based on pagan foundations, which remained active until A.D. 529.

During this transitional period, the "Hellenizing" of the state and the seat of empire proceeded slowly; the first purely Greek emperor was Tiberius II (578-582), and the complete Hellenization of the state's character was not finished until the 7th century. Despite the rise of Christianity, the old Hellenic religion retained a transformed life, influencing the new creed's religious terminology and hieratic organization.

## Concepts and Beliefs in Pagan Faith

Pagan faith in the Grecian world included the concept of dæmons. Initially introduced as essentially good intermediate agents between gods and men, the doctrine of dæmons evolved. Later philosophers, including Empedoclês, Xenocratês, and to some extent Plato, admitted the existence of dæmons with defective or malignant characters. These beings were used to explain phenomena without attributing physical or moral evils to the gods, and were also used to justify objectionable ceremonies as a means of appeasing malignant beings. Over time, the term "dæmon" shifted in meaning to signify an evil being, a distinction later utilized by Christian writers to identify pagan gods as evil dæmons.

In contrast to certain periods of Christianity, which maintained notions of future retribution and life after death, paganism was characterized by the absence of such beliefs. Additionally, during the intellectual peak of Greece and Rome, a scientific understanding of nature limited the scope of religious legends. However, as pagan literature and philosophy declined before the 6th century, a more simple and naïve religious interpretation of nature returned.

## Paganism in Other Regions

Paganism also manifested in other global contexts:

*   **Arabia:** In the primitive Arab world, Mecca served as a stronghold for the old pagan cult, which contributed to the city's prosperity and importance. This paganism existed alongside other influences, as the region had seen proselytizing during the periods of the Herods and Maccabeans in Judea, and the Yemen had been ruled by Zoroastrians, Christians, and Jews.
*   **Norway:** In early Norway, the eating of horse-flesh during religious festivals was viewed in later times as direct proof of paganism. This ceremony was believed to commemorate ancestors and an Asiatic origin. These practices were later punished by death or mutilation by Saint Olaf.

## Political Interaction and Decline

In the Roman Empire, the decline of paganism was marked by a complex relationship with the state. Under Emperor Theodosius, Pagan subjects were not forced by positive law to immediately embrace Christianity, nor was the profession of Christianity a requirement for civil rights. The senate, army, schools, and palace remained filled with devout Pagans who received military and civil honors. Figures such as Libanius and Symmachus were allowed to maintain their religious opinions.

However, while personal belief was tolerated, the Imperial laws prohibiting pagan ceremonies and sacrifices were rigidly executed. This targeted the public exercise of worship, which was the primary foundation of the religion's influence. By the time of the poet Claudian, the "visible extinction" of the old religion was occurring, though his works often displayed a religious impartiality toward both Christians and Pagans.

## Religious Architecture in Pagan and Successor Cities

In the context of the Burman empire, the city of Pagan served as a predecessor to the capital of Ava. Ava was founded in A.D. 1364 by Thadomin Pay. The religious buildings of Pagan were reproduced in Ava to a certain extent, although they were not constructed on the same scale of splendour or size. Over time, many of these religious edifices in Ava became dilapidated.
