# Use of Accents in Ancient Manuscripts and Linguistics

In the study of ancient manuscripts and linguistic structures, accents serve various functions, ranging from aids for reading unfamiliar dialects to markers of phonetic stress and calendar notation.

## Accents in Greek Manuscripts
The use of accents in ancient Greek manuscripts often served as a practical tool for the reader. In the Bacchylides manuscript, the punctuation is described as very sparse and not always authoritative. Scribes frequently omitted or incorrectly placed signs denoting the end of an ode (asterisk), an epode (coronis), or a strophe or antistrophe (paragraphus).

However, the supply of accents in certain manuscripts is notably comprehensive. As observed by Kenyon, no manuscript of equal antiquity is as well supplied with accents as the Bacchylides MS. The Alcman fragment in the Louvre, which dates perhaps to the early part of the 1st century A.D., is the closest in this regard. In the Alcman fragment, the comparatively frequent accents were doubtless designed to aid readers who were unfamiliar with Alcman's Laconian Doric.

This approach aligns with the Alexandrian practice, where accents were regarded as aids to correct reading. Under this system, accents were not applied to all words, but generally only to those that might cause difficulty or doubt for the reader. This practice was employed more liberally when the dialect being used was not Attic. Following the older system, an accent was not written on the final syllable of a word; if the accent fell there, a grave accent was instead placed on the preceding syllable or on two such syllables (for example, *pauthales* or *blechras*).

## Phonetics and Stress-Accent in Bihari
In the study of phonetics, specifically regarding the Bihari language, the stress-accent follows the rules of modern Indo-Aryan vernaculars. In words containing more than one syllable, the accent cannot fall on the last syllable, regardless of whether the vowel is short, long, pronounced, half-pronounced, or not pronounced. Aside from this restriction, the accent consistently falls on the last long syllable.

In instances where a word contains no long syllables, the accent is shifted back as far as possible, though it never moves further than the syllable before the antepenultimate. Examples of this include *pa'ni*, *ha-m^a-ra*, and *ki-sa'-n(a)*. In some cases, a secondary accent may occur; for example, in the word *de-kh^a-la-h^u*, a secondary accent appears on the penultimate syllable due to the following imperfect vowel. Similarly, if the first syllable does not hold the main stress-accent, it takes a secondary one, as seen in *de-kh^a-li-ai-nh'*. 

Furthermore, when the letter *a* follows a syllable with either a primary or secondary accent, it is only half pronounced. In Mth. (though not in Bh. or Mg.), a final short *u* or *i* is often very lightly pronounced. In such cases, the preceding syllable receives a secondary accent if it does not already possess the main one.

## Other Applications of Accents and Prosody
Outside of ancient manuscripts and phonetics, accents and similar markers are used in other specialized contexts:

*   **Church Calendars:** In tables of the church calendar, epacts are typically printed in Roman numerals. However, in certain tables, a distinction is made by placing an accent over the last figure of the number 25, which is expressed in Arabic characters in the last eight columns.
*   **Prosody and Assonance:** In prosodics, assonance is defined as the riming or corresponding of one word with another in the accented vowel and the sounds that follow it, excluding the consonants. This is considered an imperfect form of rhyme where the ear is satisfied by the identity of the vowel without the aid of consonants, a feature common in much popular or rustic English verse.
