# Marcionites

The Marcionites were a religious group whose churches reached their golden age between the years 150 and 250. During this period, they were considered a significant danger to the great Church because they maintained certain genuine Christian ideas that the Catholic Church had forgotten. Knowledge of their doctrines and history from the 2nd to the 7th century is derived primarily from the controversial works of Catholic fathers, including Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Epiphanius, Pseudo-Origen Adamantius, and the Armenian Esnik.

## Beliefs and Internal Divisions
Common to all Marcionites were the rejection of the Old Testament, the recognition of a "new God," and the practice of rigorous asceticism. However, the disciples of Marcion diverged in several directions regarding their first principles:
* One group accepted three first principles: the good, the just, and the evil.
* Another group held to two first principles, identifying the Demiurge as the god of evil or the devil.
* A third party, including Apelles—the most distinguished pupil of Marcion—viewed the Demiurge as an apostate angel of the good God, thereby returning to monotheism.

## Organizational Structure and Rituals
Like the Catholics, Marcionites lived in close communities governed by presbyters and bishops, though their original constitution was loose to avoid any appearance of "legality." 

Regarding their rites, Tertullian testifies that the Marcionites observed specific particulars of the baptismal rite that date back at least to the year 150. These rites were later mirrored in the 4th century by the orthodox churches of both the East and West.

## Historical Progression and Influence
The Marcionites were active in various regions, including the field of Great Armenia. Their presence is evidenced by archaeological finds, such as the earliest known inscription on a Christian place of worship (A.D. 318), which was Marcionite and discovered on a stone over the doorway of a house in a Syrian village.

The decline of the Marcionites occurred in stages:
* **The West:** They began to die out from the beginning of the 4th century, often falling prey to Manichaeism.
* **The East:** They survived much longer than in the West, though many also transitioned to Manichaeism. They can be traced until the 7th century, after which they seem to vanish.

Despite their disappearance, Marcionite impulses contributed to the rise of new sects, specifically the Bogomils and the Paulicians. Consequently, the antinomian and anticlerical sects of the 13th century, as well as the western Cathari, may be included in the history of Marcionitism where they are connected to these groups.

## Relation to Catholic Orthodoxy
The influence of the Marcionites is reflected in the development of Catholic symbols. For example, the so-called Apostles' Creed given in Rome was originally compiled in its current form specifically to exclude the Marcionites.
