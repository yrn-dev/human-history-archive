# Antipater and the Espousals of the Orphans

## The Hatred of the Nation
Antipater was widely hated by the nation of the Jews, a sentiment stemming primarily from the slaughter of his brothers, Alexander and Aristobulus. The deaths of these two men were the result of accusations brought by Herod in an assembly of the people, which led to the captains and Tero being killed by stones and pieces of wood. Herod further ordered that his sons be strangled in the city of Sebaste, eventually burying them in the fortress Alexandrium with their maternal grandfather, Alexander.

Because of his role in these events, the public pitied the orphans of Alexander and Aristobulus and remembered the deceased brothers with great affection. This widespread hatred of Antipater was so profound that it provided him with a form of security; during a period of seven months when accusations were laid against him in Judea, he remained unaware of them because no one was willing to risk themselves to provide him with information.

## Influence Over Herod and the Court
Despite the public's animosity, Antipater managed to secure a position of absolute power within Herod's court. He became "all in all" to the king, as Herod's natural affection for the young men of his family diminished and his favor toward Antipater increased. This influence extended to the courtiers, the king's brethren, and his children, many of whom inclined toward Antipater either by their own accord or by the king's injunction. Ptolemy, the dearest friend of the king, was among those who followed this conduct.

Antipater's mother also wielded significant power, providing counsel against the queen's sons and exhibiting a hatred for them that exceeded the typical animosity toward sons-in-law. To maintain this dominance, Herod commanded his intimate friends to ignore and avoid Alexander and his associates. Antipater further isolated the young men by turning their uncle Pheroras and their aunt Salome against them, frequently irritating Salome through constant conversation.

## The Espousals and Antipater's Interference
In an effort to show commiseration for the fatherless children of Alexander and Aristobulus, Herod sought to integrate them into the family through strategic marriages. He intended for the elder son of Alexander to marry the daughter of Pheroras, and for the eldest son of Aristobulus to marry Antipater's daughter. Additionally, one of Aristobulus's daughters was allotted to Antipater's son, while the other was betrothed to a son of Herod born to the daughter of the high priest. Herod hoped these intermarriages would encourage Antipater to be kind to the children.

However, Antipater viewed these arrangements as a threat to his own future government. He feared that if the orphans were supported by Archelaus (a king) and Pheroras (a tetrarch), he would risk losing power. He was also provoked by the fact that the multitude's pity for the orphans highlighted his own "vile disposition" toward his brothers. Consequently, Antipater worked to dissolve these espousals. He successfully persuaded Herod to change his resolutions, rearranging the marriages to better serve his own advantage. Under the new determination, Antipater himself was to marry the daughter of Aristobulus.

## Political Maneuvers and Security
To offset the hatred he faced at home, Antipater sought favor with powerful figures in Rome and Syria, including Saturninus, the President of Syria, and various governors, by providing them with many presents. He used his friends in Rome to procure letters accusing Archelaus and Philip of calumniating their father regarding the slaughter of Alexander and Aristobulus. While Antipater wrote to Herod presenting these accusations as severe, he simultaneously excused the young men by attributing their words to their youth. To further his standing with great men, he spent two hundred talents on splendid ornaments as presents.

As Antipater's expectation of the kingdom grew, his behavior became "intolerable." Unable to escape the hatred of the people, he instead relied on the terror he struck into them for his security. He formed a private alliance with Pheroras, though they maintained a public facade of opposition to deceive the king. This alliance included a circle of impudent women in the palace, including Pheroras's wife and Antipater's mother, who domineered over others. Their secret meetings and cabals were only opposed by Salome, who informed Herod of their activities.
