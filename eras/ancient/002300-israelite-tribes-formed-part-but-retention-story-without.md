# The Israelite Tribes

The Israelites were organized into a system of tribes, several of which are detailed in historical and scriptural records regarding their territories, migrations, and military strength. These tribes formed part of a wider circle of connected tribes who recognized Yahweh as their deity.

## Tribal Territories and Migrations

The distribution of the Israelite tribes across Palestine and surrounding regions involved both settlement and movement. The tribe of Dan, named after a son of Jacob and Bilhah, originally held seats at Kirjath-jearim, Eshtaol, and Zorah, located west of Jerusalem. However, they were forced to seek a new home and eventually took Laish by the sword, renaming the city Dan. This location served as the northern limit of the land. In this new seat, a sanctuary was established under the guardianship of Jonathan, the grandson of Moses, which lasted until either the fall of Shiloh or the "captivity of the land" caused by Tiglath-Pileser IV in 733-732.

The tribe of Asher is associated with a district west of Galilee known to the Egyptians as Aser(u) during the time of Rameses II and Seti I in the latter half of the 14th century B.C. There are two primary theories regarding this presence: either Asher was a tribe that separated from its brethren in Egypt to migrate north, or the district was a region regarded as part of the national kingdom and incorporated into the genealogy as a "son" of Jacob. Some evidence suggests that members of the Israelite population from Asher may have followed the example of the tribe of Dan by moving from an earlier southern home.

Other tribal territories included those of the Reubenites, Gadites, and the half tribe of Manassites, who occupied the eastern parts of the country beyond the Jordan. This region included Bashan and Gilead. Bashan is described as a region north of Gilead toward the source of the Jordan. Hebrew tradition records that the tribes passed through Bashan after the defeat of Sihon, king of the Amorites, where they encountered and slew King Og at Edrei.

## Military Strength and Governance

Historical accounts provide specific data on the military capabilities of the Israelites. During the reign of King David, a census was conducted by Joab and the heads of the tribes. Excluding the tribes of Levi and Benjamin, the total number of Israelites capable of bearing arms and going to war was nine hundred thousand men. Of this total, the tribe of Judah alone accounted for four hundred thousand men.

The governance of the Israelites varied over time. In one period, King Jehu reigned over the Israelites for twenty-seven years before being buried in Samaria and succeeded by his son, Jehoahaz. Jehu's reign was marked by a failure to defend the eastern territories—specifically those of the Gadites, Reubenites, and half-tribe of Manassites—against Hazael, the king of Syria, who spoiled and burned the region. Jehu is characterized as a despiser of holiness, the laws, and religion.

## Conflict and Conquest

The Israelites engaged in significant warfare to secure their presence in Palestine. Following the death of Joshua, two tribes fought against the Canaanites at the city of Bezek. They defeated a great army and captured Adonibezek, the "Lord of Bezek," who had previously treated seventy-two kings with the same violence (cutting off fingers and toes) that the Israelites inflicted upon him. After taking the lower city of Jerusalem and slaying its inhabitants, the Israelites faced great difficulty capturing the upper city due to the strength of its walls.

Additionally, the occupation of Shechem is linked to the Israelites. While some accounts suggest the land was purchased from the sons of Hamor by Jacob, others point to a forced capture. The story of Dinah, daughter of Leah, suggests an early settlement in the vicinity, involving a treacherous massacre of Canaanite inhabitants by the tribes of Levi and Simeon, which subsequently led to the scattering of the latter.

## Religious and Prophetic Traditions

The religious history of the Israelites includes the recognition that Yahweh revealed his will to nations beyond Israel. This is evidenced by the narratives of Balaam, a non-Hebrew prophet whose inspiration was recognized by the J and E sources. This tradition suggests that Yahweh was the God of Balaam as well as the God of Moses. While the Priestly Code appears to have ignored this feature, the retention of the Balaam story implies a continuous recognition over several centuries that divine revelation was not exclusive to the Israelites.
