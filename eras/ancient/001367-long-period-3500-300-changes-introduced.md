# Religious and Cultural Developments from c. 3500 to c. 300 B.C.

The period spanning approximately 3500 to 300 B.C. was characterized by the evolution of religious systems in the Euphrates valley and the development of ancient Greek literature. In the Babylonian-Assyrian context, this era saw the stabilization of cultic practices and the rise of a complex astral theology.

## Babylonian-Assyrian Religious Periods
The development of religion in Babylonia and Assyria is categorized into four distinct periods. The oldest period extends from approximately 3500 B.C. until the time of Khammurabi (c. 2250 B.C.). This was followed by the post-Khammurabic period in Babylonia. The third phase is the Assyrian period, which lasted from approximately 2000 B.C. until the destruction of Nineveh in 606 B.C. The final phase is the neo-Babylonian period, initiated by Nabopolassar (625-604 B.C.), the first independent ruler of a new era of prosperity. This political independence ended with the conquest of Babylon and Babylonia by Cyrus in 539 B.C. However, because the religion continued undisturbed for several centuries after this political shift, the period is often extended to 331 B.C., the date of the Greek conquest of the Euphrates valley, after which new influences led to the gradual extinction of the old cults.

## Stability of Cults and Astral Theology
Following the adjustment to new conditions created by Khammurabi's union of the Euphratean states, the changes introduced were of a minor character. Despite a general tendency toward centralization, local cults in the north and south maintained themselves. While these cults varied based on the specific gods worshipped in each center, they shared the same general principles, and their rites differed only in minor details.

A primary factor in maintaining this stability was the predominance of astral theology, which served as the theoretical substratum for the religious systems of both Babylonia and Assyria. This theology assumed a close link between earthly occurrences and movements in the heavens. This belief led to the identification of gods and goddesses with heavenly bodies, including the sun, moon, planets, and stars, and the assignment of the seats of all deities within the heavens.

## Evolution of the Astral System
The development of the astral system began with the personification of the moon and the sun (Sin and Shamash). Subsequently, other deities were placed where these two luminaries held their seats. This process reached its culmination during the post-Khammurabic period, resulting in the identification of specific planets with specific deities:
*   **Jupiter** was identified with Marduk.
*   **Venus** was identified with Ishtar.
*   **Mars** was identified with Nergal.
*   **Mercury** was identified with Nebo.
*   **Saturn** was identified with Ninib.

This system combined two distinct factors. The first was of popular origin: a belief that the movements of heavenly bodies influenced earthly events, a concept naturally suggested by the human dependence on the sun and moon for guidance, vegetation, and life. The second factor was the result of speculation within the schools attached to Babylonian temples, where priests developed the theory of close correspondence between celestial phenomena and earthly occurrences.

## Ancient Greek Literary Periods
Parallel to these developments in the East, ancient Greek literature evolved through three primary stages:
1.  **The Early Literature:** Extending to approximately 475 B.C., this period included lyric, iambic, elegiac, and epic poetry, as well as the beginnings of literary prose.
2.  **The Attic Literature:** Spanning from 475 to 300 B.C., this era was characterized by philosophical, oratorical, and historical prose, as well as comic and tragic drama.
3.  **The Literature of the Decadence:** Beginning in 300 B.C. and lasting until A.D. 529, this period is further divided into the Graeco-Roman period (146 B.C. to A.D. 529) and the Alexandrian period (300-146 B.C.).
