# Arabia: Geography, Fauna, and History

## Geographical Surveys and Mapping
By the close of the 19th century, western Asia had been freed from all geographical perplexity, with the exception of Arabia. In contrast, other regions such as Mongolia, China, and eastern Siberia continued to be characterized by a primitive stage of nebulous uncertainty regarding their geography. 

Efforts to improve the accuracy of Asiatic geographical surveys were supported by extensions of the Indian triangulation. These systems pushed outwards in all directions, reaching through Baluchistan and Afghanistan to the eastern districts of Persia, and extending along the coast of Makran to the coast of Arabia. These extensions provided the necessary basis for a vast amount of exploratory survey of a strictly geographical nature, raising the standard of accuracy to levels previously deemed unattainable fifty years prior.

## Natural History and Fauna
Arabia is situated within the palaeotropical region, specifically within the Ethiopian subregion, which encompasses Madagascar and the whole of Africa (excluding the Barbary States). In the northeast, this subregion merges into the Palaearctic.

The fauna of Arabia is characterized by the presence of Ethiopian types, which occupy the desert districts of Arabia and Syria. This includes various species of jerboa and other desert-loving forms, as well as the hunting leopard and the lion. In terms of avian life, the Ethiopian type is evidenced by a prevalence of stone chats and larks, and a complete absence of the peculiar genera found in the Indian region. Specific birds found in Arabia include:
*   **Ratitae:** Specifically *Struthio*.
*   **Carpodacus githagineus:** A species of bird native to the borders or deserts of North Africa and Arabia, extending to some of the Canary Islands.

## Ancient Maritime and Trade Routes
Arabia was a focal point for ancient navigation and the distribution of goods into Europe and Western Asia. The vessels of Tyre conducted distant navigation along the Arabian coast and through the Red Sea during the era of Solomon and David. 

The distribution of Arabian and Indian products was managed by several groups, as the Persians, Indians, Assyrians, and Egyptians did not pursue a seafaring life. These intermediaries included:
*   The Arabs of Gerrha on the Persian gulf, who were later joined by Chaldæan exiles from Babylonia.
*   The Idumæan Arabs located between the Red Sea and Petra.
*   The Phenicians of Sidon and Tyre.

Additionally, historical accounts mention a nautical enterprise commanded by Skylax, a Greek from Karyanda. Under the direction of Darius, Skylax is said to have led vessels from the territory of the Paktyes and the city of Kaspatyri, down the Indus, into the Indian Ocean, around the peninsula of Arabia, and up the Red Sea to Egypt.

## Political and Social History
In the period surrounding the war about Actium, Cleopatra plotted against the kings of Arabia and Judea. She persuaded Antony to assign the war against the Arabians to Herod. Her objective was to ensure that if Herod were successful, she would become the mistress of Arabia; if he failed, she would gain Judea, thereby destroying one king by the other.

Social and cultural history also records the life of the Arabian poet A'sha (Maimun ibn Qais). Born in the village of Manfuha in al-Yemama, located in the center of Arabia, A'sha was a wandering singer who traveled from al-Hira in the north to Hadramut in the south. He frequently attended the annual fair at Okaz. A monotheist who believed in the last judgment and resurrection—beliefs possibly influenced by the `Ibadites of al-Hira and the bishop of Nejran—A'sha is known for his poems praising wine, the wild ass, and the prophet Mahomet.

Furthermore, records indicate that Arabia was a source of specialized knowledge in pharmacology. In one instance, the mother and sister of Antipater brought a woman from Arabia who was skillful in mixing drugs to prepare a potion for Pheroras.
