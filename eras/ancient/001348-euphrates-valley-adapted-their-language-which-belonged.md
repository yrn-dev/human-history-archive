# The Euphrates Valley

The Euphrates valley has served as a significant center for political development, religious evolution, and the advancement of writing systems. Its history is characterized by the interaction between non-Semitic settlers and later Semitic populations, as well as the rise of centralized states.

## Language and Writing Systems
The cuneiform script, the writing system of the region, originated with the non-Semitic settlers of the Euphrates valley, commonly referred to as Sumero-Akkadians or Sumerians. This script and the general Sumerian culture were later adopted by the Babylonians upon their settlement in the valley. The Babylonians adapted the script to their own language, which belonged to the Semitic group.

The resulting Babylonian-Assyrian syllabary utilized a double method of writing. Words could be written "ideographically," using a sign or combination of signs to represent an entire word, or they could be spelled out phonetically in syllables. In the phonetic method, signs representing Sumerian words—which were largely monosyllabic—were treated merely as syllables without reference to their original meaning to spell Babylonian words. Over time, this system was enlarged and modified as the culture spread north to Assyria. For example, the Sumerian sign MU for "name" was read as *shumu* in Semitic texts, and the Sumerian SIG-BA for "clothing" was read as *lubushtu*.

## Political and Religious Evolution
The religious development of Babylonia closely followed the political history of the Euphrates valley. In the pre-Khammurabic age, the region was divided into various states, each with its own religious center. These centers included Agade, Eridu, Erech, Kutha, Nippur, Sippara, Shirgulla, and Ur. In each city, a chief deity—usually associated with a female consort—held jurisdiction limited to the political extent of that district. While some attempts were made in Ur and Nippur to group these deities into a regular pantheon, these efforts were not enduring.

A new epoch began around 2250 B.C. when Khammurabi effected the union of the Euphratean states. Under Khammurabi, Babylon became the permanent political center of the united states of the Euphrates valley. Correspondingly, Khammurabi pursued a policy of raising Marduk, the patron deity of Babylon, to the head of the entire Babylonian pantheon.

## The Deity Marduk
Marduk's rise to the head of the pantheon reflected the political shift toward Babylon. Originally a solar deity personifying the springtime sun that conquers winter storms, Marduk was viewed as the god who triumphs over the chaos that existed at the beginning of time. 

As Babylon's political influence grew, Marduk absorbed the traits and powers of earlier head deities, specifically Bel and Ea. The transfer of power from Ea to Marduk is described as pacific; Marduk is viewed as the son of Ea, and the father voluntarily handed over the control of humanity to him. This relationship may reflect the spread of culture in the Euphrates valley from south to north, recognizing Eridu as the older center. Evidence of a cult of Marduk at Eridu suggests that Babylon paid homage to this old settlement located at the head of the Persian Gulf.

## Geography and the Vilayet of Bagdad
In more recent history, much of ancient Babylonia was included in the vilayet of Bagdad, a province of Asiatic Turkey situated between the Syrian desert and Persia. Historically, this vilayet was the largest province of the Turkish empire from the mid-17th century until the mid-19th century, though it later lost territory to the creation of the Basra and Mosul vilayets.

The geography of the region between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers varies by latitude. North of Bagdad, the land is an arid limestone steppe inhabited by roving Arabs. South of Bagdad, the land consists of alluvial soil deposited by the rivers. While this soil possesses great potential for fertility, the terrain is absolutely flat and subject to inundations during flood seasons, leaving the surroundings of Bagdad under water. During other times of the year, the region can become a parched desert or a series of lagoons and swamps. The fertility of the valley is heavily dependent on irrigation canals, the neglect of which has turned a large portion of the vilayet into desert.
