# Beginning of the Peloponnesian War and the Invasions of Attica

The Peloponnesian War began following a period of tension and suspicion between the Athenians and the Lacedaemonians. Initial disputes arose from affairs at Corcyra and Epidamnus. Although the Athenians, following the advice of Pericles, had previously voted to settle complaints through the legal methods prescribed by their truce, communication eventually ceased except through heralds. Hostilities then commenced and were prosecuted without intermission.

## The Peloponnesian Alliance and Strategy
The Peloponnesian alliance was a powerful confederacy, larger than any assembled even to resist Xerxes. It included the entire strength of the Peloponnesus—with the exception of the Achæans and Argeians, who were initially neutral, though the Achæan town of Pellênê joined at the start. The alliance also comprised the Bœotians, Phocians, Opuntian Lokrians, Ambrakiots, Leukadians, and Anaktorians. 

The maritime force was furnished by cities including Leukas, Ambrakia, Elis, Pellênê, Sikyon, Megara, and Corinth. Cavalry was supplied by the Lokrians, Phocians, and Bœotians. The Peloponnesians relied heavily on their hoplites and the Bœotian cavalry for their primary operation: the devastation of Attica. Driven by a common antipathy toward Athens and a desire for plunder, the confederacy hoped that ravaging the richest country in Greece would provoke the Athenians into a battle or force their submission. There was a general belief among the Peloponnesians that Athens could not endure more than two or three summers of such invasions.

## The First and Second Invasions of Attica
The first year of the war resulted in an unaltered position of relative strength. While the Peloponnesians invaded Attica and the Athenians conducted flying descents along the Peloponnesian coast, no decisive progress was made. The Peloponnesians failed to achieve their most pressing goal: the relief of Potidæa, as the Athenians maintained their blockade of the city.

For the second invasion, which began around the end of March or beginning of April, the Peloponnesian force—consisting of two-thirds of the men from each confederate city—assembled under the command of Archidamus. This campaign was more systematic than the first; the invaders ravaged the eastern and western coasts, the Thriasian plain, the area immediately surrounding Athens, and the mines of Laurium. They remained in the country for forty days.

In accordance with the defensive policy recommended by Pericles, the Athenian population retired within the city walls, leaving the territory deserted. While the citizens eventually acquiesced to this necessity, their suffering was aggravated shortly after Archidamus entered Attica by the unexpected outbreak of a pestilence or epidemic sickness.

## The Plague and Subsequent Campaigns
The epidemic sickness caused great suffering during the second and third years of the war, not relaxing materially until the winter of the third year. This calamity enfeebled Athenian military efforts, even as the maritime reputation of Athens was elevated by the victories of Phormio. During the second invasion, the plague killed men both in Athens and within the armament. It is asserted that the Peloponnesians' departure was hastened by fear of the disorder after they observed burials and heard reports from deserters.

In the third year, the Peloponnesians did not repeat their annual march into Attica, deterred by the raging epidemic and the desire of the Thebans to seek revenge on Platæa. However, in the fourth year, Archidamus again led the Peloponnesians into Attica around the beginning of May, continuing the devastation until their provisions were exhausted. This occurred simultaneously with the revolt of the greater part of Lesbos and Mitylênê.

By the seventh year of the war, the invasion of Attica had become an ordinary annual enterprise, though the initial hopes for a quick victory had faded. In the spring of that year, King Agis of Sparta conducted the army into the territory around the end of April to repeat the usual ravages.

## Long-term Impact on Athens
The repeated invasions and the eventual occupation of Decelea by the enemy severely impacted Athens. The transport of provisions from Euboea, previously handled quickly overland via Decelea from Oropus, had to be rerouted by sea around Sunium at great cost. Athens ceased to function as a city and became a fortress, with citizens worn out by constant guard duty on the fortifications.

These pressures, combined with the financial burden of maintaining a siege of Syracuse in Sicily while simultaneously being besieged in Attica, led to financial embarrassment. To compensate for decaying revenues, the Athenians replaced the tribute imposed on their subjects with a tax of a twentieth on all maritime imports and exports. Despite predictions that Athens would fall within three years of the first invasion, the city continued to fight for seventeen years after that initial attack.
