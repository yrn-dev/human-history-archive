# GRECIAN AFFAIRS AFTER THE EXPULSION OF THE PEISISTRATIDS

The expulsion of the Peisistratid dynasty in 510 B.C. marked the end of a fifty-year period of rule in Athens that began with the first usurpation of Peisistratus in 560 B.C. The fall of the dynasty was achieved through the intervention of foreign powers, specifically Sparta, and led to the liberation of Athens and the subsequent establishment of democracy through the revolution of Kleisthenes.

## The Fall of the Peisistratids
The final years of the Peisistratid government were characterized by the rule of Hippias. Following the conspiracy of 514 B.C., in which Harmodius and Aristogeitôn killed Hipparchus, Hippias became the sole despot. His remaining four years of rule (514–510 B.C.) were marked by cruelty and a sense of conscious insecurity.

The process of his removal was initiated by the exiled Alkmæônids. After the Delphian temple was destroyed by fire, the Alkmæônids rebuilt it with magnificence. In gratitude, the Delphians provided them with directions from the oracle to seek help from Sparta to expel Hippias. Although the Peisistratids had previously been "particular guests" of the Spartans and maintained a close intimacy with them, the Spartans acted in reluctant obedience to these oracles. Following Spartan expeditions into Attica, Hippias was expelled in 510 B.C.

## Public Sentiment and Historical Memory
The expulsion of Hippias was welcomed unanimously by the vast majority of Athenians. To commemorate the "past iniquity" of the dethroned dynasty, an altar and a column were erected in the acropolis listing the names of all the dynasty's members. Hippias, his family, and his chief partisans went into exile.

In the subsequent democracy, a significant historical mistake persisted regarding the liberation of the city. Many Athenians, including philosophers and poets, believed that the conspiracy of Harmodius and Aristogeitôn in 514 B.C. had actually deposed the government. This belief was immortalized in a popular song (Skolion) celebrating the two friends as the authors of liberty who "slew the despot and gave to Athens equal laws." Consequently, Harmodius and Aristogeitôn were revered as protomartyrs of liberty; statues were erected in their honor, and their descendants were granted immunity from public burdens and taxes. This public misconception also led to the belief that Hipparchus, rather than Hippias, had been the predominant member and reigning despot of the family.

## Relations with Sparta and Macedonia
The relationship between Athens and Sparta shifted dramatically following the expulsion. While Sparta had provided the aid necessary to remove the Peisistratids, later tensions arose. The Spartan leader Kleomenês eventually became incensed upon discovering that the Delphian injunctions used to expel Hippias had been fraudulently procured. Furthermore, while shut up in the Athenian acropolis with Isagoras, Kleomenês discovered prophecies stored by the Peisistratids that foreshadowed disastrous events for Sparta.

Despite these later hostilities, the act of dethroning the Peisistratids remained a point of historical reference. In later centuries, when Sparta sought Athenian aid against the aggrandizement of Thebes under Epaminondas, Spartan envoys reminded the Athenian assembly of this early assistance as a basis for renewed cooperation.

Regarding Macedonia, the archive notes that king Amyntas maintained friendly connections with the Peisistratid princes, whose power was partially sustained by mercenaries from the Strymon. This friendly sentiment continued between Amyntas's son, Alexander, and the Athenians after they were emancipated.

## Historiographical Notes
The history of this period is complicated by contradictory accounts. Thucydidês noted that even a century after these events, incorrect statements regarding the Peisistratids were in circulation among Athenians. The archive specifically identifies the authority of Andokidês (writing between 420–390 B.C.) as being of no value regarding the history of 510–480 B.C. His assertions are described as loose, confused, and unscrupulous, notably confounding the battle of Marathon with the repulse of Xerxes and inventing leadership roles for his great-grandfathers to create a "factitious analogy" for his own legal pleadings.
