# Babylon

Babylon, known in modern times as Hillah, is an ancient city situated on the left bank of the Euphrates, approximately 70 miles south of Bagdad. The city grew around the E-Saggila ("the house of the lofty head"), a temple dedicated to Bel-Merodach that was founded in pre-Semitic times. Because Merodach was the son of Ea, the culture god of Eridu (located near Ur on the Persian Gulf), it is possible that Babylon was established as a colony of Eridu. The town of Borsippa was adjacent to Babylon.

## Etymology and Names
The name "Babylon" is the Greek form of *Bab-ili* or *Babel*, meaning "the gate of the god." This is a Semitic translation of the original Sumerian name, *Ka-dimirra*. The god referenced in the name was likely the city's divine patron, Marduk (or Merodach). 

The city is associated with several other names and titles:
* **Su-anna**: A second name for the city which may have originally referred to a separate quarter or village. In later inscriptions, this is often represented ideographically as *E-ki*, though the meaning and pronunciation of this term are uncertain.
* **Din-tir**: One of the oldest names, frequently used by poets. In Sumerian, this signifies "the life of the forest," although a native lexicon translates it as "seat of life."
* **Uru-azagga**: A title meaning "the holy city," which was applied to Babylon as well as other cities in Babylonia.

Other interpretations of the name exist through foreign *Volksetymologie*. In Genesis xi. 9, the name is derived from *balbal*, meaning "to confound." Additionally, an inscription by the Kassite conqueror Gaddas uses the name *Ba-ba-lam*, suggesting a derivation from the Assyrian *babalu*, meaning "to bring."

## Marduk and Religious Context
Marduk served as the divine patron of Babylon. While the original meaning of his name is unknown, word-play has led to the interpretation "the son of the chamber," possibly referring to the sacred chamber of fate where he judged during the New Year's festival. Ideographically, he is described as the "strong and universal ruler" and "child of the day" (or "of the sun"), the latter of which alludes to his original solar character.

Marduk's consort was Sarpanit, whose name means "the shining" or "brilliant one," further referencing solar traits. Babylonian priests also playfully interpreted her name to mean "the seed-producing" (*zer* for seed and *banit* for producing), viewing her as the female counterpart to the creator of life and mankind. This punning etymology suggests the priests wished to see Sarpanit as a manifestation of the great mother-goddess Ishtar.

## Regional Geography
Babylon was located within the alluvial plain of Babylonia. This plain was called *Edin* (the Eden of Gen. ii.), though the term specifically referred to the "plain" on the western bank of the river used by Bedouins for pasturing the flocks of Babylonian masters. 

The broader region of southern Babylonia was known as *Kengi* ("the land") or *Kengi Sumer* ("the land of Sumer"). In contrast, northern Babylonia was known as *Urra* (or *Uri*), which originally perhaps meant "clayey soil" but came to signify the "highlands" or "upper country," while *kengi* denoted the "lowlands." 

Following the rise of Sargon's empire, the name *Akkad* (the Semitic form of *Agad[e]*) was extended to represent northern Babylonia. Consequently, the imperial title "Sumer and Akkad" eventually denoted the entirety of Babylonia. After the Kassite conquest, northern Babylonia was called *Kar-Duniyas* ("the wall of the god Duniyas"), named for a line of fortification.
