# Ascanius and the Julian Gens

## Identity and Lineage
In Roman legend, Ascanius is identified as the son of Aeneas. Depending on the tradition cited, his mother was either Creusa or Lavinia. According to the writings of Livy, tradition recognized two different sons of Aeneas who both bore the name Ascanius: one born to Aeneas's Trojan wife and the other to his Latin wife. 

Ascanius was also known by the names Iulus and Ilus. He is described as the grandson of Anchises and Venus. This divine ancestry was later emphasized by Gaius Julius Caesar, who constructed a temple to Venus Genetrix in his forum to honor this lineage.

## Early Life and the Founding of Alba Longa
According to the usual account, Ascanius accompanied his father, Aeneas, on the flight from Troy to Italy. Following the death of Aeneas, the government of Latium was entrusted to Lavinia because Ascanius was too young to assume the responsibility of leadership. After a period of thirty years, Ascanius departed from Lavinium and founded the city of Alba Longa.

## The Julian Gens
The Julian gens (or Iulia Gens), the patrician family to which Gaius Julius Caesar belonged, claimed a legendary descent from Iulus, the founder of Alba Longa. While this descent from Venus through Iulus provided a prestigious ancestry, the patrician status of the family had limited impact on Caesar's political career; specifically, it disqualified him from holding the office of the tribunate, a position he would have naturally sought as a leader of the popular party. Despite this, the Julii Caesares had attained the *nobilitas* associated with those who held great magistracies, evidenced by Caesar's father holding the praetorship and his uncle serving as consul in 91 B.C.

## Related Traditions and Divergent Lines
Historical and legendary accounts provide various perspectives on the names and roles associated with these figures. In the Trojan and Dardanian lines, a distinction is made between Ilus and Assaracus. Ilus is credited with founding the holy city of Ilium in the plain of Troy, and his line led to Laomedôn, Priam, and Hectôr. In contrast, the line of Assaracus led to Capys, Anchisês, and Æneas.

Other traditions offer differing roles for Ascanius. Some sources, such as Conôn and Mela, state that Ascanius served as the king of Ida following the departure of the Greeks. Additionally, there is mention of an *Ascanius portus* located between Kymê and Phokæ.
