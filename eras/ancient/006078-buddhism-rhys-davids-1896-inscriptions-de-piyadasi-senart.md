# Buddhism

Buddhism is a religious and philosophical tradition based on the teachings of the Buddha. The archive's records indicate that the Buddha consistently used the title "Arahat" to refer to himself, and his followers did not address him as "the Buddha."

## Life of the Buddha
The life of the Buddha is documented through various canonical Pali sources, which reached their current form before the 4th century B.C., as well as Buddhist Sanskrit texts. The Pali records include episodes such as his birth (found in the *Majjhima Nikaya* and *Anguttara Nikaya*), the "adoration of the babe" (recorded in the *Sutta Nipata*), and his youth at home.

Other significant phases of his life include "the going forth," his years of penance and study, and the eventual attainment of Buddhahood and Nirvana. Following this attainment, he converted his first five converts. His early teaching period is detailed in the *Vinaya*, including the end of the first year of teaching and a visit to Kapilavastu. The final days of the Buddha are recorded in the *Mahaparinibbana Suttanta* of the *Digha Nikaya*.

Additional accounts are found in Buddhist Sanskrit texts, including the *Mahavastu* (likely 2nd century B.C.), which covers events up to the end of the first year of teaching, and the *Lalita Vistara* (likely 1st century B.C.), which extends to the first sermon.

## Teachings and Practices
Buddhist training for the Bhikshu includes the practice of ecstatic meditation, a tradition adopted from earlier beliefs such as those found in the *Vedas*, the *Upanishads*, and ancient Yoga. These exercises, which involve being rapt from the outside world to acquire insight, are referred to as "Conditions of Bliss." 

Within the Buddhist framework, these states of rapture are viewed as useful tools for removing mental obstacles to the attainment of Arahatship. Arahatship consists of thirty-seven constituent parts, and the Conditions of Bliss comprise one group of four. However, the archive notes that seeking Arahatship through the practice of ecstasy alone is considered a "deadly heresy"; while these practices are pleasant and useful, they are not the ultimate end and can be omitted entirely.

Other aspects of the tradition include the doctrine of Karma and the "sermon on the lessons to be drawn from burning" (*aditta-pariyaya*).

## Institutional Development
The Buddhist community utilized retreats during the rainy season. These began as simple huts built for mendicants in groves of palm-trees but gradually grew in magnificence and splendor before the eventual decay of Buddhism.

The spread and history of the faith are further documented in the *Inscriptions de Piyadasi* and the edicts of Asoka (also pronounced Ashoka).

## Literary Sources and Scholarship
The archive contains a vast array of translated texts and scholarly manuals. Key canonical and non-canonical works include:
*   **The Vinaya Texts**: Including the *Vinaya* and *Vinaya Texts*.
*   **Suttas**: Such as the *Digha Nikaya*, *Majjhima Nikaya*, *Samyutta*, *Anguttara Nikaya*, and the *Sutta Nipata*.

Modern scholarly analysis has been provided by figures such as T.W. Rhys Davids, who authored *Buddhism*, *Buddhist India*, and *Dialogues of the Buddha*, as well as Mrs. Rhys Davids, who wrote *Buddhist Psychology*. Other contributors include Oldenberg, Kern, and Windisch, the latter of whom conducted a critical analysis of the legends of Mara.
