# BONA DEA

Bona Dea, meaning the "good goddess," was an ancient Roman deity associated with fruitfulness in both women and the earth. Through later syncretism, she was identified with Maia and Ops, as well as Fauna. Her temple on the Aventine had a dedication day of May 1st, a fact that likely contributed to her identification with Maia.

## Cult and Rituals
The cult of Bona Dea was characterized by its exclusivity and secrecy. The temple on the Aventine was attended and cared for by women only. Similarly, a second celebration took place at the beginning of December in the house of a magistrate possessing *imperium*. According to Cicero, the actual name of the goddess remained unknown to men, a secret that Roman ladies maintained effectively.

Certain items were tabooed within the cult, specifically myrtle and wine. The temple served as a repository for snakes—traditional symbols of the medicinal art—and herbs possessing healing properties. The ritual victim for the goddess was a *porca* (a sow), which was referred to as *damium*. This practice aligns with other fertility deity cults. Consequently, the goddess was known as Damia, and her priestess was titled *damiatrix*.

## Origins and Influence
The names Damia and *damiatrix* are almost certainly Greek. Damia was worshipped in various locations in Greece and at Tarentum, where a festival called *Dameia* was held. It is highly probable that the Greek cult of Damia was engrafted onto the original Roman goddess, possibly following the conquest of Tarentum in 272 B.C. Due to this blending, it is no longer possible to clearly distinguish the Roman elements from the Greek elements in the cult, though it remains intelligible as an Earth-goddess cult with associated mysteries.

## Historical Events and Profanation
The mysteries of Bona Dea became a subject of significant political and social noise in Rome due to an act of profanation by P. Clodius in 62 B.C. This event is detailed in the letters of Cicero to Atticus. In 61 B.C., the Roman general Quintus Fufius Calenus, acting as tribune of the people, was the primary figure instrumental in securing the acquittal of Publius Clodius regarding the charges of profaning these mysteries.

## Symbolic Associations
In the broader context of Roman and Greek beliefs, the snakes kept in the temple of Bona Dea were regarded as *sacri dracontes*. Unlike the oriental view of serpents as symbols of evil, these "sharp-eyed dwellers in the inner parts of the earth" were often conceived as beneficent powers, guardian genii, or entities capable of discovering the secrets of the earth and delivering them via oracles.
