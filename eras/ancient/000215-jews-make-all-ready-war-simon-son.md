# The Jews Make All Ready For The War; And Simon, The Son Of

## Preparations for War in Jerusalem
Following the cessation of civil dissensions in Galilee, the region turned its focus toward preparing for war with the Romans. In Jerusalem, the high priest Ananus and other powerful men who opposed Roman interests undertook the repair of the city walls and the mass production of warlike instruments. The city became a center of industry, with darts and various types of armor being forged on anvils throughout all parts of the city.

While the youth of the city engaged in exercises, these activities lacked regularity and were characterized by tumultuous behavior. This atmosphere of preparation was met with sadness and lamentations by the moderate citizens, who foresaw coming calamities. Omens were observed during this time; those who desired peace viewed them as precursors of evil, while those inciting the war interpreted them to suit their own goals. Ananus attempted to restrain the "zealots" and persuade the seditious to consider their own interests, but his efforts were overcome by their violence.

## The Military Leadership of Simon
In an earlier era of conflict, Simon, the brother of Judas and Jonathan, served as a general and high priest. Simon demonstrated courageous leadership in the management of public affairs, capturing the cities of Jamnia, Joppa, and Gazara. He successfully demolished the citadel and brought the garrison under his control.

Simon acted as an auxiliary to Antiochus in the siege of Trypho at Dora. Despite Simon's assistance in the death of Trypho, Antiochus later sent his general, Cendebeus, with an army to subdue Simon and devastate Judea. Despite his advanced age, Simon conducted the war with the vigor of a younger man. He employed strategic ambushes in the mountains and deployed his sons with a band of strong men to attack Antiochus from one quarter while Simon led another part of the army from a different direction. Following these victories, Simon freed the Israelites from Macedonian dominion, ending an empire that had lasted one hundred and seventy years.

## Conflict with Trypho
Simon's leadership was tested when Trypho entered Judea with a large army, bringing Simon's brother, Jonathan, in bonds. At the city of Adida, Trypho attempted to deceive Simon, demanding a hundred talents of silver and two of Jonathan's sons as hostages in exchange for Jonathan's release. Trypho claimed Jonathan was held due to debts owed to the king.

Simon recognized the offer as treacherous and believed that Trypho would neither release Jonathan nor return the hostages. However, fearing that the multitude would accuse him of failing to save his brother, Simon informed his army of the situation and decided to comply with the demands to avoid calumny. Despite receiving the money and the sons, Trypho broke his promise and did not free Jonathan. Simon subsequently tracked Trypho's army, pitching camp opposite him as Trypho moved toward Jerusalem via Idumea.

## The Siege and Roman Engines
During a later siege, a leader named Simon positioned himself near the site of the conflict. He deployed engines of war upon the walls, including those seized from Cestius and those taken from the garrison in the tower Antonia. However, the Israelites possessed little skill in operating these machines; only a few, taught by deserters, could use them, and even then, they did so awkwardly. They used these engines to cast arrows and stones at those building banks.

In contrast, the Roman legions utilized highly advanced engines, particularly those of the tenth legion. These machines threw stones weighing a talent that could travel two furlongs or further. The impact was devastating to both the front lines and those positioned further back. To survive, the Israelites utilized watchmen on towers who would shout "The Stone Cometh" in their own language upon the release of an engine, signaling the soldiers to throw themselves on the ground to avoid the white stones.

## Other Historical Figures Named Simon
The archive records other individuals named Simon. One was Onias, the son of Simon, a high priest who fled Antiochus of Syria and sought refuge with Ptolemy in Egypt. Onias built a temple in the region of Onion, allowing Israelites in Egypt to worship according to their customs.

Another Simon was a Jew born in Cyprus and a friend of Felix. This Simon claimed to be a magician and was described as wicked. He persuaded Drusilla, the sister of Agrippa junior and a Jewess, to leave her husband, King Azizus of Emesa, to marry Felix, the heathen procurator of Judea.
