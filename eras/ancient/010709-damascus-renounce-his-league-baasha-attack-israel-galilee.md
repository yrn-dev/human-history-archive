# The Conflict Between Asa of Judah and Baasha of Israel

## Political Context and the Fortification of Ramah
During the reign of Asa, king of Judah—who was the son (or possibly brother) of Abijah and grandson of Rehoboam—conflict arose with Baasha, the king of Israel. Baasha, who succeeded the house of Jeroboam after destroying Nadab, sought to pressure Judah by fortifying Ramah. This location was situated five miles north of Jerusalem. The presence of this army and the fortification of the city caused Asa significant concern regarding the potential mischief the enemy might inflict upon his country.

## The Alliance with Damascus
To counter the threat posed by Baasha, Asa sought external military assistance. He sent ambassadors to the king of the Damascenes (the king of Damascus), offering gold and silver to secure his aid. In his appeal, Asa reminded the king of Damascus of the friendship that had existed between their respective forefathers. This policy of seeking Syrian aid followed a precedent set by Asa's father, Abijah, who had previously made a league with Tab-Rimmon of Damascus to assist in wars against Israel.

The king of Damascus accepted the payment and entered into a league with Asa. As part of this agreement, he renounced his existing friendship and league with Baasha.

## The Invasion of Israel and Galilee
Following the agreement, the king of Damascus dispatched his military commanders to attack the cities under Baasha's dominion. These forces invaded and caused widespread destruction, burning and spoiling several cities, including Dan, Ijon, and Abelmain. 

This invasion of Israel and the region of Galilee forced Baasha to abandon his efforts at Ramah. He ceased the building and fortification of the site and returned immediately to assist his own people in the face of the Syrian distresses.

## Aftermath and Fortifications in Judah
Asa took advantage of Baasha's retreat by seizing the building materials that had been collected for the fortification of Ramah. He used these materials to construct two strong cities to the immediate north of Jerusalem: Geba and Mizpah. 

These fortifications ensured that Baasha had no further opportunity to make expeditions against Asa. Baasha subsequently died and was buried in the city of Tirzah, where he was succeeded by his son, Elah.

## Later Syrian-Israelite Relations
Hostilities between Syria and Israel continued long after the reign of Asa, lasting into the days of Ahab. During the reign of Omri, the king of Syria captured cities and established a merchant quarter in Samaria. Later, Ben-Hadad of Syria launched an unsuccessful attack on Israel at Aphek. 

In 843, Hazael assassinated Ben-Hadad to become king of Damascus. Hazael later attacked the borders of Israel to avenge the fact that Jehu, king of Israel, had paid tribute to Assyria. Later, during the reign of Ahaz of Judah, the king of Damascus, Rezon (also referred to as Rasun or Rezin), attacked Judah. This led Ahaz to seek the protection of Tiglath-Pileser III of Assyria. Tiglath-Pileser subsequently invaded Syria, reduced Damascus in 732, and seized all of Galilee and the Israelite territories beyond the Jordan from Pekah.
