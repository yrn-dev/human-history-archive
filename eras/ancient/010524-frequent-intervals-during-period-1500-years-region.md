# Strife and Lawlessness in the Border Regions

## Overview of Regional Instability
For a period of 1500 years, the region was characterized by frequent intervals of lawlessness and strife. This instability was marked by persistent disturbances, particularly along the borders, which remained volatile until the union of the crowns occurred in 1603.

## Roman Infrastructure and Control
During the early history of the region, the Romans established roads to maintain order and hold warlike populations in check. Watling Street, a Roman road, crossed the Cheviots at Brownhartlaw (1664 ft.), situated close to the camp of *Ad Fines*. This route was utilized to control the Gadeni and Otadeni to the north and the Brigantes to the south. Additionally, the Wheel Causeway served as another Roman road, passing into Scotland near the Liddel and North Tyne headwaters.

## Religious Foundations and Border Conflict
The 12th century saw the establishment of several significant religious institutions. On the English side, these included the cathedral of Carlisle, the priory church of Lindisfarne, and the abbeys of Alnwick and Hexham. On the Scottish side, the abbeys of Dryburgh, Melrose, Kelso, and Jedburgh were founded.

The region was later plunged into wars of independence and succession following the deaths of Margaret the Maid of Norway in 1290 and Alexander III in 1286. This era of conflict saw repeated assaults on Carlisle and Berwick. Notable battles during this period include:
* Halidon Hill (1333)
* Otterburn (1388)
* Nisbet (1402)
* Homildon (1402)
* Piperden (1435)
* Hedgeley Moor (1464)
* Flodden (1513)
* Solway Moss (1542)
* Ancrum Moor (1544)

## Family Feuds and Freebooters
Beyond large-scale warfare, the region suffered from raids and family feuds involving families such as the Grahams, Eliots, Armstrongs, Maxwells, and Johnstones. Some of the most serious of these encounters occurred at the Raid of Reidswire (1575), the combat at Dryfe Sands (1593), and Arkenholme (Langholm) in 1455.

The peace of the region was further threatened by mosstroopers and freebooters, who remained a perpetual menace until their suppression in the 17th century. To counter these threats and defend the kingdoms, castles were constructed at various points on both sides of the border.

## Later Conflicts and Destruction
In 1544 and 1545, English expeditions resulted in exceptional disaster, including the sack of numerous towns, the obliteration of Roxburgh, and the destruction of the four Scottish border abbeys. Another significant conflict occurred during the time of the Covenanters, specifically in 1645, when the marquess of Montrose suffered defeat at Philiphaugh.
