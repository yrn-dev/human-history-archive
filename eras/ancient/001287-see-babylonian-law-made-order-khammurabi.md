# BABYLONIAN LAW

The study of Babylonian law is supported by a singularly extensive, though not exhaustive, body of material. This evidence includes thousands of "contracts," which encompass a wide variety of receipts, accounts, bonds, conveyances, and deeds. Most significant among these are the actual legal decisions rendered by judges within the law courts. Supplementary information is provided by royal rescripts, charters, historical inscriptions, private letters, and general literature. Even lexicographical and grammatical works, created to aid in the study of ancient literature, contain short sentences or extracts regarding custom and law, including the preserved "Sumerian Family Laws."

## The Code of Khammurabi
A more systematic study of the legal framework of Babylonia became possible with the discovery of the Code of Khammurabi (also written as Hammurabi, Ammurapi, or Khammuram). A copy of this Code was found at Susa by J. de Morgan and is currently housed in the Louvre. 

The compilation of this code was ordered by Khammurabi after he had settled his kingdom and expelled the Elamites. This achievement was a central work of the "First Dynasty of Babylon," as the native historians termed it. While other fragments of a later code exist and have been published, the Code of Khammurabi remains the primary source for systematic legal study, although many points of law still lack evidence.

## Legal and Social Application
The legal environment of Babylonia was characterized by a diverse population of many races and incessant intercommunication between cities. Resident aliens were common in every city, and this freedom of intercourse tended to assimilate customs. 

The genius of Khammurabi was his ability to weld together his vast empire through a uniform system of law, establishing Babylon as his metropolis. The influence of Babylonian law and administration was extensive; the prologue to Khammurabi's law-code mentions that the cities of Assur and Nineveh came under his beneficent influence. Evidence from the period indicates that Assyria was a Babylonian dependency, as seen in a letter from Khammurabi ordering the dispatch of 240 soldiers from Situllum and Assyria.

The application of city laws remained relevant long after the First Dynasty. As late as the accession of Samas-sum-yukin and Assur-bani-pal, Babylonians appealed to city laws to maintain specific protections. These included the right for groups of aliens, up to twenty at a time, to enter the city freely. Additionally, city laws dictated that foreign women who had married Babylonian husbands could not be enslaved, and that even a dog entering the city could not be put to death without a trial.

## Historical Context of the First Dynasty
The legal unification of Babylonia followed a period of foreign influence and instability. After the fall of a previous dynasty, Sumuabi (from Canaan or southern Arabia) mastered northern Babylonia, while Elamites occupied the south. Babylon first became a capital in the fifth year of the reign of Sumuabi's son, Sumu-la-ilu. 

Khammurabi, the son and successor of Sin-muballidh, eventually shook off Elamite supremacy. In the thirtieth year of his reign (2340 B.C.), he defeated the Elamite forces in a decisive battle and drove them from Babylonia. Over the following two years, he added Yamutbal and Larsa to his dominion, forming a single monarchy with Babylon at its head. This political union, effected around 2250 B.C., coincided with a great literary revival and the expansion of Babylonian rule as far as the Mediterranean shores.

## Transition to Kassite Rule
The First Dynasty eventually ended with Samsu-ditana, the son of Ammi-zadok. He was followed by a dynasty of 11 Sumerian kings who reportedly reigned for 368 years, though this number is considered exaggerated. This group was eventually overthrown by the Kassites (or Kossaeans) from the mountains of Elam. 

The Kassite dynasty, founded around 1780 B.C. by Gaddas (also called Gandis or Kandis), lasted for 576-3/4 years. Under this foreign dominion, Babylonia lost its empire over western Asia, and regions such as Palestine and Syria became independent. During this era, the Semitic kings' divine attributes disappeared; Kassite sovereigns were never given the title of "god." Despite this, Babylon remained the holy city of western Asia and the capital of the kingdom, where the priests held all-powerful status.
