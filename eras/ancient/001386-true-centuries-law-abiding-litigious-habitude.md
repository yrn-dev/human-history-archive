# Legal Traditions and Litigious Habitudes

## Ancient Legal Codes and Precedent
In certain ancient societies, the development of law was characterized by centuries of law-abiding and litigious habitude. This history resulted in the accumulation of vast stores of precedent within the temple archives of individual cities, consisting of judicial decisions and ancient deeds. While city customs were assimilated through intercourse, the primitive custom and ancient precedent were further modified by a universal habit of writing and a perpetual reliance on written contracts.

The legal Code of this period did not simply conserve ancient law or embody contemporary custom. It was logically and carefully arranged by subject-matter, though not in the manner of modern scientific treatises. Under this system, parties were generally free to contract provided they could reach an agreement. These deeds of agreement were executed in the temple by a notary public and confirmed via an oath "by god and the king." To ensure the stipulations were legal and not impious, the documents were publicly sealed and witnessed by professional witnesses and collaterally interested parties. Public opinion and custom served as safeguards against agreements to commit wrongs.

## Judicial Process and Appeals
In the event of a dispute, judges focused first on the contract. While judges had the authority to not sustain a contract, they allowed parties to observe it if the contract itself was not disputed. Decisions made by judges could be appealed. Many contracts included a specific proviso stating that parties would abide by "the decision of the king" in the event of a future dispute. The Code detailed the likely outcome for a vast number of these cases; consequently, many appeals sent to the king were returned to the judges with instructions to decide the matter in accordance with the Code.

## English Litigious Costs and Solicitor Relations
In the law of England, the expenses of litigation are initially borne by the person who employs the lawyer or undertakes the proceedings. The general principle is that "costs follow the event," meaning the unsuccessful party defrays the expenses of the successful party. However, several exceptions exist to prevent injustice:
*   **Frivolous Actions:** If an action is vexatious or frivolous, or brought in a higher court when a lower court was competent, the rule may be modified.
*   **Defendant Conduct:** If a defendant's behavior rendered the action justifiable or necessary, they may be held responsible despite escaping judgment.

The relationship between a solicitor and a client is governed by a contract to pay proper charges and disbursements. For conveyancing, remuneration is largely regulated by *ad valorem* scales based on the property value (Solicitors' Remuneration Order 1882). For non-litigious business, clients may use written agreements to fix costs as a percentage of the amount involved. 

Regarding litigious business, the courts and the profession object to "no cure no pay" arrangements, as they are seen as leading to speculative actions. Furthermore, stipulations involving a share of the proceeds from a successful action are considered illegal and champertous. An English solicitor's bill traditionally takes the form of a voluminous itemized narrative of every act and letter. Before recovering charges, the solicitor must provide a signed bill of costs and wait one month before suing. The High Court maintains a threefold jurisdiction over these costs: through its authority over solicitors as officers, via statutory power (such as the Solicitors Act 1843), and through ordinary jurisdiction to determine if charges are reasonable.

## Comparative Legal Approaches
Historical accounts highlight different attitudes toward the law. In Sparta, Agesilaus is noted for his obedience to the law and his ingenuity in preserving it. When faced with a choice between ruining friends or abolishing laws, he permitted the laws to "sleep for one day" to readmit survivors of the battle of Leuktra to Spartan citizenship privileges. This is contrasted with Pompeius, who is described as breaking laws he had enacted himself to demonstrate his power.

In other contexts, such as Roman law, certain doctrines—such as *de aqua de pluvia arcenda*—were mocked by figures like Cicero and Lactantius as insignificant, yet they remained of frequent use among litigious neighbors in both country and town.
