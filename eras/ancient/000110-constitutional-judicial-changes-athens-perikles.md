# Constitutional and Judicial Changes at Athens under Periklês

The period of Periklês' leadership marked the full development and consummation of the Athenian democratical system. Building upon the earlier foundations laid by Kleisthenês, Periklês and his associate Ephialtês implemented a series of constitutional innovations that shifted the balance of power in Athens, specifically by separating judicial authority from administrative functions.

## The Transition from Early Democracy
Prior to the reforms of Periklês, the Athenian system—originally shaped by Solon and later by Kleisthenês—maintained a union of administrative and judicial functions. Magistrates, who were generally wealthy men, held significant judicial powers. Similarly, the senate of Areopagus possessed a large, undefined controlling power and a semi-religious character. Over time, the oligarchical tendencies of the Areopagus became inconsistent with the growing democratical sentiments of the citizenry following the Persian invasion.

While Kleisthenês is regarded as the real author of Athenian democracy through his rearrangement of tribes and extension of the franchise, his system was imperfect. It continued to recognize archons as judges to a considerable extent and maintained the Solonian law of exclusion, which barred the fourth class of the census from individual office.

## The Judicial Revolution
The central achievement of Periklês and Ephialtês was the establishment of a democratical judicial system. This was achieved through the creation of popular, numerous, and salaried dikasteries (jury-courts). These dikasts were organized into ten regiments and summoned to act systematically throughout the year. 

Contrary to the view that Periklês merely added pay to existing institutions, the archive indicates that the introduction of pay coincided with the first time these dikasteries were formally organized for regular judicial action. The dikasts were sworn to decide cases in accordance with written and known laws. This system allowed the poorer citizens to gain a new sense of power and habit.

Under this new arrangement, the judicial process for civil actions or accusations of injury to the state still began with an address to an archon; however, the archon's role was reduced to a preliminary police function. The actual trial and decision were reserved for the dikastery.

## Reduction of Magisterial and Senatorial Power
The rise of the dikasteries resulted in a significant loss of influence for the Athenian magistrates and the senate of Areopagus. Magistrates were stripped of nearly all judicial power, leaving them only with the ability to impose small fines. Their roles were confined strictly to administrative functions.

The senate of Areopagus experienced an even more severe restriction of its jurisdiction. Once deprived of its unmeasured range of supervision, the Areopagus lost all judicial sanction except for the specific cognizance of homicide, a procedure that remained reserved for them due to its religious and judicial nature. Individual magistrates and the senate of Five Hundred also retained a small power of fining.

## Political Conflict and the Ascendency of Periklês
These changes emerged from a period of intense political opposition. Periklês and Ephialtês led the democratical party, while Kimon represented the conservative or oligarchical party, with the senate of Areopagus serving as Kimon's center of action. Periklês is described as reserved, philosophical, and business-like, acting less as a demagogue than Kimon.

Ephialtês was originally equal to Periklês in influence and led the efforts against magisterial abuse. After the Athenian troops were dismissed from Laconia and Kimon was ostracized, Ephialtês and Periklês were able to carry out the measures to abridge the power of the Areopagus and individual magistrates. Following the assassination of Ephialtês by the conservative party, Periklês began a great ascendency, eventually reaching a compromise with Kimon, likely facilitated by Kimon's sister, Elpinikê.

## Legacy and Impact
The constitutional structure established by Periklês remained largely intact until the era of Macedonian interference, with only temporary interruptions caused by the Thirty and the Four Hundred. This system persisted into the days of Demosthenês, although the energy and character of the people changed over time.

Historians such as Thucydidês suggest that Periklês' work was eminently valuable to the Athenian character and power. By regularizing the democratical movement into formal judicial institutions, Periklês is credited with improving the best characteristics of the Athenian mind and providing individual security.
