# THE DRAMA, RHETORIC, DIALECTICS, AND THE SOPHISTS

The intellectual landscape of Athens underwent a significant transformation characterized by the development of dramatic genius and the rise of formal rhetoric and dialectics. These movements created a transition toward ethical philosophy and a new era of Greek prose composition.

## The Development of Athenian Drama
Athens experienced an extraordinary development of dramatic genius, marked by the gradual enlargement of tragedy and an abundance of new works. The theater was accessible even to the poorest citizens through the *Theôrikon*, or festival-pay. The primary figures of this era included Æschylus, Sophoklês, and Euripidês, who introduced modifications to the tragedy. Parallel to this, comedy grew and developed; before Aristophanês, poets such as Kratinus were active. For a period, the practice of exposing citizens by name in comedy was forbidden, though it was later renewed, eventually leading to the milder comedy of Kratês and the works of Aristophanês.

The innovation of the dramatists lay not merely in imagination or judgment—as the ancient epical structure of the *Odyssey* already demonstrated architectonic talent—but in the infusion of a rhetorical, dialectical, and ethical spirit into poetry. Unlike previous epic or lyric forms that recounted exploits or single-minded impressions, the drama brought mythical persons to the stage to discuss, accuse, defend, confute, persuade, and advise. In the drama, action is assumed to have happened elsewhere; the substance of the work is talk. This format required rhetorical exigencies to influence characters and dialectical skill to manage the constant debate, consultation, and retort necessary for characters to defend their proceedings or attack opponents.

## Rhetoric and Dialectics
The drama served as a stage of transition to the separate lines of intellectual activity known as rhetoric and dialectics. Rhetoric was associated with men of active life, while dialectics was the domain of men of speculation. 

Empedoklês of Agrigentum is recognized as the first name in the rhetorical movement. Dialectics found its first name in Zeno of Elea, who, along with Melissus, launched dialectical attacks against the opponents of Parmenidês within the Eleatic school. Zeno later visited Athens, where he engaged in conversation with both Sokratês and Periklês. This period saw the powerful efficacy of the "negative arm" in Grecian philosophy.

While rhetoric focused on expatiating and declaiming, dialectic strove to refute opponents through the employment of question and answer, often in a brief form. This dialectical method was exemplified by Sokratês, whose skill in conversation was celebrated for its suggestive influence, mastery of homely illustrations, and the use of an ironical affectation of ignorance to convict opponents of contradiction. Ambitious young men, such as Alkibiadês and the oligarchical Kritias, sought to acquire this "knack" for interrogations to improve their standing in public debate.

## The Sophists
The term "sophist" carries a complex history. While Protagoras claimed that sophists had existed since the earliest times of Greece, he asserted that earlier men avoided the title due to the odium attached to it, making him the first to call himself a sophist openly. Other historical figures, such as Anaxagoras and Damon, were called sophists by Isokratês, and Diogenês of Apollonia used the term for Ionic philosophers. The comic poet Kratinus applied the label to all poets, including Hesiod and Homer.

In the Sokratic age, the sophists were paid teachers, including Protagoras and Gorgias. Much of the invidious sentiment and the peculiar sense of the word "sophist" stems from the polemics of Plato, whose unfriendly spirit contributed more to the negative attachment of the title than the teachers' own assumptions.

## Educational Influence and Written Works
The field of education in Athens gradually enlarged, with increased capacity among musical teachers. Some sophists published treatises on the art of rhetoric; Thrasymachus and Theodôrus wrote precepts regarding the component parts of an oration. Although Aristotle viewed the theories of Thrasymachus as unimportant hints and materials compared to his own comprehensive theory of rhetoric, these early works were influential for young men learning to analyze the parts of a harangue. Quintilian also considered the precepts of Theodôrus and Thrasymachus worthy of attention.

The influence of these movements extended into later prose. Lucian, a follower of Attic models like Plato, utilized the dialogue—a prose representative of the drama—to create witty and polished compositions. However, the immense influence of rhetoric as an art had previously thrown some discredit on the style of composition introduced by Plato, as Aristotle avoided the dialogue, perhaps viewing it as beneath the dignity of philosophy.
