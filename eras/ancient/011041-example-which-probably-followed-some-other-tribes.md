# Tribal Groups and Classifications in Human History

The study of tribal organizations across different eras and regions reveals diverse patterns of migration, social structure, and linguistic classification. From the ancient Near East to Central Asia and the African continent, the identification of tribes often involves a combination of archaeological evidence, genealogical traditions, and historical accounts.

## The Israelite Tribes and the District of Aser(u)
In the latter half of the 14th century B.C., during the reigns of Rameses II and Seti I, Egyptian records identify a district west of Galilee known as Aser(u). Historians suggest two primary possibilities regarding this region. One theory posits that Asher was an Israelite tribe that may have separated from its brethren while in Egypt and migrated north—a pattern of movement that some other tribes may have followed. Alternatively, the district may have been regarded as part of the national kingdom and integrated into the Israelite identity through the genealogical device of designating it as a "son" of Jacob.

Some evidence suggests that the Israelite population of this area might have moved from a southern home, following an example set by the tribe of Dan. There are associations between the clans of Malchiel and Heber and the Habiri people and a hostile chief named Milk-ili, as mentioned in the Amarna Tablets; however, it is considered unlikely that events from approximately 1400 B.C. would survive only in this form. Additionally, while some suggest the name Asher derives from Abd-ashirta of the same period, a connection to the goddess Ashir(t)a is considered more likely.

## Nomadic and Semi-Nomadic Tribes of Persia and Kurdistan
Historical accounts of Persia describe various nomad and semi-nomad tribes, including the Shúls, Lúrs, Karaunahs, and Kurds. Specifically, the Bakhtyáris of Luristán have been characterized as individually brave but savage and cruel, known for pursuing exterminating blood feuds and for being notorious thieves. It is noted as a proverb in Persia that the Bakhtiyaris have had to forego the reading of the prayer for the dead, the *Fatihah*, because they would otherwise have no other occupation.

Among the Kurds, 14th-century records enumerate several prominent tribes:
* **Guranieh** of Dartang (modern Gurans).
* **Zengeneh**, located in the Hamadan hills.
* **Hasnani** of Arbil and Kerkuk, who tradition says originated in Khorasan.
* **Sohrieh** of Tel-Haftun and Shekelabad, the ancestors of the Baban of Suleimanieh.
* **Zerzari** of the Hinjarin mountains.
* **Julamerkieh**, said to be descended from the caliph Merwan-ibn-Hakam.
* **Hakkarieh** (Hakkari), inhabiting Zuzan.
* **Bokhtieh** (modern Bohtan).
* **Rowadi**, the tribe to which Saladin belonged, likely the modern Rawendi.

## Central Asian and Scythian Tribes
In Central Asia, the Ephthalites (also known as the Ye-tha or Hoa) were a section of the Yue-Chi. While their customs resembled those of the Turks (Tu-Kiue), they remained a warlike and energetic group that fought against both the Turks and the Yue-Chi. The Ephthalites were characterized as raw barbarians, more ferocious and less assimilative than other conquering tribes, possibly due to their brief contact with civilization before being annihilated as a nation in just over a hundred years. They likely contributed to the physical types of the Indian population.

In the region of Scythia, Herodotus distinguished between the Scythians and other groups such as the Gelôni and the Agathyrsi. While the Pontic Greeks viewed the Scythês, Gelônus, and Agathyrsus as three brethren and sons of Hêraklês and the *μιξοπάρθενος* Ἔχιδνα, Herodotus maintained a more precise distinction. He noted that within a single *ἔθνος* (nation), some portions could be *ἀροτῆρες* (settled) while others were *νόμαδες* (nomadic), a phenomenon also seen among the Persians and the Iberians between the Caspian and the Euxine.

## Tribal Structures in Athens and Bantu Languages
In the context of the Kleisthenean constitution in Athens, there is historical debate regarding the *naukraries*. Some sources suggest they were continued as fifty in number, with five assigned to every Kleisthenean tribe. However, this is questioned because the *τριττὺς* (one-third of a tribe) was a functional division, making the coexistence of a fifth-part division improbable.

In the study of Bantu dialects, the prefix *Ma-* is identified as a collective numeral of indefinite number. This prefix was applied to various masses, including a herd of beasts, liquids (especially water), or a tribe of men. Over time, these prefixes varied as dialects became more corrupt, leading to a lack of correspondence between modern prefixes and their accompanying particles.
