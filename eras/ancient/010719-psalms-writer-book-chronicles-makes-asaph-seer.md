# The Psalms

The Psalms, also referred to as the Psalter, constitute a central part of the Old Testament and the Hebrew Bible. They are characterized as the section where the devotional aspect of religious character is most completely expressed. Through lyrics of beauty and tenderness, the psalmists convey a wide range of human emotions to their God, including adoration, praise, jubilation, thankfulness, hope, confidence, resignation, penitence, distress, and despondency.

## Composition and Structure
The Psalter was not the work of a single compiler but was formed gradually over time. This gradual formation is evidenced by the presence of double recensions of the same psalm; for example, Psalm 53 is equivalent to Psalm 14, Psalm 70 is equivalent to Psalm 11:13-17, and Psalm 108 is a combination of Psalm 57:7-11 and Psalm 60:5-12.

In the Hebrew canon, the Psalter is organized into five books:
1. Psalms i–xli
2. Psalms xlii–lxxii
3. Psalms lxxiii–lxxxix
4. Psalms xc–cvi
5. Psalms cvii–cl

The collection is manifestly based on several independent smaller collections, as indicated by groups of psalms attributed to "Asaph," the "sons of Korah," "David," and the "Songs of Ascents."

## Authorship and Historical Context
While later Jewish and Christian opinions—including the Talmud and Augustine—claimed that David authored the entire Psalter, and Hebrew titles ascribe seventy-three psalms to him (with the Septuagint adding fifteen more), recent criticism suggests it is no longer possible to view the psalms as a record of David's spiritual life throughout his career.

The compilation of the Psalter was likely not completed until after the return from the Captivity. The majority of the psalms presuppose religious experiences or historical conditions that followed the age of Jeremiah. Consequently, the Psalter is often termed the "Hymn-book of the second Temple" and represents the deeper spiritual feelings of the later stages of Israel's history. Scholars suggest the compilation may not have been finalized before the 3rd century B.C., and if certain psalms date to the Maccabee struggle (such as Psalms xliv, lxxiv, lxxix, and lxxxiii), the work could not have been completed until after 165 B.C.

## Canonical Classification
Within the Jewish canon, the Old Testament is divided into three parts: The Law (Torah), the Prophets, and the Writings (Hagiographa). The Psalms are categorized under the "Writings," specifically within the group of poetical books, alongside Job and Proverbs. In early history, the Psalms existed as separate books before being incorporated into the present Hebrew Bible. This broader literature, which includes the psalms, appears in history during the fifth or fourth century B.C.

## Associated Figures
### Asaph
Asaph is the eponym of the Asaphite guild of singers, one of the hereditary choirs responsible for the musical services of the temple at Jerusalem during post-exilic times. His name appears in the titles of certain Psalms. According to the writer of the Book of Chronicles (2 Chron. xxix. 30), Asaph was a seer and the chief of the singers of his time, contemporary with Solomon and David.

### David Kimhi
Between 1160 and 1235, David Kimhi (also known as Redaq) produced significant scholarly work. After completing his *Sefer Miklol* and *Sefer Hashorashim*, he wrote commentaries on portions of the Scriptures. Following his work on Chronicles, he authored a commentary on the Psalms. His annotations on the Psalms are noted for containing polemical excursuses directed against Christian interpretations.
