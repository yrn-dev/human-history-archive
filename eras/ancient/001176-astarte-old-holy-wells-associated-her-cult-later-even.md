# Astarte

Astarte is a Semitic goddess, referred to in the Bible as Ashtoreth, who represents the great female principle. She served as the counterpart to the Baal of the Phoenicians and Canaanites, as well as the Dagon of the Philistines. Her worship was widespread across the Semitic world, with temples located in Tyre and Sidon, and likely in Ascalon among the Philistines. In Jerusalem, King Solomon constructed a high-place for her, which remained until the reign of King Josiah. The extent of her influence among the Israelites is evidenced by frequent biblical references and various representations of the deity discovered on Palestinian soil.

## Attributes and Nature
As a great nature-goddess, Astarte is characteristically associated with reproduction and fertility. These attributes were accompanied by a perceived immorality that may have originated as primitive magic. In her role as a patroness of the hunt, she was later identified with Artemis. This led to further fusions with other deities, including Venus, Juno, Diana, and Aphrodite. Her celestial association was the planet Venus, and classical writers assigned her the epithets Urania and Caelestis.

There has been scholarly debate regarding whether Astarte was a lunar goddess. Some point to the place-name Ashteroth-Karnaim in Gilead ("Ashteroth of the horns") and testimony from late writers like Herodian and Lucian, who claimed she was represented with horns, as evidence. However, it is probable that these were ram's horns. Robertson Smith argued that Astarte may have originally been a sheep-goddess, citing the term "Astartes of the flocks" in Deuteronomy vii. 13 to refer to offspring. While she may have been a sheep-goddess to nomads, other animal associations exist, such as the doves of Ascalon and the gazelle or antelope of the goddess of love.

## Cult Practices and Symbols
The worship of Astarte included several characteristic features of heathen worship found throughout the Semitic world. These included the preparation of sacred mystic cakes, the burning of incense, and ceremonial acts of bowing and kissing. Israelite prophets denounced these practices, along with violent and ecstatic exercises and human sacrifice.

In terms of physical representation, Astarte was sometimes believed to be immanent in a conical stone or pillar. At Paphos, the original home and temple of Astarte, pilgrims visited a cone of stone as the object of public adoration. While these shapeless cones served as the focus for public pilgrimage, small portrait statues were likely produced for private worship.

## Regional Variations and Fusions
Astarte's identity often merged with other deities depending on the region:
*   **Carthage:** Her worship was transplanted from Tyre to Carthage, where colonists eventually identified her with Tanit-Astarte and the legendary figure Dido.
*   **Moab:** The Moabites created a compound deity known as Ashtar-Chemosh.
*   **Egypt:** Astarte was introduced into Egypt, where she had a temple at Memphis.
*   **Syria:** Astarte is often fused with Atargatis, a Syrian "fish-goddess" associated with sacred pools. While the two were of common origin, their cults were historically distinct. However, they became so indistinguishable in some cases that the temple at Carnion is considered by some to be identical to the temple of Astarte at Ashtaroth-Karnaim.
*   **Gabal (Byblus):** In this region, she was known as *ba'alath* (the feminine form of *baal*), and her real name was often left unpronounced out of reverence.

## Influence on Other Religions
Elements of Astarte's cult entered into other religious types. Specifically, the old holy wells associated with her cult and her sacred cakes were later transferred to the worship of the Virgin.
