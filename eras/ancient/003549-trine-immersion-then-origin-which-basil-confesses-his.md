# Trine Immersion

Trine immersion is a baptismal practice involving the three-fold dipping of a person in water. In the early Christian church, this practice was often carried out in a baptistery—a separate hall or chapel, frequently circular or polygonal, containing a baptismal font (also known as a *piscina* or *kolumbethra*) where those being baptized were immersed thrice.

## Origins and Traditions
The origins of trine immersion are subject to various interpretations. Basil, in his work *On the Holy Spirit*, confessed his ignorance regarding how this specific feature, along with others such as the blessing of the water and oil, originated. He stated that these practices had been handed down through a silent and sacramental tradition, consisting of an unspoken and unpublished teaching.

Some analyses suggest that trine immersion may be older than the theological explanations created to justify it. These explanations are viewed as aetiological, invented to explain a custom the church adopted from its pagan medium. In Greek and Latin antiquity, pagan lustrations were normally threefold. This is evidenced in the writings of Virgil, Ovid, Persius, and Horace. Regarding a passage by Horace, the scholiast Acro noted that the phrase "thrice purely" was used because people plunged themselves in three times when expiating their sins.

## Theological Interpretations
Different church fathers and traditions provided varying reasons for the practice of triple immersion:

*   **Trinitarian Symbolism:** Tertullian explained trine immersion as a reflection of the triple invocation, stating that the dipping occurs thrice for the several names and several persons. Jerome similarly asserted that the three-fold plunging was intended to show forth the one sacrament of the Trinity.
*   **Christological Symbolism:** Other figures, including Athanasius, Gregory of Nyssa, Cyril of Jerusalem, Leo of Rome, and Theophylactus, regarded trine immersion as symbolic of the three days Christ spent in entombment. This interpretation was enjoined in the Armenian baptismal rubric and in an epistle from Macarius of Jerusalem to the Armenians (circa 330), where it was further associated with the concept of baptism into the death of Christ.

## Relation to the Baptismal Formula
Trine immersion and the trinitarian formula (baptism in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost) were not always used uniformly or in tandem from the beginning. While the *Teaching of the Apostles* prescribes the trinitarian formula, it also mentions baptism into the name of the Lord. 

Historically, there were disputes regarding the validity of formulas that omitted parts of the Trinity. For example, Celtic missionaries omitted one or more persons of the Trinity, leading to an anathema from the church of Rome. Conversely, Pope Nicholas (858-867) allowed baptism to be valid if performed *tantum in nomine Christi* (in the name of Christ alone), as seen in the Acts. However, Basil condemned baptism into the Lord alone as being insufficient.

## Evolution of Baptismal Modes
The practice of total immersion was not always the insisted mode of baptism. The *Didache* instructs to "pour water on the head," and the *Key of Truth* (a manual of old Armenian Baptists) describes a rite where the bishop pours water over the head of a penitent who has advanced on his knees into the water. Christian art from the 1st to the 10th century often depicts the baptizand standing in water while the baptizer pours water from a bowl or hand.

Total immersion only came into general vogue in the 7th century. This shift occurred as adult baptism became rarer, allowing the Greek word *baptizo* to be interpreted in a way that suited the baptism of infants.
