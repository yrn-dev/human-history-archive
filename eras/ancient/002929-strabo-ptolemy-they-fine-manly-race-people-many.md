# Strabo and Ptolemy

Strabo and Ptolemy are cited across various historical records as primary sources for ancient geography, ethnography, and political history. Their works provide early accounts of diverse regions ranging from Europe and Africa to Asia.

## Geographical and Ethnographic Records

The works of Strabo and Ptolemy are utilized to identify and locate ancient peoples and territories. In the region of Baluchistan, specifically the Kharan desert and Mashkel, the Naushirwanis—a Persian race—are mentioned. This group is identified as being identical to the Tahuki or Tahukani found in Perso-Baluchistan. Records from Nearchus, Strabo, and Ptolemy specifically mention a place called Taoce in this region.

In the area around the Caspian and Euxine seas, the Barsileens (also known as the Passils of Moses of Chorene) are traced through the writings of Ptolemy, Strabo, Pliny, and Pomponius Mela. These people are linked to the "Royal Scyths" (Skythai basilêes), a group known for their commercial enterprise and political superiority.

Regarding ancient Germany, the state of the region before the Roman wars is known through the works of Caesar and, more specifically, Ptolemy. Strabo, along with Pliny and Tacitus, described Germany as it was changed by those wars. According to these records, Germany was bounded by the Rhine to the west, the Vistula to the east, the southern point of Norway, Sweden, and Esthonia to the north, and the Maine and the mountains north of Bohemia to the south.

## Accounts of the Levant and Egypt

Strabo is cited as a witness to the political dynamics between Cleopatra and her son, Ptolemy Lathyrus. According to Strabo, Cleopatra placed great trust in her generals, Chelcias and Ananias—sons of Onias who built a temple in the prefecture of Heliopolis. Strabo notes that while many who went to Cyprus revolted to Ptolemy, the party of Onias remained faithful to the queen because their countrymen, Chelcias and Ananias, held a position of chief favor with her.

Additionally, Strabo and Nicholaus of Damascus are cited as affirming the brutal conduct of Ptolemy during his campaign in Judea. After a victory, Ptolemy occupied villages filled with women and children, commanding his soldiers to strangle them, cut them into pieces, and cast them into boiling caldrons to be devoured as sacrifices. This was a strategic command intended to terrify those fleeing the battle by making the enemy appear to be cannibals.

## Historical Limitations and Analysis

Modern historical analysis has highlighted the limitations of these ancient sources. In the study of countries eastward of Cape Comorin, the "ignorance" of Strabo, Ptolemy, Pliny, Arrian, and Marcian is noted as being illustrated in the work of D’Anville. While these ancient geographers provided early frameworks, subsequent knowledge of India has been improved through later commerce, conquest, and the maps and memoirs of figures such as Major Rennel.
