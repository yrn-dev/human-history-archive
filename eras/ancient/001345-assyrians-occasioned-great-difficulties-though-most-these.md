# The Assyrian Empire

The Assyrian Empire was one of the most powerful entities in the ancient world. Its history is characterized by significant military expansion, the development of sophisticated art and architecture, and a complex chronological record preserved through cuneiform inscriptions and archaeological remains.

## Duration and Early History
The exact duration of the Assyrian Empire is a subject of scholarly debate due to obscure early records and contradictory monuments. Some authors, including Ctesias and Justin, suggest the empire lasted thirteen hundred years, while others, such as Herodotus, reduce this period to five hundred and twenty years. These differing views may be reconciled by considering possible interruptions or diminutions of power over time. Some perspectives suggest the empire's antiquity is equal to that of its capital, Babylon, which holy Scripture attributes to the conqueror Nimrod.

Ancient accounts provide varying perspectives on Assyria's early reach. Plato described the kingdom of Troy as dependent on the Assyrian empire during the time of Priam. Ctesias recorded that Teutamus, the twentieth king after Ninyas, sent troops under Memnon, son of Tithonus, to assist the Trojans at a time when the empire had already existed for over a thousand years. However, the silence of Homer regarding these events makes such claims doubtful.

## Political History and Expansion
Knowledge of Assyrian history becomes more complete starting with Assur-nazir-pal III (883-858 B.C.), who expanded the empire in all directions and established Calah as a favorite residence. He was noted for his revolting cruelties. His son, Shalmaneser II, reigned for 35 years, turning the capital into an armed camp from which armies launched annual campaigns of plunder and destruction. During his reign, Babylon was reduced to vassalage. In 853 B.C., Shalmaneser II shattered a confederacy of Syrian princes that included Benhadad of Damascus and Ahab of Israel. Twelve years later, the forces of Hazael were annihilated, and ambassadors from Jehu of Samaria brought tribute to the "great king."

The empire faced internal instability toward the end of Shalmaneser II's life due to a rebellion by his eldest son. This revolt, supported by Assur and Arbela, was suppressed by his second son, Samsi-Raman (or Samsi-Hadad), who succeeded him in 824 B.C. Later, in 804 B.C., Hadad-nirari IV captured Damascus and received tribute from Samaria.

In other regions, the monarch Gyges of the eighth century B.C. initially paid tribute to Sardanapalus, though he later rebelled against Assyria and assisted Psammetichus I in liberating Egypt from brief Assyrian servitude.

## The Later Empire and Fall
Assurbanipal (known to the Greeks as Sardanapalus), the son of Esarhaddon, was a notable late monarch. Unlike typical Assyrian leaders, he received a Babylonian education rather than a military one due to his father's reconciliation with the priests of Bel-Marduk. Assurbanipal was a prolific collector of clay documents; his unearthed library is considered the most precious source of historical material in the world. Despite his scholarly interests, he maintained military control, suppressing a rebellion in Babylon and making a temporary conquest of Egypt.

The empire eventually succumbed to pressure from the north and north-east from the Medes, Persians, and Scythians. The Medes and Persians allied with the nomadic Semitic Chaldeans of the south to destroy Assyria. The capital, Nineveh, fell to these forces in 606 B.C.

## Art, Culture, and Language
Assyrian art has been brought to light through the excavations of Mr. Layard and M. Botta, with collections now housed in the British Museum and the Louvre. These sculptures, which date to the eighth or seventh century B.C. at the latest, display a style that serves as a comparison to early Egypt.

The study of Assyrian records has faced challenges. In the early days of deciphering cuneiform, the reading of Babylonian and Assyrian proper names caused great difficulty. While scholars generally agree on the principles of pronunciation and formation, some variations in transliteration and spelling persist.

## Chronology and Administration
Assyrian chronology is well-established from 911 B.C. to 666 B.C. thanks to the *limmi* (archon) lists found in the library of Nineveh. These lists record the beginning of kings' reigns and chief annual events. This system of reckoning time was used as early as the 15th century B.C., as evidenced by cuneiform tablets found in the colony at Kara Euyuk. This differed from the Babylonian system, where years were dated by significant events determined by the government.

Other historical records include the "Synchronous History of Assyria and Babylonia," which details the hostile or diplomatic relations between the kings of the two nations.
