# Antony, Cleopatra, and the Campaigns of Herod

The political relationship between Mark Antony, Cleopatra, and Herod was characterized by shifting alliances, financial negotiations, and the persistent influence of Cleopatra's ambitions regarding the territories of Judea and Arabia.

## Cleopatra's Influence and Territorial Ambitions
Cleopatra exerted significant pressure on Antony to alienate himself from Herod. Driven by an "avaricious humor" and a "covetous temper," she sought to acquire the kingdoms of the Jews and Arabians for herself. She secretly labored to have both Herod and Malichus, the kings of these nations, slain by Antony's order.

While Antony initially found the idea of killing "good and great kings" to be abominable, he complied with some of Cleopatra's injunctions. He alienated his friendship with Herod and transferred a substantial portion of Herod's country to Cleopatra. These territories included the plantation of palm trees and balsam trees at Jericho, as well as all cities on the side of the river Eleutherus, with the exceptions of Tyre and Sidon.

## Herod's Efforts to Regain Favor
Herod employed a strategy of diplomacy and lavish gift-giving to counteract Cleopatra's influence. When Cleopatra traveled through Judea—having already secured parts of Judea and Arabia from Antony—Herod pacified her indignation with large presents and conducted her to Pelusium with the utmost respect. To recover the lands that had been torn from his kingdom, Herod entered into an agreement to hire them back from Cleopatra at a yearly rent of two hundred talents.

Later, during deliberations where letters from Herod were presented, Herod traveled to meet Antony in person. Through the presents he brought from Jerusalem and the arguments he presented, he successfully recovered his interest with Antony. Antony eventually told Cleopatra that she should not "busily to meddle with the acts of the king's government," asserting that it was improper to require an account of a king's government in a way that would render him unable to be a king. Antony further pacified Cleopatra by granting her Celesyria instead of the territories she had desired in Judea.

## The Conflict Over Aristobulus
The relationship between Antony and Herod was further strained by the murder of Aristobulus. Alexandra, grieving the death of her son, wrote to Cleopatra detailing the "treacherous scene." Cleopatra, commiserating with Alexandra, urged Antony to punish Herod, arguing that it was unworthy for a man who had been made king of a kingdom that did not belong to him to commit such crimes against those of royal blood.

Persuaded by these arguments, Antony summoned Herod to Laodicea to make a defense of his actions. Fearing both the accusation and Cleopatra's ill-will, Herod obeyed the summons. Before departing, he appointed his uncle Joseph as procurator. Because Herod suspected that Antony had fallen in love with his wife, Mariamne, due to her beauty, he gave Joseph a private charge to kill Mariamne immediately if Antony were to kill him, to prevent her from being engaged to another man.

## Campaigns Against the Arabians
The archive records that Antony, at the persuasion of Cleopatra, sent Herod to fight against the Arabians. This conflict involved several battles before Herod eventually secured victory. Following this conquest, the Arabs chose Herod to be the governor of their nation.

Regarding the financial obligations associated with these territories, Herod had undertaken to pay Cleopatra the tribute of the king of Arabia. For a period, Herod paid this tribute, amounting to two hundred talents. However, he later became "niggardly and slow" in these payments, making deductions and resisting the payments.

## Final Relations and the Transition to Caesar
The bond between Antony and Herod persisted even as Antony's fortunes declined. After Antony was overcome by Caesar at Actium, Herod continued to offer assistance, sending many ten thousand cori of corn and as many auxiliaries as possible. Herod later claimed that he had advised Antony that the only way to recover his affairs was to kill Cleopatra, promising that if she were dead, he would provide money, walls, and an army to assist Antony in his war against Caesar.

Ultimately, following Antony's final fortune, Herod laid aside his diadem and traveled to Rhodes to meet Caesar as a private person, seeking safety through Caesar's virtue and testifying to his faithfulness as a friend to Antony.
