# The Church of the West

The Church of the West is characterized by a high degree of uniformity in its constitution and language, a trait that distinguishes it from the Eastern Church. While the East experienced a multiplicity of languages and a corresponding variety of doctrine and constitution, the West maintained a complete organic unity in its ecclesiastical system and services until the Reformation.

## Linguistic and Cultural Unity
A defining feature of the Western Church was the "complete Latinizing of the West." Latin maintained its position in church services and other branches of the ecclesiastical system across the region. This linguistic uniformity extended even to populations that were not Romanized, including the Germans, the Irish, and the Anglo-Saxons. In contrast, the Eastern Church saw several foreign tongues—such as Coptic, Syrian, Georgian, Armenian, Gothic, and Old-Bulgarian—compete with Greek, leading to the translation of sacred books into these languages and the subsequent formation of national churches and various sects.

## Liturgical Practices and Beliefs
The Western Church utilizes the *Gloria Patri* (Lesser Doxology), which functions as both a hymn and a creed. In the West, this prayer is repeated at the close of every psalm, whereas in the East, it is only repeated at the close of the last psalm. The specific phrasing of the *Gloria Patri* was influenced by the Arian heresy; the Catholic Church adopted the form "Glory be to the Father, and to the Son, and to the Holy Ghost, world without end, Amen" as a symbol of orthodoxy. Additionally, the phrase "as it was in the beginning, is now, and ever shall be" was added to the prayer. A decree from the second council of Vaison in 529 ordered the adoption of this addition throughout the churches of the West.

Regarding the rite of baptism, evidence from sculptures and pictures between the 1st and 10th centuries shows the baptizer pouring water over the head of a person standing in water. Early fonts were generally not deep enough for complete submersion, and total immersion did not come into general vogue until the 7th century. This shift occurred as adult baptism became rarer, allowing the Greek word *baptizo* to be interpreted in a way that suited infants.

## Architecture and Vestments
In the West, the basilican form—originally termed *dromical* due to its resemblance to a race-course (*dromos*)—was a standard for church construction. 

Regional architectural variations developed over time:
* **Italy:** The cathedral of Pisa features an elliptical dome. Domes were a favorite feature over the crossing of Italian churches, often carried on squinch pendentives. In cities such as Piacenza, Trieste, Parma, and Florence, the baptisteries have internal domes enclosed by sloping roofs and vertical walls. In Sicily, Saracenic influence led to the use of ovoid domes and simple versions of stalactite pendentives, as seen in the church of San Giovanni-dei-Leprosi (A.D. 1072).
* **Spain:** Domes were introduced as external architectural features over the crossings at the cathedrals of Salamanca, Tarragona, and Burgos.
* **Germany:** Domes are rarely found externally. While the churches of Sankt Maria im Capitol and St Martin in Cologne, as well as the cathedrals of Mainz, Spires, and Worms, have crossings covered by domes on squinch pendentives, they built lanterns around them externally.
* **France:** Domes are generally absent except in La Charente and Perigord.

In terms of vestments, the Western Church utilizes the alb and the dalmatic (a liturgical upper tunic). These differ from Eastern vestments; for example, the Eastern *sakkos* replaced the phelonion (chasuble), whereas the Latin church maintained different practices.

## Political and Administrative History
The history of the Western Church includes periods of significant tension between papal authority and secular rulers. In the 13th century, Pope Gregory IX excommunicated Frederick II and issued a public letter detailing the Emperor's heresies, vices, and misconduct. Frederick II responded by attacking the Pope's ambition to be the absolute ruler of Europe and drawing attention to the wealth of the church.

On a local administrative level, the ecclesiastical history of regions like Buckinghamshire shows a transition of jurisdiction. Early churches there were likely subject to the West Saxon see of Dorchester before the bishop's stool was moved to Lincoln after the Conquest. By the 12th century, the region included various deaneries; taxation records from Nicholas IV list eight deaneries comprising 186 parishes.
