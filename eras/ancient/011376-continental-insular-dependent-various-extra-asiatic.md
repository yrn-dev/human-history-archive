# Territories of Asia: Continental and Insular Dependencies

The geography of Asia, encompassing both continental and insular territories, has historically been characterized by a complex distribution of power, ranging from vast imperial dependencies to autonomous city-states. The total area of Asia, continental and insular, is estimated to be somewhat over 16,819,000 square miles, though detailed estimates vary among authorities. The total population is roughly 823,000,000, with 330,000,000 residing in Chinese territory, 302,000,000 in British territories, and 25,000,000 in Russian territories.

## Persian Influence and the Asiatic Greeks
In antiquity, the Persian Empire exerted significant ascendency over the Greeks of Asia, both continental and insular. This control was often maintained through the installation of despots in various cities, who served as both the supports and instruments of Persian power. 

During the revolt led by Aristagoras of Miletus, a widespread revolution occurred across most towns of Asiatic Greece. Despots were expelled to secure popular favor, and the government in Miletus was formally placed in the hands of the people. However, this independence was short-lived. Within six years, the capture of Miletus led to the rapid submission of neighboring towns in Karia. By the following summer, Persian and Phoenician forces by sea and land reconquered all Asiatic Greeks, including the towns of the Chersonese, Selymbria and Perinthus in Thrace, and Prokonnêsus and Artakê in the Propontis. Other affected areas included the islands of Chios, Lesbos, and Tenedos.

The scale of Persian mobilization was extensive; for instance, Kambysês summoned the maritime force and skill of the Aegean sea, including both Ionic and Æolic Greeks, insular and continental, for his enterprises.

## Spartan and Athenian Dependencies
The maritime and continental regions of the east also saw shifts in dependency between Greek powers. Following the decline of Athenian power, the Lacedæmonian (Spartan) empire imposed a "double oppression" on subject cities through native decemvirs and foreign harmosts. These harmosts remained in maritime and insular cities for approximately ten years until the battle of Knidus, though they persisted in various continental dependencies until the defeat of Leuktra in 371 B.C.

Lysander sought to aggrandize Sparta by establishing oligarchies—specifically "dekadarchies" (oligarchies of Ten)—in many Asiatic and insular cities. His goal was to convert these cities into Spartan dependencies, assessed with an annual tribute of one thousand talents.

Conversely, Athens previously led an insular confederacy. Discontent eventually grew among larger dependencies near the Asiatic coast. The city of Byzantium and the islands of Rhodes, Kos, and Chios declared themselves detached from Athens. This secession was aided by the Karian prince Mausôlus, who occupied Kos and Rhodes, the latter of which was placed under an oligarchy supported by his mercenary troops.

## The Case of Tyre
The relationship between continental and insular settlements is exemplified by the history of Tyre. There was a continental town known as Palæ-Tyrus located on a fertile plain. While some sources suggest the Tyrians moved from the mainland to the island, other accounts, including those of Herodotus and Arrian, indicate that insular Tyre—home to the temple of Hêraklês—was the original foundation.

The insular position provided Tyre with significant strategic strength. During the invasion of Salmaneser, king of Nineveh, the continental Palæ-Tyrus was forced to yield, while the insular Tyrians were able to hold out. Later, Nebuchadnezzar conducted a long siege of the insular city. The insular Tyrians remained subject to Nebuchadnezzar and his successors until the Chaldæan monarchy was destroyed by Cyrus.
