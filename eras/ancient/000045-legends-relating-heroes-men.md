# Legends Relating to Heroes and Men

In the study of Grecian myths, a distinction is made between those belonging to the Gods and those belonging to the Heroes. While myths of the Gods often grow from faith and feeling without a basis in allegory or matter of fact, the richness of Grecian mythical literature stems from the juxtaposition of three class-types: God, Hero, and Man. Heroic legends, which concern both heroes and heroines, are considered as important as those concerning the Gods.

## Races of Men and Early Didactic Poetry
The Hesiodic poem "Works and Days," identified as the earliest didactic poem, presents a vision of the races of men. These are categorized into five distinct stages: the Golden, the Silver, the Brazen, the Heroic, and the Iron. This presentation differs from both the *Theogony* and the works of Homer, characterized by an ethical vein of sentiment that is intersected by the mythical. This work also introduces dæmons, though the idea of these beings underwent changes over time and was occasionally employed to attack the pagan faith.

## The Iapetids and the Condition of Man
The legends of the Iapetids, as found in Hesiod, focus on Promêtheus and Epimêtheus. A central narrative involves the counter-manœuvring between Promêtheus and Zeus, leading to the creation of Pandôra. The poet's general sentiment suggests that while man is wretched, Zeus is not to blame; instead, mischiefs are attributed to women. The punishment of Promêtheus is a key element of this legend, with Æschylus specifying the locality where Promêtheus was confined.

## The Hero Theseus and Athenian Identity
Theseus serves as a primary example of a hero whose legend was deeply integrated into the civic and religious life of Athens. He is remembered as the mythical champion of democracy. His legends include the adventurous enterprise in Krete, where he rescued seven noble youths and maidens from the Minôtaur. While some Attic logographers attempted to rationalize this by claiming the Minôtaur was actually a powerful athlete or general named Taurus, the fanciful poetic version persisted.

The Athenian public commemorated Theseus through the annual departure of the Theôric ship to Dêlos and festivals such as the Oschophoria, the Pyanepsia, the Kybernêsia, and the Thêseia. In 476 B.C., an oracle directed Athens to retrieve the bones of Theseus from Skyros. After Kimon took the island, the body was brought to Athens in 469 B.C. and deposited in the Theseium. This monument and its precinct served as a sanctuary for slaves and men of poor condition seeking protection from the powerful.

## Other Heroic Figures and Legends
Heroic legends often functioned as explanations for local customs or prohibitions:
* **Amphiaräus:** In Thêbes, the sanctuary and chapel of the hero Amphiaräus stood in the city, yet citizens were restrained from consulting him as a prophet due to originally hostile relations between the hero and the city.
* **Hippolytus:** In Trœzen, youths and maidens offered their hair at the Herôon of Hippolytus, recalling the legend of his punishment by Aphroditê.
* **Adrastus and Melanippus:** In Sikyôn, the despot Kleisthenês sought to banish the hero Adrastus. After the Delphian Apollo refused permission for a direct banishment, Kleisthenês introduced the hero Melanippus—an enemy of Adrastus in Theban legend—and transferred the rites and festivals of Adrastus to him.
* **Kleomedes and Aristeas:** Some legends describe heroes who vanished. Aristeas of Proconnesus disappeared from a fuller's shop, only to be seen walking toward Kroton. Kleomedes of Astypalaea, a man of great strength who accidentally destroyed a school building, vanished from a wooden chest. The Pythia at Delphi later declared him the "last of the heroes."

## Epic Cycles and Relics
The greatest enterprises of war during the time of the Heroes were the two sieges of Thêbes and the sieges and capture of Troy. The latter represents the culminating point of the Grecian epic, focusing on the destinies of both Grecian and Trojan heroes.

The memory of these figures was often sustained by physical relics. In Chæroneia in Bœôtia, the sceptres of Agamemnôn and Pelops were preserved, attributed to the craftsmanship of the god Hêphæstos. Similarly, the prose logographers, such as Hellanikus and Pherekydês, compiled these legends into chronological series, such as the *Atthides*, which integrated the legends of the Erechtheids and the eponymous hero Kekrops.

## Comparative Hagiography
Beyond Greek legends, the archive notes patterns in later "pious legends" or hagiographies. These often follow a formula where the hero experiences a youthful vision or prophecy, performs recurring miracles (such as exorcising possessed people or resurrecting the dead), and is warned of their end via a dream. Following their death, similar miracles are reported at their tomb, such as the blind seeing or the lame walking.
