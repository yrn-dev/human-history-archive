# Fourth and Fifth Years of the War—Revolt of Mitylene

The fourth and fifth years of the Peloponnesian War were characterized by continued Athenian suffering, renewed invasions of Attica, and the significant revolt of Mitylene and much of Lesbos.

## Invasions of Attica and Internal Athenian Struggle
The third year of the war concluded with the Athenians and Phormio returning from Acarnania to Athens in the spring, bringing captured ships and freemen prisoners who were exchanged man for man. However, the second and third years had been periods of great suffering for Athens due to a continuing epidemic. This calamity enfeebled Athenian military efforts, though Phormio's victories had simultaneously elevated their maritime reputation.

In the fourth year, the epidemic's disorder was suspended, though its destructive effects were still felt. Taking advantage of this, the Peloponnesians and their allies, under the command of Archidamus, son of Zeuxidamus (king of the Lacedaemonians), invaded Attica during the summer as the corn was ripening. The invaders ravaged the land, while the Athenian horse attacked them where practicable to prevent light troops from advancing near the city. The Peloponnesians remained until their provisions were exhausted before dispersing to their respective cities.

## The Revolt of Mitylene
While the invaders were still in Attica, Athens received intelligence regarding the revolt of Mitylene and the greater part of Lesbos. At the time, Lesbos was divided into at least five separate city governments: Mitylene in the south-east, Methymna in the north, and Antissa, Eresus, and Pyrrha in the west.

Athens responded by sending a fleet under Kleippides. Kleippides failed to surprise Mitylene and initially carried out an imperfect blockade. Although he later received reinforcements and pressed the siege with greater vigor, the Mitylenians showed a lack of resolution.

Mitylenian envoys traveled to the Olympic festival to entreat aid from the Spartans. In their address, the envoys offered few practical grievances or oppressions to urge against Athens; they did not cite the enormity of tribute, the hardship of trials in Athens, or the misconduct of officers. Instead, they argued that they were degraded and defenceless subjects and expressed a hatred for the principle of the Athenian empire. Despite the weakness of their case, the Peloponnesians promised assistance.

## The Siege and Fall of Mitylene
The blockade of Mitylene was eventually carried on closely by the Athenian general Paches. To encourage the Mitylenians to hold out, the Lacedaemonians sent Salaethus in a galley. Salaethus entered Mitylene unperceived by passing through a passable section of the line of circumvallation at a torrent's bed. He informed the magistrates that Attica would be invaded and that forty ships were destined to relieve them. This news encouraged the Mitylenians to abandon negotiations with Athens.

Mitylene held out until its provisions were exhausted. Salaethus attempted to arm the population for a general sally, but the people refused to join. Consequently, the city surrendered to Athens at discretion. Following the capture, Paches reduced Eresus and Pyrrha. He captured Salaethus, who had been in hiding, and sent him to Athens along with approximately one thousand Mitylenian prisoners who were primarily concerned in the revolt. Paches then settled Mitylene and the rest of Lesbos as he saw fit.

## Athenian Deliberations and the Role of Kleon
The fate of the Mitylenian prisoners was debated in the Athenian assembly, led by Kleon. Kleon, representing a new class of politicians including Lysikles, Hyperbolus, and Eukrates, used fierce invective to argue against mercy. He asserted that the revolt was inexcusable because the Mitylenians had been treated honorably and with special privilege, yet chose to revolt when Athens was worn down by pestilence, invasion, and the costs of war. Furthermore, they had been the first to invite a Peloponnesian fleet across the Aegean.

Kleon proposed the harshest penalties of the custom of war: the execution of the entire male population of military age (approximately six thousand persons) and the sale of all women and children into slavery. Despite opposition from Diodotus and others, the assembly passed the motion, and a trireme was dispatched to Paches to execute the order.

## Other Military Actions
During this period, other conflicts persisted. The Platæans attempted a dangerous and difficult escape from their blockaded town, resulting in half of the garrison reaching Athens. Additionally, a Peloponnesian fleet under Alkidas arrived off the coast of Ionia, causing alarm. Paches pursued this fleet after the fall of Mitylene, but Alkidas returned to the Peloponnesus without taking action. Paches subsequently captured Notium, where he acted with perfidy toward the garrison leader, Hippias; Notium was then recolonized from Athens as a separate town.
