# BASE

The term "base" is derived from multiple linguistic roots and is utilized across a wide variety of disciplines, ranging from linguistics and ethics to chemistry, geometry, and heraldry.

## Etymology and General Usage
The word "base" has two distinct etymological origins that inform its different meanings. The first is derived from the French *bas* and Late Latin *bassus*, meaning "low." In this context, it serves as an adjective describing something that is low or deep. This sense extends metaphorically to describe that which is wicked, worthless, or mean.

The second origin is the Greek *basis*, which strictly means "stepping." This root informs the use of "base" as a term for a starting point or a foundation.

## Technical Applications
The concept of a base as a foundation or starting point is applied in several specialized fields:

*   **Mathematics and Science:** In geometry, a base is the face or line upon which a solid or figure stands. In crystallography, the term is used in the phrase "basal plane." In surveying, a "base line" refers to a distance that has been accurately measured between two points to conduct a survey.
*   **Sports:** The term is used as a foundation or starting point in sports such as baseball and hockey.
*   **Heraldry:** In the study of armorial bearings, the phrase "in base" refers to any emblem or figure positioned in the lowest part of a shield.

## Chemical Properties
In chemistry, a base is defined as a substance that combines with an acid to produce a salt. This relationship makes bases antithetical to acids; when a base is used, it neutralizes an acid. 

The nature of these substances varies by the branch of chemistry:
*   **Inorganic Chemistry:** Bases are almost invariably hydroxides or oxides. During the combination with an acid, water is eliminated.
*   **Organic Chemistry:** Many compounds act as bases, specifically ammonia derivatives, which combine directly with acids.

A characteristic of chemical bases is their ability to reverse certain color reactions associated with acids. For example, a base will turn red litmus blue, a process referred to as an "alkaline reaction."

## Related Architectural Terms
While not always using the word "base" itself, related structural foundations appear in architectural history. In Saxon architecture, the "baluster shaft" divides a window. In the abbey at St Albans in England, some of these shafts are believed to have originated from an old Saxon church, to which Norman capitals and bases, along with plain cylindrical Norman shafts, were later added.
