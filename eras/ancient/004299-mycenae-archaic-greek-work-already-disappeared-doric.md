# Mycenae

Mycenae is an ancient site of prehistoric significance, known for its architectural contributions and the antiquities recovered from its ruins. Its remains provide insight into early Greek construction techniques and the development of artistic decoration.

## Architecture and Construction
The architectural remains at Mycenae demonstrate specific early Greek approaches to structural support and entryways. In primitive Greek work, the base of a column or shaft—originally designed as a raised slab to keep timber columns dry—took the form of three small steps at Mycenae. This differs from the stone plinths found at Tiryns and in Crete.

The site is also notable for its doorway construction. In the entrance doorway of the tomb of Agamemnon at Mycenae, the structure reaches a height of 18 feet. A characteristic feature of early Greek doorways, also seen at Tiryns, was the use of jambs that sloped inwards to reduce the bearing of the lintel. Consequently, the opening was narrower at the top than at the bottom; in the tomb of Agamemnon, the width at the top is approximately 6 inches less than at the bottom.

## Decorative Arts and Antiquities
Mycenae is a primary source of prehistoric antiquities. A famous collection of these objects, discovered by Schliemann, is currently held in the National Museum in Athens. These "Mycenaean" objects are categorized alongside finds from Tiryns, Attica, and Nauplia.

Decorative elements from Mycenae also influenced later Greek art. The north portico of the Erechtheum (420 B.C.) features a band with rosettes that recall early decorative features found in Mycenae and Crete.

## Transition to Archaic Greek Work
The architectural styles present at Mycenae shifted as Greek architecture evolved. While the three-step base was a feature of primitive work at the site, this element disappeared in the Doric order by the time of archaic Greek work. In contrast, the Ionic and Corinthian orders of the archaic period utilized more richly moulded bases, such as those found in the temple of Apollo at Branchidae in Milesia.
