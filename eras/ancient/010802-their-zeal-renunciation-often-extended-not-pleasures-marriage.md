# Renunciation of Pleasures, Marriage, and Property

The practice of renunciation—the intentional rejection of worldly comforts, social ties, and material possessions—has appeared across various philosophical and religious traditions. While some forms of asceticism focused specifically on the avoidance of luxury, other movements extended this zeal to the repudiation of marriage, cleanliness, and social conventions.

## The Greek Cynics
The Greek Cynics were significant precursors to later ascetic traditions, such as Christian monasticism. In their effort to imitate the independence and rugged strength of Socrates, the Cynics adopted extreme behaviors that often bordered on caricature. They lived as individualists and "citizens of the world," rejecting loyalty to the ancient city-state during a period of its decay.

The Cynic lifestyle was characterized by a total renunciation of riches, honors, and pleasures. They affected the appearance of beggars, carrying only a wallet and a staff. Their zeal for renunciation was not limited to property and marriage; it extended to the rejection of good manners, knowledge, and cleanliness. This stood in stark contrast to the views of thinkers like Aristotle and Plato, who maintained that the perfect life was that of a householder and citizen.

## Early Christian and Sectarian Asceticism
The eschewing of property and marriage was practiced by the Therapeutic and Jewish Essene sects. Within Christianity, those who specifically repudiated the use of wine and marriage were known as Encratites. Rather than forming a single sect, this represented a widespread impulse.

Early popular apocryphal histories, such as the *Acts of Thecla* (likely dating to the first century), depict the apostles insisting that converts either avoid wedlock entirely or dissolve existing marriages. This repudiation of marital ties by wives or betrothed women frequently led to popular persecution and domestic friction.

By the 4th century, some Syriac churches viewed the married state as incompatible with the perfection of the initiated. Renunciation of marriage was also required for the faithful undergoing lengthy or lifelong penance for sins. Later, as monasticism emerged, celibacy and virginity—alongside the renunciation of money-making and property—were considered essential components of primitive baptism and rigorous penance for the lapsed.

## Philosophical and Moral Perspectives on Pleasure
Other frameworks approach renunciation not as a total rejection of the law of life, but as a means of achieving internal peace. One such moral attitude acknowledges that while pleasure is the law of life, its attainment is not the ultimate end of existence. In this view, the goal of man is to achieve a "pure heart" and peace. This requires a triumph over luxury, passion, ambition, and fear. Through self-control and renunciation, nature provides everything necessary for happiness.

## Legal and Social Restrictions on Marriage
Beyond voluntary asceticism, various societies implemented mandatory periods of renunciation regarding marriage and pleasure due to mourning or legal requirements.

In China, the Penal Code forbade the contracting of marriages during the mourning period for parents, which typically lasted twenty-seven months. During this time, near relatives were also forbidden from playing musical instruments. Following the death of an emperor, national restrictions were imposed: for twelve months, the official classes could not marry or give in marriage (a period reduced to one hundred days for the general public). Additionally, theatres were closed for a period of one hundred days to a year.

In the United States, specifically in Idaho during the late 19th century, legislation was enacted to force the renunciation of specific marital practices. Between 1884 and 1893, various laws required county officers and voters to take test oaths abjuring or renouncing polygamy, bigamy, or "celestial marriage."
