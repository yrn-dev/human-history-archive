# Chronology of Ancient Mesopotamia

The determination of dates for ancient Mesopotamia relies upon various systems of record-keeping, ranging from official administrative lists to later antiquarian research and dynastic compilations. These methods differ significantly between the regions of Assyria and Babylonia.

## Assyrian Chronological Systems
Assyrian chronology is considered certain from 911 B.C. to 666 B.C. This precision is due to the existence of lists of *limmi*, or archons, who gave their names to the years of their office. Several copies of these lists were found in the library of Nineveh; the earliest dates to 911 B.C., and the latest extends to the middle of the reign of Assur-bani-pal. These lists record the beginning of a king's reign and, in some instances, the chief events of the year associated with the archon.

The *limmi* system of reckoning time originated in Assyria and was used during the early days of the monarchy. Evidence of its use extends to the 15th century B.C. in the distant colony of Kara Euyuk near Kaisariyeh (Caesarea) in Cappadocia, as shown by cuneiform tablets. Additionally, astronomical events have helped verify these dates; for example, a solar eclipse visible in the month Sivan in 763 B.C. has been calculated to have occurred on June 15th of that year.

## Babylonian Chronological Systems
Unlike the Assyrians, Babylonia adopted a system where years were dated by the chief events that distinguished them. This practice was similar to the system used in Egypt during the Old Empire. The government determined which event would define the year and notified officials; one such notice from the reign of Samsu-iluna (son of Khammurabi) was discovered in the Lebanon.

Babylonian dates were maintained in registers divided by reigns, which were later used to compile dynastic lists. These lists provided the duration of each king's reign and the length of various dynasties, though the two discovered compilations are in an imperfect state. Beyond these tables, literary chronicles were produced, such as the "Synchronous History of Assyria and Babylonia." Written by an Assyrian, this work contains brief notices regarding the hostile or otherwise relations between the kings of the two countries.

## Dynastic Records of Babylonia
Records indicate several successive dynasties in Babylonia:
*   **Kassite Dynasty:** This dynasty consisted of 36 kings over a period of 576 years and 9 months. Notable figures include Gandis (16 years), Agum-sipak (22 years), and Bitilyasu I (22 years). Kadasman-Bel, a son in this line, corresponded with Amon-hotep (Amenophis) III of Egypt around 1400 B.C. Later rulers include Burna-buryas (22 years), Kuri-galzu III (26 years), and Sagarakti-suryas (13 years).
*   **Dynasty of Isin:** Comprising 11 kings over 132.5 years, starting around 1203 B.C. This dynasty included Nebuchadrezzar I and Merodach-nadin-akhi (22 years).
*   **Dynasty of the Sea-coast:** Beginning around 1070 B.C., including Simbar-sipak (18 years).
*   **Dynasty of Bit-Bazi:** Beginning around 1050 B.C., including [E]-Ulmas-sakin-sumi (17 years).
*   **Dynasty of Elam:** Beginning around 1030 B.C., featuring an Elamite ruler who reigned for 6 years.
*   **Second Dynasty of Babylon:** Beginning around 1025 B.C.

## Antiquarian Research and Sargon I
The dating of early Mesopotamian history is also influenced by the research of Nabonidus, a highly educated monarch and the last ruler of the Chaldean Empire (the Second Babylonian Empire). Nabonidus conducted antiquarian researches and left inscriptions recording his determination that Sargon I dated to 3750 B.C. This traditional date, based on Babylonian records, is still accepted by many authorities.

## Pre-Sumerian and Early Foundations
Excavations at Eridu conducted by Capt. R. Campbell Thompson revealed a Neolithic agricultural stage that predates the use of bronze and the invention of writing. In this stage, earthenware sickles were used to cut crops. Thompson suggests these people were proto-Elamites rather than Sumerians, a theory supported by similar Neolithic remains found at Susa, the chief city of Elam.
