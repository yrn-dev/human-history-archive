# KRETAN LEGENDS: MINOS AND HIS FAMILY

The legends of Krête center largely on the figure of Minôs, a character described in various sources as a god or hero rather than a man. He is identified as the son of Zeus and Europê, the latter being the daughter of Phœnix and born in Krête. Minôs is depicted as the ruler of Knossus and a familiar companion of Zeus, possessing a golden sceptre. In the afterlife, he is described as a judge in the under-world who settles disputes among the dead.

## Family and Descendants
Minôs' brother, Rhadamanthus, also a son of Zeus, shares similar judicial functions and posthumous dignity. Rhadamanthus was conveyed by the Phæacians to Eubœa to inspect the corpse of Tityus and was eventually promoted to the Elysian plain.

The descendants of Minôs include his daughter Ariadnê and his son Deukaliôn. Deukaliôn's son, Idomeneus, later led Krêtan troops to join Agamemnôn's host during the Trojan war. Another grandson of Minôs was Althæmenês, the son of King Katreus. According to Rhodian legend, Althæmenês founded a colony on Rhodes and the temple of the Atabyrian Zeus after fleeing Krête to avoid a prophecy that he would kill his father. Despite these efforts, Althæmenês accidentally killed Katreus during a nocturnal skirmish on the shores of Rhodes.

## The Minôtaur and the Athenian Connection
The legends of Minôs are closely linked to those of Athens. Minôs' anger against Athens followed the death of Androgeos, leading to a requirement that Athens provide victims for the Minôtaur. This cycle of sacrifice ended when Thêseus devoted himself to the task and killed the Minôtaur. During his adventures in Krête, Thêseus carried off Ariadnê, the daughter of Minôs. Ariadnê had previously been the subject of a complicated dance representation fabricated for her by the artist Dædalus in Knossus. Ariadnê eventually died on the island of Dia after being deserted by Thêseus and betrayed by Dionysos to the wrath of Artemis.

## Conflict with Dædalus and the Sicilian Expedition
Minôs' relationship with the artist Dædalus ended in flight, as Dædalus escaped to Sicily, where he left behind various architectural and mechanical works. Minôs launched a large-scale expedition with a fleet and army to retake him from Kokalus. While Kokalus appeared friendly, his three daughters drowned the Krêtan king in a bath of hot water to protect Dædalus. 

Following the death of Minôs, many of his accompanying Krêtans remained in Sicily and founded the town of Minoa. The remaining inhabitants of Krête (excluding the towns of Præsus and Polichna) launched a five-year siege against Kamikus to avenge their king. This expedition ended in failure due to famine. While returning along the Italian coast in the Gulf of Tarentum, a storm destroyed their fleet, forcing the survivors to settle permanently and found Hyria and other cities, becoming the Messapian Iapygians.

## The Wrath of Minôs and Historical Interpretation
Legend holds that the departed Minôs was offended that the Krêtans cooperated in avenging Menelaus, particularly since the Greeks had not aided the Krêtans in their campaign against Kamikus. Consequently, Minôs visited Krête with pestilence and famine after the return of Idomeneus from Troy, causing the population to die out or expatriate. This legendary suffering was later cited by the Krêtans as the reason they could not resist the invasion of Xerxês, a claim they allegedly received encouragement for from the Delphian oracle.

Historians and critics have struggled to reconcile these disparate legends. Some have suggested there were two kings named Minôs—one a lawgiver and judge, the other a "thalassokrat"—but this is viewed by some as a gratuitous conjecture to impose a chronological order on legend. While the Odyssey designates Minôs specifically as the king of Knossus and notes a diversity of languages and inhabitants on the island, it also suggests that the Krêtans continued to practice piracy, contradicting the idea that Minôs had successfully put an end to it.
