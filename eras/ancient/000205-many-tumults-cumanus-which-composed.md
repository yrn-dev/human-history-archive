# Many Tumults Under Cumanus

The administration of Cumanus as procurator of the Roman province of Judea, succeeding Alexander, was marked by significant unrest and several violent tumults involving the Jewish population and the Samaritans. These events eventually led to the intervention of the president of Syria, Ummidius Quadratus, and the judgment of Caesar in Rome.

## The Passover Tumult in Jerusalem
During a feast of unleavened bread (Passover), a large multitude gathered in Jerusalem. To prevent any "innovation" or sedition, Cumanus followed the practice of former procurators by ordering a regiment of the army to stand armed in the temple cloisters. 

On the fourth day of the festival, a soldier committed an impious and indecent act by letting down his breeches and exposing his privy members to the crowd. This action provoked a furious rage among the people, who claimed the act was an affront to God. Some accused Cumanus of inciting the soldier. When Cumanus's exhortations for the people to remain quiet failed, he ordered the entire army to assemble at the fortress of Antonia, which overlooked the temple. 

The sight of the soldiers caused the multitude to flee in haste. Due to the narrowness of the exit passages and the fear that enemies were pursuing them, the people were crowded together. This resulted in a catastrophe where a great number were pressed to death; sources estimate the casualties at between ten thousand and twenty thousand people. The festival consequently turned into a day of lamentation and weeping.

## The Desecration of the Law
A second calamity occurred following a robbery on the public road at Beth-boron. Robbers seized furniture being carried by Stephanus (also referred to as Stephen), a servant of Caesar. In response, Cumanus sent soldiers to neighboring villages to bring inhabitants to him in bonds, charging them with failing to pursue the thieves.

During this operation, a soldier seized the sacred book of the law (the laws of Moses), tore it to pieces, and threw it into the fire while using reproachful and scurrilous language. This act caused great disorder among the Jews, who assembled in large numbers and traveled to Caesarea to make supplication to Cumanus. They demanded that the soldier be punished for the affront offered to God and the laws of their forefathers. Fearing further sedition and acting on the advice of friends, Cumanus ordered the soldier to be beheaded.

## Conflict with the Samaritans
Further unrest arose between the Jews and the Samaritans. The Samaritans accused the Jews of plundering and setting fire to their villages, arguing that such actions showed contempt for Roman authority. Conversely, the Jews claimed the Samaritans were the authors of the fighting and alleged that Cumanus had been corrupted by Samaritan gifts, leading him to ignore the murder of those slain.

The matter was brought before Ummidius Quadratus, the president of Syria. While Quadratus initially deferred his judgment, he later concluded that the Samaritans were the authors of the disturbance. However, upon learning that certain Jews were making innovations—specifically a man named Dortus and four other innovators who persuaded the multitude to revolt from the Romans—Quadratus ordered them put to death.

## Resolution by Quadratus and Caesar
Quadratus took several decisive actions to compose the tumults:
* He crucified those whom Cumanus had previously taken captive.
* He beheaded eighteen Jews involved in the fighting at Lydda.
* He sent several eminent figures to Rome to account for the events before Claudius Caesar, including the high priests Jonathan and Ananias, Artanus the son of Ananias, and Ananus the commander of the temple.
* He ordered Cumanus and the tribune Celer to sail to Rome to provide their accounts.

Upon hearing the testimonies in Rome, Caesar condemned the Samaritans, ordering the execution of three of their most powerful men. Cumanus was banished, and Celer was sent bound to Jerusalem to be tormented, drawn around the city, and beheaded.

## Succession and Aftermath
Following these events, Caesar appointed Felix, the brother of Pallas, as the procurator of Samaria, Galilee, and Perea. Additionally, Agrippa was advanced from Chalcis to a greater kingdom, receiving the tetrarchy of Philip (containing Gaulonitis, Trachonitis, and Batanae), the kingdom of Lysanias, and the province previously governed by Varus.
