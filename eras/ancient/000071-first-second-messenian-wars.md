# First and Second Messenian Wars

The Messenian wars were a series of fierce and bloody conflicts between the Lacedæmonians (Spartans) and the inhabitants of Messenia, a country of considerable strength located in the west of the Peloponnesus near Sparta. Governed by its own kings, Messenia became the subject of Spartan conquest through two primary wars.

## The First Messenian War
The first Messenian war is recorded as lasting twenty years. According to Pausanias, the conflict began in 743 B.C. and concluded in 724 B.C., though some sources suggest the end date was 723 B.C. The war broke out during the reigns of the Spartan kings Polydorus and Theopompus, at a time when Athens still utilized a decennial archon.

The causes alleged by the Spartans included the ravishing of Spartan daughters by Messenians who visited a temple on the border of the two nations, and the subsequent murder of the Spartan king Têleklus at the temple of Artemis Limnatis. However, it is suggested that the true cause may have been a Spartan desire to seize convenient territory and extend their dominion.

During the conflict, the Messenians were led by kings Euphaês and Aristodêmus. The Messenians eventually concentrated their forces on Mount Ithôme, but after a long siege, they were completely conquered. The poet Tyrtæus authenticates the twenty-year duration of the war and the significant services rendered by the Spartan king Theopompus.

## Aftermath of the First War and Helotism
Following their defeat, the Messenians became subjects of Sparta. They were subjected to harsh treatment and "Helotism." The poet Tyrtæus describes the conquered people as being "like asses, worn down by heavy burdens." They were forced to give half of their field produce to their masters and were required to travel to Sparta in the "garb of woe" to serve as mourners during the deaths of principal persons and kings.

## The Second Messenian War
The second Messenian war arose from a revolt by the descendants of the conquered Messenians against this oppressive yoke. Dates for this conflict vary; Pausanias places the start in 685 B.C. and the end in 668 B.C., while other records suggest a date of 648–631 B.C. 

The Spartans suffered during this war, and the poet Tyrtæus, an ally of Sparta, was personally engaged in the conflict. His poetry had a powerful ethical effect and great influence over the Spartan mind, utilizing the musical susceptibilities of the Spartans. During this period, the Spartans acquired the country west of Taygetus and punished Aristokratês, the king of the Arcadian Orchomenus, for treason.

The war is associated with the figure of Aristomenês, noted for his narrow escapes and chivalrous exploits. The second war ended with the Messenians being conquered once again.

## Historical Sources and Traditions
The history of these wars is characterized by a lack of ancient traditions and frequent contradictions. Much of the narrative provided by Pausanias, which was borrowed from the poet Rhianus, is considered by some to be undeserving of credit or reflective of late romance. The most indisputable facts are derived from the poet Tyrtæus.

A significant point of contention among historians concerns the hero Aristomenês. Because the Messenians were not re-established in the Peloponnesus for centuries, many "traditions" were created later. Consequently, sources disagree on which war Aristomenês fought in:
* Diodôrus, Myrôn, and Clemens Alexand. place him in the first war.
* Rhianus and Pausanias place him in the second war.
* Some accounts suggest he killed or wounded the Spartan king Theopompus during the first war.

## Re-establishment of Messênê
The Messenians remained without a fixed position in Greece until 369 B.C., when Epameinondas inflicted a capital wound on Sparta by re-establishing the Messenians and founding the city of Messênê on Mount Ithomê. This new city incorporated descendants of the old Messenians, various Helots, and miscellaneous settlers. During the foundation ceremony, the gods and heroes of the Messenian race, particularly Aristomenês, were reverentially invoked.
