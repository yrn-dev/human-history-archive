# Assyria and Babylonia

Assyria and Babylonia were sister-states that developed on the banks of the Tigris and Euphrates. While they were similar in race and language, they differed essentially in character. Historically and geographically, the two empires were closely intertwined, with the power of one often serving as a measure of the weakness of the other.

## Political and Social Character
Babylonia was characterized as a land of agriculturists and merchants. Its monarchy was established when a revolution raised priests to the throne, and the Babylonian king remained a priest under the control of a powerful hierarchy. In contrast, Assyria was described as an organized camp. Its dynasties were founded by successful generals, and the Assyrian king functioned as an autocratic general of an army. In early periods, the king was supported by a feudal nobility, and from the reign of Tiglath-pileser III onwards, an elaborate bureaucracy was established.

Socially, neither state utilized a caste system. However, the military aristocracy of Assyria served as the counterpart to the priesthood of Babylonia. While the Babylonian priesthood included various classes, such as doctors, the Assyrians focused on military organization. Assyria is credited with the first organization of a standing army, which was raised in part by conscription. Under the kings of the second Assyrian empire, the military evolved; cavalry superseded chariots, and Tiglath-pileser III introduced high boots and saddles for riders, while Sennacherib created a corps of slingers. The *tartan*, or commander-in-chief, held a rank second only to the king.

Both societies possessed a large population of slaves, followed by commercial and agricultural classes, though these latter groups were less numerous in Assyria. Scribes held a more important social position in Assyria than in Babylonia. Both nations employed poets, musicians, money-lenders, and artisans.

## Historical Development and Interdependence
Babylonia is regarded as the mother country of Assyria, a relationship explicitly stated in the tenth chapter of Genesis. Assyria began as an offshoot of Babylonia to the north-west and was apparently colonized before the second millennium. For much of its history, with the exception of some seven or eight centuries, Assyria may be described as a dependency or province of Babylon.

The city of Babylon became a metropolis around 2285 B.C. under Khammurabi. During this era, Samsi-Hadad (or Samsi-Raman) appears to have been a vassal-prince at Assur. The Assyrian monarchy's foundation is attributed to Zulilu, who lived after Bel-kapkapi or Belkabi (1900 B.C.). 

Assyria eventually grew in power at the expense of Babylonia. A pivotal moment occurred when Assur-yuballidh of Assyria married his daughter to the Kassite king of Babylonia. After the Kassite faction murdered the king, Assur-yuballidh intervened to place Burna-buryas on the throne. Following Burna-buryas's 22-year reign, Shalmaneser I (1300 B.C.) openly claimed supremacy in western Asia. His son, Tukulti-In-aristi, later conquered Babylon and executed its king, Bitilyasu, establishing Assyria as the mistress of the oriental world. By the 9th and 8th centuries B.C., Assyria had become the suzerain of its parent, Babylon.

## Culture, Religion, and Art
Assyrian culture, literature, arts, and sciences were derived from Babylonia. The religion of both states was based on an astral theology, which assumed a close link between heavenly movements and earthly occurrences. This system identified deities with heavenly bodies: Marduk with Jupiter, Ishtar with Venus, Nergal with Mars, Nebo with Mercury, and Ninib with Saturn. 

The Assyrian pantheon was identical to the Babylonian one, with the exception that Assur substituted for Marduk. Because of the warlike nature of the Assyrians, their gods were conceived as "little Assurs" alongside the great protector of arms, the big Assur. Rituals, hymns, and myths from Babylonia were transferred to northern cities such as Assur, Nineveh, Calah, Arbela, and Harran, though they underwent some modifications.

In architecture, Assyria was initially a "servile pupil" of Babylonia, using brick for palaces and temples even though stone was naturally available in the north. Babylonian structures were massive, shapeless brick buildings featuring frescoes, enamelled tiles, and columns. Later Assyrian architects began using stone, lining palace walls with sculptured and coloured stone slabs. These bas-reliefs evolved through three periods: the vigorous and simple style of Assur-nazir-pal III, the realistic style of Sargon, and the refined but less bold style of Assur-bani-pal. Babylonia, lacking stone, focused on figures in the round and achieved high perfection in gem-cutting.

## Decline and Fall
The collapse of Assyria was sudden, occurring after the fighting population was drained during the age of Assur-bani-pal. Following his death, the empire broke up. Under Assur-etil-ilani, Scythians penetrated Assyria and burned Calah. The last king of Assyria was likely Sin-sar-iskun (or Sin-sarra-uzur). 

War eventually broke out between the last Assyrian king and Nabopolassar of Babylonia. With the assistance of the Scythian king Cyaxares, the Babylonians captured and destroyed Nineveh. This event marked the end of the Assyrian empire in 606 B.C. Babylonia survived longer, eventually falling to Cyrus in 555 B.C., though it continued to influence religion and culture for many centuries thereafter.
