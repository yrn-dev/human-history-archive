# EUBŒA AND THE CYCLADES

The regions of Eubœa and the Cyclades form a significant part of the Ionic portion of Hellas, which also includes Athens and various Ionian cities and colonies along the coast of Asia Minor. These areas are characterized by their complex geography, early commercial power, and strategic importance in the Aegean.

## Geography and Geological Origin
The island of Eubœa is defined by a long, lofty, and naked mountain backbone. This range is viewed as a continuation of the mountain chains of Othrys and those stretching from the eastern extremity of Olympus through Ossa and Pelion to the south-eastern corner of Thessaly. To the north-east of Eubœa, the islands of Skyrus, Skopelus, and Skiathus further mark the outlying peaks of this same range.

The Cyclades comprise a numerous group of islands situated between the eastern coast of the Peloponnesus, the north-western coast of Krete, and the southernmost promontory of Eubœa. These islands are categorized by their geological origins:
* **Attic Prolongations:** Islands that are south-easterly extensions of the mountain system of Attica include Keôs, Kythnus, Serīphus, Pholegandrus, Sikinus, Gyarus, Syra, Paros, and Antiparos.
* **Eubœan Prolongations:** Islands extending from the Eubœan system include Andros, Tênos, Mykonos, Dêlos, Naxos, and Amorgos.
* **Volcanic Origin:** A third group, consisting of Mêlos, Thêra, and Kimôlus, appears to be of volcanic origin.

Historically, these islands were referred to as the Cyclades and the Sporades. The term "Cyclades" specifically denoted those islands immediately surrounding the sacred island of Dêlos, while "Sporades" referred to those that were more scattered. In more recent times, the title Cyclades has been applied to the entire group.

## Eubœa: Towns and Commerce
Eubœa was peopled with six or seven towns, most notably Chalkis and Eretria. In early times, both Chalkis and Eretria, along with Naxos, possessed significant power. However, Chalkis and Eretria were engaged in an early war, with each city establishing extensive alliances.

The two cities were active in commerce and the establishment of colonies. This commercial activity led to the creation of the Euboic scale of money and weight. This was one of three different Grecian scales, the others being the Attic and Æginæan scales, each maintaining a specific ratio to one another.

## The Sacred Island of Dêlos
Dêlos served as a sacred center for the Ionic Greeks, being the birthplace of Apollo and Artemis. In the days of early freedom, the island hosted solemn periodical festivals that were crowded and wealthy. However, these festivals declined around 560 B.C. Evidence regarding early Ionic life during this period is found in the Homeric Hymn to the Delian Apollo.

The religious significance of Dêlos was recognized even by foreign invaders. During a campaign, the Persian commander Datis treated the island with reverence, prohibiting his fleet from touching the island and offering a magnificent sacrifice at the altar, partly to satisfy the religious feelings of the Ionic Greeks within his own armament.

## Naxos and the Cyclades in Conflict
Naxos is the largest of the Cyclades and was historically wealthy and prosperous. It possessed a large population of slaves and freemen and was defended by a force of eight thousand heavy-armed infantry (hoplites) and armed ships.

The island's wealth made it a target for Persian expansion. Aristagoras, aided by Naxian exiles who had been expelled by a popular rising, proposed to the satrap Artaphernês that the conquest of Naxos would lead to the easy reduction of other Cyclades islands—such as Tênos, Andros, and Paros—and eventually the large island of Eubœa. Darius subsequently granted a fleet of two hundred ships under the command of Megabatês to pursue these conquests.

Later, Naxos became a member of the confederacy of Delos. Approximately thirty years after its period of peak military strength, Naxos led the first breach of union in the confederacy by revolting. This revolt was likely driven by the jealousy of the larger islands toward Athens and a belief that they no longer required the confederacy's protection. Following a siege by Athens and confederate forces, Naxos was forced to surrender, its fortifications were razed, and it was reduced to a tributary subject.

## Regional Power Shifts
Control of the Cyclades shifted between various Mediterranean powers. During the 3rd century B.C., the Ptolemies of Egypt maintained a large fleet and held overlordship of the islands. This control was contested by the Antigonids, who wrested the Cyclades from the Ptolemies after winning two major sea battles at Cos (c. 256) and Andros (227).
