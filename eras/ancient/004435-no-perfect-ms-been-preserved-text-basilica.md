# Basilica

The term "basilica" refers to both a specific architectural form used for civil and religious purposes and a significant code of law from the East Roman Empire.

## The Basilica Code of Law
The Basilica was a code of law written in the Greek language. It was created during the 9th century to resolve legal uncertainty within the East Roman Empire. This uncertainty had arisen because 6th-century jurists held conflicting opinions on how to interpret the legislation of Emperor Justinian, leading to a system of teaching that stripped that legislation of its authority and left imperial judges without clear rules for their decisions.

While Emperor Leo the Isaurian attempted a reform, his efforts were thought to increase uncertainty. The task of purifying the administration of justice and creating an intelligible code was instead achieved by Basil the Macedonian. Although there is controversy regarding Basil's personal role in framing the code, he formally abrogated ancient laws that had fallen into desuetude. It is believed he revised the laws that remained in force, dividing them into forty books. This code was later enlarged and distributed into sixty books by his son, Leo the Philosopher, who appointed a commission of jurists led by Sympathius, the captain of the body-guard, to conduct the revision. A further revision is attributed to Leo's successor, Constantine Porphyrogenitus, according to the 12th-century canonist Theodorus Balsamon, though most authorities disagree with this claim.

## Preservation and Publication of the Code
No perfect manuscript of the Basilica's text has been preserved. The code's existence was largely ignored by Western European jurists until 1533, when Viglius Zuichemus highlighted its importance to Roman law in the preface to his edition of the Greek *Paraphrase of Theophilus*. 

A century later, Charles Annibal Fabrot published an edition of the sixty books in seven volumes. This work was supported by Louis XIII of France, who provided the royal printing-press and an annual stipend of two thousand livres to Fabrot. Despite its merit, this edition was an imperfect representation of the original. A more complete, newly-restored text of the sixty books was published in Leipzig between 1833 and 1870, edited by G. E. Heimbach and K. W. E. Heimbach.

The lack of an intact original text is attributed to the great bulk of the code, which hindered the multiplication of copies. Furthermore, the need for full copies was reduced by the publication of *encheiridia* and synopses composed by eminent jurists. Evidence of the scarcity of the text is found in a preserved letter from Mark, the patriarch of Alexandria, to Theodoras Balsamon in the 12th century, stating that the patriarch was unable to procure a copy of the work.

## Architectural Form and Civil Basilicas
Derived from the Greek word *basilikê*, a basilica was originally used as a place of business for merchants and as a court of law. The first known basilica in Rome was the Basilica Porcia, erected in 184 B.C. by the censor M. Porcius Cato, who may have imported the design after visiting Athens. Other examples include the Basilica Pauli, built by L. Æmilius Paulus (consul B.C. 50), as well as the Basilica Julia and Basilica Ulpia in Rome. The basilica at Pompeii, dating possibly to the 2nd century B.C., is better preserved than many others.

Architecturally, the "normal" form of the civil basilica was essentially a columned structure. It typically featured a central space surrounded by galleries, generally in two stories, and an arrangement for clerestory lighting.

## Christian Basilicas
Many great Roman churches are called basilicas and resemble the ancient civil form. However, they differ in key respects: early Christian basilicas in the west rarely had galleries over side aisles and were dominated by a semi-dome apse terminating the central nave—a feature not found in normal columned civil basilicas.

Notable examples include:
*   **S. Sabina (Aventine):** Dating from approximately A.D. 430, it preserves a primitive aspect with an open roof, antique Corinthian columns, and unique early Christian carved wooden doors.
*   **S. Clemente:** Notable for retaining original ritual arrangements. It features an *atrium* with a *cantharus* (ablution fountain), a 9th-century marble screen for the *chorus cantorum*, and an octagonal gospel-ambo.
*   **S. Lorenzo fuori le Mura:** Originally constructed in A.D. 432 with a short nave of six bays, it was altered in the 13th century by Honorius III, who erected a twelve-bay nave on the site of the original apse.
*   **Torcello (near Venice):** Originally built in the 7th century and repaired c. A.D. 1000, it preserves the arrangement of seats in the apse, including a central bishop's throne and six rows of stone benches for presbyters.
*   **Carthaginian sites:** Excavations by Pere Delattre have uncovered a Christian basilica on the hill of the Petit Seminaire and the great Christian basilica of Damus-el-Karita.
