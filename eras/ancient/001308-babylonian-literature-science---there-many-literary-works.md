# Babylonian Literature and Science

The intellectual legacy of Babylonia is characterized by a diverse array of literary works, a sophisticated approach to law, and early advancements in astronomy and astral theology that influenced the wider ancient world.

## Literary Works
A variety of Babylonian literary titles have been preserved, including both epic poetry and mythological narratives. One of the most prominent works is the *Epic of Gilgamesh*. Composed by Sin-liqi-unninni and arranged according to an astronomical principle, this work consists of twelve books. Each division of the epic details a single adventure from the career of Gilgamesh, though the overall story is a composite product, and some adventures may have been artificially attached to the central figure.

Another significant epic focuses on the Creation, designed to glorify Bel-Merodach through a description of his conflict with Tiamat, the dragon of chaos. This narrative begins with the creation of the world from the primeval deep and the birth of the gods of light. It describes a struggle between these gods of light and the powers of darkness, culminating in the victory of Merodach. After cleaving Tiamat asunder to form the earth from one half of her body and the heaven from the other, Merodach established the sun, moon, and stars, providing them with laws they were never to transgress. Following this, plants, animals, and finally man were created. In this account, Merodach replaces Ea, who is identified as the creator in older legends and is credited with fashioning man from clay.

## Law and Custom
The study of Babylonian law is supported by extensive, though not exhaustive, material. This includes thousands of "contracts," which encompass receipts, bonds, accounts, conveyances, and various deeds. Most significant among these are the actual legal decisions rendered by judges in law courts. Supplementary information is provided by private letters, royal charters, rescripts, historical inscriptions, and general literature. Even lexicographical and grammatical works, created to aid the study of ancient literature, contain short sentences or extracts regarding custom and law, including the preservation of the "Sumerian Family Laws."

The discovery of the Code of Hammurabi (also referred to as the Code of Khammurabi) allowed for a more systematic study of the legal system than was possible through the classification of other materials. While fragments of a later code have been published, several points of Babylonian law remain without evidence.

## Science and Astral Theology
The Babylonians cultivated astronomy at an early period. It is probable that the knowledge of the movements and nature of heavenly bodies spread across Asia from the Babylonians. This astral theology had a general effect on the ancient world, including the Romans and the Greeks.

The influence of Babylonian-Assyrian religion and astral theology was particularly profound among the Semites. The civilization of the Euphrates valley provided a significant impetus for the purification of the old Semite religion, which was shared by the Hebrews and various branches of nomadic Arabs for a long time. Traces of direct borrowing from Babylonia are discernible in the Old Testament's myths, legends, and traditions. Furthermore, indirect influences are noteworthy in the Psalms, the prophetical books, and the "Wisdom Literature."

These influences extended into the New Testament period. Babylonian elements, though transformed and modified, are largely present in early Christian gnosticism. Additionally, many scholars attribute the growth of apocalyptic literature to a recrudescence of views originating in the astral-theology of Babylonian and Assyrian priests.
