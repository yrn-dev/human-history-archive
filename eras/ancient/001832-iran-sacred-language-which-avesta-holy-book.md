# Iran and the Sacred Language of the Avesta

Iran, also known as Aria, historically comprised a vast territory bounded by the Indus to the east, the Persian Gulf and Indian Ocean to the south, the line of Mount Zagros to the west, and the Caspian Sea and Oxus (Amu Darya) to the north. The region is characterized by a high, cold plateau that is largely a salt and sandy desert, though it contains eminently fertile areas where irrigation is possible. Historically, the population consisted of both settled inhabitants and wandering nomadic tribes.

## The Aryan Origins and the Iranian People
The inhabitants of this region belonged to the Eastern branch of the Indo-Europeans, who called themselves *arya*. Scholars use the term "Indo-Aryan" for those who settled in India and "Iranian" for those who remained in Persia and Bactria. It is acknowledged by linguists that the Aryans likely originated in Europe. The eastern part of Iran is believed to be the region where the Aryans lived as a single people before separating into Indians and Iranians.

Bactria, located between the Oxus and the Hindu Kush range with its capital at Bactra (Balkh), was the home of one of the Iranian tribes. This mountainous, fertile country is regarded as the place where the prophet Zoroaster preached and gained his first followers, from which his religion subsequently spread to the western parts of Iran.

## The Avesta and the Zend Language
The holy book of Zoroastrianism is the Avesta, a term meaning "word," "oracle," or "revelation." It is not a single work but a collection of books representing the revelation of Ormuzd. This collection is sometimes referred to as the *Zendavesta* or simply the *Zend*. The term *Zend* signifies "life" or "living" and refers to either the collection of canonical books or the language in which they are written.

The Zend was the ancient language of Media and is noted for its affinity with the dialects of Georgia and Armenia. While some early critics suggested the language was an invention of the Magi, it has been proven to be a living, spoken tongue. Linguists have noted a strong affinity between Zend and Sanskrit, with some viewing them as sister dialects and others seeing Zend as a derivative of Sanskrit. The language of the commentary on these texts is known as Pehlvi, which is more modern than Zend but has long since ceased to be a living language.

## Teachings and Social Structure in the Zend Avesta
The *Zend Avesta* describes a social organization for the ancient inhabitants of Iran based on a fourfold division:
*   Priests
*   Warriors
*   Agriculturists
*   Artificers

Additionally, the text speaks of a sevenfold division corresponding to the seven *amschespands*, or servants of Ormuzd. This structure is attributed to the tradition of the golden age of Diemschid (or Jemshid) rather than being an invention of Zoroaster.

The priestly caste, the Magi, was further subdivided into *Herbeds* (disciples), *Mobeds* (masters), and *Destur Mobeds* (complete masters). Only the *Destur Mobeds* were permitted to read the liturgies of Ormuzd, predict the future, and carry sacred objects, including the *barsom* (a bunch of twigs), the *havan* (cup), and the *costi* (girdle).

## Rituals and Traditions
The Avesta contains instructions regarding sacred rituals. In the *Vendidad*, the use of incense is mentioned; the priests of the Persians burnt incense five times a day on sacred fire altars. The specific incense used is called *vohu gaono*, which has been identified as either benzoin or frankincense.

Iranian tradition, as preserved in the Avesta and Firdousi's *Shahnama*, includes myths and stories of heroes located in eastern Iran. These narratives often depict the kings of Iran fighting against the Turanians, reflecting an eternal feud between the peasants of Iran and the nomads of Turan based on geographical conditions.
