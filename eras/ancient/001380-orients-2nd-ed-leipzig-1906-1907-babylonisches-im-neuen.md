# Orients and Babylonian Studies

The study of the ancient Orient, specifically the civilizations of Babylonia and Assyria, is documented through a vast array of specialized literature covering religion, law, linguistics, and historical chronology. These works rely heavily on cuneiform texts, including tablets from the British Museum and various inscriptions from Western Asia.

## Religion and Mythology
The religious landscape of Babylonia and Assyria has been analyzed by numerous scholars. Key general works include L. W. King's *Babylonian Religion and Mythology* (1899) and T. G. Pinches' *Religion of Babylonia and Assyria* (1906). Other significant contributions include Morris Jastrow jun.'s *Religion Babyloniens und Assyriens* (1904) and A. H. Sayce's *Religions of Ancient Egypt and Babylonia* (1902).

Specialized research has focused on specific aspects of spiritual life:
*   **Magic and Divination:** L. W. King authored *Babylonian Magic and Sorcery* (1896), while A. Boissier produced *Documents assyriens relatifs aux presages* (1894-1897) and *Choix de textes relatifs a la divination assyro-babylonienne* (1905-1906). Ch. Fossey's *La Magie assyrienne* (1902) and K. L. Tallqvist's *Die assyrische Beschwoerungsserie Maql[=u]* (1895) further explore these practices.
*   **Spirits and Hymns:** R. C. Thompson wrote *Devils and Evil Spirits of Babylonia* (1903-1904), and G. A. Reisner published *Sumerisch-babylonische Hymnen* (1896).
*   **Astrology:** Virolleaud's *L'Astrologie chaldeenne* (1906-) and Craig's *Astrological-Astronomical Texts* (1892) address the celestial studies of the region.
*   **Specific Texts:** Notable monographs include L. W. King's *The Seven Tablets of Creation* (1902) and Paul Haupt's *Das babylonische Nimrodepos*.

## Law and Legal Documents
The legal systems of the ancient Orient are reconstructed from contracts and official codes. General studies on Babylonian and Assyrian laws, contracts, and letters were published by C. H. W. Johns (1904), and the transfer of property was detailed in Johns' three-volume *Assyrian Deeds and Documents* (1898). Other researchers such as Oppert, Menant, J. Kohler, F. E. Peiser, and Br. Meissner have contributed to the understanding of Babylonian legal life and private law.

A central focus of legal study is the Code of Hammurabi, King of Babylon (dated by some sources to approximately 2250 B.C.). The *Editio princeps* was provided by V. Scheil in 1902. Further analyses of these laws were conducted by H. Winckler, D. H. Mueller, J. Kohler, F. E. Peiser, and R. F. Harper. S. A. Cook also provided a comparative study titled *The Laws of Moses and the Code of Hammurabi* (1903).

## Linguistics and Proper Names
Research into the formation of proper names is divided by historical period. For the Sumerian period, specifically the time of the kings of Ur and Nisin, Huber's *Die Personennamen in den Keilschrifturkunden* (1907) is a primary resource. Ranke's *Early Babylonian Proper Names* (1905) covers the old Babylonian period, while Tallqvist's *Neu-Babylonisches Namenbuch* (1905) addresses the neo-Babylonian period.

## Chronology and Historical Records
The determination of chronological systems remains a subject of scholarly debate. Discrepancies exist in the recorded reigns of various rulers. For example, one compiler assigns 30 years of reign to Sin-muballidh, whereas a list of dates from the time of Ammi-zadok's accession assigns 20 years. Similarly, Khammurabi is credited with 55 years of reign by one source and 43 by another, while Samsu-iluna is credited with 35 years versus 38. Additionally, some records omit the seven-year reign of the Assyrian king Tukulti-In-aristi at Babylon.
