# The Mind in Contemplating God

In the philosophical and mystical traditions detailed within the archive, the process of the human mind contemplating the divine is often described as a progression through specific stages, aspects, or modifications. These frameworks vary significantly between the scholastic mysticism of Bonaventura, the metaphysical inquiries of Plato, and the theological developments of early Christianity.

## The Stages of Contemplation in Bonaventura

Bonaventura presents a mode of speculation that is mystical and Platonizing, placing a higher value on the living power of the heart and the affections than on purely intellectual elements. In his philosophy, the supreme end of human life is the achievement of union with God, characterized by intense absorbing love and contemplation or intellect. While this union is a hope for the futurity and cannot be fully reached in the present life, the mind employs specific grades to move toward it.

The mind in contemplating God possesses three distinct aspects, stages, or grades. The first of these is the senses, which provide empirical knowledge of the external world and allow the mind to discern the *vestigia*, or traces, of the divine. To move beyond these and obtain divine illumination, the soul must utilize specific means: prayer, the exercise of virtues to make the soul fit for divine light, and meditation. This process of meditation may eventually rise to a state of ecstatic union with God.

## Platonic Modifications of the Divine Nature

The Athenian sage Plato explored the mysterious nature of the Deity through meditation and traditional knowledge from the priests of Egypt. Plato struggled to reconcile the simple unity of the divine essence with the infinite variety of successive ideas that form the intellectual world, as well as how an incorporeal Being could mold the independent chaos of the physical world.

To address these difficulties, Plato considered the divine nature under a threefold modification:
1. The first cause.
2. The reason, or *Logos*.
3. The soul or spirit of the universe.

In the Platonic system, these three original principles were sometimes represented as three Gods linked by an ineffable generation. The *Logos*, in particular, was viewed as the Creator and Governor of the world and the Son of an Eternal Father. These doctrines were treated as secret knowledge within the gardens of the academy, with some disciples suggesting they could only be fully understood after thirty years of assiduous study.

## Theological Aspects and the Trinity

The concept of the divine appearing in different aspects or persons was a central point of contention in early Christian theology, leading to various schools of thought:

*   **The Sabellians:** This group taught that Christ was a mode or aspect of God. In this view, God functioned as Creator, Saviour, and Comforter, similar to how one man might simultaneously be a father, a guest, and a trustee.
*   **The Trinitarians:** Led by figures such as Athanasius, this group taught that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost were three distinct Persons, yet remained one God.
*   **The Arians:** This group followed Arius, who taught that Christ was less than God.

These differences regarding the constitution of the Deity often interwove with international disputes and politics, resulting in official persecutions, banishments, and riots.

## Comparative Divine Aspects in Other Traditions

The archive also notes the use of "aspects" to reconcile different deities in other cultures. In Hellenic Alexandria, the process of *theocrasia* (the fusing of one god with another) was used to make gods more universal. For example, the Egyptian god Osiris was identified with the sacred bull Apis to become Serapis (Jupiter-Serapis). This trinity included Serapis, the goddess Isis (Hathor), and the child-god Horus. These three were viewed as distinct aspects of the one God, and were simultaneously three and one.

In Babylonia and Assyria, different gods were invested with specific traits or aspects. Shamash, the sun-god, was associated with justice, while Marduk was portrayed as full of mercy and kindness. Ea was seen as the protector of mankind.
