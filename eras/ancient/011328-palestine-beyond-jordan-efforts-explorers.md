# Palestine and Beyond Jordan

The regions of Palestine and the lands beyond Jordan have been the subject of extensive geographical survey, archaeological research, and historical analysis. These efforts, conducted by English surveyors and various international explorers, have primarily served to elucidate the history of the region rather than to foster new commercial enterprises or increase material wealth.

## Geographical and Historical Definitions
The term Palestine is used in various contexts, sometimes referring to all of Judea—including cities such as Jerusalem, Samaria, and Caesarea Palestina—and other times referring to the land of Canaan. The land of Canaan extended along the Mediterranean and included chief cities such as Gaza, Ascalon, Azotus, Accaron, and Gath. The region is watered by the river Jordan.

The "land beyond Jordan" (trans-Jordan) has been distinguished from the land of Canaan in various historical and biblical contexts. Some interpretations suggest that this area constituted one-seventh of the total land of Canaan or the land inhabited by the twelve tribes together. Despite its size, the land beyond Jordan was noted for being peculiarly fruitful and suitable for pasturage, capable of maintaining approximately one-fifth of the whole people.

## Exploration and Survey Efforts
Significant geographical and archaeological data have been gathered through the work of the Palestine Exploration Fund, which published various reports and quarterly statements between 1871 and 1907. Notable contributions include:
*   **Surveys and Travels:** Works such as G. Schumacher's studies on the Jaul[=a]n (1888), Northern Ajlun (1890), and his 1886 account *Across the Jordan*.
*   **Regional Research:** Sir R. F. Burton and C. F. T. Drake authored *Unexplored Syria* (1872), while Rev. W. Ewing conducted a journey in the Hauran that resulted in a large collection of inscriptions.
*   **International Expeditions:** In 1900, an American-sponsored survey of the Hauran and neighboring regions was directed by Dr. Enno Littmann, with results beginning to be published in 1906. Other researchers include U. J. Seetzen, Rev. J. L. Porter, and the archaeological voyage of Rene Dussaud and Frederic Macler to the Safa and Djabel ed-Dr[=u]z in 1901.

## Regional Transitions and Migrations
The history of the region is marked by the movement of peoples across the Jordan. The Edomites, facing constant westward pressure from eastern Arabs after the decline of Mesopotamian kingdoms, were forced across the Jordan-Araba depression. This migration led to the establishment of a cis-Jordanic Idumaea in western Palestine. Josephus defines Idumaea as encompassing Amalekitis (the land of Amalek), Gobalitis (the original Mount Seir), and Akrabatine. While Jerome describes Idumaea as extending from Petra to Beit Jibrin, later accounts by Ptolemy restrict the name entirely to the cis-Jordanic district, with the old trans-Jordanic region being absorbed into Arabia.

Additionally, the region serves as a historical marker where the Moabites and Ammonites settled across the Jordan, while the Hebrews commenced their independent history, with Abraham moving south to Hebron.

## Military and Political History
The region has been a site of significant imperial conflict. The king of Assyria, Tiglath-Pileser, took advantage of a proposal from Ahaz, king of Judah, to add Syria and Palestine to his empire. During this campaign, the Assyrians took Damascus and seized all territories belonging to the kingdom of Israel that lay beyond Jordan, as well as all of Galilee.

In more recent history, the region remained a strategic point during the Great War. Military fronts curved from the Palestine coast in a loop that ran north of Jerusalem, crossed Jordan, and extended to the hills of Moab.

## Traditions and Landmarks
Local tradition and geography often intersect in the region. The *Jebel Usdum* at the southwest end of the Dead Sea, consisting of crystallized salt with pinnacles and pillars, is associated with the story of Lot and his wife. In Arabic tradition, the daughters of Lot are named Ra'wa and Zahy. Some identifications, such as those by Paton, link Lot-Lotan with *Ruten*, one of the Egyptian names for Palestine.
