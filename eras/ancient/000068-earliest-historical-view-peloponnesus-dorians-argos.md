# Earliest Historical View of Peloponnesus: Dorians in Argos and Neighboring Cities

The early historical landscape of the Peloponnesus, specifically around 776 B.C., differs significantly from the later period of Spartan dominance. While Sparta eventually became the primary power in both the Peloponnesus and all of Hellas, the earliest records of real Grecian history present Argos as the preeminent seat of Dorian power in the peninsula.

## Early Dorian Distribution and the Role of Argos
At the dawn of historical cognizance, Argos served as the metropolis for a confederacy of neighboring Dorian cities, including Trœzên, Epidaurus, Phlius, and Sikyôn. These towns were connected to Argos through a union that was both semi-political and semi-religious. According to Herodotus, the influence of Argos once extended across the entire eastern coast of the Peloponnesus down to Cape Malea, encompassing the island of Cythêra and other islands.

During this era, the comparative importance of Dorian establishments followed a specific hierarchy: Argos held the first position, followed by Sparta in second, and Messênê in third. This early preëminence was so deeply ingrained in the Argeian memory that they later maintained pretensions to the headship of the Greeks as a matter of right, even after Spartan growth had extruded them from their leading position. The rise of Spartan power is characterized as a succession of encroachments upon the territory and influence of Argos.

## The Legend of the Herakleids
The Dorian presence in the Peloponnesus is explained through the legendary "Return of the Herakleids." According to mythical accounts, the Herakleids—who had previously existed in a state of exile and low condition—reappeared as a powerful force in alliance with the Dorians. This alliance involved three Dorian tribes.

The legendary account describes Têmenus, Aristodêmus, and Kresphontês invading the Peloponnêsus across the gulf of Corinth, guided by Oxylus. During this invasion, the prophet Karnus was slain by Hippotês. The lands of the Peloponnêsus were subsequently divided among the invaders. This mythical narrative provides the Dorians with a title to the land, though Plato is noted to have proposed a different title for the same purpose. The legend also recognizes the early importance of Argos by designating Têmenus as the eldest of the three brothers.

## Pheidôn the Temenid
The historical significance of Argos's early ascendency is exemplified by Pheidôn the Temenid, a king of Argos. Pheidôn utilized the privileges and influence of the Argeian state to pursue ambitious projects, claiming the right to preside over the Olympic games as a representative of Hêraklês. This led to conflicts between Pheidôn and the Spartans, as well as tensions between Sparta and Elis regarding the relations of Pisa.

Pheidôn is distinguished as the earliest Greek to determine a scale of weight and to coin money. While some have attributed the Æginæan scale to elsewhere, the archive indicates that the Pheidonian coinage and statical scale originally belonged to Argos. This development in coinage was facilitated by the early commerce conducted by Dorians in the Argolic peninsula with Dorian islands in the Ægean.

## Regional Geography and Population (776 B.C.)
The distribution of the population in 776 B.C. varied across the peninsula:
*   **Eastern Region:** The mountainous region and western shores of the Argolic gulf down to Cape Malea were independent of Sparta and belonged to Argos or its allied Dorian towns.
*   **Central and Northern Regions:** Achaia and Arcadia existed in a similar state to their later forms, though the southern frontier of Arcadia was conterminous with Sparta.
*   **Western Region:** Elis embraced the same territory in 776 B.C. as it did in 550 B.C., although Triphylia remained independent and the Pisatid was only recently and imperfectly conquered by the Eleians.
*   **Southern Region:** Sparta occupied only a small fraction of the territory it would later hold. West of Mount Taygetus were the Messenian Dorians, centered on the hill of Stenyklêrus. Their possessions covered the fertile plain of Messêne along the river Pamisus. At this time, "Messênê" referred to the plain generally; no town by that name existed until after the battle of Leuktra.

The broader Peloponnesus was shared unequally among the Dorians, Achæans, Arcadians, and Ætolo-Eleians. Other emigrant groups included the Dryopes and Triphylians.
