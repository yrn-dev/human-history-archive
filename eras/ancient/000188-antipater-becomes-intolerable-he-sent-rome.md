# Antipater and His Exile to Rome

## Relations with the Nation and the Royal Court
Antipater held an indisputable title to the succession, yet he was met with an intolerable hatred from the nation. This animosity stemmed from the widespread belief that Antipater had contrived the calumnies that led to the slaughter of his brethren. Despite this, Antipater utilized his wealth to secure favor among the friends of Caesar in Rome and with Saturninus, the President of Syria, as well as the governors under him. He provided these officials with large sums of money and presents; however, these actions did not earn him genuine good-will. Instead, the receivers viewed his generosity as a product of fear rather than nobility, while those who received nothing became even more bitter enemies.

Within the royal family, Antipater’s influence was significant. He used flattery to sway King Herod, successfully arranging marriages that benefited his interests. He contrived a match between Bernice, the daughter of Aristobulus, and his own uncle on his mother's side to reconcile the two. Additionally, one of the daughters Herod had by Mariamne was married to Antipater, while the other was married to Phasaelus, the king's brother's son.

## Conflict with Pheroras and Departure for Rome
Tensions rose within the court when Herod became inflamed with anger toward Pheroras's wife, following accusations made by Salome. Herod accused the woman of offering affronts to his daughters, funding the Pharisees, and using love potions to turn Pheroras against him. Herod eventually forced Pheroras to choose between his brother and his wife. When Pheroras declared he would rather die than forsake his wife, Herod commanded Antipater to cease all intercourse with Pheroras, his wife, and their associates.

While Antipater publicly obeyed, he secretly attended their night meetings. Fearing that Salome was observing these actions, Antipater contacted his friends in Rome. They wrote to Herod suggesting that it was proper for Antipater to be sent to Caesar for a period of time. Herod complied, sending Antipater to Rome with a splendid attendance and a great deal of money. Crucially, Herod gave Antipater his testament to carry, which bequeathed the kingdom to Antipater, with Herod (the son of Mariamne) named as Antipater's successor should Antipater die first.

## Accusations and Legal Proceedings
During his time in Rome, Antipater was accompanied by Sylleus the Arabian. Antipater accused Sylleus of various crimes, including the killing of Fabatus, a servant of Caesar, and several chief Arabians at Petra. It was later revealed that Sylleus had attempted to bribe a member of Herod's guard, Corinthus, to murder the king—a conspiracy discovered through torture.

Upon his return from Rome, Antipater faced severe accusations. It was discovered that he had prepared a poison potion intended for his father. Further evidence emerged through the examination of Bathyllus, Antipater's freedman, who had brought a second potion made from the poison of asps and other serpents to ensure the king's death if the first potion failed. 

Antipater was also accused of forging letters to alienate Herod from his other sons, Archelaus and Philip. He used bribes to convince friends in Rome to write that the youths reproached their father and lamented the deaths of Alexander and Aristobulus. Additionally, Antipater forged a letter from Salome containing bitter reproaches against Herod, which he persuaded a maid-servant of Julia, named Acme, to send to the king.

## Condemnation and Death
Antipater was brought before Quintilius Varus, the President of Syria. When asked to make his defense, Antipater remained silent except to claim that God was his witness to his innocence. To prove the lethality of the potion Antipater had prepared, Varus had it administered to a condemned prisoner, who died immediately. Varus then wrote an account of the assembly to Caesar and departed.

Herod bound Antipater and awaited Caesar's decision. Eventually, ambassadors from Rome arrived with news that Caesar had executed Acme for her role in Antipater's practices. Regarding Antipater, Caesar left the decision to Herod, granting him the power to either banish his son or take his life.

While Herod was gravely ill and near death, a moment occurred where he nearly stabbed himself with a knife, but was prevented by his cousin, Achiabus. Believing his father had died, Antipater grew bold and attempted to bribe the jailer to release him so he could seize the kingdom. The jailer informed the king of these solicitations. In a final act of anger, Herod commanded his guards to kill Antipater immediately and to bury him in an ignoble manner at Hyrcania.
