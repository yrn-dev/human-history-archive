# Assyrian Eponym Lists

The Assyrian Eponym Lists, also referred to as lists of *limmi*, are chronological records used to fix the dates of Assyrian history. These lists consist of the names of archons, or *limmi*, who gave their names in succession to the years of their office.

## Function and Structure
The system of reckoning time by *limmi* was of Assyrian origin and dates back to the first days of the monarchy. Evidence of this system's use has been found as far as the distant colony at Kara Euyuk near Kaisariyeh (Caesarea) in Cappadocia, where cuneiform tablets show that Assyrian settlers employed the system in the 15th century B.C.

In these lists, the beginning of a king's reign is noted. Furthermore, some copies of the lists include the chief events of the year added to the name of the corresponding archon. Several copies of these lists have been recovered from the library of Nineveh.

## Chronological Certainty
The lists of *limmi* allow Assyrian chronology to be fixed with certainty from 911 B.C. to 666 B.C. The earliest existing list goes back to 911 B.C., while the latest extends to the middle of the reign of Assur-bani-pal. 

A critical point of reference in these lists is the eponymy of Pur-Sagale, which records a solar eclipse that occurred on June 15th, 763 B.C. (stated to have been visible in the month Sivan). This astronomical event places the dead reckoning for these later periods on an absolutely certain basis.

## Relationship to Other Chronological Records
The Assyrian Eponym Lists serve as a control to confirm the accuracy of other historical records, specifically the Ptolemaic Canon. The Ptolemaic Canon is a list of Babylonian, Assyrian, and Persian kings who ruled in Babylon, including the length of their reigns from the accession of Nabonassar in 747 B.C. to the conquest of Babylon by Alexander the Great in 331 B.C.

While Assyria used the *limmi* system, Babylonia adopted a different method of dating. In Babylonia, years were dated by the chief events that distinguished them, a practice also seen in Egypt during the Old Empire. The Babylonian government determined which event would serve as the date and notified officials; one such notice from the reign of Samsu-iluna (son of Khammurabi) was found in the Lebanon. 

Other supporting documents include:
* **The Babylonian List of Kings:** A document arranged by dynasties from the First Dynasty of Babylon to the Neo-Babylonian period.
* **The Babylonian Chronicle:** A record that, like the Ptolemaic Canon, begins with Nabonassar.
* **Synchronous History of Assyria and Babylonia:** A work written by an Assyrian consisting of brief notices regarding the hostile or otherwise relations between the kings of the two countries.

## Comparative Dynastic Data
The archive's sources note discrepancies in some chronological compilations. For instance, one compiler assigned 55 years to the reign of Khammurabi instead of 43, and 35 years to Samsu-iluna instead of 38. Additionally, one record omits the seven-year reign of the Assyrian king Tukulti-In-aristi at Babylon.
