# The Neo-Babylonian Empire and the Fall of Assyria

## The Transition of Power
The collapse of the Assyrian Empire began following the death of Assur-bani-pal. Under his successor, Assur-etil-ilani, the empire faced significant instability as Scythians penetrated Assyria, reaching the borders of Egypt and burning Calah. Although the walls of Nineveh protected the remnants of the Assyrian army, the subsequent construction of a new palace among the ruins of a neighboring city was characterized by small size and architectural poverty, indicating that Assyrian resources were at a low ebb. 

During this period of decline, rule in Babylonia was potentially disputed by the Rab-shakeh (vizier) Assur-sum-lisir. The final king of Assyria was likely Sin-sar-iskun (also referred to as Sarakos), the brother of Assur-etil-ilani. While Sin-sar-iskun worked to restore ruined temples and their priests—coupling Merodach of Babylon with Assur of Nineveh—Babylonia remained restless. Following the overthrow of Samas-sum-yukin, Kandalanu served as viceroy before being succeeded by Nabopolassar.

## The Rise of Nabopolassar
Nabopolassar, the first independent ruler of the neo-Babylonian period (625–604 B.C.), inaugurated a new era of prosperity and power. To secure his sovereignty, Nabopolassar formed an alliance with Cyaxares, the king of the Medes (referred to by the Greeks as the Scythian king of Ecbatana). Together, their joint forces besieged and captured Nineveh, killing Saracus and utterly destroying the city. This victory, along with the destruction of northern Babylonian cities that had sided with Assyria, brought the Assyrian empire to an end. Consequently, the seat of empire was transferred to Babylonia, and the city of Babylon became the sole capital.

The rise of this new power drew the jealousy of neighboring states. Necho, the king of Egypt, marched toward the Euphrates with a powerful army to halt their progress, resulting in several conquests. When Nabopolassar discovered that Syria and Palestine had revolted following Necho's capture of Carchemish, he appointed his son, Nabuchodonosor, as his partner in the empire. Due to his own age and infirmities, Nabopolassar sent his son with an army to bring those territories back under subjection.

## The Reign of Nebuchadrezzar II and the Late Empire
Nabopolassar was succeeded by his son, Nebuchadrezzar II, who reigned for 43 years. Under his rule, Babylon once again became the mistress of the civilized world. Fragmentary annals from his reign record an invasion of Egypt in 567 B.C., mentioning "Phut of the Ionians."

The final phase of the Babylonian empire occurred under the last king, Nabonidus. Records of his reign include a chronological tablet and an inscription detailing his restoration of the temple of the Moon-god at Harran. During the sixth year of Nabonidus's reign (either 553 or 549 B.C.), Cyrus, the "king of Anshan" in Elam, revolted against his suzerain Astyages, the king of the Manda (Scythians) at Ecbatana. After the army of Astyages betrayed their king to Cyrus, the latter established himself at Ecbatana, ending the Scythian empire.

## The Conquest by Cyrus
By 538 B.C., Cyrus had become the king of Persia and launched a campaign in northern Mesopotamia. Nabonidus established a camp at Sippara near the northern frontier, placing his son—likely Belshazzar—in command of the army. In June of that year, a battle was fought at Opis, resulting in a Babylonian defeat. Sippara surrendered shortly thereafter. 

Nabonidus fled to Babylon, where he was pursued by Gobryas, the governor of Kurdistan. On the 16th of Tammuz, the soldiers of Cyrus entered Babylon without fighting. Nabonidus was discovered in a hiding place, and Kurdish guards were stationed at the gates of the temple of Bel to ensure services continued without interruption. This conquest in 539 B.C. ended the political independence of Babylonia.

## Religious Context and Continuity
The neo-Babylonian period represented the fourth stage of Babylonian-Assyrian religious development. While political independence ended with Cyrus, the religion continued undisturbed for several centuries until the Greek conquest of the Euphrates valley in 331 B.C. 

The religious landscape was marked by a tendency toward centralization. In the south, the cult focused on Marduk and his consort, while the north focused on Assur and Ishtar. The title *Belit* (meaning "the lady") was used for the consort of Bel (Nin-lil) at Nippur, but was later transferred to Ishtar and to Sarpanit, the consort of Marduk, as Babylon rose as the seat of Marduk's cult. Additionally, the era was characterized by an elaborate system of astral theology and divination, involving the observation of heavenly phenomena and the examination of the livers of sacrificial animals.
