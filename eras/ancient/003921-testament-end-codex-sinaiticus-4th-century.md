# Codex Sinaiticus

The Codex Sinaiticus (designated as $\aleph$ by Gregory and $\delta^2$ by von Soden) is an uncial manuscript of the Bible dating from the 4th century. It is written on thin vellum and is characterized by a layout of four columns per page, with each column containing forty-eight lines. This multi-column arrangement reflects the influence of the earlier *volumen* (roll) format, which featured narrow columns of writing.

## Discovery and History
The manuscript was discovered in 1844 by C. Tischendorf at the monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai. It was eventually acquired by the tsar in 1869 and housed in the Imperial Library in St Petersburg. According to Tischendorf, the text was produced by four different scribes; one of these individuals was identified as the same scribe who worked on the Codex Vaticanus.

The Codex Sinaiticus underwent multiple rounds of correction. Significant corrections were made in the 6th century by a scribe identified as $\aleph^a$ and in the 7th century by a scribe identified as $\aleph^c$. The work of $\aleph^c$ is considered particularly important because a note at the end of the book of Esther indicates that these corrections were based on an early copy that had been corrected by Pamphilus. Pamphilus was a friend of Eusebius, a disciple of Origen, and the founder of a library at Caesarea.

## Contents and Textual Character
Originally, the Codex Sinaiticus contained the entire Bible. While the New Testament remains complete, the manuscript includes additional materials at its conclusion. It contains the Epistle of Barnabas and the *Shepherd of Hermas*, though the latter is unfortunately incomplete. Evidence suggests that another document may have originally existed between these two texts.

In terms of its textual character, the Codex Sinaiticus generally possesses a "Neutral text." This text is noted to be less mixed in the Epistles than that of Codex Vaticanus, although it is not as pure in the Gospels.

## The Epistle of Barnabas and Canonical Status
The inclusion of the Epistle of Barnabas at the end of the Codex Sinaiticus serves as a sort of appendix to the New Testament. The presence of this text in the 4th-century codex, as well as its appearance in an 11th-century manuscript alongside the *Didache*, indicates that the writing once held quasi-canonical authority. This standing was particularly strong in Alexandria, where Origen cited it in the same manner as scripture and Clement commented upon it as he did the canonical scriptures in his *Hypotyposes*.

Clement ascribed the work to "the prophet" or "the apostle" Barnabas, referring to the fellow-apostle of Paul. However, internal evidence suggests this ascription is impossible, and the text itself does not claim such authorship. While Lightfoot suggested the author might have been an unknown namesake, it is otherwise posited that the Alexandrian Church attributed the work to the apostle because the writing was so highly favored.

## Textual Variations
The Codex Sinaiticus is cited in studies regarding the address of the Epistle to the Ephesians. While the title "To the Ephesians" is found in the earliest versions and manuscripts, the specific words "in Ephesus" ([Greek: en Epheso]) from verse 1 of the first chapter are omitted in Codex Sinaiticus (as well as in Codex Vaticanus and Codex 67). This omission supports the theory that the address was originally general or left blank, and that the specific reference to Ephesus was later inserted based on the title of the letter.
