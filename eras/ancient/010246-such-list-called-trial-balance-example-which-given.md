# Trial Balance

In the field of book-keeping, a trial balance is a complete list of all ledger balances. Its primary purpose is to demonstrate an agreement between the total debit balances and the total credit balances within a ledger.

## Principles of the Trial Balance
The trial balance is based on the essential principle of double-entry book-keeping. This system constitutes a complete record of every business transaction. Because these transactions are invariably cross-dealings—meaning they simultaneously involve the imparting of a benefit by one party and the receipt of a benefit by another—a complete record requires an entry of an equal amount on both the debit and credit sides of the ledger.

Consequently, if clerical work is performed correctly, the aggregate amount entered on the debit side must always equal the aggregate amount entered on the credit side. The trial balance serves as the mechanism to verify this equality.

## Limitations and Accuracy
While a trial balance confirms that the totals match, it provides a purely mechanical test. It does not prove that the ledger is an absolutely accurate record of transactions. There are several ways in which a ledger may be inaccurate despite a balanced trial balance:

*   **Omissions:** Transactions that actually occurred may have been left out of the books entirely.
*   **Misclassification:** Transactions may have been recorded in the wrong accounts.
*   **Valuation Errors:** The money values attached to transactions may be incorrect.
*   **Fictitious Entries:** Records of transactions that never took place may have been entered into the ledger.

Because of these possibilities, a trial balance is not considered a very adequate safeguard against fraud, nor does it reveal mistakes regarding the monetary values of recorded transactions. This is particularly significant when altered circumstances render a previously correct monetary value inaccurate; such a change constitutes an additional transaction that may have been omitted.

## Related Accounting Records
The trial balance is used in conjunction with other accounting tools to maintain financial records:

### The Journal
In smaller businesses, a single book of first-entry known as the journal may be sufficient. This allows one person to record all transactions as they occur. For many years, the journal was the only book of first-entry used in book-keeping. Entries in the journal are used to bring the capital account up to date and to prepare profit and loss accounts and trading accounts from the trial balance.

### Capital and Current Accounts
In standard practice, profits are added to the capital account, and withdrawals of capital are deducted from it. However, some systems keep the balance of the capital account constant. In these instances, net profits and drawings are transferred to a separate "current account." While this method is rarely used by private firms or undertakings owned by individuals, it is invariably adopted by joint-stock companies, where it is generally referred to as an "appropriation of profit account."
