# Semitic Empire of Sargon of Akkad

The Semitic Empire of Sargon of Akkad was a major power in western Asia, emerging after a period during which Semitic princes had already established themselves at Kis. Early Semitic rulers of Kis included Manistusu, a contemporary of Uru-duggina, and Alusarsid (also known as Urumus), who is credited with subduing Barahs and Elam. However, these early figures were eclipsed by the achievements of Sargon of Akkad and his son, Naram-Sin.

## Sargon of Akkad
Sargon was the son of Itti-Bel. According to legend, he was born in concealment and set adrift on the Euphrates in an ark of bulrushes, where he was rescued and raised by a husbandman named Akki. Upon the revelation of his true origin, Sargon ascended to the crown of Babylonia and began a series of foreign conquests.

Sargon's military campaigns were extensive. He invaded Syria and Palestine four times, spending three years subduing the countries of "the west" to unite them with Babylonia into a single empire. To commemorate these victories, he erected images of himself on the shores of the Mediterranean. He also subjugated the northern part of Mesopotamia and Elam, while suppressing rebellions within Babylonia and Kazalla. The spoils from these conquered lands were used to build palaces and cities at home. Contract tablets from his reign mention campaigns against Palestine and Sarlak, the king of Kurdistan or Gutium, and note that copper was brought from the Sinaitic peninsula or Magan.

Sargon is also associated with the city of Babylon; a dated tablet indicates he built sanctuaries there to Ea and Anunit. Some records suggest Sargon may have given Babylon its name and made it the capital of his empire. Additionally, it is probable that Sargon established a library for the first collection of terrestrial omens and astronomical observations.

## Naram-Sin and Imperial Administration
Sargon was succeeded by his son, Naram-Sin, who continued his father's conquests. Naram-Sin marched into Magan and took its king captive. He adopted the imperial title "king of the four zones" and, like his father, was addressed as a god, specifically "the god of Agade." A bas-relief of Naram-Sin discovered at Diarbekr shows similarities to early Egyptian art.

The empire was maintained through a sophisticated infrastructure, including a regular postal service operating along a network of roads. Clay seals bearing the names of Naram-Sin and Sargon have been recovered from the Louvre. The administration also appears to have instituted a cadastral survey; one document mentions a governor of the land of the Amorites (the Babylonian term for Syria and Palestine) named Uru-Malik, whose name suggests Canaanitish origin.

## Geography and Terminology
The empire's rise extended the use of the name Akkad (the Semitic form of Agade). Sargon's capital, Akkad, was located near or within Sippara. Consequently, the imperial title "Sumer and Akkad" came to denote the entirety of Babylonia. 

The region was characterized by various geographical terms:
* **Edin:** The alluvial plain of Babylonia, specifically the western bank of the river used for pasturing flocks.
* **Kengi (or Kengi Sumer):** A comprehensive name for southern Babylonia, meaning "the land."
* **Urra (or Uri):** Originally meaning "clayey soil," this term signified the "highlands" or "upper country," in contrast to the "lowlands" of Kengi.
* **Kisad tamtim:** The coast-land, or "bank of the sea."

## Chronology and Decline
The dating of Sargon's empire is a subject of historical debate. Nabonidus placed Sargon at 3800 B.C., based on the claim that 3,200 years separated his own time from that of Naram-Sin. Other sources, such as Wells, date Sargon to approximately 2750 B.C. Some scholars, including Lehmann-Haupt and Winckler, have questioned the accuracy of Nabonidus's figures, suggesting they may be exaggerations or the result of counting contemporaneous dynasties as consecutive.

The empire, characterized as the Sumerian Akkadian Empire, endured for over two hundred years. Sargon's people, the Akkadians, were described as illiterate barbarians who adopted the Sumerian language for their learned class and officials, as well as Sumerian writing. Following the decay of the empire and an incursion by the Elamites, a new Semitic people, the Amorites, gradually established rule over Sumeria, eventually centering their power in Babylon.
