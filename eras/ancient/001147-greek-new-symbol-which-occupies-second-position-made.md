# Greek Alphabetic Symbols and Adaptations

The development of the Greek alphabet and its subsequent influence on other writing systems involves a series of adaptations from earlier Semitic forms and the creation of new symbols to meet specific phonetic needs.

## Origins and Early Forms
The Greek alphabet borrowed symbols from the Phoenician alphabet, though not always in their original forms. One such example is the symbol corresponding to the second position in the Phoenician alphabet. In the earliest Phoenician forms, this symbol appeared as either pointed or more rounded; the rounded version is also found in early Aramaic.

In early Greek usage, the symbol underwent various adaptations. Rock inscriptions from Thera, dating between 700 and 600 B.C. and written from right to left, feature a form resembling the ordinary Greek lambda. This specific variation likely resulted from writing the Semitic symbol upside down. During the 5th and 6th centuries, inscriptions found in Syracuse, Selinus, and Melos suggest an Aramaic influence, characterized by an open head of the letter. Other adaptations of this symbol appear in Corcyra, Corinth, and on Byzantine coins. In the alphabets of Delos, Naxos, and other Ionic islands during the same period, the symbol took a form that is difficult to explain. Generally, the primary variations of the symbol are between rounded and pointed loops.

## Phonetics of the Symbol
The symbol represents a voiced stop produced by vibrating the vocal chords while closing the lips. It is distinguished from the sound *p* by the vibration of the vocal chords and from *m* by the closure of both the lips and the nasal passage. When produced with an audible emission of breath, it forms the aspirate *bh*, a sound frequent in the pro-ethnic period of Indo-European languages and preserved in Indo-Aryan languages.

## Adaptation into Slavonic
The Greek alphabet served as a basis for other European alphabets, including the Russian alphabet. However, because the pronunciation of the second symbol in medieval Greek had shifted from *b* to *v*, a new symbol had to be invented to represent the genuine *b* sound in Slavonic, as there was no corresponding sound in Greek at the time the alphabet was adopted.

This new symbol was created by removing the upper loop of the Greek letter B, resulting in a character that resembles a lowercase *b*. This new symbol was placed in the second position of the alphabet. While the original B retained the numerical value of the Greek beta (2), no numerical value was assigned to the new Slavonic symbol.
