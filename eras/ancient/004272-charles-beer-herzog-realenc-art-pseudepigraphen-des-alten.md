# Charles and Beer (Herzog's Realenc.)

The scholars R. H. Charles and Beer are associated with the critical analysis of the Book of Enoch, specifically regarding the diversity of its authorship. Their views are documented in the *Realencyklopädie* (Herzog's *Realenc.*) in the article titled "Pseudepigraphen des Alten Testaments" (p. 250).

## Theory of Authorship
In the study of the Book of Enoch, critics have debated whether the conflicting elements within the text are the result of a diversity of sources or a diversity of authorship. While scholars such as Ginzberg and Ryssel advocate for the view of diverse sources, R. H. Charles and Beer, along with Kabisch and de Faye, support the theory that the book was produced by a diversity of authors.

## Critical Analysis of the Text
The analysis provided by Charles (pp. 24-33) was further developed by Beer and Clemen. Beer's analysis of the book is described as very complex, as he identifies fifteen separate elements within the work. These elements are divided between the Enoch tradition and the Noah tradition.

According to Beer, the elements from the Enoch tradition include:
*   Chapters i.–v.
*   Chapters xii–xvi.
*   Chapters xvii.–xix.
*   Chapters xx.–xxxvi.
*   Chapters xxxvii.–lxix. (derived from diverse sources)
*   Chapters lxx.–lxxi.
*   Chapters lxxii.–lxxxii.
*   Chapters lxxxiii.–lxxxiv.
*   Chapters lxxxv.–xc.
*   Chapter xciii. and cxi. 12-17.
*   Chapter xci. 1-11, 18, 19, xcii., and xciv.–cv.
*   Chapter cviii.

Additionally, Beer identifies elements from the Noah tradition in:
*   Chapters vi.–xi.
*   Chapter xxxix. 1-2a, liv. 7-lv. 2, lx., and lxv.–lxix. 25.
*   Chapters cvi.–cvii.

For comparison, the scholar Clemen identified eleven separate sources in the text.

## Related Critical Perspectives
The broader critical context of the book involves identifying composite elements. Kabisch, who first drew attention to these elements in 1891, argued that certain sections (xxiv. 3-xxix., xxxvi.-xl., and liii.-lxxiv.) were independent sources written before the fall of Jerusalem in A.D. 70. 

Another scholar, Appel, proposed a different final analysis in 1906. Appel suggested that the original groundwork of Enoch consisted of chapters i.–xvi. and xx.–xxxvi. This was later edited as a "Book of Methuselah" (lxxii.–cv.) by imitators. A final redactor then interpolated these sections, added two of his own, and worked up the "Similitudes" from later sources, attributing them to Noah. Appel dates the publication of the entire work to the years immediately following the death of Herod.

## Bibliography and Commentaries
Beer contributed to the study of the text through the work "Das Buch Henoch," published in 1900 in Kautzsch's *Apok. u. Pseud. des A.T.* (ii. 217-310). Other significant translations and commentaries on the Book of Enoch include works by Laurence (1821), Dillmann (1853), Schodde (1882), and Charles (1893), as well as Flemming and Radermacher (1901) and Martin (1906).
