# Spain

## Early Inhabitants and Ethnology
From the 5th century B.C., the geography of Spain was characterized by a collection of tribes, primarily identified as Celts, Celtiberi, and Iberi. These groups lacked cohesion and were frequently engaged in conflict with one another, moving constantly, while the more primitive tribes were pushed northward by Mediterranean civilization. 

In the southern region of Baetica, according to Strabo and Trogus, the inhabitants possessed a literature, poems of ancient date, and written laws, though no examples of these have survived. Evidence of an early general language spoken across Spain and the northern slope of the Pyrenees exists in the form of megalithic monuments, marks on pottery, legends on coins, and inscriptions. These utilize alphabets of Levant origin that differ slightly across six geographical districts. Some scholars, including W. von Humboldt in 1821 and earlier figures such as Juan B. Erro (1806), Larramendi (1760), and Andres de Poza (1587), noted that ancient Spanish toponymy—such as Iriberry, Iliberis, Oloron, and Mendigorry—bears a strong resemblance to Basque place-names. This has led to the theory, accepted by many students, that a people speaking a language akin to Basque were extended throughout Spain and possibly into Sardinia and Sicily.

## Ancient Conquests
The Carthaginians entered Spain at an unknown date. Their initial conquests were slow because they encountered warlike nations who defended themselves with courage. Strabo observed that the Carthaginians would have been unable to achieve their designs had the Spaniards formed a single state and assisted one another; however, because the people and districts were entirely detached and lacked connection, the Carthaginians subdued them individually. While this lack of unity eventually led to the loss of Spain, it initially made the conquest more difficult and protracted. Despite this, the Carthaginians completed the conquest of almost the entire country within twenty years, following the wars of Hannibal, Asdrubal, and Hamilcar.

Spain was the first province the Romans invaded on the continent, but it was the last they subdued. The region offered vigorous opposition for more than 200 years before it was entirely subjected to Roman power. Later, during the reign of Tetricus, who served as governor of Aquitaine, Spain was part of a realm including Britain and Gaul. This period ended when the emperor Aurelian defeated the rebel legions near Chalons in Champagne, restoring power from the columns of Hercules to the wall of Antoninus.

## The Ommiades of Spain
Following a revolution that dissolved the unity of the Saracen empire, a royal youth named Abdalrahman escaped his enemies, traveling from the Euphrates to the valleys of Mount Atlas. His arrival on the coast of Andalusia revived the zeal of the white faction. Invited by those prompted by fear, indignation, and gratitude, Abdalrahman established the throne of Cordova. He became the father of the Ommiades of Spain, who reigned for over 250 years, ruling from the Pyrenees to the Atlantic.

During this era, Abdalrahman defeated a lieutenant of the Abbassides who had invaded with an army and fleet. The head of this lieutenant, Ala, was sent in salt and camphire to be suspended before the palace of Mecca. Although the caliph Almansor felt secure due to the distance between them, the two powers remained in a state where declarations of offensive war evaporated without effect. Consequently, Spain was separated from the main monarchy and entered a period of perpetual hostility with the East, while tending toward friendship and peace with France and the Christian sovereigns of Constantinople.

## Diplomatic and Military History
In the early 17th century, England engaged in significant diplomatic efforts with Spain. Between 1611 and 1618, John Digby, 1st Earl of Bristol, served as an ambassador to Spain on multiple occasions. His missions included negotiating a marriage between the infanta Anne and Prince Henry, as well as championing the cause of English merchants, which resulted in the appointment of consuls at Seville and Lisbon and other substantial concessions. Digby also uncovered a list of English pensioners at the Spanish court, including some ministers. Later negotiations involved a proposed union between Charles and the infanta Maria.

By 1745, Spain's international position had shifted; the archive notes that during this period, Spain was almost neglected and was unable to perform any actions of an offensive character.
