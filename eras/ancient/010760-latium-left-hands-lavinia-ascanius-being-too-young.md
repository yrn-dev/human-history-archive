# Ascanius and the Early Governance of Latium

## Identity and Parentage
Ascanius is a figure of Roman legend, identified as the son of Aeneas. According to the records of Livy, tradition suggests there may have been two sons of Aeneas sharing this name: one born to his Trojan wife, Creusa, and another born to his Latin wife, Lavinia. Ascanius was also known by the names Iulus and Ilus. The Julian gens claimed direct descent from him.

## Arrival in Italy and Governance
The usual account of the legend states that Ascanius accompanied his father, Aeneas, to Italy during his flight from Troy. Following the death of Aeneas, the government of Latium was entrusted to Lavinia. This arrangement was made because Ascanius was considered too young to undertake the responsibilities of governance at that time.

## Later Life and Foundation of Alba Longa
Ascanius eventually transitioned from the governance of Latium. After a period of thirty years, he departed from Lavinium to found the city of Alba Longa.

## Related Legends and Conflicting Traditions
The origins of the region and the lineage of its founders are subject to several contradictory traditions found in the writings of Strabo, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, and other authors. 

Some accounts regarding the founding of Rome and its early figures include:
* One tradition suggests that Roma, the daughter of Ascanius and son of Aeneas, gave her name to the city.
* Other narratives claim the city was founded by Romanus (the son of Odysseus and Circe), Romus (the son of Hemathion), or Romis (a despot of the Latins who expelled the Tyrrhenians).
* Regarding Romulus, some say he was the son of Aeneas and Dexithea, brought to Italy as a child and miraculously preserved during a river flood. Others claim his mother was Roma, who married Latinus, or Aemilia (the daughter of Aeneas and Lavinia), through an intrigue with Mars.

## Regional Context of Latium
Historical perspectives on the population of Latium indicate it was originally occupied by a Sikel or Œnotrian population. These people were ethnically akin to the primitive population of Rome and the Epirots. Over time, Latium was partially overrun and subdued by fiercer tribes from the Apennines, specifically the Opicians, Oscans, or Ausonians. This resulted in an intermixture of the Oscan race within the region. Aristotle extended the designation of the country of the Opici to include both Rome and Latium.
