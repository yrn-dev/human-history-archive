# Sin-liqi-unninni

Sin-liqi-unninni was a composer of Babylonian literature, most notably credited with the creation of the *Epic of Gilgamesh*.

## The Epic of Gilgamesh
The *Epic of Gilgamesh* is one of the most famous literary works to survive from Babylonian history. The work is composed of twelve books. In terms of its structure, the epic was arranged upon an astronomical principle, with each individual division of the work containing the story of a single adventure from the career of Gilgamesh. 

The archive notes that the overall story is a composite product. Because of this composite nature, it is possible that some of the stories included in the epic were artificially attached to the central figure of Gilgamesh.

## Context of Babylonian Literature and Science
The work of Sin-liqi-unninni exists within a broader tradition of Babylonian literary and scientific achievement. Other significant epics from this culture include the story of Creation. This particular text was designed to glorify Bel-Merodach by detailing his contest with Tiamat, the dragon of chaos. 

According to the Creation epic, the world was created from the primeval deep, followed by the birth of the gods of light. After a struggle between the gods of light and the powers of darkness, Merodach emerged victorious. He created the heaven and the earth by cleaving Tiamat's body in two. Merodach further organized the universe by arranging the sun, moon, and stars in order and establishing laws for them that were never to be transgressed. Following the arrangement of the celestial bodies, plants, animals, and finally man were created. While older legends credit Ea as the creator who fashioned man from clay, in this account, Merodach takes that place.

## Babylonian Administrative and Scientific Foundations
The intellectual environment that produced such literature was supported by an organized state. Under Sargon and his son and successor, Naram-Sin, the Babylonian empire was integrated via roads and a regular postal service. Naram-Sin, who took the imperial title "king of the four zones" and was addressed as a god (specifically "the god of Agad"), oversaw a territory that adjoined Egypt.

The Babylonians also engaged in systematic recording and surveying. This included a cadastral survey, and documents show that Uru-Malik, likely of Canaanitish origin, served as governor of the land of the Amorites (the Babylonian term for Palestine and Syria). Furthermore, it is probable that a library established by Sargon housed the first collection of terrestrial omens and astronomical observations.
