# Ancient Traditions and Early Records

The study of human history relies upon the analysis of ancient traditions, early written records, and the physical remnants of early civilizations. These sources range from the legislative codes of the Israelites to the early inscriptions of the Greek alphabet and the traditional accounts of the Bohemian people.

## Early Israelite Legislation and Traditions
Among the oldest legislation possessed by the Hebrews are the laws found in Exodus xx. 23-xxiii. 19 and Exodus xxxiv. 17-26. These regulations consist primarily of civil ordinances designed to regulate a community living under simple social conditions, focused chiefly on agriculture, alongside elementary regulations for religious observances such as festivals, sacrifices, and altars.

These laws are associated with different traditional sources. One writer, referred to as "E," lived apparently in the northern kingdom and recorded traditions current in northern Israel. "E" preferred the name *Elohim* ("God") over "Jehovah." The traces of "E" appear in the life of Abraham and various passages in Genesis, Exodus, Numbers, and Joshua. "E" often provides a parallel, though sometimes divergent, version of events recorded by another writer known as "J."

One such tradition involves Balaam, a Gentile seer and the son of Beor. According to the narrative in Numbers xxii-xxv, during the Israelites' sojourn in the plains of Moab at the end of their forty years of wandering, Balak, the king of Moab, sought Balaam's help to curse Israel. Despite this, Yahweh compelled Balaam to bless them. This story, which includes seven oracular poems, is believed to be based on ancient traditions and was compiled from the documents denoted as J and E between 900 and 700 B.C.

## Greek Writing and Early Science
The development of writing in Greece is evidenced by external inscriptions. The oldest known stage of the Greek alphabet is represented by inscriptions from Crete, Melos, and Thera, dated to the 40th Olympiad (620 B.C.). Additionally, a distinctively Ionian alphabet is found in the inscription of the mercenaries of Psammetichus in Upper Egypt, dated to either the 40th or 47th Olympiad. These findings indicate that writing was well established in Greece by the early 7th century B.C.

Parallel to the development of writing was the advancement of celestial science. Celestial globes preceded terrestrial ones; one example in the museum of Naples, measuring 2 metres in diameter and supported by an Atlas, is judged to date from the 4th century B.C. and may be the work of the astronomer Eudoxus. Other notable figures associated with such apparatus include Archimedes, who possessed a glass celestial globe with a small terrestrial globe at its center, and Hero of Alexandria.

## Traditions of Bohemia and Lotharingia
In Bohemia, ancient traditions accepted by modern historians state that the land was originally inhabited by a Celtic tribe called the Boii, whose capital was Boiohemum. The Boii were eventually subdued by the Teutonic tribe of the Marcomanni around 12 B.C. Later, Slavic tribes, most notably the Cechs, conquered the region, becoming masters of the country in the 5th century. Early records mention a ruler named Samo who defeated the Franks at Wogatisburg in 630 and also defeated the Avars. Later chroniclers, such as Cosmas, describe the foundation of a Bohemian principality by Krok and his daughter Libussa, who married a peasant named Premysl.

In the region of Lotharingia (eastern Belgium), early architecture was heavily influenced by German traditions until the 12th century. Because most Lotharingian bishops were of German descent, German architects, sculptors, and painters directed the artistic revival. While Italian artists occasionally contributed—such as a painter named Giovanni who worked on St. James’s Church at Liège—the local style only began to emerge as artists started using native materials from the valley of the Meuse.

## Early Islamic Exegesis and the Koran
Following the death of Mahomet, individuals began the exposition of the Koran to clarify sections that were obscure or required knowledge of the circumstances of their origin. Early expositions focused on the connection and sense of whole verses rather than individual words. Ibn 'Abbas, a cousin of Mahomet, served as a chief source of traditional exegesis, though some accounts suggest he gave currency to falsehoods.

The revelations of the Koran are categorized into different periods. The shortest suras, put forth in Mecca, are considered the oldest and differ in style from the Medina pieces. There is a transition between these early Meccan suras and those assigned to the later part of the Prophet's work in Mecca. While the exact year of Mahomet's appearance as a prophet is not certain due to imperfect records, it is probably A.D. 610. Later, the commentary of Tabari (A.D. 839-923) recorded numerous traditions and the channels (*isnad*) through which they were transmitted.
