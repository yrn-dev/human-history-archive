# HEROIC LEGENDS.—GENEALOGY OF ARGOS

The heroic legends of Greece consist of genealogies designed to connect historical men with the gods. These mythical pedigrees often served to unite specific clans or gentes rather than entire communities. While some genealogies, such as the Herakleid pedigree of the Spartan kings, were numerous throughout the Hellenic world, they are generally viewed as documents of religious feeling intended to connect the human race to a divine original rather than as precise historical evidence.

## The Nature of Heroic Genealogies
Heroic genealogies often blended gods, heroes, and men to establish ancestral claims or explain the origins of cities. For example, the Æakid genealogy—a family known for military excellence—connected various eminent gentes across Cyprus, Salamis, Ægina, and Achaia Phthiôtis, as well as among the Epirotic Molossians. Such pedigrees were not always shared by the general population; for instance, most Athenians did not participate in the Æakid genealogy, though specific Athenian families claimed it.

The historical reliability of these lists is limited by the lack of early written enrolment. Aside from the list of Olympic victors dating back to 776 B.C., there are few registered lists of antiquity; the Karneonicæ, for example, only reach back to 676 B.C. Consequently, the length of a pedigree or the number of names it contains does not provide a presumption of superior antiquity.

## The Æakids and the Connection to Thêbes
Æakus, the progenitor of the Æakids, was the son of Zeus and Ægina. Ægina was the daughter of Asôpus, whom Zeus had carried off to the island that now bears her name. Ægina later married Aktôr, and together they had Menœtius, who was the father of Patroclus.

The genealogy of the Æginêtans was linked to both Phlius and Thêbes because there were two rivers named Asôpus—one located between Thêbes and Platæa, and another between Phlius and Sikyôn. This perceived kinship had practical political consequences. During the 68th Olympiad, when Athens pressed Thêbes in war, the Delphian oracle advised the Thêbans to seek help from their next of kin. Recognizing that Thêbê and Ægina were sisters and daughters of Asôpus, the Thêbans appealed to the Æginêtans, who provided aid by sending armed force and their common heroes, the Æakids.

## The Æolid Lines and Corinthian Legends
The legends of Æolus involve seven sons and five daughters, branching into several distinct lines:
*   **First Line:** Includes Salmôneus, Tyrô, Pelias, Nêleus, Pêrô, Bias, Melampus, Periklymenos, and Nestor.
*   **Second Line:** Includes Krêtheus, Admêtus, Alcêstis, Pêleus, Pelias, Jasôn, and Mêdea.
*   **Third Line:** Includes Sisyphus and Bellerophôn.
*   **Fourth Line:** Includes Athamas, Phryxus, Hellê, Inô, and Palæmôn.

In Corinth, different genealogical traditions existed. While some applied the Æolid genealogy through Sisyphus, the poet Eumêlus framed an independent heroic genealogy. In Eumêlus's version, the primitive tenant of the Corinthian territory was Ephyrê, daughter of Oceanus and Têthys. Following a dispute between Poseidôn and Hêlios, Briareus assigned the territory to Hêlios, who divided it between his sons, Æêtês (Corinth) and Alôeus (Sikyôn). 

Æêtês eventually emigrated to Kolchis, leaving Corinth under the rule of Bunos, son of Hermês, with the condition that the territory be restored to his descendants. After Bunos, the land was held by the wicked Epôpeus, son of Alôeus. Epôpeus's son, Marathôn, eventually succeeded him and divided the land between his own sons, Corinthos and Sikyôn. Because Corinthos died without issue, the Corinthians invited Mêdea (representative of Æêtês) and her husband Jasôn from Iôlkos to take sovereignty. This specific version of the legend was designed to provide Mêdea with an hereditary title to the throne.

## Literary Sources of Heroic Legends
Many of these genealogical details were preserved in poems written in the "Hesiodic style," characterized by a diffusive and rambling nature. Key works include the *Catalogue of Women* and the *Great Eoiai*, which commemorated celebrated women of heroic Greece. Other works attributed to Hesiod or similar poets include the *Marriage of Kêyx*, the *Melampodia*, and the *Astronomia*. Other genealogical compositions were produced by Kinæthôn, Asius, and the Orchomenian epic poet Chersias.
