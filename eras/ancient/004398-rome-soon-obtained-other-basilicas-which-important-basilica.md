# Roman Basilicas

The basilica was a prominent architectural form in the Roman world, serving primarily as a court of law and a place of business for merchants. Derived from a Greek word (*basilikê*), the structure evolved from early prototypes—possibly Athenian or from other Greek cities—into a universal feature of Roman urban life.

## Civil Basilicas of Rome and the Empire
The first known basilica in Rome was the Basilica Porcia, erected in 184 B.C. by the censor M. Porcius Cato, who may have been inspired by the beauty of Athens during a visit. This was followed by the Basilica Fulvia-Aemilia. By the era of Augustus, at least five basilicas existed near the forum, including the extensive Basilica Julia, which ran parallel to the forum's southern side. Other notable examples include the Basilica Pauli, built by L. Æmilius Paulus, and the great Basilica Ulpia, constructed by Trajan around A.D. 112 in connection with his forum. In the early 4th century, the vaulted basilica of Maxentius or Constantine was built on the Via Sacra.

While approximately twenty public basilicas are recorded in Rome alone, private basilicas for recreation or business also existed, such as the one in the palace of Domitian on the Palatine. The form was nearly universal across the empire; Suetonius records that upon the death of Augustus, his body rested in the basilica of every town (*in basilica cujusque oppidi*) as it was transported from Nola in Campania to Rome. Outside Rome, an early example exists at Pompeii, dating potentially to the 2nd century B.C.

## Architectural Variations
Civil basilicas exhibited significant variety in their design:
* **Columned/Gallery Style:** The "normal scheme" featured a central space surrounded by galleries supported by piers or columns. This is seen in the basilicas of Pompeii, Julia, Ulpia, and Aemilia.
* **Colonnaded Style:** Some structures, such as those at Silchester, in North Africa, and the palace basilica of Domitian, featured colonnades along the long sides but lacked them across the ends.
* **Open Style:** Basilicas at Timgad and Trier had no interior divisions.
* **Vaulted Style:** The basilica of Maxentius was entirely vaulted, resembling the great halls of Roman Thermae.

Regarding internal features, fixed tribunals were found at Silchester, Timgad, and Pompeii. While the basilica of Maxentius had vaulted apses that may have contained tribunals, the Basilica Julia had no tribunal at all, despite its use for the centumviral court. Similarly, the semicircular projection of the Basilica Ulpia was not a vaulted apse and remained distinct from the basilica itself.

## Transition to Christian Basilicas
The relationship between the civil basilica and the Christian church is a subject of historical discussion. While some early Christian churches resemble the columned civil basilica, they differ in key respects. For instance, Christian basilicas in the west rarely had galleries over side aisles and were always dominated by a semi-dome apse terminating the central nave—a feature absent in normal columned civil basilicas (with the possible exception of Silchester).

Some Christian basilicas originated as private halls in palaces handed over by Constantine, such as S. Maria Maggiore (from the Liberian palace) and S. Croce in Gerusalemme (from the Sessorian palace). A 3rd-century text, *The Recognitions of Clement*, also mentions Theophilus of Antioch granting "the basilica of his house" for a church. However, most existing Christian basilicas were built from the ground up for sacred purposes. In Rome, these were often constructed using materials—columns and friezes—salvaged from public buildings and desecrated temples. This led to architectural inconsistencies, such as the use of columns of varying diameters and heights in a single colonnade, or the mixing of Ionic, Corinthian, and Composite orders at S. Maria in Trastevere.

## Notable Christian Examples
In Rome, the basilica of St Paul without the walls (dedicated 324 A.D.) and St Peter's shared almost identical plans and dimensions. The S. John Lateran is noted for its baptistery—an octagonal building with a double range of columns—and a rare double-vaulted aisle surrounding the apse. S. Maria Maggiore (dedicated 365, reconstructed 432 A.D.) is a prime example of a three-aisled basilica, featuring grey granite Ionic pillars and mosaics depicting Old Testament history and the infancy of Christ.

Outside Rome, the basilicas of Ravenna are noted for their harmony and unity of design. S. Apollinare Nuovo (erected 493-525) features a nave separated from single aisles by twenty-two pillars and is famous for its mosaic procession of saints. The later S. Apollinare in Classe (A.D. 538-549) shares a very similar design. Additionally, the cathedral of Ravenna, a noble basilica with double aisles, was erected by Archbishop Ursus in A.D. 400 before its later destruction.
