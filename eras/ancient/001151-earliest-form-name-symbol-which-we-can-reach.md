# The Earliest Forms of Alphabetic Symbols

The development of early alphabetic symbols is characterized by a progression from Semitic origins through Phoenician and Greek adaptations before reaching the Latin alphabet. The earliest reachable forms of these names and symbols are found in the Hebrew and Phoenician records.

## The Symbol B
The earliest reachable form of the name for the symbol B is the Hebrew *beth*. This name was closely akin to the Phoenician version, from which the Greek *beta* was borrowed with the addition of a vowel. 

In the Phoenician alphabet, the earliest forms of the symbol were either pointed or more rounded. The rounded version is also present in the earliest Aramaic. The Greek alphabet did not borrow the symbol in its original form. For example, in early rock inscriptions from Thera (dating between 700 and 600 B.C.), the symbol appears in a form resembling the ordinary Greek *lambda*, a result of writing the Semitic symbol upside down. Other adaptations appeared in the 5th and 6th centuries in Syracuse, Selinus, and Melos, where the head of the letter was opened, suggesting Aramaic influence. Further variations occurred in the alphabets of Delos and Naxos, as well as in Corinth and Corcyra.

## The Symbol M
The Phoenician name for the symbol M was *mem*. The Greek name, *mu*, was formed based on the analogy of the name for *n*. Originally written from right to left, the symbol's earliest representations in Greek are characterized by the first limb of the *m* being of greater length. 

In early Greek alphabets, a four-stroke M with legs of equal length did not represent *m*, but rather *s*. When written with four strokes to represent *m*, it took a different form. Five-stroke forms were primarily limited to Cumae, Melos, and Crete. The Romans received the symbol from Cumae. It is believed that the Latin abbreviation M' for the praenomen Manius developed from this form when written from left to right, with the apostrophe representing the original letter's fifth stroke.

## The Symbol C
The symbol C in the Latin alphabet corresponds in origin and position to the Greek *Gamma*, which was borrowed from the third symbol of the Phoenician alphabet, known in Hebrew as *Gimel*. 

The earliest Semitic records show this symbol in several forms. In the earliest inscriptions of Attica, Naxos, Crete, and some Ionic islands, a specific form is found. In Euboea and Argolis, a form with legs of unequal length was used, which eventually transitioned into the widely spread ordinary Greek form. Other variations existed in Elis, Ozolian Locris, and Corinth (and its colony Corcyra), where a form inclined at a different angle was used. This led to a rounded version found in Arcadia, Rhodes, Megara, and on Chalcidian vases of the 6th century B.C. The Romans likely adopted this rounded form with the value of G, as evidenced by the abbreviations *Cn.* for Gnaeus and *C.* for Gaius.

## Phonetic Characteristics
The symbols represent specific vocal sounds. The symbol B represents a voiced stop created by vibrating the vocal chords and closing the lips; it differs from *m* because the nasal passage is also closed. The sound *m* is a bilabial nasal sound, generally voiced. The primary difference between *m* and *b* is that in *m*, the nasal passage remains open, allowing the sound to be prolonged, whereas *b* is an explosive or instantaneous sound.
