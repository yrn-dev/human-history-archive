# Seventh Year of the War: Capture of Sphakteria

The seventh year of the Peloponnesian War was characterized by a shift in momentum resulting from an unexpected Athenian victory at Sphakteria. While the first seven years of the conflict had been a period of trial for Athens—marked by the revolt of Mitylênê, a destructive pestilence, and annual invasions of Attica—the capture of Lacedæmonian prisoners provided the Athenians with a significant strategic advantage and a surge of confidence.

## Invasion of Attica and the Pylus Expedition
The year began with the customary Lacedæmonian invasion of Attica. In approximately late April, King Agis of Sparta led the Peloponnesian army into the territory to repeat the usual ravages. This annual invasion had become an ordinary enterprise, though the hopes associated with it had diminished since the start of the war.

Simultaneously, distress arose in Korkyra due to attacks by oligarchical exiles, prompting both the Athenians and Peloponnesians to send fleets to the region. Demosthenês accompanied the Athenian fleet with a separate command and proposed the erection of a fort at Pylus in Laconia. Although the admiral of the fleet, Eurymedon, initially insisted on proceeding directly to Korkyra, a storm drove the fleet into Pylus. With the voluntary zeal of the soldiers, Demosthenês fortified the location and remained there with a garrison while the fleet continued to Korkyra.

## The Conflict at Sphakteria
The Lacedæmonians marched slowly to recover Pylus. Upon arrival, they sent a detachment to occupy the island of Sphakteria, located opposite Pylus, and launched attacks by land and sea. During the seaside assault, Brasidas demonstrated gallant conduct. However, Eurymedon and the Athenian fleet returned to Pylus and defeated the Lacedæmonian fleet in the harbor. This victory blocked the Lacedæmonian detachment on the island of Sphakteria.

An armistice was concluded at Pylus, and Lacedæmonian envoys were sent to Athens to propose peace and request the release of the soldiers trapped on Sphakteria. At the instance of Kleon, the Athenians demanded the restoration of Achaia, Trœzen, Pegæ, and Nisæa as conditions for peace and the release of the prisoners. The envoys refused these demands, and Kleon prevented further negotiation. Consequently, the armistice was terminated and war resumed.

## The Blockade and Capture
The Athenian fleet maintained a blockade of Sphakteria, a process marked by uncertainty and hardships for the seamen. Demosthenês eventually requested reinforcements from Athens to attack the island. In the Athenian assembly, political enemies of Kleon attempted to maneuver him into serving as general at Pylus against his will.

The operations culminated in the capture of Sphakteria around July 425 B.C. This event, combined with the surrender of the entire Lacedæmonian fleet, was viewed as a momentous occurrence across Greece. By the spring of the eighth year, Athens possessed the Spartan prisoners as well as Kythêra and Pylos, leading to the possibility of a rising among the Helots.

## Aftermath and Strategic Shift
The capture of the prisoners profoundly altered the psychological state of the belligerents. The Lacedæmonians and their allies—including Perdikkas, king of Macedonia, and the revolted Chalkidic allies in Thrace—were intimidated. This fear led to the concerting of the expedition of Brasidas. Conversely, the Athenians were filled with "prodigious confidence," leading them to abandon the conservative precepts of Periklês.

In 424 B.C., emboldened by their success, the Athenians attempted to recover Bœotia and Megara by force. While they succeeded in capturing Nisæa, they failed to reconquer Megara and suffered a disastrous defeat at Delium in Bœotia.

The influence of Kleon was pivotal during this period. Initially, his ascendency was a primary reason Athens rejected the first Lacedæmonian peace offers. After the captives were brought home, the general temper of Athens became so warlike that Kleon's personal influence was no longer required to maintain the war effort. Even Nikias and other citizens shared this sanguine outlook. However, the subsequent failures in Bœotia and losses in Thrace eventually lowered the impression of Athenian power, leading to a period where Athens became more disposed to peace.
