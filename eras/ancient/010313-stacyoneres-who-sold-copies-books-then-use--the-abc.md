# Stationers and the History of Bookselling

The trade in books is of ancient origin, evolving from oral recitations to the establishment of professional guilds and regulated markets. Early poets and orators often recited their works in public to encourage audiences to acquire written copies, which were frequently transcribed *viva voce* and sold to wealthy patrons.

## Ancient and Classical Bookselling
In early biblical history, the book of Jeremiah describes the prophet dictating to Baruch the scribe; such scribes served as the earliest booksellers, providing copies upon demand. In ancient Greece, Aristotle maintained an extensive library, and Plato is recorded to have paid one hundred minae for three small treatises by Philolaus the Pythagorean. The founding of the Alexandrian library around 300 B.C. further stimulated Athenian booksellers, known as *biblion kapaeloi*.

In Rome, during the late republic, libraries became common household furniture. Booksellers, referred to as *librarii* or *bibliopolae*, operated flourishing shops (*taberna librarii*) primarily in the Vicus Sandalarius and the Argiletum. These shops displayed lists of available books on their doors or side posts. For example, Martial noted that a copy of his First Book of Epigrams was available for five denarii. During the era of Augustus, the Sosii were the prominent booksellers. Additionally, a law passed under Justinian secured the property of materials used by scribes, which may represent the earliest origin of modern copyright law.

## Medieval Stationers and Guilds
The spread of Christianity increased the demand for sacred texts, including the Gospels, missals, and devotional volumes. In 671, Benedict Biscop, founder of the abbey at Wearmouth in England, imported a cargo of books from France, some of which he had purchased.

Prior to the Reformation, text writers known as stationers (*stacyoneres*) operated in the neighborhood of St Paul's in London. They sold manuscript copies of prayers and common texts such as the Creed, the Paternoster, the Ave Maria, and the ABC. In 1403, these stationers formed a gild. Some maintained stalls or stations built directly against the walls of the cathedral.

In Oxford, "sworn" university stationers held a significant monopoly on supplying books to pupils. John More of Oxford is cited as a sworn stationer who supplied books and also acted as a pawnbroker. Because many students could not afford books and instruction was primarily oral, books frequently changed hands. However, the presence of non-sworn booksellers led to a 1373 statute prohibiting any bookseller—other than two sworn stationers or their deputies—from selling books valued at more than half a mark, as it was claimed that non-sworn sellers cheated owners and deprived sworn stationers of lawful business.

## Printing and Early Modern Production
Early block-books, which resemble Chinese specimens in their use of brown paint or ink and the practice of placing blank sides of pages opposite each other, are believed to predate moveable type. Tradition suggests Guttemberg may have learned his art from specimens brought home by Marco Polo.

Early printed books were often large and heavy, bound with wooden boards and stamped leather. This was especially true for Bibles, legal commentaries, and choir-books. Smaller formats, such as small folios and later thin quartos, were used for popular works, while prayer-books, books of hours, and psalters were the only very small books in use.

The introduction of printing significantly reduced costs; in 1467, the bishop of Aleria reported that prices dropped to one-fifth of previous levels. For example, fifteen "Legends" bequeathed by Caxton to St Margaret's, Westminster, sold for between 5s and 6s. 8d. Conversely, a rubricated and illuminated vellum copy of the first printed Bible (Mainz, c. 1455) was valued at 100 florins.

## Evolution of Prices and Bindings
By the 17th century, English printing reached a low point in the second quarter before improving via modifications to presses adopted from Holland. During this era, prayer-books and small Bibles were often decorated with gold or silver thread and colored silks. Trade bindings typically used sheepskin or rough calf, and lettering on the back of the book became popular.

Prices rose during the 17th century due to monopolies, increased author payments, and inflation. Church Bibles, which cost 10s. in 1541, rose to 40s. by 1641. Other notable prices included:
*   Shakespeare folio (1623): £1
*   *Paradise Lost*: 3s.
*   *The Pilgrim's Progress* and Izaak Walton's *Compleat Angler*: 1s. 6d.
*   Dryden's *Virgil* (subscription): £5 5s.

In the 18th century, paper and presswork improved in England, France, and later Germany and Italy. Trade bindings shifted toward paper boards. The subscription model remained lucrative for poets like Pope and Prior. Novels were typically sold in multiple volumes; for instance, Miss Burney's *Evelina* (3 vols., 1778) sold for 7s. 5d., while her *Camilla* (5 vols., 1796) cost £1 1s. Johnson's *Dictionary* (1755) cost £4 4s. in sheets.

## Regulation and Collecting
The first English Copyright Act of 1709 allowed individuals to complain to the archbishop of Canterbury if a book's price was unreasonably high, though this was largely a "dead letter." In London, brokers were licensed by the corporation until 1884 to act as intermediaries between buyers and sellers, using "bought notes" and "sold notes" to document contracts under the Statute of Frauds.

Modern book collecting often focuses on first editions or copies associated with famous figures, such as Mary queen of Scots. While first editions of Greek and Latin classics are now primarily valued as specimens of printing, the prestige of first editions of English and French literary masterpieces has increased significantly.
