# The Spoliation of Ascalon

## Overview of Ascalon
Ascalon (currently known as 'Askalan) was one of the five primary cities of the Philistines. It was situated on the Mediterranean coast, approximately 12 miles north of Gaza. The city was well-fortified and served as the center for the worship of Derketo, a fish-goddess. While Ascalon was located within the nominal territory of the tribe of Judah, the Israelites never possessed the city for any significant length of time.

## Biblical Record and the Spoliation
The Bible contains only one recorded incident regarding the history of Ascalon: the spoliation of the city by Samson, as detailed in Judges xiv. 19. However, it is noted that this event may not have occurred at the coastal city, but possibly at another location of the same name situated in the hill country of Judaea.

## Ancient and Classical History
Ascalon appears several times in the Tell el-Amarna correspondence. Its early history was marked by conflict with Egypt; the city revolted from Egyptian rule on two separate occasions, though it was reconquered both times. A sculpture located at Thebes depicts the storming of the city.

In 701 B.C., the city was taken by Sennacherib. Later, the conquests of Alexander led to the hellenization of Ascalon's civilization. Following the era of Alexander, the city became a tributary to Egypt and Syria alternately. Herod the Great, a native of Ascalon, contributed significantly to the beauty of the city. In later periods, the city suffered severe damage during the wars involving the Romans and the Israelites.

## Medieval Period and Decline
Ascalon regained importance in the 4th century and remained a center of learning and the seat of a bishopric until the 7th century, when it was conquered by the Moslems. During the first crusade, Christians achieved a signal victory in the neighboring plain on August 15, 1099, but the city remained under the control of the caliphs until 1157. At that time, Baldwin III, the king of Jerusalem, captured the city after a five-month siege. In 1178, Baldwin IV gave the city to his sister Sibylla upon her marriage to William of Montferrat.

The city's fortunes shifted in 1187 when Saladin, having nearly annihilated the Christian army in the plain of Tiberias, took Ascalon after it offered only feeble resistance. Saladin initially strengthened and repaired the fortifications. However, after Richard Coeur de Lion captured St Jean d'Acre (Acre) in 1191, Saladin ordered Ascalon to be dismantled. The English king restored the city the following year, but it was subsequently abandoned.

The importance of Ascalon continued to decline until 1270, when Sultan Bibars filled the port with stones and almost totally destroyed the city's fortifications.
