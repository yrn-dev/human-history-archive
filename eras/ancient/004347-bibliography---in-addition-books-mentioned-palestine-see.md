# BIBLIOGRAPHY

## Definition and Evolution
The term bibliography originates from the Greek word *bibliographia*. In post-classical Greek, it referred to the writing of books. This meaning persisted into the 18th century; for instance, Fenning's *English Dictionary* (1761) defined a bibliographer as someone who copies or writes books. 

A shift in meaning from "a writing of books" to "a writing about books" occurred in France during the 18th century, as evidenced by the 1763 publication of de Bure's *Bibliographie instructive*. In England, this updated meaning was popularized in the early 19th century by the Rev. Thomas Frognall Dibdin. During this period, Southey used the alternative term *bibliology*, though that form is now rarely used.

## Scope and Practice
Modern custom generally restricts the province of bibliography to printed books, distinguishing them from manuscripts. However, the field is broad and encompasses various interests of the book-loving antiquary, including book-collecting, book-binding, book-illustration, and the history of printing (typography). 

In a technical sense, bibliography is the art of describing, collating, and examining books. It involves the enumeration and arrangement of books into lists for informational purposes. The study of the literature regarding this subject is referred to as the "bibliography of bibliography."

## National and Universal Bibliographies
The pursuit of a universal bibliography has become increasingly difficult as the output of the printing press has grown. The closest existing approximations to such a work are the printed catalogues of the Bibliotheque Nationale at Paris and the library of the British Museum. For books of great rarity not found in these catalogues, researchers may consult the *Bibliographer's Manual* by Lowndes, Brunet's *Manuel du libraire*, or the bibliographical collections of Mr. W.C. Hazlitt.

Due to the impossibility of a universal record, national bibliographies have emerged:
* **Great Britain:** Robert Watt's *Bibliotheca Britannica* (1824) is noted as an extraordinary achievement by a single individual. Additionally, catalogues of English books printed up to the end of 1640 provide significant material.
* **France:** Querard's *La France litteraire* (1827-1839) focuses primarily on books from the 18th and early 19th centuries written by historians, savants, and "gens de lettres."
* **Germany:** The works of Kayser (*Buecherlexikon, 1750*, etc.) and Heinsius (*Allgemeines Buecherlexikon, 1700-1815*) provide records of German book output over two centuries.

## Bibliographies of Bibliography
Several key works serve as bibliographies of bibliographies, often incorporating the value of their predecessors. Notable examples include:
* *Bibliotheca bibliographica* by Dr. Julius Petzholdt (1866).
* *Manuel de bibliographie generale* by Henri Stein (1898).
* *Manuel de bibliographie historique* by Ch. V. Langlois (1901).
* *A Register of National Bibliography* by W.P. Courtney (1905).
* *Some Aspects of Bibliography* by Professor Ferguson (1900), which includes an eclectic list and introduction.

Furthermore, the British Museum provides the *List of Books of Reference in the Reading-Room* (first published 1889) and a *Subject-index of the Modern Works added to the Library of the British Museum in the years 1881-1900*, edited by G.K. Fortescue.

## Specialized Bibliographical Applications
Bibliography is applied across various academic disciplines to organize literature and textual criticism:

**New Testament Textual Criticism:** Due to the vast amount of literature, key modern works include those by H. von Soden, E. Nestle, F.G. Kenyon, C.R. Gregory, and Westcott and Hort. The history of this criticism is further detailed by F.H. Scrivener.

**Regional and Archaeological Studies:** For those studying Palestine and surrounding regions, sources include the *Quarterly Statement* of the Palestine Exploration Fund (1895), works by G. Schumacher, and the 1900 American survey of the Hauran directed by Dr. Enno Littmann. Other relevant texts include works by U. J. Seetzen, Rev. J. L. Porter, and the *Inscriptions of Syria* by W. H. Waddington.

**Biblical and Apocryphal Studies:** Bibliographies for the Book of Daniel include works by Kamphausen, Behrmann, J. D. Prince, and G. A. Barton. Regarding apocryphal writings, the *Book of Enoch* (or *Ethiopic Book of Enoch*) is considered a conglomerate of fragments circulating under the names of Noah, Enoch, and possibly Methuselah.

**Literary Figures:** Bibliographies are also compiled for specific authors, such as Rudyard Kipling, utilizing sources like John Lane, Richard de Gallienne, and the *English Illustrated Magazine*.
