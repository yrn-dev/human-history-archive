# Ezra and Related Literary Works

The figure of Ezra is associated with several distinct literary traditions, ranging from historical narratives and canonical collections to apocryphal legends and scholarly commentaries.

## The Books of Ezra and Nehemiah
The books of Ezra and Nehemiah serve as a direct sequel to the narrative found in Chronicles. These works are characterized by a structure based on excerpted sources, specifically the Memoirs of Ezra and Nehemiah, which were written by the figures themselves in the first person. Examples of these memoirs include Ezra vii. 12-ix. (which incorporates the decree of Artaxerxes in vii. 12-26), Neh. i. 1-vii. 73a, xii. 31-41, and xiii. 

Additionally, the books contain a narrative written in Aramaic (Ezra iv. 8-vi. 18). It is probable that Ezra x. and Neh. viii.-x. are based directly on the Memoirs of Ezra, while the remaining portions of the texts are the work of a compiler. In the broader context of the Jewish canon, the books of Ezra and Nehemiah are counted as one book within the "Writings" (Hagiographa).

## Relation to 4 Ezra and Baruch
There is a significant scholarly debate regarding the relationship between the book of Baruch and 4 Ezra. Due to numerous affinities, some scholars, such as Ewald and Ryle, have assumed they share the same author. However, others argue that their points of divergence are too weighty to sustain this view. 

If the works are viewed as composite, it suggests that some parts of 4 Ezra are older than the latest parts of Baruch, while some parts of Baruch are older than those of 4 Ezra. Regarding the priority of the two works, a group of scholars including Langen, Hilgenfeld, Wieseler, Staehelin, Renan, Hausrath, Drummond, Dillmann, Rosenthal, and Gunkel maintain that 4 Ezra came first. Conversely, Schuerer, Bissell, Thomson, Deane, Kabisch, De Faye, Wellhausen, and Ryssel argue for the priority of Baruch.

## Ezra and the Old Testament Canon
There is a persistent but unfounded popular idea that Ezra closed the canon of the Old Testament. This notion is supported by a legend found in the apocryphal book of 2 Esdras, written toward the end of the 1st century A.D. According to this text, after the law was burnt, Ezra was miraculously inspired to rewrite it. He is said to have dictated 94 books to five skilled scribes over forty days; these included the 24 books of the Old Testament and 70 apocryphal books, the latter of which were claimed to possess a superior or esoteric wisdom.

Modern scholarship regards this account as a legend unworthy of credit, noting it provides no evidence of a formal collection or redaction of sacred books by Ezra. Similar legends grew in the 13th century A.D., later appearing in the works of Elias Levita (1538) and Johannes Buxtorf (1665). These accounts claimed that Ezra presided over the "men of the Great Synagogue" to collect the Old Testament into a single volume and divide it into the Law, the Prophets, and the Writings. However, there is no authority in antiquity to support these claims.

## Other Literary and Historical Associations
Ezra appears in the writings of Jesus ben Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), written approximately 200-180 B.C., though he is described there as a prominent priest and scholar rather than a prophet. 

In the realm of biblical commentary, Moses Kimhi composed commentaries on Ezra and Nehemiah. These works were later included in great rabbinical bibles, though they were falsely attributed to Abraham ibn Ezra.
