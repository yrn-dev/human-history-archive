# Balaam

Balaam, the son of Beor, was a Gentile seer and prophet who appears in the biblical history of the Israelites. His narrative takes place during the Israelites' sojourn in the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan, near the end of their forty years of wandering and shortly before the crossing of the Jordan and the death of Moses.

## Biblical Narrative and Oracles
The primary account of Balaam is found in the book of Numbers. Following the Israelite conquest of Og, king of Bashan, and Sihon, king of the Amorites, Balak, the king of Moab, became alarmed. Balak sent for Balaam to curse the Israelites. Although Balaam initially hesitated, he eventually set out with two servants to meet Balak.

During this journey, the Angel of Yahweh met Balaam. Initially, the Angel was visible only to Balaam's ass, which attempted to avoid the Angel, provoking Balaam's anger. The ass was miraculously enabled to speak to Balaam, and eventually, Yahweh allowed Balaam to see the Angel. The Angel informed him that he would have been slain if not for the ass. Although Balaam offered to return, he was instructed to proceed.

Upon arriving, Balaam warned Balak that he could only speak the words God told him. Despite Balak's offerings of sacrifice and attempts to move Balaam to different locations—including the top of Pisgah—to achieve a more favorable result, Yahweh compelled Balaam to bless the Israelites rather than curse them. The narrative serves as a framework for seven oracular poems.

## Divergent Traditions and the Priestly Code
The archive's sources indicate that the Balaam narratives are compiled from different ancient documents. While the J and E documents focus on the inspiration of a non-Hebrew prophet, the Priestly Code presents a different account. In this version, Balaam advises the Midianites on how to bring disaster upon Israel by seducing the people and leading them away from their loyalty to Yahweh. This tradition concludes with Balaam being slain in battle while fighting in the ranks of Midian.

## Interpretations and Folklore
Balaam has been a subject of significant interest across various traditions:

*   **Historical and Philosophical Views:** Josephus describes Balaam as the best prophet of his time, though possessing a disposition unable to resist temptation. Philo characterizes him as a great magician or a "sophist," using him to symbolize a "vain crowd of contrary and warring opinions."
*   **Rabbinical Traditions:** Later Targums and Talmuds represent him as a typical sinner. Various Rabbinical fables claim he was blind in one eye, was the Elihu mentioned in Job, or served as a governor of an Ethiopian city under Pharaoh before being put to flight by Moses. One Rabbinical identification (Yalkut) associates Balaam with Laban, the father-in-law of Jacob.
*   **Islamic Traditions:** Various fables describe Balaam as one of the Anakim (giants of Palestine). It is said he read the books of Abraham, through which he learned the name Yahweh, allowing him to predict the future and receive whatever he asked from God. Some conjecture that the Arabic wise man Luqman is identical to Balaam, noting that "Luqman" means "devourer" or "swallower," a meaning derived from a popular etymology of Balaam.
*   **Scholarly Interpretations:** Regarding the speaking ass, Maimonides, E. W. Hengstenberg, and other Christian scholars suggested the episode was a vision. Others, such as Volck, argue the account is figurative, suggesting Balaam translated the braying of the ass into words.

## Prophetic Significance
Balaam is noted for his prophecy regarding Amalek, stating that while Amalek was the first of the nations, his end would be to perish forever. Josephus suggests this prophecy was fulfilled when the Israelites destroyed seventy-five thousand eight hundred of their enemies, likely Amalekites.

From a religious history perspective, the recognition of Balaam as a genuine prophet by the J and E sources suggests that Yahweh was recognized as a deity by a circle of connected tribes beyond the Israelites, and that He revealed His will to nations other than Israel.
