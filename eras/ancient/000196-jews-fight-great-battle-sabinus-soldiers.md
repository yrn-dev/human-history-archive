# The Conflict Between the Jews and Sabinus at Jerusalem

The conflict involving Sabinus in Jerusalem was characterized by a series of violent engagements, the siege of the royal palace, and a significant battle centered around the temple and its surrounding structures. These events were driven by the indignation of the local population toward the actions and "madness" of Sabinus.

## The Gathering of Forces
As the festival of Pentecost approached, tens of thousands of men assembled. While the occasion was a traditional festival of their forefathers, the multitude gathered primarily due to their indignation regarding the injuries and madness of Sabinus. This force included a large number of Idumeans and Galileans, as well as men from Jericho and those who had crossed the river Jordan.

The assembled forces divided themselves into three distinct bands to enclose the Romans on all sides:
* One band seized the hippodrome.
* A second band positioned themselves on the east quarter, stretching from the northern to the southern part of the temple.
* A third band occupied the western part of the city, where the king's palace was located.

## The Siege of the Palace
The Jews focused their efforts on besieging the Romans and encompassing the palace. They dug mines beneath the palace walls and urged those who had defected to the Romans not to hinder them, as they viewed this as an opportunity to recover the ancient liberty of their country.

During this period, the Jews offered a promise of lenity: they commanded those in the palace to leave immediately, promising that neither the occupants nor Sabinus would be harmed if they departed. While the greatest part of the king's troops deserted to the Jews, other elements of Herod's army remained with the Romans. Specifically, Rufus and Gratus, leading three thousand of the most warlike men of active bodies, joined the Romans, along with a band of horsemen under Rufus's command.

Sabinus, despite being desirous of leaving, distrusted the Jews' assurances, viewing their gentle treatment as a "bait laid as a snare." Relying on the hope that Varus was coming to his assistance, Sabinus continued to bear the siege.

## The Battle at the Temple
Sabinus became affrighted by the courage and multitude of the Jews. He retreated to the highest tower of the fortress, known as Phasaelus—named after Herod's brother, who was destroyed by the Parthians. From this tower, Sabinus signaled his soldiers to attack, though he did not venture to go down to his own men, fearing they would be exposed to death because of his own avarice.

The Roman soldiers leaped into the temple, and a terrible battle ensued. Initially, the Romans prevailed due to their superior skill in war compared to the Jews' lack of skill. However, the tide shifted when many Jews ascended to the top of the cloisters and threw darts downward onto the heads of the Romans. This tactic destroyed a great many Romans, who found it difficult to avenge themselves against those attacking from above or to sustain the fight against those engaging them hand-to-hand.

## Destruction and Plunder
The conflict resulted in great destruction. The Romans set fire to the cloisters, which were noted for their costliness and magnitude. This caused many people to perish in the flames; some were destroyed by the enemy, some threw themselves backward from the walls, and others committed suicide with their own swords.

Following the dispersal and destruction of the Jews, the Roman soldiers plundered the deserted treasure of God. They seized approximately four hundred talents, the majority of which was collected by Sabinus. The loss of the money dedicated to God in the temple, along with the deaths of their friends in battle, caused the Jews further grief and intensified their resolve.
