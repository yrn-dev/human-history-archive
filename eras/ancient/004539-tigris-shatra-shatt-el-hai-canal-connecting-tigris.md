# Tigris

The Tigris is one of the two great rivers of the region, flowing through a flat country composed of alluvial soil. It is a primary waterway for transportation and irrigation, though its navigation and the stability of its channel are subject to significant environmental challenges.

## Geography and Navigation
The Tigris flows through an absolutely flat landscape, which makes the river prone to changing its channel. During the dry season—occurring in autumn and winter—navigation is hindered by shifting shoals and flats that pose a danger of grounding. Conversely, during flood times, the surrounding country can become a huge lake, making it extremely difficult to locate the river channel. 

Navigation is further complicated by the inhabitants of the eastern bank, who frequently dig new irrigation canals. These activities reduce the river's water volume and contribute to the shifting of its course. From Bagdad to Korna, navigation is described as slow and uncertain. Above Bagdad, sailing vessels of 30 tons or more navigate the river to Samarra and beyond. Local service in the vicinity of Bagdad is characterized by the *kufa*, a circular basket-work boat covered in bitumen, a design used since remotest antiquity and depicted on old Assyrian monuments. Other ancient craft include rafts known as *kellek*.

## Major Settlements and Strategic Points
Several important towns and posts are situated along the Tigris:

*   **Bagdad:** The capital of its Turkish vilayet, Bagdad lies on both sides of the Tigris in an extensive desert plain. At this location, the Tigris and Euphrates reach their closest proximity, separated by little more than 25 miles.
*   **Kut-el-Amara:** A small town located on the east bank of the Tigris. It serves as an important Turkish post for controlling the lower Tigris and acts as a coaling station for steamers traveling between Bagdad and Basra. It is situated at the point where the Shatt-el-Hai leaves the river.
*   **Amara:** A principal settlement located on the Tigris.
*   **Korna:** Located at the junction where the Tigris and Euphrates meet to form the joint stream known as the Shatt el-Arab.

## Canal Systems and Irrigation
The Tigris has historically been linked to the Euphrates through an extensive network of canals used for irrigation and transport. 

**The Shatt el-Hai**
The Shatt el-Hai is a canal connecting the Tigris and the Euphrates. The town of Shatra is located on this canal, while Nasrieh is situated at the junction where the canal meets the Euphrates. In early Babylonian history, the city of Lagash (Tello) was located eastward of the Shatt el-Hai, which is believed to be the ancient channel of the Tigris.

**The Sakhlawieh and Bagdad's Waterways**
Near Bagdad, the Sakhlawieh canal (formerly Isa) connects the two rivers. It leaves the Euphrates near the ruins of ancient Anbar and enters the Tigris below Bagdad via the Masudi canal. While once navigable for craft of some size, it now primarily increases periodic floods that can turn Bagdad into an island city. Historically, Bagdad was intersected by numerous aqueducts and canals that brought Tigris water into homes; today, only one aqueduct remains, conveying water to the shrine of Abd al-Qadir.

**Ancient and Historical Canals**
Herodotus noted that Babylonia was intersected by canals, including one great navigable canal flowing from the Euphrates to the Tigris. Xenophon described four distinct canals drawn from the Tigris to the Euphrates near the Wall of Media. These canals were reportedly 100 feet broad and deep enough for heavy vessels, distributing water through smaller ditches for irrigation.

## Regional Context and Economy
The lower reaches of the Tigris, along with the Euphrates and Shatt el-Arab, support vast plantations of date-palms producing the finest known dates. Other cultivated crops include rice, wheat, barley, liquorice, and roses. However, many ancient canals essential for fertility have fallen into ruin, and the land is subject to inundations that make settled agriculture impracticable. Consequently, the population consists largely of nomadic and semi-nomadic tribes possessing herds of goats, sheep, horses, and buffaloes.

The combined stream of the Tigris and Euphrates was known to the Babylonians as the *nar marrati*, or "the salt river," a term also applied to the Persian Gulf.
