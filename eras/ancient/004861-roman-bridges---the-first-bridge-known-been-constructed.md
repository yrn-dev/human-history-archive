# Roman Bridges

Roman bridge construction utilized a variety of materials and engineering techniques to span rivers and valleys, ranging from early timber structures to massive masonry and concrete works. These structures served diverse purposes, including urban transit, military movement, and the transport of water.

## Bridges of Rome
The earliest known bridge constructed in Rome over the Tiber was the timber Pons Sublicius, which was defended by Horatius. Over time, the city developed several other significant crossings:

*   **Pons Milvius:** Now known as Ponte Molle, this bridge was reconstructed in stone by M. Aemilius Scaurus in 109 B.C. The current structure is believed to contain portions of the old bridge, with arches featuring spans between 51 and 79 ft.
*   **Pons Fabricius:** Dating to approximately 62 B.C., this bridge (modernly called Ponte dei Quattro Capi) remains practically intact.
*   **Pons Cestius:** Built likely in 46 B.C., this structure retains much of its original masonry.
*   **Pons Aelius:** Constructed by Hadrian in A.D. 134, this bridge is now the bridge of St Angelo. It featured eight arches, with the largest span measuring 62 ft. It was later repaired by Pope Clement IX and Pope Nicholas II.

## Imperial and Provincial Works
Roman engineers extended their bridge-building capabilities across the empire to facilitate military and administrative control. 

In A.D. 104, Trajan constructed a bridge over the Danube, which Dio Cassius notes may have reached 3,000 to 4,000 ft. in length. While some piers are said to still exist, a bas-relief on the Trajan column depicts the bridge with timber arches and masonry piers, though this representation is considered probably conventional. Trajan also built the bridge of Alcantara in Spain. This stone block structure was built without cement, reaching a total length of 670 ft. and standing 210 ft. above the stream with six arches.

In the 6th century, the bridge of Narses was built to carry the Via Salaria over the Anio. This bridge featured a fortification, a design element that became common in later bridges for the purposes of enforcement of tolls or defense. The bridge of Narses was destroyed in 1867 during Garibaldi's approach to Rome.

Additionally, Julius Cæsar is noted for constructing bridges for military crossings. One such bridge was built near Coblenz, allowing Cæsar to cross the Moselle and reach the territory of the Ubii after defeating the Germans. Cæsar later constructed a second bridge nearby to provide passage from the territory of the Treviri into that of the Ubii.

## Aqueducts and Materials
Roman engineers also constructed great lines of aqueducts starting from 300 B.C. In areas where these lines were carried above ground, they took the form of arched bridge structures of remarkable magnitude. These were generally constructed using concrete and brick.

Broadly, during classical times, bridges were built using masonry, timber, or later, concrete and brick. While many bridges featuring timber superstructures and stone piers were constructed from Roman times onward, most of these have since perished.
