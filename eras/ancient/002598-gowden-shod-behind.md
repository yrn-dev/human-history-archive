# And gowden shod behind

The phrase "And gowden shod behind" appears in the context of traditional ballads and folk songs, specifically referring to the splendid accoutrements of horses. In the ballad *Willie's Lady*, a steed is described as being "Silver shod before, And gowden shod behind."

## Luxury in Folk Traditions and Epics
The use of gold in folk songs and early epics often reflects a theme of primitive luxury. This is seen not only in the description of horses but also in other objects within various cultural traditions:
*   **Ballads and Songs:** In a ballad shared by Scotland and France, a lover uses "ciseaux d'or fin" (fine gold scissors) to cut a winding-sheet. Similarly, a song from Poitou mentions a *faucille d'or* (golden sickle), and a variant of the story of Hugh of Lincoln includes tables and chairs made of gold.
*   **Regional Ballads:** In Romaic ballads, the horses of the Klephts are described as being gold shod.
*   **Early Epics:** This pattern of luxury is also observed in the *Chanson de Roland* and the works of Homer, the latter of which may reflect the actual radiance of "golden Mycenae."

## Practical Applications of Shodding
While gold is used as a symbol of luxury in song and epic, historical and practical records indicate the use of various other materials for shodding horses and tools.

### Metal and Natural Materials
*   **Iron and Bronze:** In early Japanese farming, archaeology and history show the use of wooden hoes shod with either iron or bronze. In modern military contexts, such as German columns marching to Ghent, the "eternal clatter of iron-shod hoofs" is noted against stone paving.
*   **Horn:** In Lyons, horn shoes made from sheep horn have been used for horses in towns to prevent slipping on slippery pavements. Similarly, it is noted that the horns of the *Ovis Poli* (the Great Sheep of Pamir) supply shoes for Kirghiz horses.
*   **Absence of Shodding:** In certain mountainous regions, horses are noted for their speed and ability to navigate steep descents and bad roads despite not being shod at all.

### Specialized Shodding
In military history, shodding has been used strategically. During a blockade of the Plataeans, soldiers attempting to scale an enemy wall were lightly equipped and had only their left foot shod. This was intended to preserve them from slipping in the mire, as the left foot must be firmly planted for a warrior using his right arm.

## Other "Iron-Shod" Objects
The term "iron-shod" is also applied to weaponry and tools of revolt. During periods of unrest in France, specifically around Versailles, "iron-shod clubs" (*batons ferrés*) were used by crowds. These objects ended in swordblades or knives, serving as a type of extempore billhook.

## Related Motifs in Folk Song
Beyond the luxury of gold, folk traditions often include other recurring elements. For example, talking birds are common in various songs:
*   The "omniscient popinjay" of Border minstrelsy.
*   The *tria poulakia* (three little birds) in Romaic ballads.
*   A "wicked dove" in French folk-songs.
*   The bird in *Water o' Wearies Well* that came out of a bush to dine on water.

Additionally, Scottish verse, such as that of Burns, captures a wide range of northern life, including the "red-wat-shod" march of veterans.
