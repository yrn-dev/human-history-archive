# Ninu ilu [s.]irum

*Ninu ilu [s.]irum* is the title of the chapters into which the Code (the backbone of the reconstructed sketch of early Babylonian law) was divided. The title is derived from the opening words of the text.

## Transmission and Study
Fragments of the Code were recovered from the library of Assur-bani-pal at Nineveh, as well as from later Babylonian copies. These records indicate that the text was studied and recopied for a period of fifteen hundred years or more. 

The legal framework established by the Code remained largely in force through several successive conquests, including those of the Persians, Greeks, and Parthians. These foreign conquests had very little effect on private life in Babylonia. Consequently, the Code survived to influence later Mahommedan law in Mesopotamia, as well as Syro-Roman law.

## Legal Context in Mesopotamia
The history of law in the region is categorized into "early" law—the customs and laws that preceded the Code—and "late" law, which refers to the law of the New Babylonian empire and the subsequent Persian and Greek periods. 

The legal development of the region was influenced by the settlement of Semitic tribes in Babylonian cities, where tribal customs were integrated into city law. Early history was characterized by struggles for supremacy between these cities. While a dominant metropolis would demand military support and tribute from subject cities, the local customs and cults of those subject cities remained unaffected, as kings and conquerors generally respected city rights and usages. In Assyria, law was derived from Babylonia, though it maintained early features longer than they were preserved in other areas.

## Related Historical Figures and Traditions
While *Ninu ilu [s.]irum* refers to the legal chapters of the Code, the name "Ninus" appears in various historical and mythological traditions. Diodorus describes Ninus as the most ancient of the Assyrian kings, characterized by a warlike disposition and ambition for glory. He is credited with training young men in laborious exercises and hardships to prepare them for the fatigues of war. Some writers confound Ninus with Nimrod, the son of Chus and grandson of Ham, who is described in Scripture as a "mighty hunter before the Lord."

According to tradition, Ninus formed a state bounded by the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, uniting different people under a single polity and set of laws. He is also associated with the conquest of Bactria, achieved with the assistance of his wife, Semiramis. Semiramis, born in the Syrian city of Ascalon, directed the attack on the citadel of Bactria, allowing Ninus to take the city and its treasure.

## The City of Nineveh
The city of Nineveh, associated with these traditions, underwent significant changes over time. According to Herodotus, the second Median king, Phraortes, was slain while attacking Nineveh. Later, Kyaxarês, the son of Phraortes, destroyed the city after capturing it. It is suggested that the ruins of Nineveh were used by the Medes to form two towns, Nimroud and Kouyunjik, on the Tigris. 

Following the transition from Median to Persian dominion, these towns became depopulated. However, traditions regarding the original Ninus persisted, leading to the subsequent rise of a second town bearing the name Ninus. This later city is recognized by Ammianus, Ptolemy, and Tacitus, and it claimed an uninterrupted succession from the ancient "caput Assyriæ."
