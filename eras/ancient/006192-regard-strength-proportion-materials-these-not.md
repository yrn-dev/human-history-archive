# Strength and Proportion of Materials

The analysis of strength and proportion varies significantly across different fields, ranging from civil engineering and chemistry to the biological characteristics of human populations and the composition of consumables.

## Building Codes and Material Standards
In the field of construction, there are notable differences between the regulatory approaches of New York and London. The New York Building Acts (specifically parts 4 and 5) provide detailed prescriptions for the strength and quality of materials. These include requirements that bricks be well-burned, hard, and good. Mortar sand must be clean, sharp, grit sand, free from dirt or loam, and must meet standard samples held by the department of buildings. The New York code also fully describes the quality of mortar and lime, as well as the strengths of cast-iron and steel, and includes tests for new materials.

In contrast, the London Building Act does not deal with the strength and proportion of materials to the same extent. While local by-laws in various districts cover some areas, the English acts do not provide definitions for the quality of steel, new materials, or timber. This disparity is attributed to the fact that iron and steel construction in England was in its infancy compared to its development in America.

Further specifications in the New York code include:
* **Foundations and Soil:** Plans must include a statement on the character of the soil at the footing level. The code defines the pressure under footings of foundations and the bearing capacity of soils.
* **Walls and Floors:** Part 6 defines the materials and methods for building walls. Part 23 addresses the strength of floors made of various materials and the required strength of temporary supports used during construction.
* **Calculations:** Part 24 covers wind pressure and the calculations regarding the strength of materials.

## Engineering and Chemical Properties
The interaction between different building materials is often governed by their physical coefficients. Steel and concrete possess nearly identical thermal coefficients of expansion, which prevents shearing stress at their junction during temperature changes. The load carried by these materials is in direct proportion to the coefficient of elasticity and inversely proportional to the moment of inertia of the cross section. 

The coefficient of elasticity (E) for steel averages about twelve times that of concrete, with steel ranging from 28,000,000 to 30,000,000 lb per sq. in. and concrete varying between 1,500,000 and 3,000,000 lb per sq. in. (the latter diminishing as the ratio of stone and sand to cement increases). In terms of working stress, concrete has a maximum compressive stress of 500 lb per sq. in., a tensile stress of 50 lb per sq. in., and a shearing stress of 75 lb per sq. in., while the tensile stress on steel may reach 16,000 lb per sq. in.

Regarding cements used for mortar:
* **Portland Cement:** Used for general building and underground work where durability and extra strength are required. Adding a small proportion of it can enhance the strength of ordinary lime mortar.
* **Roman Cement:** Sets rapidly (usually within fifteen minutes) and is used for embankments, tidal work, and underwater constructions. It possesses about one-third of the strength of Portland cement.
* **Selenitic Cement:** Lias lime mixed with a small proportion of plaster of Paris to induce quicker setting and suppress slaking. It can take a larger proportion of sand than ordinary lime.

In industrial smelting, the height of a blast furnace is limited by the strength of the "burden" (the column of materials), which must resist crushing under its own weight.

## Chemical and Biological Proportions
In chemistry, bichromates and trichromates are viewed as combinations of one molecular proportion of a normal salt with one or more molecular proportions of chromium trioxide.

In the analysis of British and Irish beers, strength and proportion are defined by gravity and solids:
* **Mild Ales:** Medium original gravity and alcoholic strength, with a relatively large proportion of solid matter. They have a high proportion of maltose to dextrin (roughly 1:1), creating a sweet taste.
* **Light Bitters and Ales:** Low original gravity, but a higher proportion of alcohol to solids than mild ales.
* **Pale and Stock Ales:** Similar in character to light bitters but with higher figures overall; they are "dry" and contain more dextrin (ratio of 1:1-1/2 or 1:2).
* **Stouts:** Characterized by high gravity and more solids compared to alcohol than light-colored heavy beers.

General beer composition includes mineral matter ("ash") at 0.2 to 0.3% (one-fourth of which is phosphoric acid) and proteid content ranging from 0.3 to 0.8%. Extractives consist roughly of 20-30% maltose and 20-50% dextrinous matter.
