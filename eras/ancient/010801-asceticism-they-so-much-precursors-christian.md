# Asceticism

Asceticism is defined as the theory and practice of self-mortification and bodily abstinence, which is generally religious in nature. The term is derived from the Greek verb *askeo* ("I practise"), leading to the noun *askaesis* and the adjective *askaetikos*. This terminology employs a metaphor from the ancient Greek *palaestra* (wrestling-place), where victory was granted to those who had most effectively trained their bodies. While various technical terms of Greek philosophic asceticism—used initially by Neo-pythagoreans and Cynics and later adopted by Christians and Greek Jews—were borrowed from athletic contests, these remained metaphors. True asceticism is based on a moral purpose, centered on the eternal contrast between what is "right" and what is "pleasant."

## Primitive and Philosophical Origins
The ascetic instinct is likely as old as humanity, though primitive forms often lacked lofty spiritual meaning. Early religious practices frequently involved self-torture and abstentions that appear valueless for the discipline of feelings or character training from a modern perspective. However, these non-moral restraints may have helped develop the human faculty of preferring a larger good over immediate impulses. Aristotle, in his *Ethics*, characterized the "barbarian's ideal of life" as "living as one likes," yet savages were often more restricted by superstitious scruples than civilized men. Great religious reformers, including Buddha, Moses, Paul, Mani, Mahomet, Luther, and St. Francis, worked to direct this instinct toward higher aims, replacing ritual purity with true personal holiness.

The Greek Cynics played a significant role in the history of asceticism. In an attempt to imitate the independence and rugged strength of Socrates, they adopted extreme behaviors that often caricatured him. The Cynics renounced riches, honors, pleasures, marriage, and property, affecting to live as beggars with only a wallet and staff. Unlike Plato and Aristotle, who viewed the perfect life as that of a householder and citizen, the Cynics were individualists and "citizens of the world" who lacked respect for the decaying ancient city-state. Their renunciation sometimes extended to knowledge, good manners, and cleanliness.

## Asceticism in Early Christianity
The Greek Cynics are considered precursors to Christian hermits; their descriptions in profane literature are sometimes mistaken for early monasticism. Within the early Christian Church, there was a leaning toward excessive asceticism. Early enthusiasts, particularly those expecting a speedy millennium, condemned permanent social institutions—such as public office, commerce, war, and the ownership of property—as impediments to the life of grace and alien to the kingdom of God. This necessitated a struggle by Paul and subsequent Catholic teachers to ensure that the baptized maintained the right to own property, engage in commerce, and marry.

Paul dealt with various questions of asceticism, including a Greek reaction against naturalism which viewed nature as vile and marriage as wrong. While Paul had a qualified tendency toward asceticism, he applied good sense to these matters. Additionally, the developing religion grafted upon itself various practices from competing cults, such as Mithraism, the cult of the Serapeum, and Judaism. These included self-tormenting asceticism and the practice of the fasting and shaven priest. While these adaptations made the teaching more acceptable in Syria and Egypt, they were viewed as disgusting by Arab nomads and were seen as unnatural by Nordic barbarians, such as the early Northmen and Anglo-Saxons, who felt an instinctive hostility toward the habits of priests, nuns, and robed monks.

## Sectarian and Global Variations
Different sects within and around the Christian tradition adopted varying degrees of ascetic rigor. The disciples of Marcion, active between 150 and 250 A.D., practiced rigorous asceticism and rejected the Old Testament. These Marcionites lived in close communities ruled by presbyters and bishops. Marcionite impulses later influenced the emergence of the Bogomils and Paulicians.

Beyond the Western and Near Eastern traditions, other forms of extraordinary abstinence existed. The Sensin, for example, practiced a lifelong fast, eating nothing but bran mixed with hot water and drinking only water. They slept on mats, wore black and blue hempen clothing, and refused to take wives. Their practice included the worship of fire and feminine idols.
