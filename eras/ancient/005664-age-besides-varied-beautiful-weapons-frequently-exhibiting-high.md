# The Bronze Age in Europe

The Bronze Age in Europe is a distinct stage of human civilization characterized by the production of utensils, implements, and weapons that differ in size and design from those of the preceding Stone Age or the succeeding era. A notable feature of this period is the uniformity of these objects; items found in one part of Europe are identical in pattern and size to those discovered in other regions.

## Material Culture and Craftsmanship
The implements of the Bronze Age include a variety of tools such as hammers, knives, gouges, awls, daggers, and arrow-heads. The period is particularly noted for its varied and beautiful weapons, which frequently exhibit high workmanship. 

Evidence suggests a potential Eastern origin for this culture, as the patterns of the arms are distinctly oriental. This theory is further supported by the design of the handles on daggers and swords, which are so short and narrow that they appear unlikely to have been designed for the large-handed races of Europe. Additionally, some swords feature handles in the shape of a cross and tracery believed to be an imitation of the Svastika, an ancient Aryan symbol likely created with a consecutive meaning and definite intention.

Beyond weaponry, the barrows of this period contain luxury items, including:
* Coronets and diadems made of solid gold.
* Amulets.
* Vases of elegant ornamentation and form, crafted from bronze and gold.

The ornamentation of the era is generally limited to concentric circles, bosses, and spirals. Pottery from this time was hand-made, consisting largely of cinerary urns ranging from 12 to 18 inches in height. These pots are typically decorated with a projecting flange or a band around the upper portion.

## Funerary Customs
The Bronze Age is characterized by the practice of cremation as the primary mode of disposing of the dead, a shift from the Stone Age where burial was the rule. This change in custom led to a modification in the structure of sepulchral mounds. Because the burial chamber was no longer viewed as a habitation for the deceased but rather a simple cist to hold an urn of ashes, the chambers and the mounds covering them became smaller and less imposing than those of the Stone Age.

Barrows from the Bronze Age often served as family or tribal cemeteries. In Denmark, a single mound has been found to contain as many as seventy deposits of burnt bones, indicating the site was used over a long succession of years. While these mounds are smaller than their predecessors, they are often surprisingly rich in the art workmanship and relics of the time.
