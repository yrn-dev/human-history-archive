# Hellenists and the Hellenistic Period

The Hellenistic period was characterized by the expansion of Hellenic culture and the establishment of powerful monarchies in the East following the campaigns of Alexander the Great. This era saw a significant transformation in governance, the spread of Greek intellectual life, and the development of a distinct linguistic tradition.

## Hellenistic Monarchies and Governance
The national monarchy of the Macedonians underwent an essential transformation when Alexander adopted the guise of an Oriental great king. This shift resulted in an absolute despotism, lacking the checks previously provided by a hardy peasantry and a proud territorial nobility. In these new Eastern monarchies, the principle enunciated by Seleucus—that the king's command was the supreme law—became the literal governing principle.

Despite this move toward absolutism, certain rights of the Macedonian army persisted. The army continued to function in capital trials of Macedonians and played a role in the transference of royal authority. This was symbolized by the popular acclamation required upon the accession of a new king. In Alexandria, "the people" occasionally made their will effective in filling the throne, such as when they put Euergetes II on the throne following the capture of Philometor, or compelled Cleopatra III to choose Soter II as her associate. Similarly, in Syria, the usurper Tryphon claimed his right based on election by "the army" or "the people." When supreme authority was delegated, such as when Seleucus I appointed his son Antiochus as king of the eastern provinces, the army was convoked to ratify the appointment.

## Culture and Intellectual Life in the Courts
Hellenistic courts viewed the presence of Hellenic culture as a primary source of prestige, frequently competing to attract distinguished men of letters and philosophers. 

*   **The Ptolemaïc Court:** This court was exceptionally prominent in scientific and literary history due to its attached museum. The first Ptolemy contributed to historical literature via his account of Alexander's campaigns, the fourth Ptolemy published tragedies and instituted a cult of Homer, and Ptolemy Euergetes II issued a book of memoirs.
*   **The Pergamene Court:** This court exhibited literary and artistic zeal comparable to the Ptolemaïc court. It is credited with the preservation of much of the masterpieces of Attic prose, and kings Attalus III and Eumenes I were authors.
*   **The Seleucid Court:** While not as brilliant as the others, it still hosted figures of distinction. Under Antiochus I, Berossus composed a Babylonian history in Greek and Aratus performed a recension of the *Odyssey*. Antiochus III appointed Euphorion as the keeper of the library at Antioch. Antiochus IV, described as an enthusiastic Hellenist, welcomed Athenian philosophers and filled Antioch with Greek artists.
*   **The Antigonid Court:** Antigonus Gonatas, despite his soldier-spirit, welcomed Stoic philosophers; while Zeno did not come to Macedonia, his disciple Persaeus of Citium entered his service. The poet Aratus also illustrated this court.

## Language and Literary Tradition
The Greek language evolved through the Hellenistic period, leading to a long-standing tension between different forms of the tongue. The "purified" language (*he kathareuousa*) is based on the Greek of Hellenistic writers, modified over successive ages. In contrast, the vernacular (*he kathomiloumene*) is the oral language spoken by the Greek world.

During the period of decadence, a *koine dialektos* existed as a midway point between popular speech and the classical language. By the 19th century, a common literary dialect derived from this Hellenistic tradition remained in use. Reformers like Coraes used this as a basis for linguistic reforms, purging foreign elements and enlarging the vocabulary using the ancient lexicon.

## Hellenists in Religious and Social Contexts
The term "Hellenist" also appears in the context of early Christianity. Christian Hellenists generally occupied a mediating role, positioned between Pauline Christianity and Jewish Christianity. An example is Barnabas, who is spoken of with respect in the Clementines, whereas Paul is discountenanced for being a radical regarding the Law.

Additionally, the term applies to "Hellenistic Jews." In the 2nd century A.D., this group favored the translation of the Old Testament by Aquila over the older Septuagint. Aquila's version was characterized by extreme literalness and a slavish adherence to the original Hebrew text, reflecting the exegesis of the Jewish rabbis of that era.

## Athens in the Hellenistic Age
During this period, Athens was overshadowed in material strength by the great Hellenistic monarchies and republican leagues. However, the prestige of her past history reached its zenith. While the city suffered periods of Macedonian control—including the virtual autocracy of Demetrius of Phalerum and the imposition of garrisons—her democracy remained respected by Macedonian kings. The rulers of Syria, Egypt, and Pergamum courted Athenian favor through donations of works of art and edifices.
