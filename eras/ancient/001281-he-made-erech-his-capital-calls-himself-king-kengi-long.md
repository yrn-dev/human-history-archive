# Lugal-zaggisi and the Early Empires of Kengi

## The Rise of Lugal-zaggisi
Lugal-zaggisi was the high-priest of Gis-ukh and is recognized as the founder of the first known empire in Asia. He rose to power by overthrowing Uru-duggina, the eighth successor of Ur-Nin, and capturing his city. Following these conquests, Lugal-zaggisi established his capital at Erech and adopted the title of king of Kengi.

To commemorate his achievements, Lugal-zaggisi commissioned a long inscription to be engraved on hundreds of stone vases. These vases were dedicated to El-lil of Nippur. In this text, he declares that his kingdom extended from the "Upper Sea" (the Mediterranean) to the "Lower Sea of the Tigris and Euphrates," which is identified as the Persian Gulf. During this period, the city of Erech became known ideographically as "the City."

## Geography and Terminology of Southern Babylonia
The region of southern Babylonia was known by several names. A comprehensive term for the area was Kengi ("the land") or Kengi Sumer ("the land of Sumer"), though "Sumer" alone was used in later periods. This region was contrasted with northern Babylonia, known as Akkad or Urra. While *kengi* referred to the "lowlands," *Urra* (later pronounced *Uri*) likely meant "clayey soil" or "the upper country."

The alluvial plain of Babylonia was called Edin, a name specifically restricted to the plain on the western bank of the river used by Bedouins for pasturing flocks. This bank, or *kisad*, and the corresponding western bank of the Tigris gave the name to the land of Chesed. In early Lagash inscriptions, the district was referred to as Gu-Edinna, and the coast-land was known as Gu-bba ("bank of the sea").

## Early City-States and Political Dynamics
Before the consolidation of larger empires, Sumerian cities generally functioned as independent states, each possessing its own priests and a specific god. However, certain cities occasionally established ascendancy over others to exact tribute. 

In Lagash (Tello), records mention a "king of Kengi" and Me-silim, the king of Kis, who had dealings with the high-priest of Lagash, Lugal-suggur, and the high-priest of the neighboring town of Gis-ukh. Later, the high-priests of Lagash assumed kingship, leading to a dynasty founded by Ur-Nin. Archaeological finds from this era include onyx plates and lion heads—described as "booty" dedicated to the goddess Bau—and terra-cotta bas-reliefs of the king and his sons.

Further expansion occurred under E-anna-du, the grandson of Ur-Nin, who gained mastery over southern Babylonia, including the district of Sumer and the cities of Larsa, Ur, and Erech. He also annexed the kingdom of Kis, though it regained independence after his death. During this time, Gis-ukh was made a tributary, with grain levies collected from each person and paid into the treasuries of the god Ingurisa and the goddess Nin. These victories were commemorated on the "Stele of the Vultures," which depicts the king in a chariot with a curved metal weapon, followed by helmeted soldiers with lances, as well as scenes of vultures feeding on fallen enemies.

## Successive Empires and Religious Shifts
The era of early priest-rulers and independent city-states eventually gave way to larger imperial structures. Following the early empire of Erech, a Semitic leader named Sargon (c. 2750 B.C.) united nomadic Semitic-speaking tribes and conquered the Sumerians. Sargon extended his rule from the Mediterranean in the west to beyond the Persian Gulf in the east, establishing the Sumerian Akkadian Empire, which lasted over two hundred years.

This political evolution mirrored a shift in religion. In the pre-Khammurabic age, religious centers such as Ur, Erech, Nippur, Sippara, Agade, Eridu, Kutha, and Shirgulla (Lagash) each had a chief deity and a female consort. The jurisdiction of these gods was limited to the political extent of the city's control. This changed around 2250 B.C. when Khammurabi united the Euphratean states. Khammurabi implemented a policy to place Marduk, the patron deity of the new capital, Babylon, at the head of the entire Babylonian pantheon.
