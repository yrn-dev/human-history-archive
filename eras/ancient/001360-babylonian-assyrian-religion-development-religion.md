# Babylonian and Assyrian Religion

The religion of Babylonia and Assyria developed in close alignment with the political history of the Euphrates valley. Its evolution is characterized by a transition from localized cults to a centralized pantheon, the integration of a complex astral theology, and a lasting influence on subsequent Semitic and ancient Mediterranean cultures.

## Historical Periods of Development
The development of Babylonian-Assyrian religion is divided into four primary periods:
1. **The Oldest Period (c. 3500 B.C. to c. 2250 B.C.):** This era precedes the time of Khammurabi.
2. **The Post-Khammurabic Period:** Following the union of the Euphratean states around 2250 B.C.
3. **The Assyrian Period (c. 2000 B.C. to 606 B.C.):** This period concludes with the destruction of Nineveh.
4. **The Neo-Babylonian Period (625 B.C. to 331 B.C.):** Beginning with Nabopolassar (625–604 B.C.), this era saw a brief return to power and prosperity ending with Cyrus's conquest in 539 B.C. However, the religion continued undisturbed for several centuries until the Greek conquest of the Euphrates valley in 331 B.C., after which new influences led to the extinction of the old cults.

## Evolution of the Pantheon
In the pre-Khammurabic age, religion was centered around various religious hubs, including Eridu, Agade, Ur, Nippur, Erech, Sippara, Kutha, and Shirgulla (Lagash). Each center had a chief deity associated with a female consort, and the jurisdiction of these gods was limited to the political extent of their respective districts. While early attempts were made in Ur and Nippur to group these deities into a regular pantheon, these efforts were not enduring.

The ascension of Khammurabi marked a new epoch. He pursued a policy of raising Marduk, the patron deity of Babylon, to the head of the entire Babylonian pantheon as Babylon became the center of the Euphrates valley. In the post-Khammurabic period, the pantheon shifted to reflect the secular court; just as rulers were surrounded by loyal attendants, Marduk was surrounded by the gods of minor local cults and chief centers. This structure persisted despite a progress toward a monotheistic conception of divine government.

In the north, the Assyrians adopted a similar structure but transferred the role of the head of the pantheon from Marduk to Assur, the patron god of the city of Assur. Despite this, Assyrian rulers continued to recognize Marduk due to the religious predominance of Babylon. The ceremony of "taking the hand of Bel-Marduk" was considered an essential preliminary for any ruler, Babylonian or Assyrian, to exercise authority in the Euphrates valley.

## Astral Theology and Ritual Practice
A defining feature of the religion was the astral theology, a system devised by priests that unified diverse religious elements by interpreting them through the heavens. An example of this is the modification of the early view that the universe was controlled by three gods: Anu, Bel, and Ea. Under astral theory, these gods were disassociated from local connections and assigned to the "ways" (zones) of the ecliptic circle:
* **Anu:** Presided over the heavens and the northern zone.
* **Bel:** Assigned to the earth, the atmosphere immediately above it, and the middle zone.
* **Ea:** Ruled over the deep and the southern zone.

While theology was subject to these theoretical changes, the ritual practices remained constant and were connected to popular elements of the religion. These practices, which were free from the influence of astral theology, included animal sacrifice, libations, ritualistic purification, the sprinkling of water, and symbolical rites accompanied by short prayers. These rituals were central to the celebration of festival days and the worship of deities. Divination practices and incantation formulae also relied on these rituals.

## Cultural Foundations and Influence
The early civilization of Babylonia is described as Sumerian. The Sumerians developed the pictorial hieroglyphs that evolved into cuneiform and established the foundation of the region's law and religion. Sumerian remained the language of law and religion long after Semites became the ruling race.

The influence of Babylonian-Assyrian religion was profound, particularly among the Semites. The civilization of the Euphrates valley provided the impetus for the purification of the old Semite religion clung to by the Hebrews and nomadic Arabs. Traces of direct borrowing from Babylonia are found in the traditions, myths, and legends of the Old Testament, with indirect influences appearing in the Psalms, prophetical books, and "Wisdom Literature." These influences extended into the New Testament period, appearing in early Christian gnosticism and the development of apocalyptic literature, the latter of which scholars attribute to the astral theology of Babylonian and Assyrian priests. Additionally, the astral theology affected the broader ancient world, including the Romans and Greeks.
