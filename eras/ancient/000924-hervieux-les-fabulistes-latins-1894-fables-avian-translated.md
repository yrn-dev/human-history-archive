# Avianus and the Latin Fable Tradition

Avianus was a Latin writer of fables who is believed by some critics to have lived during the age of the Antonines, while others place his activity as late as the 6th century A.D. He appears to have been a heathen who lived in Rome.

## The Works of Avianus
Avianus is credited with 42 fables. These works are dedicated to an individual named Theodosius, whose learning is described in highly flattering terms. There is scholarly debate regarding the identity of this dedicatee; some suggest he may have been the emperor of that name, while others propose he was Macrobius Theodosius, the author of the *Saturnalia*.

Regarding the origins of these fables, nearly all are found in the works of Babrius, who likely served as Avianus's source of inspiration. However, because Babrius wrote in Greek, Avianus did not translate from the original; instead, he created an elegiac version based on a rough Latin copy, which was likely a prose paraphrase. While Avianus's language and metre are generally correct, there are deviations from classical usage, particularly in the management of the pentameter.

## Influence and Legacy
The fables of Avianus gained popularity as school-books. This popularity led to the frequent creation of paraphrases, imitations, and the addition of *promythia* (introductions) and *epimythia* (morals). One such imitation is the *Novus Avianus*, produced by Alexander Neckam in the 12th century.

The works have been preserved through various editions, including those by Cannegieter (1731), Lachmann (1845), Froehner (1862), Ellis (1887), and Bahrens in *Poetae Latini Minores*. Additionally, William Caxton produced an English translation of the fables at Westmynstre in 1483.

## Related Latin and Oriental Fable Traditions
The broader study of Latin fabulists, as explored in Leopold Hervieux's *Les Fabulistes latins* (1894), intersects with other traditions of apologues. For instance, the "Fables of Bidpai" (or Pilpay) originated from the Hindu *Pancha Tantra* (Five Sections) and the *Hitopadesa*. These stories traveled through several languages: from Sanskrit to Pahlavi in the 6th century A.D. via a physician at the court of Chosroes I Anushirvan, then into Arabic via Abdallah-ibn-Mokaffa as the "Book of Kalilah and Dimna."

These Oriental fables eventually entered the Latin tradition. A Hebrew version by Rabbi Joel was translated into Latin in the 13th century by John of Capua in his *Directorium vitae humanae*. Other Latin versions include a translation from the Greek *Sapientia Indorum* inserted by Pere Poussin.

## Other Fabulists and Collections
Other writers contributed to the fable tradition in the Latin and French spheres. Marie de France (fl. c. 1175-1190) wrote a collection called the *Ysopet* for a Count William. Marie erroneously attributed the English original of her work to Alfred the Great, claiming he had translated it from Latin. Her collection is diverse, containing popular apologues from the Renard cycle, stories derived from Jewish sources, and fables from Phaedrus. Unlike older fables intended for instruction, those in the Renard cycle were intended to amuse.

Additionally, the 4th-century Greek grammarian Dositheus Magister, who flourished at Rome, included fables of Aesop in an appendix to his Greek translation of a Latin grammar.
