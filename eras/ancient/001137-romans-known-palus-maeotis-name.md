# Sea of Azov (Palus Maeotis)

The Sea of Azov is an inland sea located in southern Europe. To the Romans, this body of water was known as the *Palus Maeotis*, a name derived from the neighboring people who referred to it in their own language as *Temarenda*, meaning "Mother of Waters."

## Geography and Physical Characteristics
The Sea of Azov communicates with the Black Sea through the Strait of Yenikale, also known as Kerch or the ancient *Bosporus Cimmerius*. It is situated between 35° and 39° E. longitude and 45° 20' and 47° 18' N. latitude. The sea measures 230 miles from south-west to north-east, with a maximum breadth of 110 miles and a total area of 14,515 square miles.

The sea is characterized by its relatively fresh water, though this varies by current and location. Its surface sits 4-3/4 feet higher than that of the Black Sea, and its level has been dropping in recent years. The depth is shallow, ranging from 3 to 10 feet near the mouth of the Don—its only very important affluent—and reaching a maximum depth of no more than 45 feet. 

The region experiences specific climatic patterns: it generally freezes from November until mid-April, and fierce, continuous easterly winds prevail during July and August. North-easterly and south-easterly winds are also common later in the year, creating a variety of currents. To the west, the main basin is separated by the long, narrow sand-spit of Arabat from the Sivash, or "Putrid Sea," which consists of marshes and lagoons with intensely salt water.

## Historical and Regional Significance
In prehistoric times, a connection existed between the Sea of Azov and the Caspian Sea, and it was long believed to have direct communication with the Northern Ocean. However, since the earliest historical times, the character and relations of the sea have remained largely unchanged.

The sea is of significant importance to Russian commerce, with the cities of Mariupol, Berdyansk, Taganrog, and Yenikale located along its shores. The Turks refer to the sea as *Baluk-deniz*, or "Fish Sea," due to the abundance of fish.

## Ancient Peoples and Migrations
The *Palus Maeotis* served as a geographical marker for several ancient nations and migrations:

*   **Scythians:** The original Scythians described by Herodotus were confined within a square of 4,000 stadia (400 Roman miles) by the *Palus Maeotis* and the Danube. Later, Scythians leaving the banks of the *Palus Maeotis* made an inroad into Media, defeating King Cyaxares and holding possession of Upper Asia for twenty-eight years, eventually pushing as far as the frontiers of Egypt.
*   **Bulgarians:** According to Greek writers, the Bulgarians touched the *Palus Maeotis* and the Euxine. After the dismemberment of the Hunnish monarchy following the death of Attila, the Bulgarians' empire included the remains of the Huns established on the *Palus Maeotis*.
*   **Other Movements:** Procopius observed an emigration from the *Palus Maeotis* to the north of Germany, which he attributed to famine, though other sources have questioned his historical accuracy.

## Early Cartography and Knowledge
The *Palus Maeotis* was part of the expanding geographical knowledge of the ancient Greeks. While earlier poets and philosophers had limited knowledge of sea boundaries, by a century after the voyage of Kôlæus, figures such as Hippônax and Anakreon had surveyed the *Palus Maeotis*, the Euxine, the Adriatic, the western Mediterranean, and the Libyan Syrtes. This allowed Anaximander to represent these bodies of water on a map.
