# Menander and Nagasena

The interactions between the Greek king Menander and the Buddhist teacher Nagasena are preserved in a significant work of early Buddhist literature known as the *Milinda*. This text is described as one of the earliest historical novels and is primarily religious and philosophical in nature.

## The Milinda
The *Milinda* purports to record a series of discussions that extended over several days. In these dialogues, Nagasena, a Buddhist elder, engages with Milinda—the name used in the text for Menander, the famous Greek king of Bactria. The narrative describes how Nagasena succeeds in converting King Menander to Buddhism. The work has been translated into English, and its Pāli text has been edited.

## Philosophical Focus on the Soul
A central theme of the conversations between Menander and Nagasena is the question of the "soul." The unknown author of the work addresses this topic first, detailing the process by which Nagasena convinces the king that the "soul," in the ordinary sense, does not exist. This subject is a recurring point of emphasis throughout the text, as the author returns to it repeatedly.

The focus on the soul in the *Milinda* mirrors a broader pattern in Buddhist expositions. For instance, the *Dialogues of Gotama* contains a first discourse that categorically and systematically rejects current theories regarding souls. Similarly, the *Kathāvatthu*, the latest book in the canon, places the question of the soul at the head of its discussions, devoting an amount of space to the topic that overshadows all other points.

## Historical Context
The *Milinda* belongs to a period of voluminous Buddhist literature that followed the era of the Buddha and Asoka. This literature developed across two subsequent periods: from Asoka to Kanishka, and from Kanishka to Buddhaghosa, each spanning approximately three centuries.

The presence of a Greek king in these Buddhist dialogues reflects the historical environment of the time. Following the adventures of Alexander, the Punjab remained under Seleucid rule, and India was home to many Greek adventurers. This era was characterized by open communication by land and sea with Alexandria, which also influenced early Buddhist art, giving it a strongly Greek character.
