# Nero Adds Four Cities To Agrippas Kingdom; But The Other

## Administrative Changes Under Nero
During the reign of Nero, several significant territorial and administrative adjustments were made regarding the governance of the region. Nero bestowed the kingdom of Lesser Armenia upon Aristobulus, who was the son of Herod. In addition to this, Nero expanded the kingdom of Agrippa by adding four cities and their corresponding toparchies: Abila, Tiberias of Galilee, Tarichea, and Julias, which is located in Perea. Specifically, the city of Julias was granted along with fourteen surrounding villages.

While Agrippa's jurisdiction was expanded, the remaining parts of Judea were placed under the authority of Felix, who was appointed as procurator. Felix was the brother of Pallas and held procuratorship over Perea, Samaria, and Galilee. During his tenure, Felix captured Eleazar, described as an arch-robber, and many of his associates who had ravaged the country for twenty years. While Eleazar and some others were sent to Rome, Felix crucified a multitude of robbers that cannot be enumerated.

## The Reign and Character of Nero
Historical accounts describe Nero as acting like a madman, driven by an extravagant degree of riches and felicity. He used his good fortune to injure others and exhibited extreme barbarity toward his own family, slaying his mother, his wife, and his brother. His instability eventually led him to become an actor in theatrical scenes.

Nero's ascent to the empire occurred after the death of Claudius, who had ruled for thirteen years, eight months, and twenty days. Claudius had adopted Nero through the delusions of his wife, Agrippina, despite having a son of his own named Britannicus (by his former wife Messalina) and daughters named Octavia and Antonia.

## King Agrippa’s Public Works and Governance
King Agrippa engaged in several large-scale building projects. He expanded Cæsarea Philippi and renamed it Neronlas in honor of Nero. In Berytus, he constructed a theater at vast expense, providing annual shows and distributing oil and corn to the people. He further adorned Berytus with statues and original images, some of which were transferred from his own kingdom. These actions caused him to be hated by his subjects, who resented the removal of their own ornaments to decorate a foreign city.

In Jerusalem, Agrippa built a large dining room in the royal palace near the portico. The palace, originally erected by the children of Asamoneus, sat on an elevation that allowed Agrippa to observe activities in the temple while eating. This caused significant displeasure among the chief men of Jerusalem, as viewing the temple—particularly the sacrifices—was contrary to the law and institutions of the country.

## Conflict Over the Temple Wall
To block the view from Agrippa's dining room and the western cloisters where Romans kept guards during festivals, the people of Jerusalem erected a wall on the uppermost building of the temple's inner court toward the west. This action displeased both King Agrippa and the procurator Festus, the latter of whom ordered the wall to be demolished.

The people petitioned Festus to allow an embassy to Nero, stating they could not endure living if any part of the temple were demolished. Nero received a delegation of ten principal men, including Helcias (the keeper of the sacred treasure) and Ismael (the high priest). Nero not only forgave the people but permitted the wall to remain. This decision was made to gratify Nero's wife, Poppea, who was a religious woman. While the ten ambassadors were allowed to return home, Nero retained Ismael and Helcias as hostages. Following this event, Agrippa appointed Joseph, known as Cabi (son of the former high priest Simon), to the high priesthood.

## Social and Political Instability
The period was marked by internal strife and disorder. A sedition arose between high priests after the king took the high priesthood from Jesus, son of Damneus, and gave it to Jesus, son of Gamaliel. These factions gathered bold groups of people, leading to frequent reproaches and stone-throwing. Ananias used his riches to gain followers, while Costobarus and Saulus—members of the royal family and kindred to Agrippa—gathered multitudes of "wicked wretches" to plunder the weak.

Later, following a calamity involving Cestius, several eminent figures fled Jerusalem to join Cestius, including Philip (the commander of Agrippa's forces) and the brothers Costobarus and Saul. Cestius subsequently sent Saul and his associates to Nero in Achaia to report their distress and to blame Florus for inciting the war.
