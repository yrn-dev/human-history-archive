# Italians

## Early Inhabitants and Regional Distribution
The Italian peninsula was historically inhabited by a diverse array of nations and ethnic groups. On the rocky coast that currently forms the republic of Genoa lived the Ligurians. To the east of the Adige were the Venetians, who were likely of Liburnian or Illyrian origin, though they were frequently confused with the Gauls. 

The middle portion of the peninsula, which later became the ecclesiastical state and the duchy of Tuscany, served as the ancient seat of the Umbrians and the Etruscans; the latter are credited with providing the first rudiments of civilized life to Italy. In the region stretching from the seven hills of Rome and the Tyber river to the frontiers of Naples, the land was occupied by the Latins, the Sabines, and the Volsci. 

Further south, the immediate territory of Naples was held by Campania and Capua. The remainder of the kingdom was inhabited by several warlike nations, including the Lucanians, the Apulians, the Samnites, and the Marsi. Additionally, the sea-coasts were covered by flourishing colonies of the Greeks. During the reign of Augustus, Italy was divided into eleven regions, a process that included the annexation of the province of Istria to the seat of Roman sovereignty.

## Roman Influence and Expansion
The Romans established their presence on the seven hills of Rome, and the surrounding lands of the Volsci, Latins, and Sabines were the sites of their early victories. Over time, the first consuls achieved triumphs on this ground, followed by their successors who built villas and their posterity who erected convents. 

Roman influence extended beyond the peninsula. For example, in 46 B.C., Julius Caesar refounded the city of Corinth as a Roman colony, settling it with Italian colonists and designating it as the residence of the Roman governor. This city became a major commercial capital and a vital trade route between Rome and the East. In other regions, such as Asia Minor (modern Anatolia), Italian strains flowed into the blood of the population from the Pergamus times, though these ancestral elements were eventually forgotten as the population blended with other Mediterranean, Nordic Aryan, Semite, and Mongolian strains.

## Religion and Deities
In the Roman state, Mars (also known as Marmar, Mavors, or Maspiter) was the most important deity after Jupiter. Mars maintained an essentially Roman and Italian character, remaining largely unaffected by foreign influences. Evidence of his worship is found throughout Latium, Picenum, Samnium, Umbria, and within certain Etruscan cities. While his character was interpreted variously at Rome—as a god of vegetation, the year, wind, storm, or a sun-god—his primary function in historical times was the protection of the state in war. Consequently, his name became a synonym for *bellum* (war). In some communities, Mars gave his name to a month, as he did for the first month of the old Roman year in Rome.

## Religious Transitions and Conflict
Later history in Italy was marked by religious distinctions between the native population and foreign conquerors. Italy was devoutly attached to the Nicene faith, while the Gothic conqueror, Theodoric, followed Arianism, the heresy of his fathers. Despite this difference, Theodoric maintained a level of religious tolerance. He acted as the guardian of public worship and showed external reverence for the faith he despised, including presenting an offering at the tomb of St. Peter. 

Under Theodoric's reign, the Catholic clergy were honorably entertained in his palace based on their merit or rank, and he esteemed orthodox bishops such as Epiphanius of Pavia and Caesarius of Arles. The Roman pontiff, who was then referred to by the name of Pope, held significant dignity and importance, and the peace of Italy often depended on the relationship with this office. During this period, the privileges of sanctuary and the jurisdiction of metropolitans were maintained according to the spirit of Roman jurisprudence.
