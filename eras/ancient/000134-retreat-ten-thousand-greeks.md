# RETREAT OF THE TEN THOUSAND GREEKS

The Retreat of the Ten Thousand refers to the journey of a large body of Greek mercenaries who, after marching into the heart of the Persian Empire, were forced to fight their way back to the coast. The event took place between approximately April 401 B.C. and June 399 B.C. and was immortalized in the *Anabasis*, one of the first personal war stories written by a general in command, Xenophon.

## Origins and the Expedition
The expedition began when a second Cyrus, the brother of Artaxerxes II, collected an army of ten thousand Greek mercenaries to challenge the throne of the Persian Empire. This occurred during a period of decay for the government of the Medes and Persians, characterized by palace crime and rebellions in Syria and Egypt. The Greeks marched into Babylonia, eventually engaging in the battle of Kunaxa.

Although the Greek troops initially felt triumphant at Kunaxa, their victory was short-lived. Cyrus was killed at the moment of victory over Artaxerxes II. The death of their employer transformed the Greeks' triumph into dismay and sorrow, as they realized they had been seduced into a venture that left them stranded.

## Immediate Aftermath of Kunaxa
Following the battle, the Greeks found themselves in a precarious position. Under the leadership of Klearchus, they initially maintained the tone of victors. Klearchus sent messengers, Proklês and Glus, accompanied by Menon, to Ariæus. He claimed the Greeks were conquerors without an enemy and suggested that if Ariæus joined them, they would place him on the throne intended for Cyrus.

During this time, the army suffered from a lack of provisions. With no bread available, the soldiers killed baggage animals for food and burned wooden Egyptian shields, baggage carts, and arrows to create fires for cooking.

## Hardships and the Retreat
The situation deteriorated rapidly as the Greeks faced a hostile environment. They found themselves ten thousand stadia from home, surrounded by enemies and blocked by rivers and impassable mountains. They lacked guides, provisions, cavalry, and, eventually, generals to provide orders. This led to a state of agonizing apprehension and helplessness among the troops.

As they retreated through the cold mountains of Armenia, the army faced extreme weather. The north wind, Boreas, blew in their faces, causing them to freeze and parch intolerably. According to the accounts, a prophet recommended a sacrifice to Boreas, after which the painful effects of the wind appeared to cease in a marked manner.

During their journey along the southern coast of the Euxine, the Greeks encountered a Paphlagonian prince named Korylas. Additionally, the retreat was opposed by the Carduchi in the mountains.

## Historical Significance
While the retreat is an item that fits more naturally into Persian history than the main stream of Grecian affairs, it had numerous and important effects on the Greek mind. It served as a preeminent illustration of Hellenic character and competence when measured against contemporary Asiatics.

The adventures of the Ten Thousand were later cited by Alexander the Great in a speech to encourage his own soldiers before the battle of Issus. The event also highlighted the military feebleness and disorganization of the Persian empire, which Xenophon suggested was not defensible without the aid of Greeks.
