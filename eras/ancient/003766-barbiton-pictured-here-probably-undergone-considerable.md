# Barbiton

The barbiton (also referred to as the barbitos) is an ancient stringed musical instrument derived from Persia. Known through the classics of Greece and Rome, it is characterized by its deep sounds and its relationship to the broader family of vaulted-back instruments of Eastern origin.

## Origins and Ancient Characteristics
The barbiton is traced back to Persia, where the term *barbat* or *barbud* was used. In the Persian tradition, the *barbut* was a variety of the *rebab*, serving as a bass instrument that differed from the *rebab* primarily in its size and number of strings. The name *barbud* is said to be derived from a renowned musician who lived during the time of Chosroes II (A.D. 590-628).

In ancient Greece, the instrument was introduced by Terpander. It was described by the Sicilian poet Theocritus as having many strings—specifically more than seven, which the Hellenes considered the perfect number for instruments like the cithara. Pollux, in the *Onomasticon*, refers to the instrument as the barbiton or barymite; the latter term is derived from the Greek words *barus* (heavy) and *mitos* (a string). This designation reflects the instrument's capacity for producing deep sounds, as its strings were twice as long as those of the pectis and sounded an octave lower. Pindar noted that one could "magadize," or play in two parts at an interval of an octave, using two such instruments.

Despite its use in Greece, Sicily, Italy, and Asia Minor, the barbiton was not widely embraced by the Greeks of Hellas. It was regarded as a barbarian instrument, favored only by those with unorthodox artistic tastes. While it fell into disuse during the era of Aristotle, it later reappeared under the Romans.

## Evolution and Technical Development
The barbiton underwent considerable modification by the Greeks, diverging from its original archetype. However, it maintained the core characteristics of the Greek lyre—specifically that its strings were plucked—which distinguished it from the rebab, which was played with a bow upon its introduction to Europe.

The instrument is part of a larger family of pear-shaped instruments with vaulted backs, a design traced to Persia, ancient India, and influenced civilizations. This family includes the pandura, tanbur (or mandoline) as treble, the lute as alto or tenor, the barbiton (or theorbo) as bass, and the chitarrone as double bass. Over time, the barbiton approximated the form of the large lute.

## Later History and the Theorbo
An instrument called the barbiton continued to be known during the early 16th and throughout the 17th century. During this period, it was a type of bass-lute or theorbo featuring a single neck bent back at right angles to form the head. Robert Fludd provided a detailed description and illustration of this version, noting that while the general public called it a "theorbo," scholars identified it with the instrument of classic Rome and Greece and thus called it the barbiton.

This later version of the barbiton featured nine pairs of gut strings, with each pair tuned in unison. The use of the name "barbiton" for this instrument was supported by 18th-century dictionaries. G. B. Doni defined it as *Barbitos seu major chelys italice tiorba*, linking it to the lyre, cithara, and other tortoiseshell instruments (*testudines*). Claude Perrault, writing in the 18th century, observed that moderns referred to the lute as the barbiton. Additionally, Constantijn Huygens recorded that he was able to learn to play the barbiton in a few weeks, contrasting this with the two years required to learn the cittern.
