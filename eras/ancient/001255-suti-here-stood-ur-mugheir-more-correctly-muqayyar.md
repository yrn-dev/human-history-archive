# Suti

The Suti were a group of Semitic nomads who inhabited the regions bordering the civilization of Babylonia. Their presence and movements are noted in the historical records of the Near East, particularly in relation to the expansion of Babylonian influence and the shifting demographics of Palestine and Syria.

## Geography and Interaction with Babylonia
The Suti occupied territory to the west of the Euphrates river, where the civilization of Babylonia encroached upon their lands. This region was adjacent to the rich alluvial plain of Chaldaea, a fertile area formed by the deposits of two great rivers. To the east of this plain rose the mountains of Elam, and to the south lay sea-marshes inhabited by other Aramaic tribes and the Chaldaeans (or Kald).

Within this broader geographic context stood Ur, also known as Muqayyar or Mugheir, which served as the earliest capital of the country. Other significant locations in the vicinity included Babylon and its suburb, Borsippa (Birs Nimrud), as well as the two Sipparas (referred to in Scripture as the Sepharvaim), which were situated on both the Chaldaean and Arabian sides of the river.

## Role in the Migration to Palestine and Syria
The Suti, along with the Khabiri, are identified as the forerunners of the Aramaean immigration. During the period of the eighteenth dynasty, when Egypt exercised suzerainty over Palestine, Damascus (known in the Tell el-Amarna correspondence as Dimashka) served as the capital of the small province of Ubi. 

Towards the end of this Egyptian period, the overrunning of Syria and Palestine by the Suti and Khabiri led to significant changes in the government, language, and general conditions of the region. A primary indicator of this transition was the shift in the name of Damascus; in an inscription of Rameses III, the city appears as the Aramaean "Darmesek."

## The Kingdom of Damascus
Following the occupation by the Aramaeans, an independent kingdom emerged with Damascus as its center. This kingdom grew in strength to the extent that it functioned as a "buffer" between the rising kingdom of Saul and the king of Assyria. Even after Tiglath-Pileser I reduced the entirety of northern Syria—victories that prompted the king of Egypt to send him presents—Tiglath-Pileser I did not venture to attack Damascus or Kadesh.
