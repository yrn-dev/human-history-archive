# IONIC PHILOSOPHERS, PYTHAGORAS, KROTON AND SYBARIS

## The Ionic Philosophers
The early development of Grecian philosophy was marked by a series of thinkers rather than a formal school or succession. This philosophical movement began with Thalês, who identified water, or the fluid, as the primeval element. Following him, Anaximander addressed the problem of the Permanent and the Variable, as well as the One and the Many. In contrast to Anaximander's doctrines was Xenophanês, from whom the Eleatic school—represented by Zeno and Parmenidês—sprang. The Eleatics exerted a significant influence on Grecian speculation through their use of dialectics. Other figures of this era included Pherekydês. A recurring vein of skepticism in Grecian philosophy is attributed to the fact that these early thinkers faced vast problems while possessing scanty means of solution.

## Pythagoras: Life and Influence
Pythagoras, an Ionian, moved from Samos to Kroton. He is described as an original and ascendent mind, functioning more as a schoolmaster and missionary than a politician. Upon his arrival in Kroton, biographers claim his preaching had an "electric" effect, leading to extensive private and public reform. These accounts suggest that luxury was discredited, incontinence disappeared, and two thousand people were converted during his first preaching. His influence reportedly extended beyond Kroton to other cities in Sicily and Italy, including Metapontum, Rhêgium, Himera, Katana, and Sybaris.

In Kroton, Pythagoras formed a powerful society or club consisting of three hundred men drawn from the wealthy classes. While later witnesses exaggerated his political efficiency, his indirect political influence stemmed from the constitution of this order. The Supreme Council of One Thousand in Kroton reportedly solicited his advice and offered to make him their president (prytanis), while his daughter and wife were placed at the head of female religious processions. Although he provided ethical training, it is noted that this may not have been applied to every member of his order.

Over time, the Pythagorean order faced subversion accompanied by violence, eventually being reduced to a philosophical and religious sect. Some historians, such as Krische, Boeckh, and O. Müller, have attempted to link Pythagorean philosophy to Dorian or Spartan habits (Grecian Dorism). However, this is contested on the grounds that Pythagoras was Ionian and that neither Kroton nor Elea were Doric cities.

## The City of Kroton
Kroton was an Achæan city in Italy known for its medical skill, specifically the excellence of its surgeons and physicians. It was also distinguished by the number of its citizens who won prizes at the Olympic games. Despite the current insalubrity of its site, the city was proverbially healthy in ancient times. 

Politically, Kroton featured an oligarchical government. Its administration included a senate of one thousand members, though the general assembly (ekklesia) was occasionally utilized. The city's prosperity was aided by a steady increase in its dominion in the interior and the ease of maintaining new populations. The Krotoniates also administered the festivals of the Lakinian Hêrê, which served as a common religious assembly for others.

## Sybaris and the War with Kroton
Sybaris was another powerful Achæan city, which until 510 B.C. appeared to be the greatest in the region, though it was frequently denounced for its luxury. The city experienced a political revolution led by a popular leader named Têlys, who overthrew the oligarchical government, banished five hundred wealthy men, confiscated their properties, and became a despot.

Tensions rose between Sybaris and Kroton, exemplified by the exile of Philippus—a Krotoniate Olympic victor—for agreeing to marry Têlys's daughter. The exiles from Sybaris sought refuge at Kroton as suppliants. When Têlys demanded their surrender under threat of war, the Krotoniates debated the matter. Pythagoras is said to have persuaded the city to risk war rather than betray the suppliants.

The resulting war ended in the defeat of the Sybarites and the destruction of their city. This victory was achieved partly with the assistance of the Spartan prince Dorieus, although statements regarding Dorieus's presence are contradictory. The destruction of Sybaris caused a sensation throughout the Hellenic world.

## Historical Discrepancies
The history of this period is imperfectly preserved. Herodotus, in mentioning the war between Sybaris and Kroton, makes no mention of Pythagoras or his brotherhood, suggesting their role may have been less overruling than Pythagorean historians claim. Furthermore, Polybius provides a perplexing account stating that after the expulsion of the Pythagoreans, Kroton, Sybaris, and Kaulonia established a permanent league. This contradicts the established timeline, as Sybaris should have been in ruins at that time.
