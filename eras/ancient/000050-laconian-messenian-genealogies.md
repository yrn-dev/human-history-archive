# LACONIAN AND MESSENIAN GENEALOGIES

The genealogical traditions of Laconia and Messenia provide the foundational myths and ancestral lineages for the southern region of the Peloponnese. These accounts blend autochthonous origins with divine descent and legendary conflicts.

## Laconian Genealogy
The earliest records of Laconian genealogy identify Lelex as an autochthonous figure. Lelex and a Naiad nymph named Kleochareia were the parents of Eurôtas. Their daughter, Sparta, married Lacedæmôn, who was the son of Zeus and Taygetê (the daughter of Atlas).

Lacedæmôn and Sparta produced a son, Amyklas. Amyklas had two sons: Kynortas and Hyacinthus. Hyacinthus is remembered as a beautiful youth and a favorite of Apollo; he was accidentally killed by Apollo while playing at quoits. This event became the basis for the Hyacinthia festival, which was celebrated by the Lacedæmônians and with particular solemnity by the Amyklæans.

The lineage continued through Kynortas, who was succeeded by his son Periêrês. Periêrês married Gorgophonê, the daughter of Perseus, and fathered several children: Tyndareus, Ikarius, Aphareus, Leukippus, and Hippokoon. Alternative genealogical accounts suggest a different order, positioning Periêrês as the son of Æolus and the father of Kynortas, with Tyndareus, Ikarius, and Hippokoon descending from Œbalus, the son of Kynortas.

## The House of Tyndareus and the Dioskuri
Tyndareus and Lêda are central figures in the Laconian lineage. Their offspring included Castôr, Timandra, Klytæmnêstra, Pollux, and Helen. Castôr and Pollux, known as the Dioskuri, are associated with legends involving the Attic Dekeleia and the figures Idas and Lynkeus. The Dioskuri are credited with great power and functions.

## Messenian Genealogy and Traditions
The Messenian genealogy includes Periêrês, as well as Idas and Marpêssa. Legends describe a competition for the hand of Marpêssa, similar to the competition for Hippodameia. In this account, many unsuccessful suitors were killed by Euênus, and their skulls were displayed on the wall of the temple of Poseidôn.

The relationship between the Dioskuri and Messenia was marked by conflict. The wrath of the Dioskuri against Messenia was historically cited as the primary cause for the eventual subjection of the Messenians by the Spartans. This wrath was said to have been appeased only when Epameinondas reconstituted the city of Messênê.

## The Messenian Wars and the Hero Aristomenês
The historical record of the Messenian wars is characterized by contradictions and a lack of ancient traditions, with many accounts appearing after the foundation of Messênê by Epameinondas in 369 B.C. 

The first Messenian war (B.C. 743-724) was allegedly caused by the slaying of the Spartan king Têleklus by the Messenians at the temple of Artemis Limnatis. During this conflict, the Messenians, led by kings Euphaês and Aristodêmus, concentrated their forces on Mount Ithôme before being completely conquered. This led to the period of Helotism, where the conquered Messenians were treated harshly under Spartan rule.

The second Messenian war (B.C. 648-631) involved a revolt against Sparta. A central figure in these traditions is the hero Aristomenês, though sources conflict regarding his era; Diodôrus and Myrôn place him in the first war, while Rhianus and Pausanias place him in the second. Despite his chivalrous exploits, the Messenians were again conquered, and the Spartans acquired the country west of Taygetus.

## Later Developments and the City of Messênê
In 369 B.C., Epameinondas inflicted a major blow to Sparta by re-establishing the Messenians in the Peloponnese and founding the city of Messênê on Mount Ithomê. This new city incorporated descendants of the old Messenians, various Helots, and other settlers. During the foundation ceremony, the Messenian race invoked their gods and heroes, specifically Aristomenês.
