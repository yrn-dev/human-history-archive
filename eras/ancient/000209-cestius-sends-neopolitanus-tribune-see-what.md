# Cestius and the Mission of Neopolitanus

The mission of Neopolitanus was a diplomatic and reconnaissance effort ordered by Cestius, the president of the province of Syria, to determine the political climate and intentions of the Jews following a period of severe civil unrest caused by the administration of Florus.

## The Conflict with Florus
The tensions leading to the mission began with Florus, who sought to provoke a war with the Jews to conceal his own "enormities" and "illegal practices." Florus feared that if peace continued, the Jews would accuse him before Caesar; consequently, he augmented their calamities to induce a rebellion. This included attempts to seize the treasures of God within the temple by gaining access through Antonia. In response, seditious elements cut down the temple cloisters that joined to Antonia to block his access.

Eventually, Florus reached an agreement with the sanhedrim and high priests to leave a garrison in the city. The Jews requested that the specific band that had fought against them be replaced due to the ill-will the multitude bore toward them. Florus complied, changed the band, and returned to Cesarea. However, Florus later contrived to oblige the Jews to begin a war by falsely accusing them of revolting from the Roman government and claiming they were the authors of previous disturbances.

## The Appointment of Neopolitanus
Upon receiving these accusations, Cestius consulted with his captains. While some advisors suggested marching immediately with the army to either punish a real revolt or stabilize Roman affairs, Cestius opted to send an intimate friend first to provide a faithful account of the state of affairs. He appointed a tribune named Neopolitanus for this task.

Neopolitanus encountered King Agrippa at Jamnia while the king was returning from Alexandria. Neopolitanus informed Agrippa of who had sent him and the specific errands for which he had been dispatched.

## Investigation in Jerusalem
Upon arriving near Jerusalem, Neopolitanus and King Agrippa were met by the high priests, the sanhedrim, and men of power. These leaders lamented the "barbarous treatment" they had received from Florus. While Agrippa felt indignation at these reports, he subtly redirected his anger toward the Jews to dissuade them from avenging themselves and to lower their "high thoughts of themselves."

The general population, coming from sixty furlongs outside Jerusalem, greeted both Agrippa and Neopolitanus. The wives of those slain under Florus led the procession in lamentation. The people complained to Neopolitanus about the miseries they had endured, showing him the plundered houses and the desolate state of the market-place.

Through the mediation of Agrippa, Neopolitanus was persuaded to walk around the city, accompanied by only one servant, as far as Siloam. This was intended to demonstrate that the Jews remained submitted to the Romans and were only displeased with the specific barbarity of Florus. After experiencing the "good temper" of the people, Neopolitanus visited the temple. There, he gathered the multitude, commended their fidelity to the Romans, and exhorted them to maintain the peace. After performing the permitted Divine worship, he returned to Cestius.

## Subsequent Military Actions of Cestius
Despite the reports of the people's temper, Cestius later engaged in extensive military campaigns. He assembled a massive force including the entire twelfth legion, thousands of footmen and horsemen from other legions, and auxiliaries from kings such as Antiochus, Agrippa, and Sohemus. 

Cestius's campaign involved the plundering and burning of Zabulon (the City of Men) and various villages in Galilee. He also took the city of Joppa, where 8,400 people, including families, were slain, and destroyed the toparchy of Narbatene. After dispersing Jewish forces at the tower of Aphek and burning the city of Lydda, Cestius pitched his camp at Gabao, fifty furlongs from Jerusalem.

## The Retreat from Jerusalem
Cestius eventually attempted to take the city but, unaware of the desperation or courage of the besieged, he retired from the city without a specific reason. This retreat proved disastrous; as the Romans withdrew toward the camp at Scopus and then toward Gabao, they were pursued by "robbers" and Jewish forces. The Romans suffered great losses, including the deaths of Longinus the tribune, Priscus (commander of the sixth legion), and Emilius Secundus.

Following this calamity, some eminent Jews, including Saul, Costobarus, and Philip, fled to Cestius. At their own request, Cestius sent Saul and his friends to Nero in Achaia to inform the Emperor of their distress and to place the blame for the war upon Florus, an act intended to alleviate Cestius's own danger by provoking Nero's indignation against Florus.
