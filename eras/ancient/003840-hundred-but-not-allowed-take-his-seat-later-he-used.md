# Historical Records of Governance, Infrastructure, and Diplomacy

The Human History Archive contains diverse records regarding the administration of states, the construction of public works, and the diplomatic interactions between ancient powers. These records span various regions, including Greece, Rome, Persia, and the territories of the Israelites.

## Political Structures and Governance

In certain historical periods, governance was restructured through the suppression of existing democratical magistracies. In one such instance, all civil functions were stripped of their salaries. A new government was established starting with a committee of five persons, who were tasked with selecting a larger body of one hundred individuals (which included the original five). Each member of this group of one hundred then chose three additional persons, resulting in a body of Four Hundred. This group was granted unlimited powers to carry on the government according to their own discretion, taking their seats in the senate-house. They maintained the authority to convene the Five Thousand whenever they deemed it fit.

In later Prussian history, political participation occurred through the diet. Bunsen, a public figure, initially refused to stand for a seat in the Lower House of the Prussian diet in the Liberal interest. However, in 1858, at the special request of the regent William, Bunsen took his seat in the Prussian Upper House. While he remained silent in this capacity, he provided support for a new ministry composed of his personal and political friends.

## Public Infrastructure and Urban Design

Archaeological and historical records describe the layout of ancient athletic and cultural centers. In one such precinct, a large gymnasium was located between the theatre and the precinct; this gymnasium was later converted for other uses, including the construction of a small odeum in its center. To the south-west lay a stadium with a preserved goal and seats. The course of the stadium featured a gutter with basins at intervals for competitors or spectators, and posts placed every hundred feet to divide the course into six portions. The goal at the upper end, similar to that at Olympia, utilized a stone sill sunk level with the ground, featuring parallel grooves for runners' feet and sockets for posts that served as guides and separated the competitors' spaces. These posts were eventually replaced by stone columns resembling those found in a theatre's proscenium.

## Diplomacy and International Relations

Diplomatic missions often involved complex negotiations and social courtesies. In Athens, Demosthenes hosted Macedonian envoys—Antipater, Parmenio, and Eurylochus. Demosthenes exhibited personal civility by providing them with a splendid entertainment and ensuring they had comfortable cushions and seats of honor at the Dionysiac festival. This occurred around the time Demosthenes proposed a decree fixing the eighteenth and nineteenth of Elaphebolion (March) as days to discuss an alliance and peace with Macedonia.

In other contexts, diplomacy was fraught with tension. Lucius Caesar, a kinsman of Caesar, sought the help of Cato to devise a plausible speech to secure pardon for three hundred people. Cato refused to personally thank Caesar, whom he viewed as a tyrant acting illegally by sparing those he had no right to lord over.

In the Persian Empire, the king's favor was a significant political tool. Themistokles, through his entreaties, restored Demaratus the Spartan to the king's favor after Demaratus had angered the king by requesting to drive through Sardis wearing his tiara upright. Later Persian kings reportedly promised any Greek who deserted to them that they would be treated better than Themistokles had been. Themistokles himself was eventually allotted several cities—Magnesia, Lampsakus, and Myous for food and drink, and potentially Perkote and Palaiskepsis for clothing and bedding.

## Military Strategy and Tribal Conflict

Military records detail specific strategic requests and the aftermath of tribal warfare. Hannibal, while advising Antiochus, proposed making Italy the seat of the war. He requested eleven or twelve thousand land forces and one hundred ships, offering to command the fleet to engage Carthaginians in Africa before making a descent upon Italy.

Regarding the Israelites, records describe a period of regret following a calamity brought upon the Benjamites. After a fast was appointed, ambassadors recalled six hundred survivors who had been seated on a rock called Rimmon in the wilderness. The ambassadors persuaded the survivors to unite with them and avoid the utter destruction of the tribe of Benjamin, granting them the land of Benjamin and any prey they could carry. To ensure the tribe's survival, the Israelites provided four hundred virgins from Jabesh Gilead as wives. To provide wives for the remaining two hundred without violating a previous oath—taken in passion—not to give daughters to Benjamites, a proposal was made involving the meetings in Shiloh where wives and daughters accompanied the men.

## Geographical Observations

Historical accounts by Herodotus provide measurements of the Pontus and the Bosphorus. The Pontus is described as having a length of eleven thousand one hundred furlongs and a maximum breadth of three thousand three hundred. The mouth of this sea, the Bosphorus, is described as being not less than one hundred and twenty furlongs in length and four furlongs broad. The Bosphorus extends to the Propontis, which measures one thousand four hundred furlongs in length and five hundred in breadth, eventually outletting into the Hellespont. The Hellespont is four hundred furlongs long and seven furlongs broad at its narrowest point.
