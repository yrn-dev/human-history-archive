# Sumero-Akkadians

The Sumero-Akkadians were an ancient civilized race of non-Semitic settlers who inhabited the Euphrates valley. They are recognized as the earlier settlers of the region and are credited with the origin of the cuneiform script. Their language is considered to be allied to the agglutinative idioms of central Asia, and some hypotheses suggest a possible alliance with the ancestors of the Huns and Turks.

## Early Civilization and Empire
The first known empire in history was established by the high priest of the god of the Sumerian city of Erech. According to an inscription found at Nippur, this empire extended from the Upper Sea (possibly the Red or Mediterranean) to the Lower Sea (the Persian Gulf). This era, occurring during the first half of the Age of Cultivation, saw the emergence of the first known priest-rulers and temples among mankind within the Tigris-Euphrates valley.

## The Sumerian-Akkadian Empire
For many generations, the Sumerians interacted with nomadic Semitic-speaking tribes who appeared on the western edge of their country through trade, raiding, and warfare. Around 2750 B.C., a Semitic leader named Sargon united these tribes and conquered the Sumerians. Sargon extended his rule from the Mediterranean in the west to beyond the Persian Gulf in the east. His people were known as the Akkadians, and the resulting state is termed the Sumerian Akkadian Empire, which lasted for more than two hundred years.

## Cultural Influence and Cuneiform
Despite the political and military conquest by the Semites, the Sumerian civilization prevailed over the simpler Semitic culture. The Akkadians adopted the Sumerian language and their system of writing, known as cuneiform, rather than establishing a Semitic writing system of their own. The Sumerian language functioned as the language of power and knowledge for the newcomers, similar to the role of Latin in Europe during the middle ages.

The cuneiform system utilized a double method of writing:
* **Ideographic method:** Words were written using a sign or combination of signs expressive of the entire word.
* **Phonetic method:** Words were spelled out in syllables.

Because the cuneiform syllabary was of non-Semitic origin, Babylonian and Assyrian texts often used Sumerian signs to represent Semitic equivalents. For example, the Sumerian sign MU (meaning "name") was used in Babylonian or Assyrian texts but read as the Semitic equivalent, *shumu*. Similarly, the Sumerian SIG-BA (meaning "clothing") was read as the Semitic *lubushtu*. Over time, the Sumerian signs were treated merely as syllables to spell Babylonian words phonetically, without reference to the original Sumerian meaning. This evolved into the Babylonian Assyrian syllabary as the culture spread north to Assyria.

## Decline and Succession
The Sumerian Akkadian Empire eventually lost its military and political vigor. It was pressured from the east by the Elamites and from the west by the Semitic Amorites. The Amorites initially settled in a small up-river town called Babylon. After a century of warfare, the Amorites became the masters of all Mesopotamia under King Hammurabi (2100 B.C.), who founded the first Babylonian Empire.
