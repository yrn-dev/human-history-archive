# Asculum

Asculum was an ancient city located in the center of difficult country, serving as the capital of Picenum. It occupied a strong strategic position and was situated 120 miles from Rome.

## Roman Conquest and the Social War
The city was first taken by the Romans in 268 B.C. Following this conquest, the Via Salaria was likely extended to reach the city. Asculum later played a prominent role in the Social War against Rome. In 90 B.C., the inhabitants of the city massacred all Roman citizens within its walls, as well as the proconsul Q. Servilius (also referred to in some records as P. Servilius).

## Siege and Capture by Pompeius Strabo
In 89 B.C., during the Marsic war, Asculum was captured by Pompeius Strabo after a long siege. The city was burnt during the conquest. The aftermath of the siege was severe: the leader of the city, Judacilius, committed suicide, the principal citizens were put to death, and the remaining population was exiled. 

Pompeius Strabo, who was the father of Pompeius Magnus and served as consul in 89 B.C., celebrated a triumph on December 25th of that year for his success against the Asculani and other inhabitants of Picenum.

## Later Roman and Imperial History
Following the period of the Social War, Caesar occupied Asculum as a strong position after he crossed the Rubicon. The city eventually received a Roman colony, possibly under the triumvirs, which increased its importance. By A.D. 301, Asculum had become the capital of Picenum Suburbicarium.

In 545, the city was taken by Totila. Despite this, the writer Paulus Diaconus describes Asculum as the chief city of Picenum shortly after that event.

## Medieval and Later Governance
From the era of Charlemagne, Asculum was governed by its bishops. These bishops held the title of prince and possessed the authority to coin money. This arrangement continued until 1185, at which point Asculum became a free republic. During its time as a republic, the city engaged in many struggles with Fermo. By the 15th century, Asculum came more directly under the sway of the papacy.
