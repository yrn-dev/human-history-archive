# Villas

Villas were country estates and residences associated primarily with the Roman world, ranging from simple farms to expansive, luxurious houses. These structures served various roles, from centers of rural economy to retreats for the wealthy and powerful.

## Villas in Roman Britain
In Roman Britain, the countryside was divided into estates frequently referred to as villas. These varied significantly in scale and purpose; while some were mere farms, others were large and luxurious country-houses. The inhabitants of these estates were diverse, including small farmers, bailiffs, and wealthy landowners or great nobles.

Some of these estates operated under a specific "villa" system. In this arrangement, the lord resided in a "great house" and utilized slaves to cultivate the land immediately surrounding the residence. The remaining land was leased to half-free *coloni*. However, other systems of land management may have also existed. Notable examples of important country-houses in this region include Chedworth and Woodchester in Gloucestershire, as well as Bignor in west Sussex.

## Luxury and Imperial Residences in Italy
In Italy, villas were often associated with the extreme luxury and pride of the Roman nobility. Some nobles, such as Lucullus, used their riches from the East to create costly and laborious works in their villas and parks. Lucullus maintained villas at Tusculum and near Naples; his estate at Tusculum was so splendid that he was eventually laid there for burial.

The city of Baiae in Campania was particularly famous as a residence for the rich due to its mild climate, luxuriant vegetation, and curative warm sulphur springs. It was a favorite location for Roman emperors. Julius Caesar possessed a villa there, the remains of which are likely found in large substructures above a 16th-century castle. Nero also built a huge villa, likely on the site of that same castle. Additionally, Hadrian died in Caesar's villa in A.D. 138, and Alexander Severus constructed large buildings there for his mother. Ancient writers frequently noted the immorality and luxury associated with life in Baiae during both the republic and the empire.

## Rural Economy and Amenities
Villas were not only places of leisure but also sites of rural economy. In some Roman villas, the art of rearing and fattening large numbers of *glires* (a genus of squirrels) was practiced as a profitable venture. This was driven by an excessive demand for them at luxurious tables, a demand that increased due to prohibitions issued by the censors.

The amenities within these estates could be magnificent. For example, the baths at the villa of Liternum, belonging to Scipio Africanus, were compared by Seneca to the public baths of Rome. Later in the empire's history, the rich and luxurious nobles became so indulged in the enjoyment of their villas, theatres, and baths that they cheerfully resigned the dangerous responsibilities of empire to soldiers and peasants.

## Decline and Later Examples
The elegance of the Italian villas eventually suffered during the invasions of the Barbarians. Gothic warriors insulted the gardens and villas along the coast of Campania, including those once inhabited by Cicero and Lucullus. The victors utilized the shade of artificially disposed plane-trees to protect themselves from the sun while their captives, the children of Roman senators, served them wine in goblets made of gems and gold.

In later periods, the term "villa" appeared in different contexts. In England, the Aston Villa Football Club utilizes a playing field in the Aston Lower Grounds of Warwickshire. Additionally, the Villa Borghese is noted as a location housing mythological pictures by the Venetian painter Paris Bordone.
