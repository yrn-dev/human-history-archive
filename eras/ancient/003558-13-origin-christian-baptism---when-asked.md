# Origin of Christian Baptism

## Early Foundations and the Baptism of John
The origins of Christian baptism are closely linked to the baptism of John and existing Jewish practices. For publicans and soldiers seeking purification from their past, John's baptism functioned similarly to the proselyte's bath. However, it differed by raising them to be children unto Abraham and providing assurance of a place in the kingdom of God without the requirement of circumcision, which was standard for ordinary proselytes. For Jews, John's baptism aligned with a practice used by those wishing to consecrate their lives through a stricter practice and study of the law; Epictetus refers to such individuals as "the baptized Jew."

According to Acts xix. 4, John baptized in the name of the coming Messiah without identifying him as Jesus of Nazareth. This identification was later provided by the apostolic age, with formulas such as "into Christ Jesus," "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ," or "of Jesus Christ." Paul described these formulas as equivalent to being "into the death of Christ Jesus," suggesting that the faithful were raised from death into everlasting life. This likeness between the ceremony and the death and resurrection of Christ was intended to ensure a real union between the believer and Christ.

## Early Administration and Rituals
The earliest detailed accounts of the rite are found in Justin Martyr and the *Teaching of the Apostles* (c. 90-120). The *Teaching* instructs that baptism be performed in living water into the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, though other water may be used if living water is unavailable, and warm water may be used if cold is not possible.

Early sources, including the New Testament, the *Didache*, and Tertullian, do not require the use of a font, generally favoring running or living water. This reflected a Jewish rule that water used in ablutions should run over and away from the body. Consequently, baptisms were performed in lakes, rivers, and the sea. In the case of martyrs, a death in itself was sufficient, though some were sprinkled with their own blood. Nearchus (c. 250) noted that true faith was the primary requirement for salvation, citing how the Lord healed the blind without asking if they had been baptized.

## Development of the Baptistery
Before the 4th century, there is little trace of dedicated baptisteries. After Constantine made Christianity the state religion, separate halls or chapels known as baptisteries (or *photisterion* in the Greek Church) became common. These were often circular, or had eight or twelve sides, consisting of an ante-room for the instruction of catechumens and an inner apartment for the sacrament.

The inner apartment featured a baptismal font (*piscina* or *kolumbethra*), typically made of stone, though metals were used later. Candidates were immersed thrice in the font, which was accessed by three steps and often featured a suspended gold or silver dove and wall pictures depicting the life of John the Baptist. Because the rite was performed only three times a year and the bishop typically baptized all catechumens in his diocese, these buildings were often large and attached to cathedrals rather than parish churches. By the 6th century, fonts began to be built in the church porch or within the church itself, and the construction of separate baptisteries declined after the 9th century.

## Theological Disputes and Variations
In the 4th century, disputes arose regarding the timing of baptism. In Spain, some baptized on January 6 (*Natalicia*), a custom Pope Siricius protested in 385. In Sicily, some believed the Saviour was reborn through the Holy Spirit during His baptism and thus baptized neophytes on January 6 to share in that grace. Pope Leo (c. 447) rejected this, arguing that the Lord did not need the remission of sins or rebirth. Leo asserted that the power of regeneration was sanctioned when the water of baptism and blood of redemption flowed from Christ's side, and therefore neophytes should only be baptized at Pentecost and Easter.

Additionally, ancient custom differed from modern practice in that the sacrament of baptism, even for infants, was immediately followed by holy communion and confirmation.

## Analogous Rites and Later Applications
While the early Church Fathers recognized that other initiation rites—such as those of the Mithraic, Isiac, and Eleusinian *mystae*—used baptism, they did not believe Christians borrowed essential features from these Gentile sources. Instead, baptism had already existed within Judaism and was imposed on proselytes long before Jesus.

In later historical contexts, such as the conversion of the Icelanders under King Olaf Trygvason, baptism was used as a means of securing political friendship or military requirement. For example, Kjartan and Bolle Thorlakson were baptized to obtain the king's friendship and wore white baptismal clothes as guests. In another instance, King Olaf ordered 900 heathen men in his army to be baptized before a battle, stating he would trust in God alone for victory and would not mix heathen people with his own. Of these, 400 agreed to be baptized by a priest and confirmed by a bishop, while 500 refused and returned home.
