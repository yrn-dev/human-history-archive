# Nineteenth Year of the War—Battles in the Great Harbour—Retreat and Annihilation of the Athenian Army

## Strategic Disagreements and Command
During the nineteenth year of the war, the Athenian command was divided over the continuation of the siege of Syracuse. Nicias advocated for maintaining the siege, basing his positive outlook on exact information regarding financial distress within Syracuse and messages from the Athenian party there urging him not to raise the siege. Nicias also expressed increased confidence in the success of his fleet.

Conversely, Demosthenes strongly opposed continuing the siege. He argued that if a decree from Athens did not allow for the army's withdrawal, the forces should relocate to Catana or Thapsus. Demosthenes reasoned that these locations would provide the land forces with a wide area of country to overrun and plunder for sustenance. Furthermore, he argued that the fleet would benefit from the open sea, allowing their science to be utilized in a wide sea-room where they could advance or retreat without being circumscribed, rather than fighting in a narrow space that favored the enemy. Eurymedon agreed with Demosthenes, though Nicias's persistence caused hesitation and suspicion among the leadership that he might possess further information.

## Operations at the Great Harbour
Athenian forces attempted to fortify a cliff above a marsh overlooking the great harbour, as this provided the shortest line for their work to reach the harbour across the plain and marsh. In response, the Syracusans constructed a second stockade and dug a trench starting from the city across the middle of the marsh to prevent the Athenians from extending their wall to the sea.

The Athenians eventually attacked the Syracusan ditch and stockade at dawn, using planks and doors to cross the muddiest and firmest parts of the marsh. While the Athenians were initially victorious—driving the Syracusan right wing toward the town and the left toward the river—the tide turned when the Syracusans, supported by most of their cavalry, routed three hundred picked Athenians who had attempted to cut off their passage. During this engagement, Lamachus and five or six of his men were killed after crossing a ditch to aid the Athenian right wing.

## Naval Engagements and Defensive Measures
Naval skirmishes in the great harbour involved periods of advancing and retreating. In one such engagement, the Syracusans sank one or two Athenian vessels before the forces parted. Following this drawn battle, Nicias ordered the refitting of damaged ships. To create a makeshift enclosed harbour, the Athenians moored merchant vessels approximately two hundred feet apart in front of a stockade they had driven into the sea. This arrangement was designed to allow hard-pressed ships to retreat in safety and sail out again at their leisure.

## Retreat and Annihilation
The campaign in Sicily concluded in a total destruction of the Athenian forces. The Athenian fleet and army were beaten at all points and destroyed. This event is described as the greatest Hellenic achievement of the war, and perhaps of Hellenic history, being most glorious for the victors and most calamitous for the conquered. Very few of the many who participated returned home.
