# Seventh Year of the War—Occupation of Pylos—Surrender of the Spartan

The events surrounding the occupation of Pylos and the subsequent surrender of Spartan forces occurred during the seventh year of the Peloponnesian War, creating a significant shift in the strategic balance between Athens and the Lacedaemonians.

## The Occupation of Pylos and Sphacteria
Pylos was first fortified by Demosthenês in 425 B.C., serving as an Athenian post and a refuge for Helots who had revolted. The capture of Sphacteria led to the surrender of a Spartan army, resulting in the Athenians taking Spartan prisoners. The Athenians determined to keep these prisoners in prison until a peace was reached, with the threat that they would be put to death if the Peloponnesians invaded Athenian territory in the interim.

The strategic value of Pylos was amplified by the involvement of Messenians from Naupactus. These individuals were sent to their old country—to which Pylos formerly belonged—and launched a series of destructive incursions into Laconia, aided by their common dialect.

## Impact on Lacedaemonian Stability
The Lacedaemonians, lacking experience in this type of warfare, became seriously uneasy as Helots began deserting and the threat of revolution grew within their own country. This instability drove the Lacedaemonians to send envoys to Athens to attempt to recover both the prisoners and the post at Pylos, though the Athenians dismissed these envoys without reaching an agreement.

The fear of the Helots' numbers and obstinacy governed Spartan policy. In an attempt to identify and remove the most rebellious elements, the Spartans proclaimed that any Helots who had distinguished themselves against the enemy would receive their freedom. After selecting two thousand such individuals, the Spartans subsequently did away with them in a manner that remained unknown. Following this, the Spartans sent seven hundred Helots as heavy infantry to assist Brasidas in his campaign through Thessaly, Chalcidice, and the regions of Perdiccas.

## Athenian Dominance and Sicilian Reactions
By the spring of the eighth year of the war, Athens held Pylos, Kythêra, and the Spartan prisoners. This position placed the Athenians in a state of high hope while their enemies were forced onto the defensive. The "astonishing humiliation" of Sparta following the capture of Sphacteria had a profound effect on the Sicilian Dorians. Intimidated by the shift in power, these cities sought pacification. The city of Kamarina first made a separate accommodation with Gela, leading to a meeting of deputies from all cities in the island to conclude a peace.

## The Fall of Pylos
Despite the earlier Athenian advantages, the city of Pylos eventually fell. Athens had stripped itself of force by sending a large armament with Thrasyllus, encouraging the Spartans to launch a combined land and sea expedition to attack the post. Pylos became heavily pressed and faced surrender.

Athens attempted to send relief in the form of thirty triremes under the command of Anytus. However, Anytus failed to reach the location due to unfavorable winds or stormy weather at Cape Malea. Consequently, Pylos surrendered, and the garrison departed under terms of capitulation. Upon his return, Anytus faced a trial for betraying his trust; it is reported that he avoided condemnation by bribing the dikastery, marking the first instance of an Athenian obtaining a verdict through corruption.

## Diplomatic Aftermath and Panactum
In the later years of the war, Pylos remained a central point of negotiation. Lacedaemon hoped to recover Pylos by offering the Athenians the fortress of Panactum and Athenian prisoners held by the Boeotians. To achieve this, Lacedaemon entered into a special alliance with the Boeotians, despite a covenant with Athens that forbade new alliances without Athenian consent. However, the Boeotians did not hand over Panactum; instead, they razed the fortress to the ground, citing ancient oaths that the district should remain a neutral strip of borderland.
