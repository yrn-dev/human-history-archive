# Causes of the War: The Affair of Epidamnus and Potidæa

The Peloponnesian War (431-404 B.C.) was preceded by a series of diplomatic failures and regional conflicts, most notably the affair at Epidamnus and the subsequent tensions between Corcyra, Corinth, and Athens. These events created a climate of suspicion and breach of treaty that eventually led to open hostilities between the Athenians and the Peloponnesians and their respective allies.

## The Foundation and Nature of Epidamnus
Epidamnus, later known as Dyrrhachium, was situated on an isthmus on the right of the entrance to the Ionic Gulf, near the territory of the Taulantii, an Illyrian people. The city was founded toward the close of the 7th century B.C. (approximately 627 B.C.) as a colony from Corcyra. Following the Greek practice of the time, the *œkist* (founder) was borrowed from the mother-city of the colony; thus, Phalius, son of Eratocleides and a member of the Heraclid family, was summoned from Corinth for this purpose. 

The settlement was a joint effort, consisting of Corcyraeans, Corinthians, and others of the Dorian race. Because of this arrangement, the Corinthians maintained a partial right of interference and control over the affairs of Epidamnus. In the century preceding the Peloponnesian War, the city grew great in population and wealth. Its early government was a close oligarchy managed by the colony's primitive leaders, where artisans and tradesmen were regarded as public slaves—though this status likely lacked the hardships of actual slavery due to the city's liberal admission of resident aliens (*metics*). Over time, this oligarchy was replaced by a more democratic form of government featuring a periodical senate and public assemblies, while still retaining a single-headed archon.

## Internal Strife and the Appeal to Corcyra
The stability of Epidamnus collapsed due to internal factions, which reportedly arose from a war with neighboring barbarians. This instability culminated in the people expelling the nobles from the city. The exiled party allied themselves with the barbarians and began plundering Epidamnus by land and sea.

Hard pressed, the Epidamnians sent ambassadors to their mother-city, Corcyra, seeking intervention to mediate with the exiles and end the war with the barbarians. The ambassadors presented themselves as suppliants in the temple of Hera. However, the Corcyraeans refused the supplication and dismissed the ambassadors without providing assistance.

## Escalation and the Corinthian Intervention
The dissolution of the original oligarchy and the subsequent democratic opposition led to a quarrel between Corcyra and the oligarchical city of Corinth. The Corinthians, asserting their historical right of control, eventually declared war. 

The Corinthians deployed a fleet of seventy-five ships and two thousand heavy infantry under the command of Aristeus, Callicrates, and Timanor, with troops led by Archetimus and Isarchidas. As the fleet reached Actium at the mouth of the Gulf of Ambracia, the Corcyraeans attempted to warn them against sailing further. When no peaceful answer was provided, the Corcyraeans met the enemy with a fleet of eighty sail (forty of which had been engaged in the siege of Epidamnus). The Corcyraeans won a decisive victory, destroying fifteen Corinthian vessels. Simultaneously, Epidamnus was forced to capitulate to its besiegers; the terms dictated that foreigners be sold and the Corinthians be held as prisoners of war.

## Path to the Peloponnesian War
The conflict at Epidamnus and the intervention of Athens on behalf of Corcyra are cited as contributory causes of the broader Peloponnesian War. Following the advice of Pericles, the Athenians voted to refuse the dictation of the Lacedaemonians, insisting instead that complaints be settled through the legal methods and impartial manner prescribed by the terms of the existing truce.

Despite this official stance, the relationship between the rival powers deteriorated. While mutual communication continued for a time, it was conducted without heralds and characterized by deep suspicion. Events occurred that were equivalent to breaches of the treaty, serving as matters for war. Eventually, all intercourse except through heralds ceased, and the war between the Athenians and the Peloponnesians and their allies began in earnest, with hostilities prosecuted without intermission.
