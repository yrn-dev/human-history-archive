# Popular Discontent in Historical Governance

Across various eras and regions, popular discontent has frequently emerged as a response to perceived injustice, administrative weakness, or the lack of constitutional means for political expression. This phenomenon often manifests as a catalyst for political agitation, insurrection, or the rise of new leadership.

## Administrative Weakness and National Agitation
In the Austrian Empire, the death of Francis I on March 2, 1835, led to the succession of his son, Ferdinand I. Because the new emperor was incapacitated by epilepsy and unable to rule, a veiled regency was established. This period was marked by divided counsels and weakness at the center, which accentuated administrative vices and allowed popular discontent to make rapid headway.

This discontent manifested in several regional movements:
*   **Hungary:** The diet showed signs of vigorous life, while Slav separatist movements in the south roused the spirit of Magyar ascendancy.
*   **Bohemia:** A Czech literary movement evolved into organized resistance against the established order. Nationalists adopted the watchword "Repeal," framing their criticism under the guise of criticizing the English administration in Ireland.
*   **Southern Slavs:** Starting in 1836, Ljudevit Gaj voiced the "Illyrian" movement through the *Illyrian National Gazette*. Initially seeking a Pan-Slav union, the movement shifted in 1844—following Austrian government interference—toward the revival of a "Triune Kingdom" (comprising Dalmatia, Croatia, and Slavonia) independent of the Hungarian crown.
*   **German Provinces:** Despite the efforts of police and Metternich's censors, national movements and advanced revolutionary views gained ground, as evidenced by the 1848 revolution in Vienna.

## Oligarchy and Political Exclusion in Ancient Greece
In ancient Greek city-states, discontent often stemmed from the restrictive nature of oligarchical governments. In early Sicilian Greek cities, such as Syracuse, the "Gamori" (wealthy proprietors descended from colonizing chiefs) held power, while the "Demos" (non-privileged freemen, including artisans and small cultivators) were excluded. Discontent arose when members of the privileged class fell into poverty or when members of the Demos rose to opulence without being admitted to the privileged body. This environment allowed ambitious leaders to head popular oppositions, overthrow oligarchies, and establish themselves as despots.

Similarly, in Corinth, a philo-Laconian or peace-party grew in strength against an anti-Laconian oligarchy. The ruling administrators, who had contracted alliances with Persia, left the opposition with no constitutional means to change public policy or administrators peaceably. Consequently, the opposition turned to meetings and conspiracies, eventually leading to the philo-Laconian leader Pasimêlus opening the gates to the Lacedæmonians.

Other causes of discontent in Greece included the hardships of land-war, losses of slaves and property, and jealousy toward Bœotia and Attica. In Corinth, these factors contributed to strong discontent against the war by the early part of 392 B.C., leading to a massacre and a *coup d’état*.

## Economic and Social Instability in Rome and Italy
In the century following the destruction of Carthage, the Roman Empire and Italy experienced widespread discomfort and anxiety. While a small number of individuals became very wealthy, the majority struggled with debts, jumpy markets, and uncertain prices. Because the popular assembly was not a workable public organ, there was no formal way to clear up this general dissatisfaction. Public will expressed itself through violence, strikes, and insurrection—the methods of suppressed or cheated peoples—whenever governing cliques refused to heed the common good.

On an individual level, discontent could also arise from perceived injustice. As noted in comparisons of political subversion, a person who loses an election or a cause due to antipathy or unjust favor is more offended and angry than one who loses under fair circumstances. This specific type of discontent, rooted in the belief that failure was unjustly brought about, tends to break out in active opposition.

## Colonial and Imperial Tensions
In the Transvaal and Free State, "diggers" who had previously enjoyed practical independence found the new government provided little benefit. Discontent grew over heavy taxation, the unrepresentative character of the government, and inadequate protection of property. This led to the formation of a society for mutual protection and necessitated the deployment of an armed force from the Cape in early 1875 to overawe agitators.

In the Roman Empire under Constantine, discontent appeared within the royal family. Crispus, a prince of mature years who had performed signal services, found himself confined as a prisoner to his father's court while his infant brother, Constantius, was named Cæsar over the Gallic provinces. This situation, combined with the calumny of enemies, led to resentment that Constantine suspected had evolved into a secret conspiracy against his government.
