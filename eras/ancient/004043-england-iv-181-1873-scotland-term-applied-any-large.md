# England

## Legal and Judicial Systems
The English legal system utilizes various forms of specialized assistance within its courts. Under the Judicature Act 1873, both the High Court and the court of appeal are permitted to call upon assessors for aid in any matter or cause. Specific legislation further defines the role of assessors in specialized cases: the Patents Act 1907 provides for them in trade-mark and patent cases, and the County Court Admiralty Jurisdiction Act 1868 allows judges in county court admiralty cases to be assisted by assessors possessing "nautical skill and experience." 

In the High Court's admiralty division, the Elder Brethren of Trinity House typically serve as nautical assessors. Furthermore, the House of Lords may call upon assessors for appeals in admiralty actions according to the Supreme Court of Judicature Act 1891. In ecclesiastical matters, the Church Discipline Act 1840 (s. 11) allows assessors to assist the bishop, while the Clergy Discipline Act 1892 (s. 2) provides for assessors to assist the chancellor in determining questions of fact. Additionally, the Appellate Jurisdiction Act 1876 (s. 14) empowers the king in council to establish rules for archbishops and bishops to attend as assessors during the hearing of ecclesiastical cases by the judicial committee of the privy council.

Regarding matrimonial law, the Matrimonial Causes Act 1857 stipulates that a decree of judicial separation may be obtained by either a husband or a wife on the grounds of desertion. To qualify, the desertion must have occurred without cause for a period of two years or more.

## Land Tenure and Administration
England has historically employed several distinct systems of land tenure and administrative division. The "hundred" is a traditional administrative unit in England, which is considered equivalent to the "baronies" found in Ireland.

Burgage is a form of tenure applicable to property associated with the privileges and old municipal corporations of ancient boroughs. In the English system, burgage tenure involved holding tenements or houses from a lord or the king at a specific rent. This tenure is often viewed as a relic of Saxon freedom that resisted Norman feudalism, though it may also be seen as a local feature of the general exemption from feudality enjoyed by *municipia* as a remnant of ancient Roman constitutions. In England, burgage-tenure was classified as a species of socage to differentiate it from military holdings. The franchises of freemen, burgesses, and other consuetudinary constituencies in burghs depended on the character of this tenure, which was subject to various customs, most notably "Borough-English."

## Economy and Agriculture
The Bank of England serves as a central financial institution, with its history documented in various texts including works by T. Fortune (1802), John Francis (1847), and J. E. Thorold Rogers (1887). Banking houses have also operated in various English regions, including Manchester, Bristol, and the areas of North Yorkshire, Durham, and Northumberland.

In the field of agriculture, the term "corn" is used generally to refer to the seed of cereal plants. In England specifically, "corn" refers to wheat. This differs from the usage in the United States, where it refers to maize, and in Scotland and Ireland, where it refers to oats.

## Urban Development
The evolution of urban structures in London provides an example of how temporary commercial setups became permanent. According to Stow, the houses in Old Fish Street began as "movable boards" used on market days to sell fish. After obtaining licenses to establish sheds, these structures evolved into shops and eventually grew into "tall houses." This process mirrors the general evolution of the "booth"—originally a temporary dwelling of boughs or slight materials—which transitioned into market stalls and eventually permanent shops.
