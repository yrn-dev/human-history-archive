# Excavations and Roman History in Cumberland

## Roman Britain and the Work of F. Haverfield
Francis John Haverfield, M.A., LL.D., has contributed extensively to the study of Roman Britain. A Fellow of the British Academy and Brasenose College, Oxford, Haverfield served as the Camden Professor of Ancient History at Oxford University and was formerly a Librarian, Tutor, Student, and Senior Censor at Christ Church, Oxford. His academic contributions include the monograph *The Romanization of Roman Britain* (1906), as well as articles in the *Edinburgh Review* (1899) and the *Victoria County History*.

Haverfield's research extends to the epigraphy of the region; he provided supplements to the Roman inscriptions collected by Hübner in the periodical *Ephemeris epigraphica*. Additionally, he authored a piece in the *Archaeological Journal* (1892) regarding the eponymous goddess Brigantia.

## Roman Fortifications in Cumberland
The Roman military presence in Britain involved a system of legionary fortresses and auxiliary *castella*. While legionary fortresses were large rectangular enclosures of 50 to 60 acres, auxiliary *castella* were significantly smaller, typically ranging from three to six acres. These auxiliary forts were positioned closer to the frontiers than the legions.

In Cumberland, specific sites of archaeological interest include:
*   **Hardknott**: A Roman fort located on a Cumberland moor overlooking Upper Eskdale.
*   **Housesteads**: A fort situated on Hadrian's wall.

Excavations at Hardknott are documented by R.S. Ferguson in the *Cumberland Arch. Soc. Trans.* xii. Reports of excavations conducted by Haverfield are found in the *Cumberland Archaeological Society Transactions* covering the years 1894 to 1904.

## The Brigantes of Northern Britain
The Brigantes were a people of northern Britain whose name is Celtic for "free, privileged" or "mountaineers." Their territory was extensive, stretching from the Belisama (the Ribble or Mersey) in the west to the mouth of the Abus (Humber) in the east, and extending north to the Wall of Antoninus. This territory encompassed most of Yorkshire, Durham, Westmorland, Cumberland, Northumberland, and the entirety of Lancashire. Their primary town was Eburacum (York).

The Brigantes first encountered the Romans during the reign of Claudius and suffered defeat by Publius Ostorius Scapula. Although they submitted to Petillius Cerealis under Vespasian, they were not fully subdued until the era of Antoninus Pius. A branch of this people also established a settlement in the south-east corner of Ireland near the river Birgus (Barrow).

## Other Roman Frontiers and Sites
Beyond Cumberland, Roman occupation extended into Scotland, where Haverfield contributed to the *Antonine Wall Report* (1899). Other notable Scottish excavations occurred at the forts of Newstead, Birrens, Lyne, Ardoch, and on the wall of Pius at Rough Castle, Castle Cary, and Bar Hill.

In England, other carefully excavated auxiliary forts include Gellygaer, located between Brecon and Cardiff, which is cited as one of the most perfect specimens of a late 1st-century A.D. Roman fort in the empire. Other significant sites of Roman study include Silchester, Caerwent, and London.
