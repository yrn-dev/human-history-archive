# Military Commands and Conflict in Spain

## Carthaginian Conquest and Administration
The Carthaginian presence in Spain was characterized by a protracted struggle for control. Because the various districts and peoples of Spain were detached from one another and lacked mutual connection, the Carthaginians were forced to subdue them individually. While this lack of unity among the Spaniards eventually led to the loss of the territory, it initially made the conquest more difficult and prolonged. Although the Carthaginians had not made considerable progress for some time, they completed the conquest of almost the entire country within twenty years.

During the lead-up to the Second Punic War, Hannibal sought to ensure the security of both Africa and Spain. To achieve this, he exchanged forces between the two regions, placing Africans in Spain and Spaniards in Africa. He believed that soldiers serving far from their home countries would be more firmly attached to him, acting as hostages for each other's fidelity. Hannibal left the command of the Spanish forces to his brother, Asdrubal, providing him with a fleet of approximately sixty ships to guard the coasts and specific directions for dealing with both the Spaniards and the Romans. The forces remaining in Spain numbered slightly over fifteen thousand, including 2,500 horse.

## Roman Intervention and the Ebro Boundary
For a period, the Romans restricted Carthaginian territory to the area north of the river Ebro, treating any crossing of the river by Carthaginians as an act of war. In 218 B.C., Hannibal crossed the Ebro and marched his army into Italy.

The Romans recognized that Spain was a vital source of money and men for Hannibal. Consequently, they increased their attention to the region. Cn. Scipio initiated this effort by attacking and defeating a Carthaginian fleet commanded by Hamilcar, capturing twenty-five ships and rich spoils. Following this, P. Scipio was given command of a fleet and sent to Spain to join his brother. Under Publius, the Romans moved beyond their previous limit of the Ebro, carrying their arms deeper into the country.

## The Campaign of the Two Scipios and Asdrubal
The two Scipios continued their command in Spain, making considerable progress. Their success was challenged by Asdrubal, who was the only Carthaginian leader seemingly capable of coping with them. However, Asdrubal received orders from Carthage to march into Italy to assist his brother. Before departing, Asdrubal wrote to the senate emphasizing the absolute necessity of sending a general capable of resisting the Romans to take his place; in response, Imilcon was sent with an army.

Upon learning of Asdrubal's departure, the Scipios subjected the greatest part of Spain. Fearing the danger of the Romans being attacked by two powerful armies led by the two brothers, the Scipios pursued Asdrubal. They forced him into a battle against his inclination, which he lost. This defeat prevented Asdrubal from continuing his march to Italy and rendered his continued presence in Spain unsafe.

## Later European and Colonial Conflicts
In later centuries, Spain remained a central actor in various military engagements. During a period of conflict involving Austria and France, Spain sent a second army to Italy via France because it was too weak to advance in the Po valley. During this time, troops from Spain and Sardinia fought combats in the Isere valley, though the French did not participate in these specific clashes.

In the late 16th century, England engaged in war with Spain. In 1589, Francis Drake commanded a fleet sent to restore Dom Antonio, the king of Portugal, while land forces were led by Sir John Norreys; however, disagreements between the commanders caused the attempt to fail. A subsequent, more formidable expedition against Spanish settlements in the West Indies was led by Sir Francis Drake and Sir John Hawkins. This expedition also failed due to disagreements between the commanders. Drake later died on his own ship near Nombre de Dios in the West Indies on January 28, 1595.

## Territorial Shifts in the Americas
Spain's influence extended to the Americas, where it took over the western part of the Louisiana region. This territory remained under Spanish control until 1800, when it was recovered by France, and was subsequently sold to the United States government in 1803.
