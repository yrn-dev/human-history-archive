# Hatshepsut: The Female Pharaoh of Ancient Egypt

## Overview
Hatshepsut (r. c. 1479–1458 BCE) remains one of the most intriguing figures of the Eighteenth Dynasty. While several women held power in Ancient Egypt, Hatshepsut is unique for transitioning from the role of Queen Regent to a full-fledged Pharaoh. Initially ruling on behalf of her stepson, Thutmose III, she eventually assumed the complete titulary and regalia of a king.

Her reign was characterized by a shift from military expansion to economic prosperity. She is most famous for establishing lucrative trade networks, most notably the expedition to the Land of Punt, which brought exotic incense, gold, and ebony into Egypt. Architecturally, she left an indelible mark on the landscape, specifically her mortuary temple at Deir el-Bahari, a masterpiece of symmetry and classical Egyptian design.

Historians have long debated the motives behind the later attempt to erase her name from monuments. For decades, it was believed that Thutmose III sought revenge against his stepmother. However, modern scholarship suggests the "damnatio memoriae" may have occurred later to ensure a clean succession line or to maintain the traditional ideology that the Pharaoh must be male.

## Key Facts
*   **Divine Right:** To legitimize her rule, Hatshepsut claimed she was the daughter of the god Amun, asserting that the deity had visited her mother in the guise of Thutmose I.
*   **Gender Fluidity in Art:** Early in her reign, she was depicted as a traditional female. As her power solidified, statues began to portray her with the traditional pharaonic beard and masculine musculature to align with the office of the Kingship.
*   **Economic Focus:** Unlike her successors, who focused on conquest in the Levant and Nubia, Hatshepsut prioritized internal stability and the restoration of temples damaged during the Hyksos period.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Obelisks:** Hatshepsut commissioned two massive obelisks at the Temple of Karnak; one remains the tallest surviving ancient obelisk in Egypt.
2. **The Punt Expedition:** The reliefs at Deir el-Bahari provide some of the earliest detailed botanical and zoological records of sub-Saharan Africa.
3. **Hidden Tomb:** While she built a grand mortuary temple, her actual burial place was intended to be in the Valley of the Kings (KV20), though her mummy was only identified in 2007 via a molar tooth found in a wooden box.
