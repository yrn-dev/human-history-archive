# Baetylic Idols of Zeus

In the study of ancient religious objects, the term baetylus (from the Greek *baitulos* or *baitulion*) refers to a sacred stone of Semitic origin (*bethel*). These objects were regarded as being endowed with life and were typically meteoric stones. In religious practice, they were either dedicated to the gods or revered as symbols of the gods themselves.

## Forms and Characteristics of Baetylic Idols
While many baetylic objects remained shapeless, in certain instances, attempts were made to give these original stones a more regular form. Examples of such modifications include the representation of Apollo Agyieus as a conical pillar with a pointed end, and Zeus Meilichius in the form of a pyramid.

Among the most notable baetylic idols were those located in the temples of Zeus Teleios at Tegea and Zeus Casius at Seleucia. These idols maintained their religious significance even during the declining years of paganism, a fact evidenced by the attacks directed toward them by ecclesiastical writers.

## Zeus Casius and Seleucia
The idol of Zeus Casius was situated in Seleucia. Within the geographical records of the region, Seleucia is listed as one of the cities of Syria, also referred to as Syria properly so called or Antiochena. Additionally, records mention a Mount Casius located in Arabia Petraea.

## Zeus Teleios and Tegea
The idol of Zeus Teleios was located at Tegea. In legendary narratives, Tegea is associated with the hero Echemus, who married Timandra, the daughter of Tyndareus.

## Related Mythology of the Baetylus
In Greek mythology, the term baetylus was specifically applied to a stone associated with the god Zeus. According to the *Etymologicum Magnum*, Cronus feared misfortune from his own children and, in an attempt to protect his infant son Zeus, swallowed a stone that had been substituted for the child by Gaea and Uranus. This stone was preserved at Delphi, where it was anointed with oil daily and covered with raw wool during festal occasions. In Phoenician mythology, Baetylus is the name of one of the sons of Uranus.

## Other Sacred Stones
Other prominent examples of sacred stones include the effigy of Rhea Cybele. This holy stone of Pessinus was black and irregular in form; it was brought to Rome in 204 B.C. and placed within the mouth of the goddess's statue.
