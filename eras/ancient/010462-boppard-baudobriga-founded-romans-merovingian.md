# Boppard

Boppard, known in antiquity as *Baudobriga*, is a town in Germany located on the left bank of the Rhine in the Prussian Rhine province. It is situated 12 miles south of Coblenz on the mainline to Cologne. As of 1900, the town had a population of 5,806.

## History and Governance
The town was founded by the Romans. During the period of the Merovingian dynasty, Boppard served as a royal residence. In the middle ages, it developed into a significant center for shipping and commerce. Under the rule of the Hohenstaufen emperors, Boppard was elevated to the status of a free imperial city.

The town's political administration shifted in 1312 when Emperor Henry VII pledged Boppard to his brother, Baldwin, the archbishop-elector of Trier. The electors maintained possession of the town until it was absorbed by France during the Revolutionary epoch. Following this, the congress of Vienna assigned the town to Prussia in 1815.

## Architecture and Landmarks
Boppard remains an old town that is still partially enclosed by medieval walls. Its most significant architectural landmarks include:

*   **Religious Buildings:** The Roman Catholic parish church, dating from the 12th and 13th centuries; the Carmelite church, built in 1318; and the Evangelical church, constructed in 1851 and enlarged in 1887.
*   **The Marienberg:** A former Benedictine monastery founded in 1123, which crowns a hill 100 feet above the Rhine. Since 1839, it has functioned as a hydropathic establishment.
*   **Administrative Sites:** The former castle, which is currently utilized for administrative offices.

## Modern Character and Economy
Due to being less constrained by hills than other locations within the picturesque gorge of the Rhine, Boppard has evolved into a residential town and a popular center for tourists. Its direct trade is centered on fruit and wine. The town also maintains some relatively insignificant industries, specifically tobacco manufacture and tanning.

## The Merovingian Context
The Merovingian dynasty, founded by Clovis, governed a period characterized by the integration of Frankish and Roman elements. The Merovingian kings were the supreme authority who ratified the compiled institutions of the Bavarians and Alemanni. They also oversaw the revision of the Salic laws; while the laws were initially composed by four venerable chieftains of the Franks and approved by three assemblies of the people, Clovis reformed articles incompatible with Christianity after his baptism. These laws were further amended by his sons and finally revised and promulgated under the reign of Dagobert.

The administration of the Merovingian kings involved specific legal practices. Those kings who were literate signed their great charters and diplomas by hand, often countersigned by chancellors or other state officers. Illiterate Merovingian kings utilized monograms composed of the letters of their names as a *signum manuale* (sign manual), a practice later adopted by Charlemagne.

Socially, the Merovingian era saw the survival of a large body of Romans in Gaul who preserved the privileges and property of citizens, though a large portion of their lands were taken for Frankish use. While the "savage jurisprudence" of the Ripuarian or Salic laws was often deplored, private matters such as inheritance, testaments, and marriage continued to be regulated by the Theodosian Code. Romans were permitted to hold offices of civil government and could even lead victorious German ranks in military service.

However, the period also saw the revival of personal slavery through the captives of war. Roman captives without specific trades were often condemned to cultivate lands or tend cattle. Under the Merovingian kings, individuals could also alienate their own personal freedom through a legal act.
