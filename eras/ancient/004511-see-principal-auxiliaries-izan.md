# Auxiliaries

The term "auxiliaries" is employed across various disciplines, including linguistics, military history, social organization, astronomy, and medicine, to describe entities, tools, or personnel that provide supplementary support to a primary system or body.

## Linguistic Auxiliaries
In linguistics, auxiliaries are verbs or particles used to assist in the construction of tenses, moods, and complex conjugations.

### Basque
The Basque language has developed an extensive conjugation system, likely influenced by neo-Latin, which combines participles (essentially declined nouns) with a few auxiliary verbs. For example, the phrase *ikusten dut* ("I see it") translates literally as "I have it in seeing," while *ikusiko dut* ("I will see it") translates as "I have it to be seen." The primary auxiliaries in Basque are *izan* ("to be") and *ukan* ("to have"). Other frequently used auxiliaries include *edin* ("to can"), *eza* ("to be able"), *egin* ("to make"), *joan* ("to go"), and *eroan* ("to draw" or "to move").

### Bantu Languages
In Bantu languages, the verb typically consists of a root that serves as the second person singular of the imperative. This root is modified by prefixed and suffixed particles, which are often reduced adverbs, prepositions, or worn-down verbs that have become auxiliaries. While the development of moods and tenses likely occurred independently across principal Bantu groups, they may share a common fundamental system. One example is the formation of the preterite tense; in Zulu, the root *tanda* (love) becomes *tandile* (have loved/did love). The suffix *-ile* (or its variants *-ili*, *-idi*, *-didi*, *-ire*, *-ine*) is likely connected to the root *li* or *di* (*ndi* or *ni*), meaning "to be" or "exist."

## Military Auxiliaries
In a military context, auxiliaries refer to supplementary troops that support a primary army.

### The Roman Empire
The Roman Empire utilized auxiliaries to complement its legions. These troops were recruited from provincials who had not yet attained the distinction of Romans, as well as from dependent communities and princes on the frontiers who maintained their security through military service. In some cases, select troops of hostile barbarians were persuaded or compelled to serve in remote climates for the benefit of the state.

Auxiliaries were often organized into cavalry *alae* or infantry cohorts, typically 500 or 1,000 strong. While the bravest bands were trained in Roman discipline under centurions and præfects, most retained the arms and habits of their home countries. This allowed each legion to possess a variety of missile weapons and lighter troops. In provinces such as Britain, auxiliaries were stationed in *castella* (forts) near the frontiers. These *castella* were significantly smaller than legionary fortresses—generally three to six acres compared to the 50 or 60 acres of a legionary fortress. Examples of such forts include Housesteads on Hadrian's wall, Hardknott, and Gellygaer.

### Other Historical Examples
In ancient Greece, the Cyclic Thêbaïs mentions auxiliaries from Messênê, Arcadia, and various parts of the Peloponnêsus during the expedition to Thêbes. Similarly, the Leontine democracy once relied on Athenian auxiliaries, the dismissal of which the Leontine Demos later bitterly regretted. In the ancient Near East, records indicate that Benhadad, the king of Syria, had thirty-two royal auxiliaries.

## Social and Medical Auxiliaries
The term is also applied to organizational structures and medical support systems.

### Social Organizations
In the context of British social movements, auxiliaries refer to local, self-controlled societies affiliated with a parent organization. For instance, a society supported by leaders such as William Wilberforce, Zachary Macaulay, and Granville Sharp expanded through these auxiliaries and their subsidiary branches (often managed by women). By 1816-1817, these auxiliaries had remitted £420,000 to the parent society. By 1905-1906, the network included over 2,000 auxiliaries abroad (primarily in British Colonies) and approximately 5,800 auxiliaries, branches, and associations in Wales and England.

### Medical Services
In German Army Corps organization, auxiliary dressing stations serve as preliminary points of care. Patients are moved from these auxiliary and principal dressing stations to field hospitals located in sheltered towns or villages behind the front.

## Technical Auxiliaries
In theoretical astronomy, certain processes are described as auxiliary to others. Specifically, the determination of orbits is auxiliary to the prediction of the apparent motions of stars, satellites, or planets. This process involves computing the orbit from observations and, for definitive results, calculating the perturbations caused by major planets.
