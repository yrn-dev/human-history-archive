# Basilica Fulvia-Aemilia and Related Historical Contexts

## The Basilica Fulvia-Aemilia
The Basilica Fulvia-Aemilia was an important public building in Rome. In the chronology of the city's architectural development, it was the next significant basilica constructed after the initial ones, preceding the era of Augustus. By the time of Augustus, there were at least five basilicas located in the immediate vicinity of the forum. Among these, the Basilica Julia was the most extensive and the latest to be built, running parallel to the southern side of the forum.

The Basilica Fulvia-Aemilia was part of a broader trend of public basilicas in Rome, which numbered approximately twenty. Additionally, private basilicas were utilized for recreation or business; the most notable example of such a private structure was located in the palace of Domitian on the Palatine. This architectural form was almost universal throughout the empire and in provincial Italian cities. Evidence of this prevalence is found in the accounts of Suetonius, who noted that when the body of Augustus was transported from Nola in Campania to Rome, it rested in the basilica of every town along the way.

## Other Notable Roman Basilicas
Following the period of the Basilica Fulvia-Aemilia and the Augustan era, other major basilicas were established. Trajan constructed the great Basilica Ulpia around A.D. 112 in connection with his forum. Later, at the beginning of the 4th century, the vaulted basilica of Maxentius or Constantine was built on the Via Sacra.

## The Via Aemilia and Bononia
Distinct from the architectural structures in Rome, the Via Aemilia was a critical road system in north Italy. A primary center of this system was Bononia (modern Bologna), the chief town of ancient Aemilia. Originally of Etruscan origin and founded as Felsina by Aucnus or Ocnus from Perusia, the site shows evidence of earlier Italic (possibly Ligurian) huts and Umbrian cemeteries dating from 800-600 B.C., followed by Etruscan civilization from approximately 600-400 B.C.

Bononia became a Roman colony in 189 B.C. Its strategic importance was solidified in 187 B.C. via the Via Aemilia, which connected it to Placentia and Ariminum, and a road to Arretium. A further road to Aquilelia was likely constructed in 175 B.C. The city acquired Roman citizenship in 90 B.C. and later served as a base of operations for Mark Antony in 43 B.C. against Decius Brutus. Augustus later added colonists and a new aqueduct from the Letta. In A.D. 53, the emperor Claudius provided a subvention of 10 million sesterces following a fire. The town remained resilient enough to resist Alaric in 410 and later became part of the Greek exarchate of Ravenna. Another station on the Via Aemilia was the ancient Forum Cornelii, which possessed Roman citizenship before the Social war and was later incorporated into the States of the Church by Caesar Borgia in 1500.

## Historical Figures: Fulvia and Aemilia
The archive records several individuals associated with these names during the Roman Republic. Fulvia was the wife of the demagogue Clodius before marrying Antonius. She is described as a bold and busy woman who did not concern herself with domestic industry, but instead aspired to command a general and rule a ruler. It is suggested that she trained Antonius to obey women, a disposition that later influenced his relationship with Cleopatra. 

Fulvia's political activities led to conflict; she and her brother Lucius warred with Cæsar and were defeated, fleeing Italy. Fulvia attempted to use disturbances in Italy to draw Antonius away from Cleopatra. She eventually died of disease at Sikyon after Antonius had left her there. Her death facilitated a reconciliation between Antonius and Cæsar, leading to a distribution of the empire where Antonius received the eastern parts and Cæsar the western.

Separately, Aemilia was the step-daughter of Sulla and the child of Metella by Scaurus. In a marriage arranged by Sulla and Metella to attach Pompeius to Sulla's interests, Pompeius was persuaded to put away his wife, Antistia, to marry Aemilia. This marriage was characterized as tyrannical, as Aemilia was pregnant at the time she was taken from another. Aemilia died in child-birth shortly after the marriage in the house of Pompeius.
