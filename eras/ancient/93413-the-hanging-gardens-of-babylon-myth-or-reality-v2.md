# The Nineveh Hypothesis: Shifting the Geography of the Hanging Gardens

While traditional historical narratives place the Hanging Gardens within the walls of Babylon, a growing body of scholarly evidence suggests we may be looking at the wrong city entirely. For centuries, the Gardens were attributed to King Nebuchadnezzar II as a romantic gesture for his Median wife. However, the glaring absence of any mention of the gardens in Babylonian records—including the extensive archives of Nebuchadnezzar himself—has led historians to question the site's authenticity.

A compelling alternative perspective, championed by Dr. Stephanie Dalley of Oxford University, proposes that the gardens were not in Babylon, but in Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire. This theory shifts the focus from a story of romantic longing to one of imperial engineering and political prestige.

## The Assyrian Engineering Marvel
If the gardens existed in Nineveh, they were likely commissioned by the Assyrian King Sennacherib (r. 705–681 BCE). Unlike the legendary descriptions of "hanging" terraces in Babylon, archaeological evidence at Nineveh reveals a sophisticated system of canals and aqueducts. Sennacherib’s inscriptions boast of creating a "palace without parallel," featuring a vast botanical collection brought from all over the known world.

The technical requirements for such a garden—specifically the ability to lift massive quantities of water to elevated terraces—align more closely with Assyrian hydraulic expertise than with the available evidence in Babylon. The discovery of a massive stone aqueduct at Jerwan, which fed Nineveh, provides the physical proof of the infrastructure necessary to sustain a lush, mountain-like garden in an arid plain.

## The Confusion of the Chroniclers
The discrepancy in location likely stems from the way ancient historians recorded their findings. Greek writers, such as Berossus and Diodorus Siculus, wrote centuries after the fall of these empires. By the time their accounts were penned, the distinction between the Neo-Babylonian and Neo-Assyrian empires had blurred in the Western imagination. 

Because Babylon was the more famous cultural hub of Mesopotamia, it is highly probable that the wonders of Nineveh were erroneously attributed to the city of Babylon. This scholarly debate highlights a critical lesson in historiography: the danger of relying on second-hand accounts when primary archaeological evidence is missing.

## Key Facts
* **Traditional Attribution:** Long attributed to Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon (c. 605–562 BCE).
* **Alternative Attribution:** Proposed by modern scholars to be the work of Sennacherib of Nineveh (c. 705–681 BCE).
* **The "Silence" of Babylon:** No contemporary Babylonian tablets mention the gardens.
* **Hydraulic Feats:** The Nineveh theory is supported by the existence of the Jerwan aqueduct.
* **Botanical Ambition:** The gardens were intended to showcase exotic flora from diverse climates.

## Did You Know?
* **The "Hanging" Misnomer:** The Greek word *kremastos* (hanging) likely referred to the gardens being built on tiered terraces with overhanging plants, rather than literally suspended in the air.
* **The First Botanical Garden:** If located in Nineveh, the gardens represent one of the earliest known attempts to create a curated botanical collection for scientific and aesthetic purposes.
* **Missing Ruins:** To date, no definitive archaeological remains of the gardens have been found in Babylon, fueling the theory that they existed elsewhere.
