# BATTLES OF THERMOPYLÆ AND ARTEMISIUM

The battles of Thermopylæ and Artemisium were coordinated Greek efforts to defend the heart of Greece against the invading forces of Xerxes. These engagements involved a combined land force and fleet positioned to block the advance of the Persian army and navy.

## Strategic Planning and Deployment
The Greek army and fleet moved to occupy Thermopylæ and Artemisium after the arrival of Xerxes at Therma became known at the Isthmus. This movement followed a period of six weeks to two months during which the Greeks had retreated from Tempê. During this time, the northerly states of Greece were successively falling away from the common cause. In response, deputies assembled at the Isthmus took a solemn engagement to punish these "recusant brethren" by titithing their property and perhaps a tenth of their persons for the Delphian god, provided they had not been driven to yield by irresistible necessity.

The land force at the pass of Thermopylæ was conducted by Leonidas, king of Sparta. This force included Phocians and Lokrians. Despite the imminent danger, the Greeks did not postpone the Olympian and Karneian festivals. Upon reaching the spot, Leonidas was informed of a path over Mount Œta that could be used to evade the pass; the Phocians engaged to defend this mountain path. Simultaneously, the combined Greek fleet under Eurybiadês occupied the Eubœan strait at Artemisium.

## The Naval Engagement at Artemisium
Artemisium is a promontory of the island of Eubœa, stretching northwards beyond Hestiaea, opposite to Olizon. The Greek fleet initially suffered a setback when three triremes sent as scouts were captured by the Persian fleet, causing the general Greek fleet to panic and retire to Chalkis. However, a destructive storm and hurricane on the coast of Magnesia inflicted immense damage on the Persian fleet, encouraging the Greeks to return from Chalkis to Artemisium.

Upon their return, the Greek commanders were alarmed to see the Persian fleet still mustering in overwhelming numbers at the opposite station of Aphetæ. The Greeks initially resolved to retire again, but the inhabitants of Eubœa entreated Eurybiadês to maintain the position so they could remove their families and property. The fleet was ultimately persuaded to stay after the Eubœans sent an envoy, Pelagon, to Themistoklês with an offer of thirty talents. Themistoklês used this money to secure the cooperation of the chiefs, including the Corinthian Adeimantus, who received three talents to silence his alarm.

Under the inspiration of Themistoklês, the Greeks eventually offered battle to the main Persian fleet near Aphetæ. Waiting until late afternoon, the Greeks marshalled their ships in a circle with prows facing outward. On a signal, they rowed out in direct impact, disabling or taking thirty hostile ships and capturing Philaon, brother of Gorgus of Salamis in Cyprus. Although the Persians rallied and inflicted considerable damage, the approach of night ended the combat.

## The Land Battle at Thermopylæ
Xerxes arrived with his army in Malian territory near the pass of Thermopylæ. Initial Persian attacks, led by Median troops and subsequently by the best troops in the Persian army, were all repulsed with slaughter. Xerxes' embarrassment was relieved when he learned of the mountain path over Mount Œta. A Persian detachment under Hydarnês marched over this path, drove away the Phocian guard, and arrived in the rear of Leonidas.

Following the fall of Leonidas and the Persian possession of the passes, the Greek fleet at Artemisium retired further into Greece. The Athenians protected the rear during this retreat. As they passed various harbors and landing-places, Themistoklês carved inscriptions on stones beseeching the Ionians to join the Athenians in the fight for liberty or, if that were impossible, to throw the barbarian army into confusion during battle.

## Aftermath and Legacy
The defeat at Thermopylæ caused disappointment and terror among contemporary Greeks. The Spartans and Peloponnesians had been so confident in the defensibility of the positions that no soldiers had been put in motion before the news of the disaster arrived. Consequently, the invading force progressed toward Attica and Peloponnesus without a concerted Greek plan for defense.

Xerxes' losses at Thermopylæ were compensated by new Grecian auxiliaries, including the Malians, Lokrians, Dorians, and the majority of the Bœotians, including the town of Thebes. The Thespians abandoned their city and fled to the Peloponnesus, while the Platæans, who had served on Athenian ships at Artemisium, disembarked at Chalkis to remove their families.

Despite the tactical defeat, later generations of Greeks viewed the battle of Thermopylæ with admiration for the patriotism and courage of Leonidas and his band. Similarly, the indecisive battles at Artemisium were valued for the experience they provided, teaching the Greeks that "multitudes of ships and splendid ensigns" were ineffective against men who fought hand to hand.
