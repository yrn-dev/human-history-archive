# The Persian Gulf and Surrounding Regions

The Persian Gulf is a significant maritime feature of south-western Asia, serving as a boundary for extensive plateaus and a conduit for ancient trade and military expeditions. Its geography is closely linked to the surrounding highlands, the Mesopotamian plains, and the river systems of the Indus and the Tigris.

## Geography and Topography
The Persian Gulf forms part of the southern border of an extensive plateau in Asia located west of British India (excluding Syria and Arabia). This high region is nearly conterminous with Iran (or Persia), Afghanistan, Baluchistan, Armenia, and the provinces of Asia Minor. The southern and south-western face of this table-land follows the coast closely up the Persian Gulf, beginning from the mouth of the Indus.

Further west, the geography is defined by a mountain scarp that rises in many points to 10,000 feet. This scarp flanks the Mesopotamian plains and the Tigris, extending through Armenia and Kurdistan nearly to the 40th meridian before turning along the Taurus range and the north-eastern angle of the Mediterranean. To the north, the high land follows nearly 36 deg. N. to the southern shore of the Caspian Sea.

The region surrounding the shores of the Persian Gulf, including south Persia, Afghanistan, and Baluchistan, is characterized as a rainless region. Its flora is continuous with the desert flora found in northern and eastern Africa, extending from the coast of Senegal to the 75 deg. E. meridian.

## Ancient Trade and Cultural Exchange
The Persian Gulf served as a vital route for early international commerce. Evidence indicates that before 750 B.C., an export trade existed between India and Babylon via the Persian Gulf. This maritime route was instrumental in the cultural development of India, as traders used it to bring the legend of the Flood, the art of brick-making, and the Brahmi alphabet to the region.

## Military Expeditions and Exploration
The Persian Gulf and the Indus river system were central to the expansions of the Persian and Macedonian empires.

### The Persian Empire
Under Darius, the Persian Empire extended to the "Indians," whom Herodotus described as the easternmost inhabitants of the earth. Darius is said to have subdued them and commissioned a Greek from Karyanda named Skylax to lead a nautical enterprise. Skylax reportedly commanded vessels from the upper regions of the Indus—specifically the territory of the Paktyes and the city of Kaspatyri—down to the river's mouth, then into the Indian Ocean, around the Arabian peninsula, and up the Red Sea to Egypt.

### The Macedonian Conquest
Alexander the Great later navigated these regions during his six-year tour. After fighting a battle against King Porus on the Indus, Alexander built a fleet and descended to the mouth of the Indus. He then divided his forces, leading the main army back to the Persian Gulf along a desolate coast. During this march, his troops suffered greatly from thirst. The fleet followed by sea and rejoined the army at the entrance to the Persian Gulf. This route from the Indus to the Persian Gulf was less extensive than the voyage attributed to Skylax.

## Regional Hydrography
The Persian Gulf is the destination for several major water systems. The Tigris and Euphrates rivers, which define the region of Mesopotamia (meaning "mid rivers"), are believed to have flowed separately into the Persian Gulf in early times. 

To the east, the Indus river serves as the chief river of western India. It flows south in a tortuous course and enters the Arabian Sea through several mouths, including the Khori, Sir, Bhitiara, Wari, Mall, Vatho, Hajamro, Sisa, Pitiani, Khudi, and Ghizri creeks. The Indus is navigable by native boats throughout certain stretches, such as the 120 m. course within the District of Dera Ismail Khan.
