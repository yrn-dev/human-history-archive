# Aulos and Related Musical Instruments

The term *aulos* refers to a pipe, and it serves as a linguistic root for several instruments and terms related to wind music and bag-pipes in Greek and other cultures.

## The Askaules and the Bag-pipe
The term *askaules* (derived from the Greek *askos*, meaning bag, and *aulos*, meaning pipe) is likely the Greek word for a bag-piper. While there is no documentary authority for its use in classical Greek authors, J.J. Reiske identifies it as such in a note regarding Dio Chrysostom. Dio Chrysostom, who flourished around A.D. 100, provides a description of a musician skilled in playing a pipe with his mouth using a bag placed under his arm-pits. 

Further corroboration is found in Martial, who uses the term *ascaules* for the Latin *utricularius*. Despite its existence, the bag-pipe does not appear in the lists of pipes compiled by Athenaeus or Pollux, suggesting that among highly cultured Greeks, the instrument remained a rustic tool used primarily by peasants and shepherds. Evidence from Dion Chrysostom confirms the instrument was known in Greece by A.D. 100. It is believed the Greeks may have received the bag-pipe from Chaldaea or Egypt, where it existed in the form of the *as-it*.

## The Sump[=o]ny[=a] and Linguistic Debates
In the book of Daniel (iii. 5 and 15), a musical instrument is mentioned as *sump[=o]ny[=a]*. While some scholars suggest this refers to a bag-pipe, arguing that the Aramaic word is a loan-word from the Greek *sumphonia*, this argument is considered weak. The original meaning of *sumphonia* was "a concord of sound" or "a concordant interval," and evidence of its use to describe a specific instrument only appears slightly in the 2nd century B.C.

Additionally, the instrument is referred to as *sipp[=o]ny[=a]* in verse 10, which suggests a derivation from the Greek *siphon* (meaning pipe or tube). Interestingly, in the middle ages, the bag-pipe was known in Spain and Italy—regions through which Eastern culture entered Europe—as *zampogna* or *sampogna*, terms that strongly recall the Chaldaean *sump[=o]ny[=a]*. In these same countries, the word *sinfonia* coexisted with *zampogna*, retaining the classical meaning of "a concord of sound."

## The Bombulum and the Fistula Hieronimi
The *bombulum* (also *bumbulum* or *bunibulum*) is a fabulous instrument described in an apocryphal letter from St Jerome to Dardanus. It is illustrated in various illuminated manuscripts from the 9th to the 11th centuries, including the *Psalter of Emmeran*, the *Psalter of Angers*, the *Boulogne Psalter*, and the Cotton MS. *Tiberius C. VI.*

In the Cotton MS., the instrument features an angular frame with a rectangular metal plate suspended by a chain. This plate has twelve bent arms ending in small plates or bells, intended to be shaken like a sistrum. Sebastian Virdung, who calls the instrument *Fistula Hieronimi*, provides illustrations where the metal arms are replaced by twelve bent pipes with a conical bore. According to the apocryphal letter, the frame represents the Holy Cross, the dangling object represents Christ on the Cross, and the twelve pipes represent the twelve apostles. There is no evidence that the *bombulum* actually existed during the middle ages; it is known only through these fanciful illustrations and hearsay. The word *bombulum* likely derives from the same root as *bombaulios*, a comic compound used by Aristophanes combining *bombos* and *aulos*, playing on the word *bombulios* (a humming or buzzing insect).

## The Bullroarer and Mystic Significance
While not an *aulos*, the bullroarer is another instrument characterized by a humming sound. It consists of a small flat slip of wood with a string passed through a hole in one end; when swung rapidly, it produces a booming, humming noise. While Europeans treat it as a toy, it holds high sanctity and mystic significance among primitive peoples.

In Australia, the "blackfellows" use two types of sacred bullroarers, or "tunduns": the "man tundun" (grandfather), which has a deep tone, and the "woman tundun," which is smaller and shriller. These are used in Bora initiation ceremonies, where the hum is believed to be the voice of the "Great Spirit." In Africa, specifically among the Egba tribe of the Yoruba race, the bullroarer produces the "Voice of Oro," their god of vengeance, and is worshipped as the god himself.

The bullroarer's use is widespread, appearing among:
*   The Greeks, where a similar instrument called the *rhombos* was whirled at the Greek mysteries.
*   North American Indians, including the Apache, Navaho, Tusayan, and Moqui (where a priest leads dancers by whirling the instrument).
*   The Koskimo of British Columbia and populations in Central Brazil.
*   New Guinea and the Torres Straits (used as a fishing-charm).
*   Ceylon (used at Buddhist festivals) and Sumatra (used to induce demons to carry off a woman's soul).
