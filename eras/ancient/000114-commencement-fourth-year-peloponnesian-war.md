# The Fourth Year of the Peloponnesian War

The fourth year of the Peloponnesian War was characterized by the continued struggle between Athens and the Peloponnesian forces, marked by the lingering effects of a devastating epidemic in Athens and the resumption of land invasions in Attica.

## The State of Athens and the Epidemic
The second and third years of the war had been periods of immense suffering for the Athenians due to a continuing epidemic. This calamity did not materially relax until the winter of the third year (B.C. 429-428). The pressure of this plague enfeebled Athenian military efforts, although the victories achieved by Phormio had simultaneously raised the city's maritime reputation to an unprecedented height.

While the disorder of the epidemic was suspended during the fourth year of the war, its destructive effects were still felt. These lingering consequences provided the Peloponnesians with both material assistance and the encouragement necessary to persevere in their campaign.

## Peloponnesian Invasions of Attica
Under the leadership of Archidamus, the Peloponnesians repeated their invasion and ravage of Attica during the fourth year, an action that had been intermitted during the preceding year. The invaders entered the country around the beginning of May and continued the process of devastation until their provisions were exhausted. The Athenians, having likely become accustomed to such damage, offered no serious resistance to these incursions.

The invasion of Attica eventually became an ordinary annual enterprise for the Lacedæmonians, occurring every year of the war with the exception of the third and sixth years, which were omitted due to accidental causes. For instance, in the seventh year, King Agis conducted the Peloponnesian army into the territory around the end of April to repeat the usual ravages, though the hopes associated with these invasions had diminished since the start of the war.

## The Revolt of Mitylênê
While the Peloponnesian invaders were still within their borders, the Athenians received intelligence of a more formidable and embarrassing event: the revolt of Mitylênê and the majority of Lesbos.

## Lacedæmonian Peace Negotiations and Pleistoanax
Following the capture of Sphakteria, the Lacedæmonians attempted to negotiate for peace and the recovery of prisoners, either directly or indirectly. These pacific inclinations were strongly instigated by King Pleistoanax, who had a personal motive to end the conflict. 

Pleistoanax had been banished from Sparta fourteen years before the war began, shortly before the thirty years' truce, on charges of taking bribes from the Athenians during an invasion of Attica. For over eighteen years, he lived in exile near the temple of Zeus Lykæus in Arcadia, keeping his home half within consecrated ground due to his constant fear of the Lacedæmonians.

His restoration was pursued through the Pythian priestess at Delphi, whom he and his brother Aristoklês paid. The priestess repeatedly informed every sacred legation from Sparta that they must bring back the "seed of (Hêraklês) the demi-god son of Zeus" from a foreign land, warning that failure to do so would result in their fate to "plough with a silver ploughshare." This divine command, combined with the influence of supporters at home, eventually changed Spartan sentiment. In the fourth or fifth year of the war, Pleistoanax was recalled and welcomed with unbounded honors, including sacrifices and choric shows similar to those offered to the primitive kings during Sparta's first settlement.
