# Inscriptions

## Definition and Study
Epigraphy is the term used to describe the collective study of inscriptions, as well as the science involved in their classification and explanation. In a more restricted sense, the term may refer to palaeography as it applies to inscriptions. Generally, epigraphy is a branch of archaeology that focuses on inscriptions engraved on permanent materials such as metal or stone. This field of study is distinct from numismatics, which deals with coins.

## Ancient Greek and Roman Inscriptions
In Greece, inscriptions have served various purposes, ranging from the recording of government decrees to the blending of legend and history. The Parian marble is an example of an inscription where gods, heroes, and men are blended across successive epochs. Other registers, such as those at Sikyôn, claimed to record the inventor of harp-music, Amphion, son of Antiopê and Zeus. Similarly, the Halikarnassian Inscription lists a series of priests of Poseidôn from the city's foundation, and the Catalogue of Priestesses of Hêrê at Argos dates back to fabulous times.

Inscriptions have also provided economic data regarding ancient Greece. A decree from the Korkyræan government, dating to approximately the second or third century B.C., details the management of funds for Dionysiac festivals, prescribing a loan rate of 2 per cent per month (24 per cent per annum). In contrast, the Theræan Inscription containing the Testament of Epiktêta calculates an annual sum at a more moderate rate of 7 per cent.

In other regions, such as Corinth, inscriptions before the Roman era were largely absent, though two in the Old Corinthian alphabet were discovered. One late-date inscription found on a marble block used as a lintel reads [Greek: AGOGEEBR], identified as *synagoge Hebraion* (synagogue of the Hebrews). In Britain, Roman inscriptions have been collected in works such as the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latin.* and supplements in *Ephemeris epigraphica*.

## Cuneiform and Near Eastern Inscriptions
The science of Assyriology was founded through the work of Sir Henry Rawlinson, who between 1835 and 1845 climbed a difficult cliff to copy and decipher cuneiform inscriptions. While Diodorus attributed the sculptures and inscriptions of this site to Semiramis, other fragments found at the foot of the rock include an Arabic inscription and a Greek inscription belonging to the Parthian prince Gotarzes from A.D. 40.

## Asian and Central Asian Inscriptions
In Japan, the oldest extant ideographic inscription is carved on a stone in Iyo province, dating from A.D. 596. Another early inscription from A.D. 607 is found on the back of an image of Yakushi at the Horyu-ji temple.

In the Russian dominions, silver Paizah (tablets) have been discovered, including one near the Dnieper and two in Eastern Siberia. A tablet found in 1846 in the Minusinsk circle of the Government of Yenisei features Chinese characters on the reverse ring meaning “Publication No. 42.” The main inscription is written in the Mongol language using the Baspa character. Its text reads: “*By the strength of the eternal heaven! May the name of the Khagan be holy! Who pays him not reverence is to be slain, and must die!*” Other discovered plates contain essentially similar meanings, some written in the Uighúr character.

## Literary and Epitaphic Inscriptions
Inscriptions are also featured in literary accounts. Plutarch records an inscription written by Timon the misanthropist during his lifetime: "Here from the load of life released I lie: Ask not my name: but take my curse, and die." However, another version attributed to Callimachus states: "Timon misanthropist I am. Away! Curse, an' thou will't, but only do not stay."
