# Baal

The term Baal is a Semitic word primarily signifying "lord," "owner," or "inhabitant." Within the context of Semitic family and religious relations, the term was used to express the relationship between a husband and wife, as well as the relationship between a deity and a worshipper. In a religious sense, it did not necessarily imply that the god was the lord of the worshipper, but rather the ruler of, or possessor in, a specific district or place.

## Evolution of the Deity
Originally, numerous baals existed as local gods associated with earthly objects, such as springs and streams that fertilized fields. Over time, these local deities became identified with the common source of all streams, leading to a development where the various baals were regarded as forms of one absolute deity. This transition from earthly associations to heavenly or astral beings is considered characteristic of a higher type of belief and occurred relatively later. Consequently, the Baal could be identified with supreme powers of nature, including the sun, the weather, the heavens, or specific planets.

## Regional Manifestations
The manifestation of Baal varied across different cultures and eras:

*   **Hittites and Phoenicians:** A specific Baal of the heavens was known among the Hittites during the time of Rameses II. By the beginning of the 7th century, this was a title for one of the gods of Phoenicia.
*   **Babylonia:** In Babylonia, Baal became a definite individual deity from a very early period and was identified with the planet Jupiter.
*   **Egypt:** During the XIXth Dynasty, Baal and Astarte were venerated in Memphis and Thebes.
*   **Greece:** Through the influence of the Aramaeans, who used the Babylonian spelling *Bel*, Baal eventually became known to the Greeks as Belos, who was identified with Zeus.
*   **Tyre:** The Tyrian Baal, also called Melkart ("king of the city"), was often identified with the Olympian Zeus or the Greek Heracles. He possessed a magnificent temple in insular Tyre founded by Hiram. This deity exhibited solar characteristics, notably in an annual feast of his awakening shortly after the winter solstice. The Tyrian Baal was also associated with symbolical pillars of gold and smaragdus.

## Compound Names and Specific Forms
The name Baal frequently appears in compounds with geographical terms or descriptive epithets:

*   **Geographical Compounds:** Examples include the Baal of Tyre, the Baal of Lebanon, and Baal-berith (or El-berith) of Shechem, often interpreted as the God of the covenant. Other examples include Baal Hazor and Baal Hermon.
*   **Descriptive Compounds:** Baalgad is identified as the Baal of Fortune. Baalzebub (or Beelzebub), the god of the Philistine city Ekron, is often identified with the Hebrew word for flies (*zebub*), making him the "Baal to whom flies belong." This association links him to the warmth of the sun. Some have suggested he was the dung-beetle worshipped in Egypt, while others view the name as a contemptuous modification of Baalzebul.

## Cult Practices and the Israelites
Among the Israelites, the title Baal was sometimes applied to Yahweh, as seen in names such as Jerubbaal (Gideon), Eshbaal, and Beeliada. However, when the name became exclusively associated with idolatrous worship, it was viewed with abhorrence; in compound names, the word *bosheth* ("shameful thing") was substituted for *baal*, resulting in names like Ishbosheth and Mephibosheth.

The cult of Baal, often paired with the goddess Astarte (the great female principle), involved various practices denounced by Israelite prophets. These included:
*   The burning of incense and the preparation of sacred mystic cakes.
*   Human sacrifice and violent, ecstatic exercises.
*   Ceremonial acts of bowing and kissing.
*   Licentiousness practiced "under every green tree" on the summits of hills and mountains to secure crop abundance.
*   Dionysiac orgies, as exemplified by the cult of Baal Peor.

## Linguistic Variations
Beyond its use as a deity's name, *ba'al* functioned as a noun of relation in various contexts. For example, a *ba'al* of hair refers to a hairy man, a *ba'al* of wings to a winged creature, and in the plural, *ba'al* of arrows refers to archers.
