# SICILIAN AFFAIRS AFTER THE DESTRUCTION OF THE ATHENIAN ARMAMENT

## The Destruction of the Athenian Armament
In 413 B.C., the Athenian armament in Sicily was annihilated. This followed a period of intense conflict at Syracuse, characterized by the efforts of Gylippus to organize the Syracusans and the arrival of a second Athenian armament under Demosthenes. Despite initial successes and the imposing entry of Demosthenes into the Great Harbor, the Athenians suffered a ruinous defeat following a failed nocturnal march to surprise Epipolæ. The subsequent retreat, which had been postponed due to an eclipse of the moon and the infatuation of the general Nikias, resulted in a lamentable fate where Nikias, Demosthenes, and nearly their entire armament perished.

## Immediate Political Aftermath
The annihilation of the Athenian force had immediate repercussions beyond the battlefield. At Thurii, a colony on the Tarentine Gulf, the defeat quickened the energies of an anti-Athenian faction. This led to the accusation of "Atticizing" against Lysias, his elder brother Polemarchus, and three hundred other individuals. Consequently, in 412 B.C., these persons were driven from Thurii and settled at Athens.

## Carthaginian Intervention in Sicily
The destruction of the Athenian armament had a significant psychological impact on Carthage, leaving Syracusan courage in high renown. However, this also rekindled a desire for revenge among certain Carthaginian leaders, specifically Hannibal, the grandson of Hamilkar. Hannibal, serving as king or first of the two suffetes (chief executive magistrates), harbored an intense antipathy toward the Greek name due to the historical dishonor of the Gelonian victory at Himera.

When the town of Egesta—a semi-Hellenic or non-Hellenic settlement near Carthaginian sites such as Panormus, Motyê, and Soloeis—sought aid from Carthage to avoid being subjugated by Selinus, the Carthaginians debated the request. They feared that allowing Selinus to aggrandize itself would endanger their own Sicilian settlements. Despite hesitations regarding the resumption of military operations in Sicily after a seventy-year hiatus, the Carthaginians agreed to grant the aid solicited by Egesta.

## Later Sicilian Governance and Conflict
Following these events, Dionysius the Elder emerged as the despot at Syracuse. His reign included a major war against the Carthaginians. During this conflict, Dionysius found himself hard-pressed and besieged; however, a sudden turn of fortune occurred when the victorious Carthaginian army stationed before Syracuse was utterly ruined by a terrible pestilence. This disaster was further compounded by the ignominious treason of the Carthaginian commander, Imilkon.

## Broader Hellenic Context
The events in Sicily occurred against a backdrop of general instability in Greece. Between 404-403 B.C. and 360-359 B.C., Greece became more disunited and destitute of presiding Hellenic authority than at any time since the Persian invasion. While Athens eventually recovered a considerable maritime power compared to its degradation in 403 B.C., its authority remained unsubstantial, lacking the imperial energy of the era of Perikles. This general state of weakness among Thebes, Sparta, and Athens created a vulnerable environment that preceded the rise of the aggressor from Macedonia.
