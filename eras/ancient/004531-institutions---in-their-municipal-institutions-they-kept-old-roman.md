# Institutions

The term "institutions" encompasses a wide array of municipal, educational, charitable, and legal structures across various global cultures and historical periods. These entities range from self-governing regional assemblies and municipal corporations to specialized medical and penal facilities.

## Municipal and Local Government

Municipal institutions have frequently drawn upon Roman legal concepts. In the Basque provinces, the Roman term *respublica* was retained to describe the *civitas* and its associated territory. Basque municipal officers were elective, utilizing a variety of methods ranging from nomination by a single person chosen by lot to universal suffrage. These municipalities sent deputies to provincial parliaments or juntas, which originally met in the open air under copses (such as the Bilzaar in the French Pays Basque) or under trees, most notably the oak of Guernica in Biscay. The cortes of Navarre met at Pamplona, and delegates from the juntas convened annually to address the common interests of the three provinces.

This system of self-government, which included administrative, judicial, and legislative functions, resulted in the *fueros* (or *fors*) of each province and the *cartas-pueblos* of the towns. While often appearing as grants from a sovereign, these were essentially codifications of ancient unwritten customary laws. The kings of Castile, Spain, and Navarre were required to swear to observe these *fueros* upon their accession. Consequently, the Basque provinces functioned as a self-governing republic (*una verdadera autonomia*) and a "land apart" (*una tierra apartada*), maintaining their independence even during periods of absolutism in France and Spain.

In other regions, municipal structures varied. In Bombay, the municipal government was framed by an 1888 act of the Bombay legislative council, consisting of a town council and a municipal corporation. The corporation comprised 72 members, including 16 government nominees, 36 elected by ratepayers, 16 elected by justices of the peace, and four elected by the senate of the university and the chamber of commerce. In Japan, under the Tokugawa and Toyotomi organizations, the *machi-bugyo* (municipal administrator) controlled general citizen affairs, primarily focusing on judicial business. The *machi-bugyo* operated a law-court from his residence and directed constables (*yoriki* or *doshiri*) to patrol the city.

In England, the municipal borough of Bridlington is governed by a mayor, 18 councillors, and six aldermen. Additionally, English law borrowed heavily from Roman jurisprudence regarding corporations. The Roman conception of a corporation was preserved by municipal and ecclesiastical bodies, influencing the development of English corporation law.

## Educational Institutions

Educational institutions often evolved from private or society-led initiatives into municipal entities. For example, the Municipal School of Art was formed in 1885 through the transference of a society of arts and an existing school of art to the corporation. Similarly, the Municipal Technical School was established in 1893 within the building of the Midland Institute before moving to its own facility in 1895.

In Bombay, key educational institutions include the government law school, the government Elphinstone College, the Grant medical college, the Victoria Jubilee technical institute, the Sir Jamsetjee Jeejeebhoy school of art, and two missionary colleges, St Xavier and Wilson. In Belgium, monastic and conventual houses are significantly engaged in educational work.

## Charitable and Medical Institutions

Charitable institutions provide a wide range of specialized services. In one city, these include the Royal Infirmary, the Royal Victoria hospital for incurables (opened 1889), and institutions for the blind, the deaf and dumb, and the Royal asylum. Other facilities include the Baldovan asylum for imbeciles (founded 1854), the Royal Orphan, the Wellburn Charitable institutions, and a rescue home for females.

In Louisiana, the State Board of Charities and Correction (organized under a 1904 act) inspects public and private charitable, correctional, and reformatory institutions. Charitable facilities in this region include the Touro Infirmary, a Hôtel Dieu, and two charity hospitals located in Shreveport and New Orleans. Specialized care is provided at the Institute for the Blind and the Institute for the Deaf and Dumb in Baton Rouge, though the latter is for whites only.

## Penal and Corrective Institutions

Penal institutions vary by jurisdiction and oversight. In Louisiana, the State Penitentiary is located at Baton Rouge, with a House of Detention in New Orleans and various parish prisons. State convicts are supervised by a Board of Control appointed by the governor, which may commute sentences for exemplary conduct. While the constitution prohibits the leasing of state convicts, parish convicts may be leased for public undertakings, such as work on levees, roads, and factories.

## Religious and Benevolent Institutions

In Belgium, the state provides financial aid to religious and benevolent institutions. In 1904, the government voted nearly 7,000,000 francs to aid the benevolent institutions and religious establishments of the Roman Church, while other cults received 118,000 francs. The Roman Catholic hierarchy in Belgium includes the archbishop of Malines and the bishops of Namur, Tournai, Bruges, Ghent, and Liege.
