# Fifth Year of the War: Trial and Execution of the Plataeans and Corcyraean Conflict

## The Fall and Trial of the Plataeans
During the fifth year of the war, the city of Plataea, which had been an ally of Athens for ninety-three years, reached a state of extreme weakness. Unable to support a siege and lacking provisions, the Plataeans were forced to surrender to the Peloponnesians after an assault on their walls that they could not repel. 

The Lacedaemonian commander sought to avoid taking the city by storm. His instructions from Lacedaemon were intended to ensure that if a future peace treaty with Athens required the restoration of captured places, Plataea would be viewed as having surrendered voluntarily and thus would not be included in such a list. A herald was sent to the Plataeans asking if they would voluntarily surrender the town to the Lacedaemonians and accept them as judges, with the condition that the guilty be punished according to the law. The Plataeans agreed to these terms.

Upon the arrival of five Lacedaemonian judges, no formal charges were preferred. Instead, the judges asked the Plataeans whether they had provided any service to the Lacedaemonians and their allies during the current war. The Plataeans deputed two representatives, Lacon, son of Aeimnestus (a proxenus of the Lacedaemonians), and Astymachus, son of Asopolaus, to speak on their behalf.

## Execution and Destruction of Plataea
Following the testimony of the Thebans, the Lacedaemonian judges ruled that the question regarding service to the allies was a fair one. The judges noted that the Plataeans had been repeatedly invited to remain neutral, in accordance with the original covenant of Pausanias following the defeat of the Mede, and had been offered the same conditions again before the blockade. Because the Plataeans had refused these offers, the judges considered themselves released from their covenant.

The Lacedaemonians brought the Plataeans forward one by one, asking each if they had performed any service for the Lacedaemonians and their allies. When the individuals answered that they had not, they were taken out and executed without exception. This massacre claimed not less than two hundred Plataeans and twenty-five Athenians who had participated in the siege. The women of the city were taken as slaves.

The Thebans initially gave the city to surviving Plataeans of their own party and political emigrants from Megara for approximately one year. Subsequently, the city was razed to its foundations. The Thebans used the roofs and doors of the Plataeans to build a two-hundred-foot square inn with rooms above and below on the precinct of Hera. They also used the iron and brass from the city walls to create couches dedicated to Hera, for whom they built a stone chapel measuring one hundred feet square. The land was confiscated and leased to Theban occupiers for ten years. The Lacedaemonians' cooperation in these events was primarily intended to please the Thebans, who were considered useful in the ongoing war.

## The Corcyraean Conflict and Revolution
Corcyra, a member of the Athenian confederacy, became embroiled in a conflict with Corinth. Exasperated by the war, Corinth spent two years building an efficient fleet and recruiting rowers from the Peloponnese and the rest of Hellas using large bounties. Alarmed and lacking other allies in Hellas, the Corcyraeans sought an alliance with Athens. Corinth sent an embassy to Athens to prevent the Corcyraean and Athenian navies from joining forces.

During the conflict, the Corinthians took Anactorium—a place shared by the Corcyraeans and Corinthians—through treachery. They established Corinthian settlers there, sold eight hundred Corcyraeans into slavery, and kept two hundred and fifty high-ranking men in captivity, hoping they would eventually persuade Corcyra to side with Corinth.

Following a naval engagement, the Corcyraeans emerged as masters of the sea in that region. They executed all their captives except for the Corinthians, whom they held as prisoners of war. The Corcyraeans ravaged the territory of Leucas (a Corinthian colony) and burnt Cyllene, the harbour of the Eleans, because the Eleans had provided money and ships to Corinth. By the end of the summer, the two parties remained in a stalemate, with Corinthian forces encamped at Actium and Chimerium, and Corcyraean forces stationed at Leukimme.

## Peloponnesian Intervention in Corcyra
During a revolution in Corcyra, fifty-three Peloponnesian ships under the command of Alcidas, with Brasidas as an adviser, arrived from Cyllene. They anchored at Sybota before sailing for Corcyra. The Corcyraeans, in a state of confusion and alarm, equipped sixty vessels and sent them out against the enemy. 

Despite Athenian recommendations to wait and sail as a unified fleet, the Corcyraeans attacked in a straggling fashion. This lack of order led to internal fighting among the crews, and two ships deserted. The Peloponnesians took advantage of this chaos, deploying twenty ships to oppose the Corcyraeans and the remainder against twelve Athenian ships, including the vessels *Paralus* and *Salaminia*.
