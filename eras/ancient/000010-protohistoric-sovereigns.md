# The Protohistoric Sovereigns

The protohistoric period of Japan is marked by the emergence of early sovereigns and the development of national administration, foreign relations, and social structures. This era begins with the 17th Sovereign, Richu, who reigned from A.D. 400 to 405.

## The Lineage of Sovereigns
The succession of sovereigns during the protohistoric era is documented through a series of rulers. Following Richu (A.D. 400–405), the throne was held by Hansho (18th Sovereign, A.D. 406–411), Inkyo (19th Sovereign, A.D. 412–453), and Anko (20th Sovereign, A.D. 454–456). Later records list the following sovereigns:
*   Seinei (22nd): A.D. 480–484
*   Kenso (23rd): A.D. 485–487
*   Ninken (24th): A.D. 488–498
*   Muretsu (25th): A.D. 499–506
*   Keitai (26th): A.D. 507–531
*   Ankan (27th): A.D. 534–535
*   Senkwa (28th): A.D. 536–539

## Administrative Power and the Uji
During the protohistoric period, administrative power was often shared between the sovereign and influential families known as *uji*. 

The Soga-uji, founded by Soga no Ishikawa, significantly affected early Japanese history. His son, Machi, shared administrative power with Heguri no Tsuku during the reign of Richu. Later, Iname, the great-grandson of Machi, promoted the introduction of Buddhism during the reign of Kimmei (A.D. 540–571).

The Heguri-uji was also prominent; its founder, Heguri no Tsuku, administered alongside Soga no Machi under Richu. Heguri no Matori served as a minister under Yuryaku (A.D. 457–459). Other notable families included the Ki-uji, whose representatives served as commanders-in-chief, provincial governors, and managers of foreign affairs, and the Katsuragi-uji. The ancestor of the Katsuragi-uji, Katsuragi no Sotsu, had a daughter, Iwa, who became Empress to Nintoku. Another descendant, Hae, was the mother of sovereigns Kenso and Ninken.

## Life and Culture
The lives of early sovereigns were characterized by a contrast in their dwellings. While many residences are described as *muro* (dwellings partially underground), there are references to high towers. For example, Emperor Nintoku is described as ascending a lofty tower to observe the poverty of his people, and Yuryaku ordered the construction of a lofty pavilion to assume Imperial dignity.

Cultural activities included dancing, which was a foremost pastime. In protohistoric times, the consort of Inkyo was involved in an offer involving dance that wrecked her happiness. Additionally, the future Emperor Kenso is recorded as having danced while disguised as a horse-boy. Other activities of the period included football.

## Foreign Relations and National Development
The protohistoric era is viewed by modern Japanese historians as the first period of national development, largely due to overseas relations. Japan formed an alliance with Paikche (referred to in Japan as "Kudara," the region around modern Seoul) and established controlling power over the territory of Imna (or Mimana) for several centuries.

To manage these territories, Japan established the *uchi-tsu-miyake* office. Following the custom of including acquired lands in the Imperial domain, these estates were managed at a *miyake* presided over by a *mikoto-mochi*. A *mikoto-mochi* was stationed in Shiragi, and during the reign of Ojin (the son of Jingo), the statesman Takenouchi-no-Sukune resided in Tsukushi to assist the *chinju-fu* and the *mikoto-mochi*.

These relations led to the import of gold, silver, iron, implements, literature, arts, and silk and cotton fabrics of improved quality, which benefited the civilization.

## Governance and Law
The protohistoric period saw the early application of criminal law and the organization of religion, with the Emperor positioned at the head of religious rites. Finance and administration were also established during this time.
