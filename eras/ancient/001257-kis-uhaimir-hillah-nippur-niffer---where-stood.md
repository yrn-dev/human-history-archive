# Kis and Nippur

## Geographic Context
Kis and Nippur were ancient cities located eastward of the Euphrates and southward of Sippara, Kutha, and Babylon. Kis is identified with the modern site of Uhaimir, situated nine miles east of Hillah. Nippur is associated with the modern site of Niffer.

## Nippur: The Sanctuary of El-lil
Nippur served as one of the two primary centers of early Babylonian culture, representing the northern center in contrast to Eridu in the south. The city grew up around the sanctuary of El-lil (also written as En-lil), who was the patron deity of Nippur. El-lil was regarded as the "lord of the ghost-land," and his influence was associated with spells and incantations used to command spirits of both good and evil. The cosmology associated with El-lil envisioned a world governed as a mountain, where created beings lived underground.

The chief temple of El-lil in Nippur was known as E-Kur, meaning "mountain house." This edifice was of such great sanctity that Assyrian and Babylonian rulers continued to restore and embellish it into the latest days of their civilizations. The name E-Kur eventually became a general designation for a temple. The sanctuary was a sacred precinct comprising the main temple and various surrounding chapels and temples dedicated to the gods and goddesses of El-lil's court. The "mountain house" may have originally referred to a staged tower built to imitate a mountain, with the sacred shrine located at the top; this tower was specifically designated as "Im-Khar-sag."

## Religious Significance and Deities
In the Babylonian pantheon, El-lil was identified as Bel, a title meaning "lord" or "master." He was regarded as the head of an extensive pantheon, holding titles such as "father of the gods," "king of heaven and earth," and "king of lands." 

The consort of Bel was Nin-lil (also called Belit, meaning "the lady," or Nin-Khar-sag, "Lady of the mountain"). Her titles, including "mother of the gods" and "ruler of heaven and earth," derived from her position as the wife of Bel. While she possessed her own temple in Nippur and received votive offerings and the dedication of fortresses in her name, she remained overshadowed by her consort.

## Historical and Archaeological Records
Nippur functioned as the center of a political district of considerable extent prior to 3000 B.C. Archaeological excavations conducted between 1888 and 1900 by the University of Pennsylvania, specifically under J. H. Haynes and Mr. Peters, uncovered the remains of the great temple of El-lil. These excavations involved removing layers of debris down to the virgin soil. 

A platform of large bricks discovered midway in the mound bears the stamps of Naram-Sin and Sargon of Akkad (dated to 3800 B.C.). Based on the 34 feet of debris above this pavement and the 30 feet of debris beneath it, it is calculated that the underlying debris represents a period of approximately 3000 years.

In terms of political history, Nippur appears in legal records. A contract discovered at Niffer (Nippur) marks the accession year of Assur-sum-lisir, the Rab-shakeh (vizier) of Assur-etil-ilani, as king of Assyria.

## Cultural Influence
The culture of Nippur eventually coalesced with that of Eridu. While the influence of Eridu was predominant in the resulting admixture, Nippur's influence is evident in other cities; for example, Ur is believed to have been colonized from Nippur because its moon-god was the son of El-lil of Nippur.
