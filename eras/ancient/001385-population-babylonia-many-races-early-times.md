# The Population and Civilization of Babylonia

## Ethnic Composition and Early Races
The population of Babylonia was characterized by a diversity of races from early times. The earliest civilization of the region is identified as Sumerian, developed by a race that spoke an agglutinative language. This group is credited with the foundation of the country's chief cities, the invention of pictorial hieroglyphs (which later became cuneiform characters), and the creation of essential engineering works, such as canals used to regulate river overflow and drain marshes.

Following this period, Babylonia was invaded by Semites, who eventually became the ruling race. While the exact date of their arrival in the plain of Babylonia (the Edin) is uncertain, it occurred at a remote period. The Semites, likely originating from Arabia, borrowed and adapted the cuneiform writing system and were profoundly influenced by the older Sumerian language in both vocabulary and grammar. Conversely, Sumerian also borrowed from Semitic Babylonian. Evidence suggests Semitic elements may have been present from the beginning in cities such as Ur and Eridu. In the north, specifically in Akkad, the first Semitic empire was established.

## Social Integration and Urban Life
Babylonia was a land of merchants and agriculturists, marked by incessant intercommunication between its cities. Every city hosted a large number of resident aliens, a factor that tended to assimilate customs across the region. The openness of the land made it susceptible to invasions by strange races, contrasting with the more protected geography of Egypt.

Legal protections for foreigners were established in Babylonian city laws. As late as the accession of Samas-sum-yukin and Assur-bani-pal, records indicate that groups of aliens, up to twenty at a time, were permitted to enter the city. Furthermore, foreign women who married Babylonian husbands were protected from enslavement. The legal system was so precise that even a dog entering the city could not be put to death without a trial.

## Governance and Law
Early Babylonian states grew around sanctuaries where the god was the nominal ruler and the human *patesi* (high-priest) served as viceregent. Over time, many high-priests assumed the title and functions of king, maintaining supreme secular power while remaining priests. This changed when Semitic princes unified Babylonia; the earthly king then became an incarnate god, though the tradition of the god Bel-Merodach adopting the king survived at Babylon.

The legal landscape evolved from tribal customs into city laws as Semitic tribes settled in urban centers. While various cities struggled for supremacy and demanded tribute, local cults and customs were generally respected by conquerors. A pivotal moment in the unification of the empire occurred under Khammurabi, who made Babylon his metropolis and established a uniform system of law. This Code served as a legal backbone for centuries, remaining in force through Persian, Greek, and Parthian conquests, and later influencing Mahommedan and Syro-Roman law in Mesopotamia.

## Contrast with Assyria
Babylonia and Assyria are described as sister-states with essentially different characters. While Babylonia was centered on commerce and priesthood, Assyria was an "organized camp" founded by successful generals. The Assyrian king was an autocratic general supported by a feudal nobility and, later, an elaborate bureaucracy. Unlike the Babylonian king, who remained a priest under a powerful hierarchy, the Assyrian king's palace was separate from and more sumptuous than the temples.

The Assyrian population consisted primarily of soldiers, whereas sailors belonged to Babylonia. This military specialization led to Assyria's sudden collapse during the age of Assur-bani-pal once its fighting population was drained. Although Assyria was an offshoot of Babylonia and used the same language, it maintained a distinct individuality in religion and art.

## Art, Architecture, and Culture
Babylonian culture was primarily literary. Due to a lack of stone and an abundance of clay, the Babylonians utilized brick for construction. Their temples were massive, shapeless structures supported by buttresses and equipped with drains. To decorate these buildings, they developed pilasters, columns, frescoes, and enamelled tiles, often plating walls with gold or bronze.

In art, Babylonia favored figures in the round over the bas-reliefs preferred by the Assyrians. The scarcity of stone led to a high level of perfection in gem-cutting, exemplified by seal-cylinders from the age of Sargon of Akkad. While early metallurgic specimens are rare—with the exception of the silver vase of Entemena—later periods showed great skill in working copper and creating gold jewelry, such as bracelets and ear-rings.
