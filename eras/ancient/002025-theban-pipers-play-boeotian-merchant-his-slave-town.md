# Theban and Boeotian Military and Social History

## Boeotian Military Organization and Conflict
The Boeotian forces were characterized by a structured disposition of troops during major engagements. In one instance, the Boeotian army consisted of seven thousand heavy infantry, over ten thousand light troops, one thousand horse, and five hundred targeteers. The army's organization placed the Thebans and those of their province on the right wing, with the Thebans forming twenty-five shields deep. The center was composed of the Copaeans, Coronaeans, Haliartians, and other peoples from around the lake, while the left wing consisted of the Orchomenians, Tanagraeans, and Thespians. Cavalry and light troops were positioned at the extremities of each wing.

Boeotian military history includes significant conflicts with Athens. In one engagement, the Boeotians, led by Pagondas, attacked the Athenians near Delium. While the Athenian forces initially worsted the Boeotian left—resulting in severe losses for the Thespians—the Thebans on the right wing successfully pushed back the Athenians. The battle was decided when Pagondas sent two squadrons of horse around a hill; their sudden appearance caused a panic that broke the Athenian line and led to a general flight.

## Theban Leadership and Political Influence
Thebes played a dominant role in Boeotian affairs. Epaminondas (c. 418-362 B.C.), a Theban general and statesman from a noble but impoverished family, is noted as one of the most original Greek generals. He is credited with establishing Theban predominance among the land-powers of Greece through daring innovations in heavy infantry tactics, most notably at the victory at Leuctra. 

Epaminondas's career included representing Thebes at a congress in Sparta, where he refused to surrender Boeotian cities under Theban control. He also led a host into Laconia in 370 B.C., where he ravaged the territory and liberated the Messenians, rebuilding their capital at Messene. Later, he secured Sicyon for Thebes and successfully managed the retreat of the Theban army from a difficult position in Thessaly.

## Inter-City Relations and Political Intrigue
Boeotia was frequently a site of political instability and external interference. Athenian generals, such as Demosthenes, received overtures from men within Boeotian cities who sought to introduce a democracy. These intrigues were often led by exiles, such as the Theban Ptoeodorus. Plots involved the betrayal of various locations, including the seaport town of Siphae in Thespian territory and Chaeronea, a dependency of the Boeotian Orchomenus.

The relationship between Thebes and neighboring Plataia was marked by oppression. The Plataians, seeking relief from Theban pressure, initially approached the Lacedemonians for help. The Lacedemonians declined, citing their distance, and instead advised the Plataians to seek the protection of the Athenians. This led to a conflict between the Thebans and Athenians, which was eventually reconciled by the Corinthians, who established boundaries for the land.

## Musical Instruments in Ancient Contexts
The use of pipers was a distinct feature of certain military traditions. In the Spartan army, pipers belonged to an hereditary caste. They played to maintain a slow, solemn, and equable march for the troops, providing a sharp contrast to enemies who lacked such instruments and rushed to charge with a "vehement and even furious" step. Additionally, pipers played solemnly during sacrifices of thanksgiving following victories.

Regarding the history of wind instruments, scholars have debated the nature of the *sump[=o]ny[=a]* mentioned in Daniel iii. 5 and 15. Some suggest it refers to a bag-pipe, potentially derived from the Greek *sumphonia* (meaning "concord of sound") or *siphon* (tube or pipe). While the bag-pipe was known in Greece by A.D. 100 (as noted by Dion Chrysostom) and was used by peasants and shepherds, it is believed to have been received from Chaldaea or Egypt.
