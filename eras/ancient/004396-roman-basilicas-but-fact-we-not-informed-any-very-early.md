# Roman Basilicas

The Roman basilica was a columned structure that served various secular and, later, religious purposes. While the origins of these buildings are debated, it is difficult to determine if they were derived directly from Athenian examples or mediately through later Greek cities, as there is no information regarding very early basilicas in those locations.

## Early Development and Examples
The earliest known basilica is located at Pompeii, which may date back to the 2nd century B.C. In Rome, basilicas appeared nearly at the beginning of that same century. The first Roman example was the Basilica Porcia, erected in 184 B.C. by the censor M. Porcius Cato. Because Cato had recently visited Athens and was struck by the city's beauty, it is possible the architectural style was a direct importation.

Other notable Roman examples include the Basilica Julia, the Basilica Ulpia, and the Basilica Aemilia (located north of the Roman forum). The Basilica of Maxentius differs significantly from the others, as it is an entirely vaulted structure resembling the great halls of Roman Thermae.

## Architectural Forms and Features
While there is variety among surviving structures, the "normal" form of the basilica—as described by Vitruvius—was essentially a columned structure. The majority of these buildings featured a central space surrounded by galleries, typically in two stories, with an arrangement for clerestory lighting.

Variations in design include:
* **Gallery Layouts:** The Julia, Ulpia, Pompeii, and Aemilia basilicas follow the normal scheme of a central space surrounded by galleries supported by piers or columns. In contrast, the palace basilica of Domitian, examples in North Africa, and the basilica at Silchester feature colonnades along the long sides but not across the ends. Some, such as those at Timgad and Trier, possess no interior divisions.
* **Tribunals:** Fixed tribunals are found at Pompeii, Timgad, and Silchester. Vaulted apses that may have held tribunals are present in the Basilica of Maxentius. The Basilica Julia had no tribunal, despite being used for the centumviral court; similarly, the semicircular projection of the Basilica Ulpia was not a vaulted apse and was distinct from the basilica itself.

## Transition to Christian Architecture
The term "basilica" was applied to many types of structures, including the meeting-places of pagan religious associations. Early Christian buildings, first expressly erected for worship in the 2nd or 3rd century A.D., likely began as oblong interiors terminated by an apse. After the time of Constantine, as the number of faithful grew, side aisles were added, making these churches resemble civil basilicas. This evolution is evidenced at S. Saba on the Aventine, where a small, aisleless church was discovered beneath the floor of the current basilica.

Despite the visual similarities, civil and Christian basilicas differ in key respects. Early Christian basilicas in the west rarely had galleries over side aisles, and their interiors were always dominated by a semi-dome apse terminating the central nave. In contrast, vaulted apses are virtually unknown in normal columned civil basilicas, with the possible exception of Silchester.

## Notable Christian Basilicas
In Rome, the basilica of S. Sabina (c. A.D. 430) is noted for preserving a primitive aspect, featuring an open roof and antique Corinthian columns. S. Lorenzo fuori le Mura and S. Agnese exhibit galleries similar to those of civil basilicas. S. Lorenzo, originally constructed in A.D. 432 with a short nave of six bays, was altered in the 13th century by Honorius III, who disorientated the church by replacing the apse with a twelve-bay nave.

Outside of Rome, other significant examples include:
* **Italy:** The two basilicas of S. Apollinare at Ravenna. S. Apollinare Nuovo (493-525) is known for its mosaic ornamentation of saintly figures, while S. Apollinare in Classe (A.D. 538-549) shares a similar design.
* **The East:** The monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai (built by Justinian), the church of Dana, St Philip at Athens, Bosra in Arabia, Xanthus in Lycia, and St Demetrius at Thessalonica.
* **North Africa:** Abundant remains exist, including the basilica adjoining the forum at Timgad.
* **Northern Europe:** In England, a 4th-century basilica was found at Silchester, featuring a prothesis and diaconicon flanking the apse. Another English example is Brixworth (c. A.D. 680), which features a nave divided by quadrangular piers and a circumscribing crypt. In Germany, an unaltered basilica from the time of Charles the Great exists at Michelstadt, and the 11th-century basilica of St Mauritius is located near Hildesheim.
