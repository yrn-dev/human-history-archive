# Agricultural Productivity and Irrigation in Ancient Babylonia

## The Babylonian Irrigation System
The Babylonian plain was originally a pestiferous and uninhabitable swamp, but it was reclaimed through elaborate irrigation, which transformed it into the most fertile country in the world. The science of engineering and irrigation is believed to have been first created in Babylonia, where a network of skillfully planned and regulated canals was established.

Three primary canals were responsible for carrying the waters of the Euphrates to the Tigris above Babylon:
*   **The Zabzallat canal** (also known as *Nahr Sarsar*), which ran from Ctesiphon to Faluja.
*   **The Kutha canal**, which passed Tell Ibrahim (or Kutha) on its way from Madain to Sippara.
*   **The King's canal** (or Ar-Malcha), located between the other two. This canal was conducted from the Euphrates toward Opis (or Upi), which was situated close to Seleucia on the western side of the Tigris; the canal may have owed its name to Khammurabi.

Additionally, the Pallacopas (referred to as Pallukkatu in Neo-Babylonian texts) started from Faluja and ran parallel to the western bank of the Euphrates as far as Teredon (or Iddaratu). This canal supplied a large lake near Borsippa and watered an immense tract of land. Some identify this as the "Canal of the Sun-god" found in early texts.

## Crop Yields and Agricultural Produce
The advanced system of irrigation led to highly advanced soil cultivation. Historical accounts provide varying estimates of the productivity of the land:
*   **Herodotus** reported that wheat commonly returned two hundred-fold to the sower, and occasionally as much as three hundred-fold.
*   **Theophrastus** provided a similar general view of the soil's fertility, though his figures for grain produce were lower, stating a return of fifty-fold or one hundred-fold.

Beyond wheat, the region supported a diverse array of vegetation. Berossus noted that palms, barley, sesame, ochrys, apples, and many types of shelled fruit grew wild. Wheat continued to grow wild in the vicinity of Anah. Pliny observed that wheat was cut twice, and after this, it served as good keep for sheep.

## Flora and Natural Resources
Date-palms (*phoínikes*) were a notable feature of ancient Babylonia. While many were cultivated for fruit, some varieties bore no fruit but provided high-quality wood used for furniture and house-purposes.

The region's climate was characterized by limited rainfall. Herodotus distinguished the "little rain" of Babylonia from the total absence of rain in Upper Egypt. Despite this general dryness, significant weather events occurred; for example, in November 1763, the traveler Niebuhr experienced hard rain during a four-day journey from the ruins of Babylon to Bagdad.

## Urbanization and Settlement
The Babylonian country was thickly studded with towns, many of which are still represented today by mounds. While the identification of most of these sites remains doubtful, recent American excavations have identified Bismya (located between Erech and Nippur) as the site of Usab, Adab, or Udab. Additionally, the neighboring site of Fara has been identified as the ancient Kisurra. The dense population of these areas was made possible by the aforementioned irrigation of the plain.
