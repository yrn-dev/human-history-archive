# Baruch, iii. 9-iv. 29

The section of the Book of Baruch spanning chapters iii. 9 through iv. 29 is a subject of scholarly analysis regarding its origin and integrity. According to some records, Baruch authored two separate epistles: one addressed to the nine and a half tribes and another addressed to the two and a half tribes located at Babylon. While the epistle to the nine and a half tribes is found in chapters lxxviii. through lxxxvi., the epistle to the two and a half tribes is considered lost. However, it is probable that this lost epistle is preserved, either in part or in its entirety, within the text of Baruch iii. 9-iv. 29.

## Relation to 4 Ezra
There is a significant scholarly debate regarding the relationship between the Book of Baruch and 4 Ezra due to the numerous affinities between the two works. Some scholars, such as Ryle and Ewald, have proposed that the two books share a single author. However, others argue that the points of divergence between the texts are too weighty to sustain the theory of identical authorship.

If the works are viewed as composite, it is suggested that certain constituents of 4 Ezra may be older than the latest portions of Baruch, while other parts of Baruch are decidedly older than the remaining sections of 4 Ezra. In the event that unity of authorship is assumed, establishing a final chronological relation between the two works remains difficult. Various scholars have argued for the priority of one text over the other:
* **Priority of 4 Ezra:** Maintained by Gunkel, Rosenthal, Dillmann, Drummond, Hausrath, Renan, Staehelin, Wieseler, Hilgenfeld, and Langen.
* **Priority of Baruch:** Maintained by Ryssel, Wellhausen, De Faye, Kabisch, Deane, Thomson, Bissell, and Schuerer.

## Editions and Versions
Several editions of the Greek text of the book are available, including those by Swete (vol. iii), Tischendorf (second volume of his Septuagint), and Fritzsche (published in 1871 as *Libri Apocryphi Veteris Testamenti Graece*). Other notable editions include Gifford's in the *Speaker's Apoc.* ii and Kneucker's *Das Buch Baruch* (1879).

## The Apocalypse of Baruch
The *Apocalypse of Baruch* is a distinct work that was long lost until its discovery by Ceriani. This text survived only in a Syriac version, specifically a 6th-century manuscript found in the Milan library. Ceriani published a Latin translation of this discovery in 1866, followed by the text in 1871 and a photo-lithographic facsimile in 1883. Fritzsche also reproduced the Latin translation in 1871.

Chapters lxxviii. through lxxxvi. of this work have been known for a longer period, as they comprise Baruch's epistle to the nine and a half tribes in captivity. These specific chapters have been published in Latin and Syriac in the Paris and London Polyglots. Additionally, Lagarde published them in Syriac from one manuscript in 1861, and R. H. Charles published them from ten manuscripts in 1896. The complete work has been translated into German by Ryssel (1900) and into English by R. H. Charles.
