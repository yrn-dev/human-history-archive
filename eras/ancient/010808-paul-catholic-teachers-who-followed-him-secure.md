# Paul and the Early Catholic Teachers

## Life and Background of Paul
Saul of Tarsus, known as Paul, is regarded by many modern authorities as the real founder of Christianity. A Roman citizen, he was likely a Jew by birth, though some Jewish writers deny this. He was a man of wide education, well-versed in the Hellenic theologies of Alexandria and fluent in the Greek of Alexandria, which he used with power and freedom. Before becoming a follower of Jesus, Paul was a religious theorist and teacher who studied under Jewish teachers, including Gamaliel. In the New Testament narrative, he initially appears as a bitter antagonist and critic of the Nazarenes.

## Theological Contributions and Teachings
Paul's doctrines introduced an idea not prominently found in the reported teachings of Jesus: the concept of a sacrificial person offered to God as an atonement for sin. While Jesus preached a new birth of the human soul, Paul presented a religion of propitiatory bloodshed, priest, and altar, viewing Jesus as the "Easter lamb"—a traditional human victim without spot or blemish. This explanation of the crucifixion provided a satisfactory elucidation of the event for the Nazarenes.

In his practical ministry, Paul spent a winter at Corinth (Acts xx. 2, 3), during which he wrote the Epistle to the Romans. This work indicates a period of milder tone following violent controversies with Judaizing emissaries in Galatia. Paul also focused heavily on collecting money for the mother church, eventually departing for Jerusalem between 55 and 59 A.D. His legacy was maintained by successors; around A.D. 139, the Catholic Hebrew Christian Hegesippus noted the fidelity to Christian truth and honest lives of those over whom Paul had exercised oversight.

## Struggle Against Asceticism
From its earliest stages, the Christian Church exhibited a tendency toward excessive asceticism. Early enthusiasts, particularly those expecting a speedy advent of the millennium, condemned permanent societal institutions as impediments to the life of grace and alien to the kingdom of God. It required a severe struggle by Paul and the Catholic teachers who followed him to secure the rights of the baptized to engage in the standard institutions of society. These rights included the right to own property, to marry, to assume public office, and to engage in commerce and war.

## Catholic Teachers and the Opposition to Heresy
The Catholic fathers and eminent Church teachers, starting from Justin, frequently addressed the challenges posed by dissenting groups. Marcion, whose churches existed from the 2nd to the 7th century, was a primary target of extensive treatises written by Catholic teachers such as Irenaeus, Tertullian, Hippolytus, Pseudo-Origen Adamantius, Epiphanius, and the Armenian Esnik. Through these controversial works, the text of Paul's epistles in Marcion's recension and the contents of the Marcionite Gospel were preserved.

Other groups that dissented from the Catholic church included the Gnostics, the Montanists of Phrygia, the Novatians, and the Valentinians. The Gnostics were eventually oppressed by the authority and wealth of the church and driven from the capitals of the East and West to the borders of the Euphrates. Later, the Marcionites were largely lost within the Manichaeans, who attempted to reconcile the doctrines of Christ and Zoroaster.

## The Paulicians
In the neighborhood of Samosata, under the grandson of Heraclius, a reformer named Constantine founded a movement known as the Paulicians. Constantine had received the New Testament from a deacon returning from Syrian captivity. Although the Catholics disputed his interpretation, they acknowledged that his text was genuine. Constantine held a peculiar devotion to the character and writings of St. Paul, the "apostle of the Gentiles." The Paulicians mirrored the first ages of the church by naming their congregations in Cappadocia and Armenia after apostolic churches and representing the disciples Titus, Timothy, Sylvanus, and Tychicus through Constantine and his fellow-laborers.
