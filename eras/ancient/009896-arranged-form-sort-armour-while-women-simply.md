# Armour and Protective Dress in Human History

The use of armour and protective clothing has varied significantly across different cultures and eras, ranging from specialized military equipment and naval vessels to tribal adornments and architectural defenses.

## Tribal and Ancient Protective Dress
In the Anglo-Egyptian Sudan, the Bongo people—a Nilotic tribe—historically utilized iron rings on the arms of men, which were arranged to function as a sort of armour. The Bongo are described as a thick-set race with red-brown complexions who traditionally lived in independent communities. While men wore these iron rings and loin-cloths, women wore girdles with attached tufts of grass; however, both sexes have largely transitioned to using cotton cloths for dress.

In Pre-Roman and Roman Britain, the inhabitants were not considered an armoured race, as they frequently fought naked from the waist up, often utilizing woad staining and tattooing. Despite this, Pliny noted that their close-woven felts were nearly sword-proof. The Britons wore various garments, including cloaks of black or blue, and bright-coloured, striped, or checkered coats and trousers, with red being the preferred hue.

## Military Armour and Combat
Historical accounts of Gallic warfare highlight the use of breastplates and shields. During the siege of Alesia, Caesar's victory was signaled to the defenders in the city by the sight of many breastplates smeared with blood and shields adorned with gold and silver. The Gallic leader Vergentorix is recorded as having worn his best armour and equipping his horse before surrendering to Caesar, at which point he threw off his complete armour.

In the context of early military roles, the armour-bearer served as a page or apprentice-in-arms. The primary warlike duty of the armour-bearer was to kill those whom the master had already struck down. In some instances among the Arabs, this specific office was performed by women.

## Naval and Mechanical Armour
The evolution of armour extended into naval warfare and aviation during the early 20th century. In the Pacific, the China squadron utilized armoured cruisers of the County class. Ships such as the *Defence* and *Minotaur* were deployed against German armoured cruisers like the *Gneisenau* and *Scharnhorst*. These vessels were comparable in speed, size, and modernity, though the British ships possessed heavier metal, firing a broadside of 2,520 pounds compared to the 1,725 pounds of the German rivals.

In aerial combat, the construction of aircraft provided limited protection. Pilots engaged in maneuvers to avoid machine gun fire; for example, a German pilot might pass over a French opponent to take advantage of the protection offered by the aeroplane's lightly armoured bottom.

Additionally, military logistics involved the use of armoured trains, which were operated by naval ratings and placed under the orders of General Rawlinson.

## Architectural and Social Protections
Protective structures have also played a role in history. The basilica was essentially a columned structure, often featuring a central space with galleries—generally in two stories—and clerestory lighting. In military sieges, such as at Alesia, the necessity of protection led Caesar to construct two walls: one facing the city and another facing the assembling nations of Gaul.

In the Bismarck Archipelago, the Duk-Duk secret society utilized a different form of "armour" or costume for its presiding spirit. The figure of Duk-Duk wore a helmet made of network, resembling a gigantic candle-extinguisher, and was dressed in leaves to the waist. This mysterious figure served as a mechanism for law and order, executing justice and levying blackmail upon women.
