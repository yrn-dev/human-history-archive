# The Role and Function of the Temple

## Economic and Administrative Functions
The temple occupied a position of significant importance, serving as a central hub for financial and material resources. It accumulated vast amounts of *naturalia*, money, and permanent gifts. These assets were derived from several sources: estates, tithes, fixed dues, and a customary share of sacrifices and other offerings provided by the faithful. The tithe is thought to have been a composition for rent due to the god for his land, though it is possible that only lands with a special connection to the temple paid this.

Beyond its role as a recipient of wealth, the temple functioned as a city archive and as great store-houses and granaries. The administration of these institutions involved many officials and servants, particularly in larger temples. Historically, it is suggested that towns may have clustered around a single temple, where the head of each family held the right to minister and share in the receipts. Over time, as cities expanded, the right to serve at a shrine or its "gate" for a set number of days per year became a form of property. This right could be shared within a family, rented, or pledged, although it could not be alienated.

## Social Responsibilities and Governance
Temples held specific social obligations toward the citizens of their cities. For instance, if a citizen was captured by an enemy and unable to ransom himself, the city's temple was responsible for doing so. Additionally, the temple provided a source of support for poor farmers, who could borrow supplies for harvesters or seed corn. These advances were repaid without interest.

The relationship between the state and the temple was administrative rather than proprietary. While a king might borrow from the temple, he was required to repay the loan just as any other borrower would.

## Sacred Architecture and Symbolism
Different cultures utilized temple architecture to reflect specific cosmological or religious views. Numa is credited with building the Temple of Vesta in a circular form. This design was intended to imitate the shape of the entire universe rather than the earth. According to the Pythagoreans, the element of fire—which they called the Unit and Vesta—occupies the center of the universe. In this system, the earth is not motionless or central, but revolves around this central fire, placing the earth in a subordinate position. This view was also shared by Plato in his old age, who believed a nobler body occupied the center of the universe.

## Notable Temples and Sacred Sites
Various temples held prominence due to their association with specific deities or their role in civic and international life:

*   **Epidaurus:** The fame of the temple of Asclepius contributed to the importance of Epidaurus, a maritime city on the eastern coast of Argolis.
*   **Olympian Zeus:** The old oracular temple of the Olympian Zeus was located on the banks of the Alpheius in Peloponnesus. It was associated with the Olympic festival, which maintained its celebrity for over eleven hundred years before being abolished by the Christian emperor Theodosius in 394 A.D.
*   **Delphi:** The sacred precinct of Delphi was a site of strategic importance and conflict. The Lokrians served as protectors of the Delphian temple against the Phokians. At one point, Phokian leaders and their mercenary bands occupied the temple.
*   **Ilium:** In the region described in the *Iliad*, there was a temple and worship of Athênê in Ilium, though the goddess was hostile to the Trojans. Additionally, the temple of the Sminthian Apollo was memorable into the time of the emperor Julian.
*   **Honno-ji:** In Kyoto, the temple of Honno-ji was the site where the murder of Nobunaga took place; subsequently, the head of his assassin, Mitsuhide, was exposed in front of this temple.
