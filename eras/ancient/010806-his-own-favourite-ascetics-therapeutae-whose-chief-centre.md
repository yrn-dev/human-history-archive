# The Therapeutae

The Therapeutae were a group of ascetics who were the favorites of Philo (20 B.C.–A.D. 40). Their chief center was located in Egypt.

## Philosophy and Lifestyle
The life of the Therapeutae was designed to impersonate the highest wisdom. Their practices were inspired by a respectable form of cynicism that had become deeply rooted among the Greek Jews of that era. Central to their way of life was the total renunciation of material possessions and the temptations associated with property.

The Therapeutae practiced a rigorous form of asceticism that prioritized the faculties of the soul over the needs of the body. Philo contrasts the "disciples of wisdom" with athletes; while athletes maintain strong, "lusty" bodies to serve as a fortification for the soul, the ascetics of the Therapeutae were pale, wasted, and reduced to a state resembling skeletons. This physical condition was the result of their decision to sacrifice their bodily strength entirely to the soul.

## Renunciation of Social Ties
The commitment of the Therapeutae to their ascetic path involved an irrevocable abandonment of their previous lives. By their own free will, they divested themselves of copious wealth and spurned things dear to the flesh. This renunciation extended beyond material goods to include all primary social and familial bonds. Members of the Therapeutae abandoned:
* Parents and children
* Wives and brothers
* Throngs of kinsmen
* The intimacy of friends
* The fatherlands where they were born and bred

## Historical Context
The ideal of renunciation practiced by the Therapeutae is noted as being similar to the ideal of early Christian renunciation, though it operated independently of the influence of Jesus. A similar ideal is also found in the writings of Epictetus.
