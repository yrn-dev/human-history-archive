# The South-West Monsoon Air Mass of Southern Asia

## Characteristics and Composition
The south-west monsoon carries a heated body of air from the Indian Ocean over southern Asia. This air mass is characterized by being highly charged with watery vapour. Due to this high moisture content, the air is in a condition to release a large volume of water as rain upon the land, provided it encounters circumstances that notably reduce its temperature.

## Mechanisms of Precipitation
A notable reduction in temperature occurs along the Burmo-Siamese peninsula and the greater part of the coasts of India. This process is driven by the interruption of the wind current by continuous ranges of mountains. As these mountains force the mass of air to rise, the air becomes rarefied. This rarefaction increases the air's specific capacity for heat, causing its temperature to fall. Consequently, the vapour originally held in suspension undergoes condensation, leading to precipitation.

## Regional Temperature Influences in Asia
The air temperatures of Asia are influenced by several geographical factors. In the southern and eastern regions, the proximity of the ocean provides a moderating effect, particularly where the land consists of peninsulas or islands. 

In contrast, the central part of Asia, as well as the table-lands of Persia and Afghanistan, experience exaggerated winter cold due to their great elevations above sea level. Summer heat in these same regions is increased by the sterility of the surface and small rainfall. On dry surface soil, solar heat accumulates during the summer because it cannot be released upwards through evaporation—as would occur if the soil were covered with vegetation or moist—nor can it be easily conveyed downwards as it is over the ocean. Similarly, during winter, the prolonged loss of heat by radiation during clear, long nights creates a negative effect, which is further intensified in areas where the air has little vapour or the surface is covered with snow.

## Comparative Atmospheric and Climatic Principles
The transmission of radiant heat is heavily influenced by the amount of moisture in the air. Sun heat passes more freely through dry air than through moist air. Consequently, periods of the earth's history characterized by extreme depths of seas and high mountains—which result in a more restricted sea surface and less surface evaporation—tend to have drier atmospheric phases. These dry phases are associated with hot days and cold nights, as heat is both received and radiated away abundantly. Conversely, moist phases result in warmer nights and cooler days.
