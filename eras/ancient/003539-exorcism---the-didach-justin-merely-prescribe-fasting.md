# Exorcism

Exorcism refers to the rites and practices intended to expel evil spirits. In early Christian traditions, these practices varied in complexity and method, ranging from simple physical discipline to elaborate liturgical ceremonies involving blessed elements.

## Early Christian Practices
In the early records of the *Didache* and the writings of Justin, exorcism was primarily associated with fasting. The purpose of fasting was to accelerate the departure of evil spirits; it was believed that these spirits preferred a well-fed body over an emaciated one when choosing a *nidus* or tenement. This belief is reflected in an interpolated saying from Matthew xvii. 21, which states, "This kind (of demon) goeth not forth except by prayer and fasting."

Justin describes a process where those who believe the teachings and undertake to live accordingly are instructed to pray and entreat God with fasting for the remission of past sins, with the community praying and fasting alongside them. Similarly, the *Didache* prescribes that both the person baptizing and the person being baptized—as well as others who are able—should fast, specifically instructing the baptizand to fast for one or two days prior to the rite.

## Evolution of the Rite
Over time, the rites of exorcism became increasingly lengthy. Later developments included the formal renunciation of the devil, his angels, his works, and his pomps. While these elements were old, they are not considered primitive; for example, the archaic rite found in the *Key of Truth* does not include them.

Basil, in his work *On the Holy Spirit*, noted that he was ignorant of the origins of several features of the baptismal rite. These features, which he described as being handed down through a silent and sacramental tradition of unpublished and unspoken teaching, included:
* The formal renunciation of Satan and his angels.
* The blessing of the baptizand.
* The blessing of the oil of anointing and the use of that oil.
* The blessing of the water of baptism.
* Trine immersion.

## Sacerdotal Benedictions and the Roman Catholic Church
In the Roman Catholic Church, the *Rituale Romanum* sets forth the ceremonies for various benedictions. While the general function of giving blessings belongs to the order of priests, certain benedictions are reserved for bishops. These include the blessing of priests at ordination, abbots, churches, cemeteries, oratories, virgins taking the veil, military colours, soldiers, their arms, and articles used with the altar, such as vestments, patens, and chalices. The pope alone holds the authority to bless royal swords, the "Agnus-Dei," the golden rose, and the pallium, and is the only one who can issue blessings involving some days' indulgence.

General requirements for priests performing benedictions outside of the Mass include being bare-headed and vested in a stole and surplice. The process typically involves specific prayers, the sign of the cross, censing (where prescribed), and the sprinkling of holy water using an *aspergillum* and a vase.

## The Use of Blessed Salt and Water
The blessing of holy water is considered the essential instrument for all benedictions, specifically intended to establish potency against evil spirits. The process involves the exorcism of two "creatures":
1. **Salt:** The salt is exorcized so that it may provide health of body and soul to those who take it, and so that every "unclean spirit" and "wile of diabolic deceit," as well as every "phantasy and wickedness," may flee and leave wherever the salt is sprinkled.
2. **Water:** The water is then exorcized to become "exorcized water," intended to uproot and expel the enemy and his apostate angels through divine virtue and to put every power of the enemy to flight.
