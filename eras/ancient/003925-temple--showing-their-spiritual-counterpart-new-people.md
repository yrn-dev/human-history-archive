# Temple

In the study of human history, the concept of the temple has evolved from a physical structure of civic and religious centrality to a spiritual metaphor representing the human person and the collective community of faith.

## Physical Temples in Early Civilizations
In various early civilizations, the temple served as a focal point for social and political organization. In the days of Solomon, the Hebrews were characterized as a people clustering around the court and temple, where they were led by the ambition of the king and the wisdom of the priest. Similarly, in the development of Greek cities, the temple was a central feature, although the sequence of urban development differed from other primitive civilizations; in Greek cities, the city walls generally preceded the construction of the temple.

Specific temples often served as markers of national or regional identity. For example, the temple of Dêmêtêr was erected at Thermopylæ, the site of the Amphiktyonic assemblies, and was known as the Amphiktyonic Dêmêtêr. In Magna Græcia, the temple of Hêrê near Cape Lakinium was renowned for its beauty and celebrity, housing a costly, skillfully wrought robe offered by a Sybarite named Alkimenes. This robe was later plundered by Dionysius and sold to the Carthaginians for one hundred and twenty talents to be placed in a newly-established temple dedicated to Persephonê and Dêmêtêr.

## Transition of Knowledge and Power
Historically, temples were not only sites of worship but also centers of record-keeping and literacy. By the fifth century B.C., however, a shift occurred as reading and writing began to escape the ranks of court scribes and the precincts of the temple. This transition allowed record-keeping to move beyond the temple and court, fostering the rise of a class of people with independent means and leisure who could exchange views and develop the "free intelligence of mankind."

## The Spiritual Temple in Early Christianity
In the transition from Judaism to Christianity, the concept of the temple underwent a spiritual reinterpretation. An early epistle, likely written by a teacher of the Alexandrine Church during the reign of Vespasian (A.D. 70-79), addresses the relationship between the Old and New Covenants. The author of this text employs an extravagant allegorism to argue that the Law and Prophecy were never intended for the Israelites as a people, but rather pointed toward the Christian Covenant.

Within this framework, the author takes typical Jewish institutions—including the Temple, the Sabbath, Ablutions, Foods, Circumcision, and the Covenant—and demonstrates their spiritual counterparts within the "New People" and its ordinances. This perspective views the Christian Church itself as the building of a "spiritual temple."

## The Human Person as Temple
The teachings of Jesus introduced a further evolution of this concept by shifting the focus from a collective or institutional structure to the individual. This phase of spiritual life emphasized the universal Fatherhood of God and the brotherhood of all men. Central to this vision is the insistence upon the sacredness of every human personality, viewing each individual as a "living temple of God." This concept contributed to a new global respect for human dignity and opposed the subjugation of man by man.
