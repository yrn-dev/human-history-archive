# Alienation and Loss of Power

In historical and philosophical contexts, the concepts of alienation and the loss of power manifest across various domains, including geopolitical rivalry, legal status, property rights, and the internal state of the human mind.

## Geopolitical and Strategic Alienation
In ancient Greece, alienation often served as a catalyst for shifts in regional primacy. During the Kleomenic war, the actions of the Spartan king Kleomenês—specifically his forced acquisition of ships from the Sikyonians and Æginetans to land troops at Nauplia—resulted in the enmity of Argos. This conflict not only enfeebled Argos but functioned to alienate the city from its natural supporters and allies, thereby clearing the path for undisputed Spartan primacy. The temporary prostration of Argos was essential for Sparta to acquire presidential power over its allies, as Argos had previously been the first power in Greece with legendary claims to headship.

Similarly, strategic alienation occurred during the conflicts involving the Four Hundred. The power of this oligarchical body was diminished in value and increased in peril due to the loss of their foreign empire and the alienation of their Samian armament. In other instances, the threat of alienation influenced military and political decisions; for example, Astyochus was aware that certain "monstrous" deeds would greatly alienate the Milêsians and Chians, a factor that influenced the movements of Alkibiadês.

## Legal and Social Status of Aliens
In the context of Greek law, legal actions (*dikai*) were categorized as either public (*demosiai*) or private (*idiai*). The status of an "alien" impacted how these legal processes were navigated. While public actions could generally be instituted by any person possessing the franchise, private suits were restricted. A private suit could only be brought by the man directly interested; however, if the interested party was an alien, the suit had to be brought by their patron.

In more modern administrative contexts, such as those detailed in United States Department policy, the term "criminal alien" refers to a foreign national convicted of a crime. Policy directives have mandated that federal prosecutors actively involve themselves in the removal of these individuals, utilizing tools such as judicial deportation orders, stipulated administrative deportation orders linked to plea agreements, and deportation as a condition of supervised release.

## Alienation of Property and Public Opinion
Alienation also pertains to the transfer or loss of property and political support. In the history of the papacy and European princes, the alienation of property to abbeys, monks, and nuns was viewed with great dislike by kings and princes. This shift replaced feudal lords, who provided military support, with lands under what was perceived as foreign dominion. This alienation of land contributed to struggles over "investitures"—the right to appoint bishops—and resulted in the clergy claiming exemption from taxation to the prince, instead paying taxes to Rome.

In the political sphere, alienation can describe the disconnect between a government and its citizenry. For example, Prime Minister Mr. Balfour took office during a period of "vast alienation of public opinion" and the consolidation of hostile forces, inheriting an exhausted political mandate following the conclusion of a war.

## Philosophical and Spiritual Loss
From a philosophical perspective, the loss of power or essence is often framed as a spiritual or cognitive deficiency. The philosopher Baader posited that human reason alone cannot reach its intended end, necessitating the presuppositions of tradition, church, and faith. He argued that existence depends on being cognized by God, suggesting that all self-consciousness is simultaneously God-consciousness.

In the teachings of Mencius, as discussed in the context of the samurai code of *Bushido*, there is a focus on the loss of the "mind" and the "path." Mencius described Rectitude (or Righteousness) as the path a man must take to regain a lost paradise. He lamented that while people seek after lost dogs or fowls, they often lose their mind and do not know how to seek it again. Rectitude is presented as the "bone" that provides firmness and stature, without which talent and learning cannot make a human frame a samurai.
