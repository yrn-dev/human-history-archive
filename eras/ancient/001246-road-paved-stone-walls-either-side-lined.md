# Stone-Paved Roads and Associated Structures

Throughout history, the construction of roads using stone paving has served various purposes, ranging from the facilitation of solemn religious processions and urban commerce to military defense and imperial utility. The materials used and the decorative elements accompanying these roads vary significantly across different civilizations and eras.

## Processional and Temple Roads
In ancient Babylon, the *A-ibur-sabu* was a raised road designed specifically for solemn processions. This road led from the Gate of Ishtar to E-Saggila and skirted the eastern side of the palace. It was paved with stone, and its walls on either side were lined with enamelled tiles featuring representations of a procession of lions.

Similarly, in the city containing the temple of the goddess (as described by Herodotus), a stone-paved road extended for approximately three furlongs opposite the temple entrance. This road, which was about four hundred feet wide, led eastward through the market-place toward the temple of Hermes and was flanked on both sides by trees of immense height.

## Roman Road Construction and Defense
The Roman approach to road building, exemplified by the work of Caius, emphasized utility, convenience, and ornament. These roads were constructed in straight lines across the countryside, utilizing a combination of tight-rammed masses of earth and quarried stone. To ensure a level and agreeable appearance, depressions were filled and bridges were constructed over deep ravines or winter torrents, with the road raised to a uniform height on both sides. Distance was marked by stone blocks measured in Roman miles, and additional stones were placed at shorter intervals along the sides of the road to assist individuals in mounting horses.

In the north of Britain, roads were integrated into defensive systems. Hadrian's wall featured a connecting road located behind a stone wall that was 6-8 feet thick and originally perhaps 14 feet high. This stone wall, which replaced an earlier "turf wall" made of sods, was reconstructed during the age of Septimius Severus.

## Urban and Industrial Paving
Urban street paving has evolved through various materials. In some cities, streets were initially paved with cobble-stones, followed by dressed granite paving-stones known as parallelepipedons, and eventually wood and asphalt. 

In industrial contexts, such as the region crossing the French frontier, high roads were provided with stone paving. In other areas, such as the road to St. André, Verlinghem, and Quesnoy, paving was inconsistent; while the center of the road was stone-paved, the sides consisted of belts about three metres wide without paving, which often converted into mud.

## Materials and Technical Specifications
Various stones and materials have been employed for paving and lining:

*   **Dolomites:** Due to their compact structure and higher specific gravity compared to limestones, dolomites are harder and more durable, making them suitable for road-mending and paving.
*   **Mosaics:** Paving and wall linings have been created using mosaic work composed of glass, pottery, stone, or small pieces of marble. Roman mosaics utilized colored marble cubes pressed into a cement float. A less expensive version, "terazzo mosaic," used irregularly shaped marble chips.
*   **Bricks and Tiles:** While ordinary brick paving (laid flat or on edge) has largely been abandoned for cement or special tiles, hard-textured bricks with grooves for drainage are still used for stables. Dutch clinkers—small, hard, light yellow bricks measuring 6 by 3 by 1½ inches—are also used for paving.
*   **Ornamental Tiles:** A wide variety of colored and shaped tiles are used for wall lining in areas such as bathrooms, provision shops, and sculleries to improve sanitary conditions, as well as for general building ornamentation.
