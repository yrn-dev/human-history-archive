# Joy

In the records of human history and spiritual practice, joy is presented both as a cultivated state of mind and a reward for righteousness, often inextricably linked with the concept of love.

## Buddhist Perspectives on Love and Joy
Within the framework of Right Conduct, Love and Joy are identified as two of the most important points. Love, referred to in Pali as *Mettā*, is described in the *Metta Sutta* as a boundless cultivation. The text instructs that a person should cultivate love toward all beings—above, below, and around—with a heart that is unstinted and unmixed with opposing or differing interests. This practice is compared to the way a mother protects her only son, even at the risk of her own life. This state of heart is considered the best in the world and is to be maintained through mindfulness while standing, walking, sitting, or lying down.

Furthermore, Buddhist teachings describe the *Brahma Vihāras*, or Sublime Conditions, which consist of four states:
* Love
* Sorrow at the sorrows of others
* Joy in the joys of others
* Equanimity regarding one's own joys and sorrows

These feelings are to be deliberately practiced, starting with a single object and expanding until the entire world is suffused with the feeling. This practice involves abiding in a tender, compassionate, and loving heart, void of malice, anger, or ill-will.

Historically, the early Buddhist communities expressed feelings of ecstasy and enthusiasm inspired by these reforming doctrines through poetry. Verses were used not only as memory aids but as lyrics to express emotion and deep thought. Additionally, the birth of Gotama, the Buddha, at Lumbini is described in the *Sutta Nipata* as an event occurring for "weal and for joy in the world of men."

## Joy in the Qur'an
The Qur'an presents joy and pleasure in both a worldly and a spiritual context. The text notes that the love of worldly joys—including offspring, women, hoarded gold and silver, cattle, land, and branded horses—is made to seem fair to mankind as the "comfort of the life of the world." However, it asserts that a more excellent abode exists with Allah.

For those who are righteous and guard against evil, the Qur'an promises a superior reward: Gardens with rivers flowing beneath, pure companions, and the pleasure or contentment of Allah. Specifically, for those who fulfill their vows and feed the orphan, the captive, and the needy for the love of Allah alone—seeking no reward or thanks—Allah delivers them from the evil of the Day of distressful Wrath and sheds over them a "Light of Beauty and (blissful) Joy."

## Joy in Bushido and Other Traditions
In the tradition of Bushido, joy is linked to the mastery of internal passions. The practitioner Kumazawa stated that "Joy cometh only as Passion and Desire part," suggesting that joy is achieved when one does not return anger for anger or blame for blame. Similarly, Saigo taught that one should love others with the same love they have for themselves, as Heaven loves all with equal love.

In literary history, joy has been a recurring theme in poetic forms. In the 19th century, Theodore de Banville published *Trente-six ballades joyeuses*. Earlier, in the medieval English ballades of Chaucer, joy and bliss are described as the result of love, where the voice of the beloved makes the speaker's thoughts "in joy and bliss abound."

Conversely, some historical accounts highlight the fragility of joy. In the *Heimskringla*, the poet Sigvat reflects on the grief of a man who lost his wife, questioning if the "price" of grief is too great for a joy that was "so brief."
