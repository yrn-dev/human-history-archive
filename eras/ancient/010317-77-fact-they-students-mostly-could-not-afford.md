# The History and Trade of Books and Learning

## Early Demand and Distribution of Sacred Texts
The spread of Christianity generated a significant demand for devotional volumes and sacred books, specifically copies of the Gospels. These texts, along with missals, were produced for both private use and for the church. In 671, Benedict Biscop, who founded the abbey at Wearmouth in England, imported a full cargo of books from France, some of which he had purchased.

## The Stationers of London and Oxford
Prior to the Reformation, the sale of manuscript copies of prayers—including the Creed, Ave Maria, the Paternoster, and the ABC—was handled by text writers or stationers (*stacyoneres*) located near St Paul's in London. In 1403, these stationers formed a gild. Some of these sellers operated from stalls or stations built directly against the walls of the cathedral.

In Oxford, the trade was managed by "sworn" university booksellers. One such stationer, John More, provided books to pupils and also functioned as a pawnbroker. The sworn stationers maintained a large monopoly over the supply of books, which changed hands in very large numbers. However, the presence of non-sworn booksellers led to the passage of a statute in 1373. This law was enacted because books of great value were being sold and carried away from Oxford, resulting in the cheating of owners and the loss of lawful business for the sworn stationers. Consequently, it was decreed that no bookseller—other than two sworn stationers or their deputies—could sell any book valued at more than half a mark.

## Student Access to Materials
Despite the availability of books through sworn stationers, most students could not afford to purchase them. Furthermore, the advantage of owning books was not as considerable as might be assumed, because the instruction provided to students was almost entirely oral.

## Learning and Authorship in Ancient China
In China, the Han dynasty was marked by a significant revival of authorship and learning. This period involved immense efforts to repair the damage done to literature by the First Emperor. Texts of the Confucian Canon, which had been hidden at the risk of death, were rescued and processed by appointed editing committees. 

While the interpretations of these texts provided by scholars of that era were eventually set aside in the twelfth century in favor of a new set of accepted standards for students, the early efforts to recover the Confucian Canon are mostly responsible for its lasting influence on the Chinese people. Over time, however, it became fashionable to discover old texts, leading to the circulation of works that falsely claim antiquity.

## Global Educational Exchange
In more modern contexts, the balance of knowledge between populations has shifted. In the relationship between Britain and India, India sends students to Britain, whereas Britain sends officials to India. There is no existing organization for sending European students to India specifically to inquire into current affairs, archaeology, and history. Additionally, while many Chinamen are conversant with English knowledge, there are very few Englishmen who possess intimate knowledge of Chinese thought and life or who know the Chinese language thoroughly.
