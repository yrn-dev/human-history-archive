# Members of the Hellenic Aggregate: Greeks North of Peloponnesus

The Hellenic aggregate was held together by a fellowship of blood, a common language (essentially one language with various dialects), and shared religious sentiments, including common sacrifices and sacred games such as the Olympic games. While the aggregate shared these cultural bonds, political sovereignty was strictly attached to each separate city. The relationship between cities was "interpolitical" or international; for example, an Athenian visiting Corinth was considered an alien who could not acquire land, marry a native woman, or sue for legal protection without a friendly citizen's mediation, unless special covenants were agreed upon by the sovereign authorities.

## The Amphiktyonic Convocation
A significant element of the Hellenic union was the Amphiktyonies, which were exclusive religious partnerships that promoted sympathies among members. The Amphiktyonic Council consisted of twelve constituent members who had superintendence of the temple of Delphi. While the council's interference in general Grecian affairs was rare and occasional, and many Hellenic states did not participate, the council and its festivals were valuable in promoting union. The Amphiktyonic meeting originally took place at Thermopylæ.

The twelve races or subdivisions of the Amphiktyonic convocation were divided by the pass of Thermopylæ:
*   **North of the pass:** Thessalians, Perrhæbians, Magnêtes, Achæans, Melians, Ænianes, and Dolopes.
*   **South of the pass:** Dorians, Ionians, Bœotians, Lokrians, and Phokians.

## Greeks North of Thermopylæ
The region north of the pass of Thermopylæ was dominated by the Thessalians and their dependents. The population of Thessaly included a villein race known as the Penestæ, though their exact origins are doubtful. Thessaly was characterized by a quadruple division and a disorderly confederacy of cities, yet it possessed great power when the cities acted in unanimity.

Several other groups in this region were tributaries of the Thessalians, though they remained Amphiktyonic races. These included the Achæans, Perrhæbi, Magnêtes (including Asiatic Magnêtes), Malians, and Dolopes. Other groups in the northern territories included the Œtæi and the Ænianes.

## Other Extra-Peloponnesian Groups
Beyond the immediate Thessalian sphere, other groups inhabited the northern and western regions:
*   **Phocians and Lokrians:** Located south of the pass of Thermopylæ.
*   **Doris and Dryopis:** The region of Doris was associated with the Dryopes.
*   **The Rudest Greeks:** The Ozolian Lokrians, Ætolians, and Akarnanians are described as the rudest of all Greeks.
*   **Bœotians:** This group included the cities of Bœotia and Orchomenus. Bœotia was organized as a confederation, and the city of Thebes had early legislation associated with Philolaus and Dioklês.

## Geography and Boundaries of Hellas
"Hellas proper" (or continuous Hellas) was understood to begin at the town and gulf of Ambrakia and extend northward to the Akrokeraunian promontory. This boundary was defined by a mountain chain including Olympus, the Cambunian mountains, and Mount Lingon, which ranges from the Ægean sea (or the gulf of Therma) to the Adriatic.

The land between Ambrakia and the Akrokeraunian promontory was called Epirus. It was occupied by the Chaonians, Molossians, and Thesprotians (the Epirots). Generally, the Epirots were not esteemed to belong to the Hellenic aggregate, although Herodotus inclined to treat the Molossians and Thesprotians as Hellens. Similarly, the more distant sections of the Ætolians and Akarnanians were viewed as being widely removed from the full type of Hellenism.

## Non-Hellenic and Bordering Peoples
The Macedonians of the seventh and sixth centuries B.C. were an aggregate of rude inland tribes divided into petty principalities. They were primarily village residents—a fact supported by Thucydides—though some towns existed. They were characterized as brave and pugnacious, with some tribes using a badge of discredit for men who had not yet slain an enemy. Herodotus distinguished the Macedonians from the Hellenic aggregate, noting a wider ethnical difference between them and the Greeks than existed between the Epirots and the Greeks. By the fourth century B.C., the Macedonians achieved high proficiency in Greek military organization, though their impact on Greece was destructive, replacing citizen-soldiers with foreign mercenaries.

Other non-Hellenic groups mentioned in the archive include the historical Pelasgians and Leleges, both of whom spoke barbarous languages.
