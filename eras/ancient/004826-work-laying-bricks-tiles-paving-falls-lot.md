# Brick and Tile Paving

The responsibility for the work of laying bricks or tiles as paving falls to the bricklayer. This practice encompasses a variety of materials and methods, ranging from utilitarian industrial surfaces to highly ornamental architectural features.

## Materials and Types of Paving Bricks
Paving has historically utilized ordinary bricks laid either flat or on their edges. However, this method has been largely abandoned in favor of cement paving or special tiles, as cement is cleaner and practically non-porous, making it more sanitary. 

Specialized bricks are manufactured for specific needs:
*   **Stable Paving:** Bricks of extremely hard texture are used for stables and similar areas. These often feature grooves on the face to provide a better foothold and assist with drainage.
*   **Blue Bricks:** Produced primarily in North Wales and South Staffordshire, these are vitrified, heavy, and impervious. Because they offer great compressional resistance and durability, they are used in engineering work, coping, channelling, and paving.
*   **Dutch Clinkers:** These are light yellow, hard paving bricks burned at high temperatures. Their dimensions are 6 inches long, 3 inches wide, and 1½ inches thick.
*   **Oven Tiles:** A variety of paving tile made from material similar to ordinary red brick, typically measuring 10 or 12 inches square and 1 to 2 inches thick.

## Installation Methods
The installation of brick paving depends on the orientation of the bricks and the intended use of the surface. Generally, a 6-inch thick bed of concrete is provided under the paving. In cases of external paving where bricks are placed on their edges, the concrete may be omitted in favor of bedding the bricks in well-rammed sand. The side joints of these bricks are then grouted using cement or lime.

## Ornamental and Specialized Paving
Beyond utilitarian surfaces, tiles and other materials are used for decorative and sanitary purposes. An immense variety of ornamental walling and paving tiles—differing in shape, size, and color—are used to line bathrooms, lavatories, provision shops, and sculleries to improve sanitary conditions. Tiles are also employed for both internal and external building ornamentation.

Mosaic work represents another form of paving and wall lining. This consists of small pieces of pottery, glass, stone, or marble laid in ornamental designs. This requires a firm concrete bed, with materials fixed in a cement float approximately three-quarters or half an inch thick. Two primary methods are noted:
*   **Roman Mosaic:** This uses various colored marble cubes pressed into the cement float.
*   **Terazzo Mosaic:** A less expensive option where irregularly shaped marble chips are strewn over the floated surface, pressed in with a plasterer's hand float, and finished with an iron roller.

In both mosaic methods, any specific designs are worked into position first, with the remaining ground-work filled in later.

## Historical Applications of Brick and Tile
The use of brick and tile for paving and construction was prevalent in ancient civilizations, particularly where stone was scarce.

### Babylonia and Assyria
In Babylonia, the lack of stone and abundance of clay led to the widespread use of brick. The brick quays of the river Arakhtu (the river of Babylon) were built by Nabopolassar. Additionally, the raised road for solemn processions, the *A-ibur-sabu*, which led from the Gate of Ishtar to E-Saggila, was paved with stone and featured walls lined with enamelled tiles depicting a procession of lions. 

Assyria, influenced by Babylonia, also built its temples and palaces of brick, even maintaining the use of brick platforms in the north despite the marshy soil of Babylonia not being present there.

### Other Regions
In Bagdad, a region lacking timber and stone, the city was constructed entirely of tiles and brick. The buildings of the eras of Mamun and Harun al-Rashid relied on gorgeous coloring and colored tiles for their effect, though these structures have since perished due to inundations and sieges. In Rome, brick and tile were used in the construction of the Pantheon (A.D. 112), specifically in the lower courses of its circular dome.
