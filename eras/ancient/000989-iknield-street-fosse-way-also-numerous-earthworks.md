# Iknield Street, the Fosse Way, and Ancient Earthworks

The study of ancient transport and fortifications in Britain reveals a complex network of roads and defensive structures that indicate early patterns of settlement and military activity.

## The Fosse Way and Roman Road Systems
The Fosse Way is identified as one of five main groups of roads in Roman Britain. Unlike the "Four Great Roads" category, which is considered an invention of antiquaries and unconnected to Roman Britain, the Fosse is a distinct route that joins Lincoln and Leicester with Cirencester, Bath, and Exeter.

The broader Roman communication network in the lowlands of Britain consisted of several other primary groups radiating from London:
* A south-east route leading to the Kentish ports and Canterbury, with Richborough (Rutupiae) being the most frequented port.
* A western route to Silchester, which branched further to Gloucester, Bath, Exeter, Winchester, and South Wales.
* Watling Street, which ran through St Albans Wall and near Lichfield (Letocetum) to Chester and Wroxeter, with a branch providing access to Lincoln and Leicester.
* An eastern route serving York, Lincoln, and the eastern counties, as well as Colchester.

Additionally, there were more obscure routes, such as Akeman Street, which provided an alternative path from London to Bath via Alchester (located outside of Bicester), and another road connecting the Humber with the lower Severn, passing through Birmingham and Derby from near Sheffield.

## Iknield Street and Early Settlements
Iknield Street is described as one of two great ancient roads that intersect at Axminster. The presence of this intersection, combined with the Fosse Way and various hill-fortresses and earthworks in the surrounding area, serves as an indicator of very early settlement in the vicinity of Axminster.

## Earthworks and Fortifications
Earthworks are found across various regions of Britain, often associated with early inhabitants or specific historical conflicts.

### British and Roman Earthworks
In Essex, the urban district of Loughton is located near the river Roding and the eastern outskirts of Epping Forest. In this area are large earthworks known as Loughton Camp, which are believed to be of British origin. In the case of the Roman town of Silchester, a line of earthworks exists on the west side of the site, running in a wider circuit than the city's massive walls.

### Saxon and Danish Era Earthworks
In Buckinghamshire, numerous earthworks and barrows mark the sites of struggles against Danish invaders during the 10th century. These relics are particularly abundant in the vale of Aylesbury, which was likely one of the best protected and richest Saxon settlements of that period.
