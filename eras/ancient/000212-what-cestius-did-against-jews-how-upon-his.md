# Cestius and the Conflict with the Jews

The military campaigns of Cestius, the president of the province of Syria, occurred during the twelfth year of Nero's reign. His actions were precipitated by the conduct of Florus, who sought to provoke a revolt among the Jews to conceal his own illegal practices and enormities from Caesar. Florus falsely accused the Jews of revolting from the Roman government and imputed the start of previous disturbances to them.

## Initial Response and Mobilization
Before the escalation into full-scale war, Cestius had visited Jerusalem during the feast of unleavened bread, where he was besought by approximately three million people to address the calamities caused by Florus. While Cestius assured the multitude that Florus would treat them more gently, Florus continued to augment their calamities to induce a rebellion.

When disturbances finally broke out, Cestius viewed the situation as an opportunity to attack. He assembled a massive force consisting of the entire twelfth legion from Antioch, two thousand soldiers from each of the other legions, six cohorts of footmen, and four troops of horsemen. He was further supported by auxiliaries from various kings: Antiochus provided three thousand footmen, two thousand horsemen, and three thousand archers; Agrippa provided one thousand horsemen and three thousand footmen; and Sohemus contributed four thousand men, primarily archers and horsemen. Additionally, auxiliaries from free cities joined the march.

## Campaigns in Galilee
Cestius, guided by Agrippa, first marched to Zabulon, a strong city in Galilee known as the City of Men. Finding the city deserted as the population had fled to the mountains, Cestius permitted his soldiers to plunder the city and set it on fire. He subsequently overran the surrounding country and burned nearby villages. However, as Cestius retired, Jews unexpectedly attacked the remaining Syrian forces, specifically those from Berytus, killing approximately two thousand of them.

Cestius then dispatched Gallus, the commander of the twelfth legion, into Galilee with sufficient forces to subdue the region. While the city of Sepphoris welcomed Gallus with joy—leading other cities to remain quiet—seditious elements and robbers retreated to Mount Asamon. Gallus engaged these forces; although the Romans initially suffered losses of about two hundred men due to the enemy's superior position, the Romans eventually gained the high ground. The lightly armored rebels were unable to withstand the fully armed Romans and the cavalry, resulting in over two thousand deaths.

## The Siege of Jerusalem
Cestius pursued the Jews to Jerusalem and established a camp at the elevation of Scopus, seven furlongs from the city. After three days of waiting and seizing corn from neighboring villages, he entered the city on the thirtieth of the month Hyperbereteus. He set fire to the timber market and the district of Bezetha (Cenopolis) and pitched his camp opposite the royal palace. 

Despite the opportunity to take the city by force immediately, Cestius was diverted from this attempt by Tyrannius Priseus, the muster-master, and several cavalry officers who had been corrupted by Florus.

During the siege, Ananus, the son of Jonathan, attempted to invite Cestius into the city and open the gates. Cestius overlooked this offer due to anger and skepticism. This delay allowed the seditious elements to discover the treachery; they threw Ananus and his party from the wall and pelted them with stones. 

The Romans attacked the walls for five days without success. On the sixth day, Cestius attempted to break into the temple's northern quarter. Though the Jews repulsed them several times with darts, the Romans utilized the *Testudo* (tortoise) formation, resting their shields upon one another to slide off the darts. This allowed them to undermine the wall and prepare to set fire to the temple gate.

## Retreat and Calamities
Despite the courage of the people and the despair of the besieged, Cestius unexpectedly decided to retire from the city without any just occasion or disgrace. This retreat emboldened the robbers, who attacked the rear of the Roman army. Cestius spent the night at the camp at Scopus, but as he continued his retreat the following day, the Jews continued to harass his flanks and rear with darts. 

The Romans, burdened by heavy armor and afraid to break ranks, suffered great losses. Among the slain were Longinus the tribune, Emilius Secundus (commander of a troop of horsemen), and Priscus, the commander of the sixth legion. The army eventually reached their former camp at Gabao, losing a great part of their baggage. After staying two days, Cestius observed an even greater number of enemies surrounding him and realized that further delay was detrimental.

To accelerate his flight, Cestius ordered the killing of mules and other animals, retaining only those carrying machines and darts. As the army descended through narrow passages toward Bethoron, the Jews blocked their path and pelted them with darts. The Roman cavalry were unable to charge due to the high ascents and steep precipices, leading the soldiers to break into lamentations of despair. The Romans were nearly taken prisoner entirely until nightfall allowed them to reach Bethoron, where the Jews surrounded them and waited for their departure the next morning.

## Aftermath
Following these events, several eminent Jews, including Philip (son of Jacimus) and the brothers Costobarus and Saul, fled the city and joined Cestius. At their own request, Cestius sent Saul and his companions to Nero in Achaia to inform the emperor of their distress and to place the blame for the war upon Florus.
