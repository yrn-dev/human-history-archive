# From the Foundation of Messene and Megalopolis to the Death of Pelopidas

## The Foundation of Megalopolis and Messênê
The establishment of Megalopolis and Messênê represented a strategic effort to weaken Spartan power. The foundation of Megalopolis is associated with a determination by assembled Arcadians to create a federative league and a Pan-Arcadian city shortly after the revolution at Tegea. While Diodorus dates this foundation to 368 B.C., Pausanias suggests it occurred between Midsummer 371 and Midsummer 370 B.C., during the archonship of Phrasikleides at Athens.

The foundation of Messênê is linked to an expedition led by Epaminondas between November 370 and March 369 B.C., occurring during the archonship of Dyskinêtus at Athens. The creation of Messênê resulted in a significant loss for Sparta, acting as an "amputation" of nearly half of its territory and substance. The more fertile western half of Laconia was severed from Sparta and divided among Messênê and other independent cities. This land was primarily tilled by those who had previously been Helots and Periœki of Sparta.

## Strategic Impact and Political Reception
The foundation of these two cities was rooted in a long-sighted view of the conditions of strength for Thebes and weakness for its enemies. Once established, Megalopolis and Messênê took such firm root that Sparta was unable to persuade its own allies to help erase them. 

However, historical accounts of these events vary. Xenophon, in the latter books of his history, omits the foundation of both cities during his description of Epaminondas's winter expedition of 370-369 B.C., though he later refers to them as accomplished facts. Xenophon represents the Theban army as having entered Arcadia simply to repel the Spartans and Agesilaus, and claims they wished to return to Bœotia as soon as the Spartans had departed. Furthermore, Xenophon fails to mention Epaminondas as the general of the Thebans in this expedition or at Leuktra.

## The Role of Epaminondas and Pelopidas
Epaminondas is credited with the sound reasoning behind the foundation of the two cities, though his advice to his countrymen to compete with Athens for naval and transmarine power is viewed as a point of questionable wisdom. Following the victory at Mantinea, Epaminondas died prematurely; it is conjectured that he was uniquely capable of handling the immense power and glory he had achieved without abusing it.

Regarding the leadership of Thebes, Diodorus states that Pelopidas served as Bœotarch without interruption from the revolution of Thebes until his death. Plutarch supports this, noting that Pelopidas was in his thirteenth year of appointment upon his decease. There is some scholarly disagreement regarding the reappointment of Epaminondas; Sievers denies it, while others argue that Epaminondas's role as commander in Peloponnesus in 369 B.C. suggests he was indeed rechosen.

## Athenian Interests and the Spartan Decline
The independence of Megalopolis and Messênê became a central point in Athenian foreign policy. Demosthenes argued that it was in the interest of Athens for both Thebes and Sparta to remain weak so as not to disturb Athenian security. He advocated for the defense of Megalopolis and Messênê against Spartan reconquest, suggesting that Athens should adopt the Theban policy of protecting these cities. By doing so, Athens could narrow Theban influence and exclude them from the Peloponnesus. Demosthenes refused to surrender these cities to Sparta, even when offered assistance in recovering Orôpus or reviving the autonomy of Bœotian cities.

## The Peace Following Mantinea
The death of Epaminondas caused profound sorrow among the victorious soldiers at Mantinea and within Thebes. Following his death, negotiations led to a peace treaty before the army left the Peloponnesus. The Thebans and Arcadians demanded the recognition of the *statu quo*, which included the independence of Messênê and the Pan-Arcadian constitution of Megalopolis.

While Sparta protested the independence of Messênê, none of its allies sympathized with its position. The Athenians, Arcadians, and Eleians all desired to see Sparta weakened and wished for the dismissal of the large armies in Arcadia. Consequently, the autonomy of Messênê was guaranteed by all parties except the Spartans, who remained without friends or auxiliaries, refusing to submit to what they viewed as an intolerable degradation.

## Cultural Legacy
The cities of the Peloponnesus, including Messene and Megalopolis, supported artistic endeavors during their period of stability. Damophon, a sculptor from Messene, executed numerous statues for these cities as well as Aegium. His work includes a group representing Artemis, Persephone, Demeter, and the giant Anytus, fragments of which were discovered at a temple of "The Mistress" in Lycosura, Arcadia.
