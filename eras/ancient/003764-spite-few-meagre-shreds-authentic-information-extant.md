# Analysis of Fragmentary and Meagre Historical Records

In the study of human history, researchers frequently encounter periods or subjects where the available evidence is described as meagre, mutilated, or consisting of only a few shreds of authentic information. This scarcity of data affects various fields, from the study of ancient musical instruments and the biographies of artists to the recording of religious movements and the lineages of ancient peoples.

## The Barbiton and Ancient Instrumentation
The barbiton is an example of an elusive instrument for which only a few meagre shreds of authentic information remain. Despite this, it can be identified through the records of Greeks and Romans. Greek writers classified it with the lyre due to shared features. Information from Arabs and Persians describes it as a chelys-lyre or a kind of lute or *rebab*. This instrument was introduced into Europe via Greece from Asia Minor, and later brought to Spain by the Moors, where it was known in the 14th century as *al-barbet*.

Physical evidence of the barbiton exists in at least four sculptural representations. These sculptures depict an instrument that combines the characteristics of the *rebab*—specifically a vaulted back and a neck that narrows gradually—with the stringing of the lyre. Depending on the period, it featured either seven strings (the best period) or nine (the decadent lyre). While some authors, such as Carl Engel, have represented the instrument as boat-shaped and without a neck, this is attributed to the fact that the junction of the neck and body in the sculptures is often hidden by drapery or the hand, leaving the area in deep shadow.

## Biographical and Personal Records
The archive contains several instances where the personal lives of significant figures are poorly documented. Antonio Allegri (1494–1534), the Italian painter known as Correggio, is noted as a consummate executant and a vivid inventor of pose and expression. However, the narratives regarding the external circumstances of his life are described as meagre, even when the question of their authenticity is set aside.

Similarly, the circumstances surrounding Marco Polo's capture at Curzola lack a contemporary authority. Paulin Paris, a learned Frenchman, noted that Rusticiano became acquainted with Polo in 1298 while both were imprisoned in Genoa, though the motives for Polo's loss of liberty remain unknown. While some biographical notices, such as those by Ramusio, contain errors regarding the interval between the Battle of Curzola and Polo's return, the main fact of Polo's command of a galley and subsequent capture is believed to be derived from genuine tradition.

## Religious and National Histories
The recording of religious history often suffers from the silence of contemporary historians. In the *Augustan History*, composed partly under Constantine, there are fewer than six lines relating to Christians. Similarly, the history of Dion Cassius does not mention them by name. This silence may be attributed to the fact that much of the *Augustan History* focuses on Diocletian, or to Dion's perceived enmity toward religious innovations. Overall, the extant histories of this period are characterized as mutilated and meagre.

In the records of the kings of Israel, a similar meagreness of historical information is observed when religious judgments and editorial details are removed. This is particularly evident in the accounts of Baasha, Nadab, and the successors of Jeroboam (2) in Israel, as well as Manasseh and Abijam in Judah. The compiler of these sources focused on religious significance rather than a strict history of the past, resulting in extreme brevity for kings of great historical importance, such as Uzziah, Jeroboam (2), and Omri.

## Ancient Lineages and Attestations
Despite gaps in knowledge, some authentic records provide strong evidence of ancient alliances. Citizens of Pergamus provided an attestation that Abraham was the father of all the Hebrews and that their own ancestors were friends of the Hebrews in the oldest times. This is supported by public arts extant in their city. Further evidence of the kindred between the sons of Israel and the Lacedemonians, both being posterity of Abraham, is preserved in a public epistle found in the First Book of the Maccabees and the writings of Josephus. Additionally, the Armenian historian Moses Chorenensis records that Arsaces, founder of the Parthian empire, was of the seed of Abraham via Chetura.

## Lost and Extant Physical Evidence
The archive notes the loss of several critical texts. A Greek version of the "Chronicle of John [Hyrcanus's] high priesthood" (also called "The Fourth Book of Maccabees") was extant in the days of Sixtus Senensis and Sautes Pagninus at Lyons, but it was subsequently burnt and lost. However, Sixtus Senensis provided an epitome of this work, which explains that John was called Hyrcanus because of his conquest of a person by that name.

Conversely, some authentic information survives through numismatics. Coins still extant confirm that Antiochus, son of Alexander Balas, was called "The God" (bearing the inscription "King Antiochus the God, Epiphanes the Victorious"). Other extant coins identify figures such as Eraess and Jotape, the daughter of the king of Commageus.
