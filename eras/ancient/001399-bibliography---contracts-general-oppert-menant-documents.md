# Bibliography of Contracts and Legal Documents

The study of contracts and legal documentation spans various historical eras and professional applications, ranging from ancient Near Eastern civilizations to modern architectural practices and general legal theory.

## Ancient Near Eastern Contracts and Laws
A significant body of literature exists regarding the legal and business documents of ancient Assyria, Chaldea, and Babylon. Key scholarly works on general contracts include *Documents juridiques de l'Assyrie et de la Chaldee* (1877) by Oppert and Menant, and *Babylonische Vertrage* (1890) and *Keilinschriftliche Actenstuecke* (1889) by F. E. Peiser. Other notable contributions include J. Kohler and F. E. Peiser's *Aus dem babylonischen Rechtsleben* (1890 ff.) and Br. Meissner's *Beitraege zur altbabylonischen Privatrecht* (1893).

Further documentation of property transfers is found in C. H. W. Johns' three-volume work, *Assyrian Deeds and Documents relating to the Transfer of Property* (1898), as well as his later work, *Babylonian and Assyrian Laws, Contracts and Letters* (1904). Historical context for these documents is provided by H. Radau in *Early Babylonian History* (1900).

Specific attention has been given to the Code of Hammurabi, King of Babylon (dated by some sources to approximately 2250 B.C.). Key texts include the *Editio princeps* by V. Scheil (1902), *Die Gesetze Hammurabis* by D. H. Mueller (1903), and *Hammurabis Gesetz* by J. Kohler and F. E. Peiser (1904). R. F. Harper also published *The Code of Hammurabi, King of Babylon about 2250 B.C.* (1904), and S. A. Cook explored the relationship between these laws and the Laws of Moses in *The Laws of Moses and the Code of Hammurabi* (1903). Additionally, the Semitic dynasty of Ur—including rulers such as Dungi, Bur-Sin, Gimil-Sin, and Ibi-Sin—left behind a large number of contemporaneous business documents and contracts.

## Construction Contracts and Specifications
In the field of building and architecture, the contract serves as a critical document signed by the builder before the erection of a structure. This document ensures the builder carries out the work for a stated sum of money, adhering to the instructions of the architect, bills of quantities, drawings, and specifications. 

The contract typically details:
* The description of workmanship and materials.
* The responsibilities of the builder.
* Indemnity clauses protecting the employer against risks and accidents involving employees.
* The timeline for completion, including penalties for non-completion (though allowances are usually made for strikes, fire, or bad weather).
* The schedule of payments to the builder.

The architect generally prepares this form. Closely linked to the contract is the "specification," a document written by the architect that describes the materials to be used, how they are mixed, and how the work is executed for every trade. Usually, two copies of the specification are produced; the copy kept by the architect is signed as the contract copy.

## Legal Concepts and Terminology
In general law, the concept of "duress" (derived from the Latin *duritia* for harshness or *durus* for hard) refers to constraint or compulsion. Duress is categorized into two types:
1. Personal restraint, including imprisonment or actual violence.
2. Threats (*per minas*), such as threats of grievous bodily harm or immediate death.

Under common law, a contract entered into under duress is considered voidable at the option of one of the parties. In some instances, duress may be pleaded as a defense for an act that would otherwise be classified as a crime, though the extent of this plea remains unascertained.

Historical documents also reveal specific terminology regarding materials used in contracts. For example, a contract from 1398 mentions *achilar* (ashlar), referring to hewn or squared stone used for facing walls, as opposed to *roghwall* (broken stone) used for the interior.
