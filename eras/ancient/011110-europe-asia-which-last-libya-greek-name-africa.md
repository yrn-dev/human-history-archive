# Europe, Asia, and Libya

The conceptualization of the world's geography in antiquity was characterized by varying divisions and shifting boundaries. Early Greek geographers frequently divided the known world into two primary portions: Europe and Asia. In this two-part system, Libya—the Greek name for the continent of Africa—was included within Asia.

## The Three-Part Division
Later, a three-part division became common among the Hellenes and Ionians, who categorized the earth into Europe, Asia, and Libya. Herodotus ranks Libya as one of these chief divisions, separating it from Asia. However, Herodotus notes a logical inconsistency in the Ionian view: they claim the river Nile divides Asia from Libya, yet they often omit Egypt from their calculations. Herodotus argues that if the world is composed of these three divisions, the Delta of Egypt must be counted as a fourth part, as it belongs to neither Asia nor Libya. He observes that the Nile is cleft at the Delta to flow around it, placing that land between Asia and Libya.

## Boundaries and Dimensions
The boundaries of these regions were often determined by arbitrary conceptions rather than natural relations. For Europe, boundaries were suggested as the river Nile in Egypt and the Phasis in Colchis, or alternatively, the Kimmerian ferry and the Maiotian river Tanaïs. While it was known that Europe stretched along the length of both Asia and Libya, it was unknown whether Europe was entirely surrounded by sea. In contrast, Libya was believed to be surrounded by sea except where it bordered Asia. This was supported by the account of Necos, king of the Egyptians, who sent Phenicians to sail from the Erythraian Sea, through the Southern Sea, and back to Egypt via the Pillars of Heracles. These sailors reported that they had the sun on their right hand while sailing around Libya, though Herodotus expresses disbelief in this specific detail.

In terms of size, Herodotus asserts that Europe is beyond comparison larger in breadth than the other two divisions. Regarding soil quality, Libya generally does not greatly excel Asia or Europe, with the exception of the region of Kinyps. This specific area possesses black soil, is watered by springs, and is resistant to both drought and excessive rain, sometimes producing crops three-hundred-fold.

## Etymology and Origins
The origins of the names Europe and Asia are subject to much doubt. Herodotus repudiates common explanations as fables. Some suggest Libya was named after a woman of that country, and Asia after the wife of Prometheus. However, the Lydians claim Asia was named after Asias, the son of Cotys and grandson of Manes, from whom the Asian tribe in Sardis also derives its name. Regarding Europe, some suggest the land was named after Europa the Tyrian, though Herodotus notes she belonged to Asia and traveled only from Phenicia to Crete and then to Lykia.

Other theories suggest the names derive from Assyrian or Hebrew roots: "Europe" signifying the west or setting sun, and "Asia" signifying the east or rising sun. Over time, these terms may have moved from restricted local applications to extended geographical meanings. Even after "Asia" acquired a broader sense, the Greeks continued to use it specifically to designate the country around Ephesus.

## Libya and Africa
In Greek terminology, Libya was the general name for the continent now known as Africa. This term was also used by the Romans. In Roman writing, "Africa" specifically denoted the Roman province of Africa, which included Carthage and significant territory, though the term was commonly used to refer to the entire continent. Some sources indicate that the general name Libya did not include Egypt.

Later geographers continued to debate the precise boundaries between Asia and Africa. While Ptolemy and Strabo fixed the Isthmus of Suez as the boundary, other writers, including Pliny, Sallust, and Mela, preferred the western branch of the Nile or the great Catabathmus. The latter definition would assign not only Egypt but also part of Libya to Asia.

## Inhabitants of Libya
The land of Libya was known to be occupied by four distinct races. Two of these were natives of the soil: the Libyans, who dwelt in the northern parts, and the Ethiopians, who dwelt in the southern parts. The other two races, the Phenicians and the Hellenes, were considered strangers to the land.
