# Fenton John Anthony Hort

## Biography
Fenton John Anthony Hort (1828–1892) was an English theologian born in Dublin on April 23, 1828. He was the great-grandson of Josiah Hort, who served as the archbishop of Tuam during the 18th century. In 1846, Hort moved from Rugby to Trinity College, Cambridge. While there, he formed lifelong friendships and professional partnerships with B. F. Westcott, J. B. Lightfoot, and E. W. Benson.

Hort earned his degree in 1850, placing third in the classical tripos, and was elected a fellow of his college in 1852. In 1854, he co-established the *Journal of Classical and Sacred Philology* alongside Lightfoot and J. E. B. Mayor. Although raised under the strict principles of the Evangelical school, Hort's views shifted toward Liberalism under the influence of Tait and Arnold at Rugby, as well as through his acquaintances with Kingsley and Maurice.

## Academic and Professional Career
Hort married in 1857 and spent fifteen years at the college living of St Ippolyts, near Hitchin, in Hertfordshire. During this period, he wrote essays for various periodicals and participated in discussions regarding university reform. His academic trajectory continued with several prestigious appointments:
* 1872: Fellowship and lectureship at Emmanuel College.
* 1878: Hulsean professor of divinity.
* 1887: Lady Margaret reader in divinity.

In 1871, Hort delivered the Hulsean lectures at the university, titled *The Way, the Truth, and the Life*, though these were not published until many years later.

## New Testament Scholarship
Hort is noted for his extensive work in theological and patristic study, specifically regarding the text of the New Testament. In 1870, he was appointed to the committee responsible for revising the translation of the New Testament. 

In collaboration with his friend Westcott, Hort published an edition of the New Testament text. This work was widely accepted by the Revision Committee as the basis for their translation. Although it faced vehement attacks in some quarters, the publication created a significant sensation among scholars and was generally regarded as the closest approximation to the original New Testament text produced to that date.

Hort also contributed specific textual analyses to the field:
* **Codex Ephraemi Syri Rescriptus**: Hort demonstrated, through an analysis of displacements in the text of the Apocalypse, that this 5th-century uncial palimpsest was copied from a very small manuscript.
* **Codex Claromontanus**: In the *Introduction* (written with Westcott), Hort discussed the relations of this 6th-century Graeco-Latin manuscript to the *codices* Boernerianus, Augiensis, and Sangermanensis.
* **Chronology of the Gospels**: In the appendix of *Westcott's and Hort's New Testament in Greek*, Hort argued that four ancient authorities omitted certain words in the fourth Gospel (vi. 4). He suggested that this omission simplifies the chronology, potentially allowing the Gospel's time-notices to fit within a single year between the Passover of ii. 13 and the Passover of xi. 55, by identifying "the feast" in vi. 4 with the feast of Tabernacles.

## Other Contributions
Beyond his primary work on the New Testament, Hort authored an exhaustive article on "Bardaisan" for the *Dict. Christ. Biog.*. This subject relates to Bardaisan, whom Ephrem acknowledges was in principle a monotheist, and a disciple of whom wrote the *Book of the Laws of the Countries*.
