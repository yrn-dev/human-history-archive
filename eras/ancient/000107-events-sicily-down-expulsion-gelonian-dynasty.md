# Events in Sicily Down to the Expulsion of the Gelonian Dynasty

The history of the Greek colonies in Italy and Sicily during the sixth century before the Christian era is characterized by the presence of powerful and flourishing cities. While early records are limited, the political landscape of Sicily was defined by a transition from original oligarchies to the rule of despots, and eventually to the establishment of popular governments.

## Early Governance and the Rise of Despots
Before 500 B.C., the cities of Agrigentum and Gela were superior to Syracuse. During this era, Phalaris served as the despot of Agrigentum. In Syracuse around 500 B.C., the government was oligarchical, led by the Gamori—privileged descendants of the original proprietary colonists—while the population included the Demos and the Kyllyrii, who were serfs.

In Gela, Kleander served as despot around 500 B.C. It was in his service that Gelo and Ænesidêmus first rose. Gelo, who had previously held high command among the mercenaries of the despot Hippokratês of Gela, eventually succeeded Hippokratês. Hippokratês had been victorious over the Syracusans and had taken Kamarina before his death.

## The Supremacy of Gelo
Upon becoming the despot of Gela, Gelo expanded his power significantly. He seized possession of Syracuse and transferred the seat of his power there, making Syracuse the first city in Sicily. Gelo conquered various Sicilian towns, transporting the existing oligarchy to Syracuse and selling the Demos into slavery. Under his rule, the population and power of Syracuse increased.

By 481 B.C., Gelo's influence was such that envoys from Athens and Sparta sought his aid. Gelo sought to strengthen Sicilian Hellenism against barbaric interests in the islands. During the simultaneous invasion of Greece by Xerxes, the Carthaginians under Hamilkar invaded Sicily and besieged Himera. Gelo achieved a complete victory over the Carthaginians at the Battle of Himera and subsequently granted them peace. The prisoners taken during this battle were distributed among Carthaginian cities, which contributed to their prosperity, particularly that of Agrigentum. Gelo was eventually succeeded at Syracuse by his brother, Hiero, who was a harsh ruler and experienced conflict with their brother Polyzêlus.

## The Expulsion of the Gelonian Dynasty
The extinction of the Gelonian dynasty at Syracuse led to the expulsion or retirement of other despots throughout the island. This revolution allowed the various Grecian cities to reorganize themselves into free, self-constituted popular governments. This transition was more than a political shift; it involved large-scale transfers of property and inhabitants. The preceding despots had exiled many old citizens, transplanted others across Sicily, and established settlements for mercenaries and emigrants.

Following the overthrow of the dynasties, much of this was reversed. After a period of severe commotion, an accommodation was reached where adherents of the expelled dynasty were settled in the territory of Messêne and the re-established city of Kamarina on the southern coast. In Syracuse, the citizens fortified the city, including the island and Achradina.

## Aftermath and Social Consequences
The transition to popular government brought about significant social friction. Aristotle notes that the Syracusans admitted foreign mercenaries to citizenship after the Gelonian dynasty. However, because these mercenaries—who had been the primary citizens under the dynasty—became inferior and were barred from honors under the new government, they rebelled.

Despite these conflicts, the period following the expulsion of the Gelonian dynasty was marked by a state of prosperous and active freedom that lasted approximately fifty years, characterized by an absence of internal despots and external invaders. This era of popularized government in Sicily and Italy also saw the rise of influential figures in rhetoric and dialectics, such as Empedoklês of Agrigentum.
