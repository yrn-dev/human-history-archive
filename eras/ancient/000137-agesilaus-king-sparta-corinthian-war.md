# Agesilaus King of Sparta and the Corinthian War

## Accession of Agesilaus
Following the death of King Agis, a dispute arose regarding the legitimacy of his son, Leotychides. Agesilaus aspired to the throne, receiving the support of Lysander. Despite an objection based on his lameness, Agesilaus was preferred as king after Lysander provided an interpretation of an oracle produced by Diopeithes. Once in power, Agesilaus demonstrated energy and unscrupulous partisanship, successfully conciliating the ephors and exerting great influence within Sparta.

During this period, Sparta faced internal instability. A dangerous conspiracy led by Kinadon, fueled by widespread discontent and inequality, was uncovered by the ephors. Kinadon was seized, interrogated, and executed, and his accomplices were arrested.

## Origins of the Corinthian War
The Corinthian War emerged from a growing disposition of hostility against Sparta, which had attained an overruling ascendency throughout the Peloponnesus. This dominance included the expulsion of the Messenians from the neighborhood of the Peloponnesus to Sicily and Kyrênê.

While Sparta was engaged in war against Persia, the satrap Tithraustes sent money into Greece to incite conflict against the Spartans. This funding found success in Argos, Corinth, and Thebes. While the Persian money did not create the hostility, it brought out pre-existing hostile tendencies. Initially, this manifested as the Bœotian war between Sparta and Thebes. During this conflict, the Spartan general Lysander was repulsed and slain before Haliartus. Following his death, an alliance was formed against Sparta consisting of Thebes, Athens, Corinth, and Argos, later joined by the Eubœans.

## Military Campaigns in Corinth
The conflict in Corinth was characterized by shifting control of strategic fortifications. The anti-Spartan allies initially occupied the lines of Corinth from sea to sea, while Lacedæmonians established themselves at Sikyon. Within Corinth, a philo-Laconian party emerged, but the government attempted to forestall a conspiracy via a *coup d'état*, banishing many members of the party. However, the leader Pasimêlus remained and eventually admitted Lacedæmonian forces within the Long Walls of Corinth. The Lacedæmonians were victorious in a battle within those walls, inflicting severe losses on the Argeians and pulling down a portion of the Long Walls to open passage.

The Athenians later restored these walls, prompting an expedition by King Agesilaus. In concert with his brother Teleutias, who served as admiral in the Corinthian Gulf, Agesilaus launched a joint land and sea attack. They successfully retook the Long Walls and captured the port of Lechæum, including its docks and ships. This victory broke the naval power of Corinth in the Krissæan Gulf and established Lechæum as a permanent Lacedæmonian garrison.

## Further Operations and the Isthmian Festival
Agesilaus continued his campaign by marching into the territory of Argos, where he ravaged the cultivated plain. He also intervened in the Isthmian festival, disturbing the celebration to allow Corinthian exiles under his protection to celebrate it; the city's residents performed the ceremony again after his departure.

Agesilaus further extended his success by attacking and capturing Peiræum, along with the Heræum, taking many prisoners and significant booty. Despite this triumphant position, and subsequent peace solicitations from Thebes, Agesilaus treated the Theban envoys contemptuously.

## The Conclusion of the War and Aftermath
The conflict eventually moved toward a resolution involving the independence of Messênê. Thebes insisted that no peace could be entertained without a formal recognition of Messênê's independence. While the Corinthians and other allies agreed, Sparta resolutely refused to relinquish the territory or recognize their former slaves as equal neighbors. The Spartan prince Archidamus, son of Agesilaus, urged his countrymen to spurn the mandates of Thebes and fight alone if necessary to reconquer Messênê.

The war eventually concluded with the Peace or Convention of Antalkidas. This agreement functioned as an edict issued by Persia upon Greece, marking a loss of Pan-hellenic dignity as Sparta sought Persian help. Through this peace, Sparta became the practical mistress of the Corinthian isthmus and Corinth.
