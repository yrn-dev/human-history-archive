# Date-Palm Cultivation and the Euphrates Region

## Geography and Agriculture of the Lower Euphrates
The lower reaches of the Euphrates and Tigris rivers, including their joint stream known as the Shatt el-Arab, are characterized by vast plantations of date-palms. These plantations produce dates recognized as the finest known. In the vilayet of Basra—an area of 16,470 square miles formed in 1884 from the southern districts of the Bagdad vilayet—settled populations are found primarily along the river banks. 

While date-palms are dominant, other crops are cultivated in the region, including rice-fields, wheat, barley, liquorice, and roses. The vilayet also produces sesame, cotton, tobacco, durra (a maize-like coarse grain), and various fruits such as pomegranates, figs, bananas, lemons, and oranges. The natural landscape is largely treeless, with the exception of the tamarisk found along river-beds and swamps. Timber for the country is provided by the date-palm, white mulberries, poplars, and the solitary *sifsaf* tree.

## Irrigation and Land Management
The fertility of the Babylonian territory historically depended on intensive irrigation. Because the land received little rain, the crops were ripened through watering from the river. This was achieved not by the river overflowing the fields, but through watering by hand or using swing-buckets. The territory was divided into channels, the largest of which were navigable for ships and ran from the Euphrates toward the Tigris.

To manage the water supply of the Euphrates, stupendous labor was employed to create embankments, artificial reservoirs, and curvilinear channels to regulate the stream's rapid flow. Broad and deep canals crossed the space between the Euphrates and the Tigris, feeding a network of ditches and rivulets. Despite these historical efforts, in the vilayet of Basra, many ancient canals have been allowed to fall into ruin. Consequently, the land is subject to inundations that make settled agriculture impracticable, leading a large portion of the population to live as semi-nomadic or nomadic tribes.

## The Date-Palm in Babylonia
In the Babylonian land, the date-palm grew across the entire plain, with most being fruit-bearing. The inhabitants utilized the fruit of these palms to produce honey, wine, and solid food. The cultivation of these trees involved a specific practice where the fruit of "male-palms" was tied to date-bearing palms; this was done so that the gall-fly from the male-palm could enter the date to ripen it and prevent the fruit from falling off.

## Regional Trade and Economy
In the Basra region, the principal exports include dates, wool, cereals, horses, gum, and liquorice-root. The population of the vilayet is estimated at 200,000, consisting almost exclusively of Moslems (three-quarters of whom are Shi'ites), with approximately 6,000 Christians and 4,000 Jews. The Sabaeans or Mandaeans, a curious sect, maintain their headquarters near Suk esh-Sheiukh.

## Sacred Perspectives on Date-Palms
The Qur'an presents the date-palm as a sign for people who believe, describing how water sent from the skies produces vegetation of all kinds. It teaches that from the date-palm and its sheaths (or pollen), clusters of dates hang low and near. The text further describes the production of gardens of date-palms and vines through water sent from the sky in measure and settled in the earth, providing abundant fruits for people to eat.

## Other Date-Producing Regions
Beyond the Euphrates, date-palms are found in other regions. In Persia, the district of Darab in the province of Fars is noted for its immense plantations of date-palms and the production of a great quantity of dates. Similarly, in Jericho, there were plantations of palm trees and balsam trees.
