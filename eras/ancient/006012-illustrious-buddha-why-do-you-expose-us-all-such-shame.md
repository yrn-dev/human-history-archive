# The Life and Teachings of Gotama Buddha

## Return to Kapilavastu
After abandoning his asceticism and becoming a Wanderer—an itinerant teacher and preacher—Gotama was urged to return home by his father, Suddh[=o]dana. Suddh[=o]dana had watched his son's career with anxiety and wished to see him once more before his death. Upon arriving at Kapilavastu, the Buddha stopped in a grove outside the town. While his father and uncles came to visit him, they were angry and refused to show him reverence. Contrary to the custom of inviting such teachers and their disciples for a meal the following day, the family departed without extending an invitation.

## The Practice of Mendicancy
The following day, Gotama entered the city carrying his bowl to beg for food. Although he considered going directly to his father's house, he decided to adhere to his custom of begging. When Suddh[=o]dana learned that his son was walking the streets as a beggar, he hurried to Gotama and asked why he exposed his family to such shame, questioning if it was necessary to go from door to door when his father could supply the needs of many mendicants.

Gotama responded that this was the custom of his race. When Suddh[=o]dana argued that no one from their illustrious Kshatriya line had ever acted so indecorously, Gotama clarified that while the family claimed Kshatriya descent, his own descent was from the prophets (Buddhas) of old, who had always acted in this manner. He stated that the customs of the law (Dharma) are beneficial for both this world and the world to come. Gotama then invited his father to share the "treasure" he had found, describing it as the most precious of jewels. Following this, Suddh[=o]dana took his son's bowl and led him home.

## Parable of the Spiritual Farmer
In another instance, the Buddha sought to preach to a wealthy brahmin farmer during a harvest home. The Buddha stood by the field with his alms-bowl to beg. The farmer questioned why the Buddha begged instead of ploughing and sowing to earn food. The Buddha replied that he also ploughed and sowed. 

When the brahmin asked how this was possible since no one saw him farming, the Buddha explained his spiritual cultivation:
*   **Seed:** Faith
*   **Fertilizing Rain:** Self-combat
*   **Plough:** Wisdom (with modesty as the guiding-shaft)
*   **Power:** Perseverance
*   **Rein:** The mind
*   **Field:** The law
*   **Weeds to be destroyed:** Cleaving to existence
*   **Harvest:** The never-dying nectar of Nirv[=a]na

The Buddha taught that those who reap this specific harvest destroy all weeds of sorrow.

## Final Teachings and Passing
Near the end of his life in Kusin[=a]r[=a], the Buddha received Subhadra, a brahmin philosopher. When asked if the six great teachers knew all laws, the Buddha stated that there is only one way to true wisdom: the path laid down in his system. He claimed that by conquering pride, anger, and lust, followers could become free from doubt, ignorance, and wrong belief, entering a state of universal kindliness and reaching Nirv[=a]na. The Buddha noted that he had striven for pure and perfect wisdom since the age of twenty-nine.

As he approached death, the Buddha instructed his disciples that he would not truly leave them, as they possessed his words, his explanations of truth, and the laws he had established for the society. He urged his followers to reverence one another and offered to clear up any remaining doubts regarding the Buddha, the law, or the society. He emphasized the importance of the final perseverance of the saints.
