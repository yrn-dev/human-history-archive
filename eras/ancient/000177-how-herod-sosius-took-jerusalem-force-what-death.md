# The Capture of Jerusalem by Herod and Sosius

The capture of Jerusalem by Herod and the Roman commander Sosius marked the end of the Asamonean government and the establishment of Herod as king. This military operation was the culmination of political maneuvers in Rome and a strategic siege of the city.

## Military Preparation and Strategy
Three years after being made king at Rome, Herod moved his army toward Jerusalem following the end of winter. He positioned his camp near the section of the city wall that was most vulnerable to assault, specifically pitching his camp before the temple. Herod's approach mirrored the tactics previously used by Pompey; he constructed three bulwarks, erected towers, and employed a large workforce to cut down surrounding trees to facilitate the attack.

While the army remained before the city, Herod temporarily departed for Samaria to complete his marriage to Mariamne, the daughter of Alexander and granddaughter of Aristobulus.

## The Combined Forces of Herod and Sosius
Following the wedding, Herod returned to Jerusalem with a significant army. He was joined by Sosius, a commander sent by Antony to assist him. Sosius had marched his forces through the midland parts, while Herod had traveled along Phoenicia. 

The combined military strength gathered at the north wall of Jerusalem consisted of:
* Eleven regiments (or legions) of footmen.
* Six thousand horsemen.
* A substantial number of Syrian auxiliaries.

In total, the forces accompanying the king were approximately thirty thousand. The legal and political basis for the operation was twofold: Herod relied upon the decree of the Senate that had named him king, while Sosius relied upon the authority of Antony. Their objective was to remove Antigonus, who had been declared an enemy at Rome, and secure the government for Herod.

## The Fall of the City and Aftermath
Herod and Sosius successfully took Jerusalem by force. Upon gaining power, Herod began a process of rewarding and punishing the city's inhabitants. He promoted private citizens who had supported his party, specifically honoring Pollio the Pharisee and his disciple Sameas, who had advised the citizens to receive Herod during the siege. Conversely, Herod spent his days avenging and punishing those who had sided with his enemies.

Following the victory, Herod sought to prevent his foreign auxiliaries from desecrating the holy house. He used exhortations, threats, and force to restrain the soldiers' eagerness to see the sacred areas of the temple, believing that such a violation would be worse than a defeat.

## The Plunder and the Fate of Antigonus
To prevent the Romans from emptying the city of its men and money—which Herod feared would leave him the king of a "desert"—he pleaded with Sosius to forbid the spoiling of the city. When Sosius argued that plunder was a just reward for the soldiers' suffering during the siege, Herod offered to pay every soldier a reward from his own funds. Through this magnificent bounty, Herod purchased the deliverance of his country, ensuring that every soldier and commander left in a wealthy condition.

In gratitude, Sosius dedicated a crown of gold to God. He then departed Jerusalem, taking Antigonus away in bonds to Antony. Antigonus, despite maintaining a desire and hope for life until the end, was executed by the axe.
