# The Evolution of Ballet in England

## Origins and Early Development
The term "balette" first entered the English language in 1667, credited to Dryden. The first instance of a descriptive ballet performed in London occurred in 1702 at Drury Lane with the production *The Tavern Bilkers*. Following this debut, ballet in England remained purely exotic, largely mirroring developments occurring in France.

## The 19th Century and Royal Patronage
The first half of the 19th century is regarded as the palmy days of English ballet. During this era, the art form served as a fashionable attraction maintained through the expenditure of royal revenue. This period featured several famous dancers, including Mdlle Taglioni—who is said to have turned the heads of an entire generation—as well as Carlotta Grisi, Fanny Elssler, Mdlle Cerito, Miss P. Horton, Mdlle Carolina Rosati, and Miss Lucile Grahn.

## Transitions in Style and Technique
In later years, the art form saw the emergence of Kate Vaughan, a dancer of a new type described by Sir Augustus Harris as having helped elevate modern art. Vaughan was responsible for making skirt-dancing popular. While skirt-dancing is considered a beautiful exhibition, it is viewed by some students of the art as a departure from true dancing because the steps are of little importance. This style eventually evolved into exhibitions characterized by whirling draperies illuminated by many-coloured lime-lights. Among the disciples and imitators of Miss Vaughan were Miss Letty Lind and Miss Sylvia Grey, both of whom contributed to the art. Conversely, Adeline Genee became the finest exponent in London of the older, classical school of ballet-dancing.

## Shift Toward Spectacle
Over time, ballet-dancing was affected by a trend in modern entertainment that prioritized the eye—the sense most easily affected—over intellectual appreciation and intelligence. Consequently, the art gradually transformed into a spectacle where the primary interest became independent of the actual dancing. 

In this developed form of spectacle, thousands of pounds are spent on dressing a small army of women. These performers do little more than group themselves according to designs of mass and colour and march about the stage. The intelligence of the audience is asked only to believe in the theme of the costume; for example, a ballet dressed in military uniforms is presented as a glorification of or a compliment to the military.
