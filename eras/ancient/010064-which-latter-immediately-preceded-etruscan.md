# The Etruscans

The Etruscans were a non-Aryan people, likely related to the Aegean peoples, who established themselves in the central part of the Italian peninsula. In a reversal of the typical regional process, the Etruscans subjugated various Aryan tribes. Their civilization is generally dated from approximately 600 to 400 B.C., following an earlier Umbrian period.

## Geography and Urban Centers
In ancient times, the Etruscans controlled a territory reaching from the Alps to the sea on both sides of Italy. This region was characterized as a well-watered, thickly wooded country with abundant pasturage. The influence of the Etruscans is preserved in ancient geography; the lower sea is known as the Etruscan Sea, and the upper sea, the Adriatic, takes its name from the Etruscan city of Adria.

At one period, the region contained eighteen large, fair cities with thriving commercial populations. Among these was Bononia (modern Bologna), which classical writers claim was founded from Perusia by Ocnus or Aucnus under the name Felsina. Another significant site was Volsinii, which, according to adopted theory, occupied the site of Orvieto.

## Culture and Material Life
Etruscan burial practices provide significant insight into their society. They utilized "hut-urns" to contain incinerated remains. Their tombs were designed to resemble the dwelling-houses of the living, featuring panelled walls, coffered ceilings, and furniture hewn from living rock, including stools, couches, and easy chairs with attached footstools.

The Etruscans influenced the dress and customs of later Roman society. The *tebenna* was the primary male garment; the *toga praetexta* worn by Roman priests and magistrates, characterized by a purple border, is derived from this Etruscan origin. For public appearances, the *tebenna* was worn over a short tunic similar to the Greek chiton. Noblemen on festal occasions wore a *corona Etrusca* (wreath) and a *bulla*, a circular gold locket containing a charm against evil. Male head-dress included the *apex* (a pointed hat) or the *galerus* (a leather hat worn by the Lucumos). Additionally, the fashion of shoes worn by Roman senators is said to have originated in Etruria.

## Relationship with Rome
Rome was originally a small trading city at a ford on the Tiber with a Latin-speaking population. While traditional chronologies date the founding of Rome to 753 B.C., Etruscan tombs found in the Roman Forum date to an earlier period. For a time, Rome was ruled by Etruscan monarchs, specifically the Tarquins. However, the tyrannous conduct of these rulers led to their expulsion in the sixth century B.C. (specifically 510 B.C.), after which Rome became a Latin-speaking aristocratic republic.

Despite the expulsion of the kings, the Romans continued to struggle with the Etruscans. For a long period, the Romans were unable to capture Veii, an Etruscan fortress located only a few miles from Rome that served as a constant threat.

## Decline and Fall
Although the Etruscans were more numerous, better armed, and more civilized than the early Romans, two major disasters weakened them, allowing Rome to eventually master them. The first was a war in 474 B.C. against the Greeks of Syracuse in Sicily, which resulted in the destruction of the Etruscan fleet. 

The second disaster was a massive raid by the Gauls from the north. Toward the end of the fifth century B.C., the Gauls swarmed into North Italy and occupied the valley of the Po, driving out the inhabitants of the eighteen large cities and occupying them. Caught between the "hammer and anvil" of the Gauls to the north and the Romans to the south, the Etruscans were defeated. The Romans finally captured the fortress of Veii and assimilated the Etruscan people, extending Roman power across central Italy from Naples to the Arno by a few years before 300 B.C. Following these events, the Etruscans disappeared from history.
