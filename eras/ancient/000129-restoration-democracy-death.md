# FROM THE RESTORATION OF THE DEMOCRACY TO THE DEATH OF ALKIBIADES

## The Restoration of Athenian Democracy
The restoration of democracy in Athens occurred following periods of violent interruption and political instability. One such restoration followed the deposition of the oligarchy of the Four Hundred. This oligarchy had been installed in the senate-house around February or March of 411 B.C. and was deposed approximately four or five months later, around July of the same year. This period of "danger and distraction" had brought Athens nearly into the grasp of its enemies before the democracy was reinstated.

A subsequent restoration occurred after the expulsion of the Thirty. The government of the Thirty was characterized by the complete subversion of all laws and the violent execution of many persons. While Diodorus places the date of this restoration in 401 B.C., this is considered a chronological error; the real date of the re-establishment of the democracy was 403 B.C. Plutarch also appears to have believed the interval between the surrender of Athens and the restoration of the democracy was longer than it actually was.

## Legal and Institutional Recovery
Following the expulsion of the Thirty and the return of the democracy, the functions of the senate of the Areopagus were restored. This included the authority to try accusations for intentional homicide. Evidence for this restoration is found in the mention of the trial of Pyrilampês for murder before the Areopagus by the anonymous biographer of Thucydidês, as well as references in the writings of Xenophon and Lysias. It is argued that the Areopagus could not have continued to hold sittings or try homicide cases during the government of the Thirty due to the violent nature of that regime and its disregard for law.

## Comparative Democratic Contexts
The nature of democratic collapse and restoration is observed in other Greek contexts, such as Syracuse. Aristotle suggests that the Syracusan democracy prior to the despotism of Gelo was ruined by its own disorder and lawlessness. However, the narrative of Herodotus presents a different view, suggesting the expulsion of the Gamori was a rising of slaves and free subjects against a governing oligarchy. In this instance, the restoration by Gelo followed the expulsion so closely that the democracy had no time to constitute itself or demonstrate its capacity for government. The submission of the Syracusan people is attributed to the superior force Gelo brought to aid the expelled Gamori. It is noted that the disorder preceding the despotism of Dionysius may have been a primary factor in his acquisition of supreme power, whereas democracy was only just beginning in Greece during the earlier times preceding Gelo.

## Diplomatic and Political Pressures
During these periods of instability, external and internal pressures influenced the Athenian system. In one instance, a club assembled to treat for the abolition of the democracy in the city and the restoration of Alcibiades. This proposal was intended to make Tissaphernes a friend of the Athenians, with Pisander and others designated as the embassy to Athens to negotiate these terms.
