# Bosnia

Bosnia is a region located in the Balkan Peninsula. In 1909, the political division of Bosnia and Herzegovina, under the administration of Austria-Hungary, covered an area of 19,696 square miles with a population of 1,568,092. Geographically, Bosnia borders Dalmatia to the north-west.

## Prehistoric and Ancient Period
Archaeological evidence from Bosnia provides insight into the development of primitive Illyrian civilization from the neolithic age onward. Key sites include the lake-dwellings at Butmir and the cemeteries of Glasinac and Jezerine. These locations have yielded weapons, iron and bronze ornaments, and stone and horn implements, as well as later objects crafted from glass, amber, tin, and silver. The La Tène and Hallstatt cultures are particularly well represented in these findings.

Additionally, Bosnia is noted as one of the locations where early safety-pin type brooches, known as *fibulae*, occurred. These objects, which likely originated in central Europe toward the close of the Bronze Age (somewhat before 1000 B.C.), appeared in Bosnia in a simple form similar to the modern safety-pin.

## Medieval and Early Modern History
During the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries, the despots of Bosnia, along with those of Servia, maintained a relationship with the city of Ragusa in the Adriatic. Ragusa derived significant financial gain through contracts to work the silver and gold mines belonging to these princes.

The city of Banjaluka, which may have been the Roman fort marked as *Castra* on the *Tabula Peutingeriana* (located on the road from Servitium in Pannonia to Salona on the Adriatic), rose to prominence in the 15th century following the fall of the rival stronghold of Jajce. Throughout the subsequent centuries, Banjaluka became a site of frequent encounters between Turks and Austrians, specifically in 1737, 1688, and 1527. By the latter half of the 18th century, no other Bosnian city surpassed Banjaluka in importance or prosperity. In 1831, Hussein Aga Borberli, known as *Zmaj Bosanski* ("the Dragon of Bosnia"), departed from Banjaluka to wage a holy war against Sultan Mahmud II.

## Modern Political History
Under the Treaty of Berlin in 1878, the provinces of Bosnia and Herzegovina, which were part of the Turkish Empire, were administered by Austria. On October 5, 1908, Austria formally proclaimed the annexation of these provinces. This abrupt action was taken by the Austrian Foreign Minister, Count Aerenthal, to forestall a potential reassertion of Turkish sovereignty following the Young Turk Revolution of that summer.

The annexation caused a significant European crisis. It was viewed as a violent act and a public affront to Russia, the Great Power with the most interest in the region, and a personal slight to the Russian negotiator, Monsieur Isvolsky. Within Austria, the crisis eclipsed domestic affairs, including the celebrations of the emperor's Diamond Jubilee in 1908.
