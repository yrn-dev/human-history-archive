# Jesus Christ, Son of God, Saviour

The figure of Jesus Christ is presented across various historical and theological contexts as a Saviour and the Son of God. These designations have served as the foundation for diverse interpretations of his nature, the rite of baptism, and the process of human salvation.

## Theological Interpretations of Nature and Identity
Historical records indicate significant disputes regarding the constitution of the Deity and the nature of Christ. The Trinitarians, led by Athanasius, taught that the Father, the Son, and the Holy Ghost were three distinct Persons but one God. In contrast, the Arians, following Arius, taught that Christ was less than God. A third view, held by the Sabellians, suggested that Christ was a mode or aspect of God, with God serving as Creator, Saviour, and Comforter in the same way one man might be a father, guest, and trustee.

Other early systems presented more varied views. According to Irenaeus, the supreme God sent his *Nous*, also called Christ, into the world to address worldly confusion. In the system of Basilides, the Saviour was termed *diakonos* or *pneuma diakonoumenon*. Basilides acknowledged an earthly Jesus but denied his sinlessness. Some followers of Basilides believed the Saviour appeared only as a phantasm, while others, according to the *Excerpta ex Theodoto*, believed the *Diakonos* descended upon Jesus at his baptism in the form of a dove. This connection between a mythical god-saviour (*Heros*) and Jesus of Nazareth led to the celebration of the day of baptism, the *epiphaneia*, as a high festival.

## The Concept of Re-birth and Baptism
The idea of re-birth is closely linked to the rite of baptism. Some interpretations of Luke iii. 22 ("Thou art my beloved Son, this day have I begotten Thee") suggest that Jesus was re-born of the Spirit as a Messiah and adoptive Son of God. This "adoptionist" school of Christology is reflected in the writings of Hilary, who stated that Jesus was born again through baptism to become a perfect son of God, having previously been the son of man.

This concept of spiritual rebirth is further illustrated through the symbolism of the fish. Tertullian wrote, "We little fishes, after the example of our Fish Jesus Christ, are born in the water," suggesting that salvation is found by abiding in the water. The Greek word for fish (*ICHTHUS*) was later explained by the orthodox as an acronym for "Jesus Christ, Son of God, Saviour."

The practice of baptism evolved from the baptism of John, which served as a purification for some and a means of establishing Messianic hope for others. In the apostolic age, this was identified with Jesus of Nazareth, with formulas such as "into Christ Jesus" or "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ." Paul described this as being equivalent to entering "into the death of Christ Jesus," allowing the believer to be raised from death into everlasting life.

## Mythology and Ritual Developments
Around the idea of re-birth, various "mythoplastic growths" emerged. The baptismal font came to be viewed as the womb of the virgin mother church. In some early churches in Gaul, this church was not seen as an abstraction but as a divine aeon who sympathized with and watched over the children of her womb.

Additionally, the role of sponsors (*anadochi*), later known as god-fathers and god-mothers, developed into a spiritual relation. Canonists eventually identified this spiritual bond with the blood-tie of real parents. This led to the conclusion that children sharing a god-parent were spiritual brothers and sisters, creating a marriage taboo in the Eastern and Latin churches, though this "fiction" was later set aside by reformed churches.

## Visions of Salvation and Influence
The vision of salvation varies by source. In the speculation of Basilides, salvation involves the "great separation of the sexes" and the restoration of all things, where that which was combined for evil is separated once more. In other contexts, such as the writings of Justin, regeneration through the washing with water in the name of the Father, the Saviour Jesus Christ, and the Holy Spirit is linked to the remission of past sins.

The influence of Jesus Christ as the "subverter of idolatry" is also noted in relation to the cessation of oracles. The Fathers alleged that oracles did not cease suddenly at Christ's birth, but gradually as his salutary doctrines and Gospel became known to mankind.

In modern contexts, such as the 1905 formation of the Baptist World Alliance, the "essential oneness in the Lord Jesus Christ, as their God and Saviour" serves as a unifying principle for churches of the Baptist order and faith worldwide.
