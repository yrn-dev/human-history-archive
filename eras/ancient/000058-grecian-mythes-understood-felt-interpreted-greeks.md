# Grecian Mythes: Understanding, Feeling, and Interpretation

The mythes of the early Greeks constituted their entire mental stock, serving as a primary means of interpreting the world. Arising from a state of mind characterized by a tendency toward universal personification and an absence of positive knowledge, these narratives filled the void of understanding with a personifying faith. What may appear to modern readers as poetical fancies were, to the early Greeks, serious realities.

## Nature and Function of Early Mythes
In the Homeric age, mythical faith and a religious point of view were paramount. The early Greek public was largely non-literate—relying on seeing, hearing, and telling—and lacked records or a grasp of positive history and the invariable sequences of nature. To regularize the phenomena they observed, they developed a theory based on the agency of intelligent, voluntary beings who were superior in power to humans.

These mythes took the form of personal narratives and adventures featuring gods, heroes, and nymphs. While these figures possessed distinct characters, they were ascribed a wide diversity of human motives and attributes. To the Greek mind, these beings belonged to both the present and the past; while they were worshipped in groves and festivals in the current day, their more intimate and sympathizing agency was cast back into the unrecorded past. The productive genius of the community used these mythes to give body and detail to these shadowy divine pre-existences.

## Religion and Mythology
A distinction exists between Greek mythology—the stories about divinities—and religion strictly understood, which comprised public and private religious service. While some myths reflected ritual or interpreted cult-ideas—such as the stories of Theseus, the trial of Orestes, the sorrow of Demeter, the triumph of Dionysus, or the abolition of human sacrifice—mythology as a whole was irresponsible and unchecked by dogma or sacerdotal prohibition.

Unlike later theological concepts, "faith" in the sense of a will to believe specific dogmatic statements was not recognized in Hellenic ethics. No individual felt compelled to believe any particular myth. Religion was an affair of ritual, prayer, and religious formulae rather than doctrine. However, a man who proclaimed a total disbelief in the gods and refused to participate in community rituals could be viewed as "suspect" and potentially persecuted.

## The Transition to Scientific Thought
The intellectual landscape shifted as the Grecian mind advanced socially, ethically, and intellectually. The opening of Egypt to Grecian commerce around 660 B.C. contributed to progress in geography, history, and society. By 500 B.C., the commencement of physical science—led by figures such as Thales, Pythagoras, and Xenophanes—introduced the study of an impersonal nature.

This development created a "scission" between the multitude and a class of superior, instructed men, including philosophers, historians, and critics. For these instructed men, the literal mythes became dissonant with their habits of judgment. However, because the mythes were a cherished inheritance interwoven with patriotic retrospect and national worship, they could not be easily repudiated.

## Interpretation and Rationalization
To reconcile their respect for the past with their new intellectual standards, critical men began to transform the mythes. They interpreted, decomposed, and altered the stories to find a "supposed real basis," promoting the "interpreted mythe" into a reality while degrading the "literal mythe" into a fiction. This process involved manufacturing fancied chronologies and dogmas of original philosophy while retaining mythical names.

Different figures handled this transition in various ways:
* **The Poets:** Pindar and tragic poets like Aeschylus and Sophocles maintained the grandeur of the mythical world. In contrast, Euripides was accused of introducing exaggerated rhetoric and pathos, thereby vulgarizing mythical heroes.
* **The Logographers:** Figures such as Hekataeus worked to rationalize the mythes.
* **The Philosophers:** Socratês, Hippocratês, and Anaxagoras navigated the opposition between the scientific method and the religious feelings of the multitude.

## Allegorical Theories
Some investigators, notably Creuzer, proposed that mythes originated from an instructed body of priests from Egypt or the East. According to this hypothesis, these priests used symbols and allegories to communicate refined monotheism and theological philosophy to the "barbarous" Greeks. Over time, these allegorical mythes were adopted by poets and confounded with purely narrative, epical mythes. The original secret doctrines were then preserved only within mystical ceremonies and religious fraternities, such as the Bacchic, Orphic, Samothracian, and Eleusinian mysteries, where initiates could hear the cosmogonic doctrine under strict secrecy.
