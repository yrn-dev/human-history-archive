# Demosthenes' De Corona

*De Corona* (On the Crown) is a significant oration by the Athenian statesman Demosthenes. The text serves as a primary source for various political, military, and administrative events in ancient Greece, though some of its included documents are viewed with skepticism by historians.

## The Golden Crown and the Theban Alliance
The oration relates to a period of conflict involving Philip of Macedon, specifically during the autumn and winter of 339-338 B.C. During this time, war was conducted in Phokis and on the frontiers of Bœotia. Demosthenes describes how the Athenians and Thebans maintained their positions against Philip and achieved advantages in two specific engagements: the "winter-battle" and the "battle on the river."

Following these successes, Demomeles and Hyperides proposed that Demosthenes be awarded a wreath of gold for his role as the chief adviser of the Theban alliance. Although a citizen named Diondas impeached the mover of the motion, claiming the decree was illegal, Diondas failed to secure even one-fifth of the votes from the Dikastery and was fined one thousand drachms. Demosthenes was subsequently crowned with a public proclamation during the Dionysiac festival in March 338 B.C.

## Political and Military Narratives
In *De Corona*, Demosthenes addresses the causes leading to the declaration of war. He asserts that war became inevitable after ships were plundered, Chersonesos was ravaged, and an individual marched toward Attica. Contrary to some historical citations (such as those from Philochorus), Demosthenes explicitly states in the text that he did not make the motions for the decisive resolution to declare war.

The text also touches upon the status of Eubœa. Demosthenes describes the Thebans attempting to make Eubœa their own, while other records indicate the Thebans had enslaved the cities of the region.

## Administrative and Legal Reforms
*De Corona* provides details regarding the trierarchy and trierarchic symmories in Athens. Demosthenes describes a reform of the law concerning these contributions. Under previous laws, the leaders of the symmories were required to contribute eleven parts, which Demosthenes claims burdened poor citizens while costing the wealthy very little. His reform proposed that contributions be based on actual property (essence). This reform is supported by a fragment from Hyperides.

## Diplomatic Missions and the Amphiktyonic Assembly
The oration mentions the spring meeting of the Amphiktyonic assembly at Delphi in February or March 339 B.C. The Athenians appointed Diognetus as Hieromnemon (chief legate), supported by three vice-legates (Pylagoræ): Thrasykles, Meidias, and Æschines. Demosthenes claims that Æschines' name was submitted without anyone's prior knowledge and that he was elected with only two or three votes. Due to Diognetus falling ill with a fever upon arrival at Delphi, the responsibility of speaking at the assembly fell to Æschines.

Additionally, Demosthenes identifies Daochus and Thrasylaus as Thessalian partisans of Philip.

## Authenticity of Documents
*De Corona* contains several documents presented as verbatim records, though their authenticity is disputed by critics. These include:
* A decree regarding the movement of Athenian movable property to a place of safety, moved by Kallisthenes. This document is dated the 21st of Mæmakterion, a date considered wrong as the decree likely passed in late Skirrophorion.
* A decree dated the 16th of Skirrophorion under the archonship of Nausikles. This is regarded as a pseudo-eponymous archon, and the document's implication that Athens was moving toward war is viewed as contrary to the facts of the time.
* Four other documents relating to the capture of Elateia by Philip, including two decrees under the archon Heropythus and two answers from Philip addressed to the Athenians and Thebans.
* A decree passed by the Senate on the 3rd of Munychion regarding envoys.

## Later Educational Use
The orations of Demosthenes, including *De Corona*, continued to be studied in later centuries. In 1555, the scholar Ascham used *De Corona* and the orations of Aeschines as Greek reading material for Princess Elizabeth.
