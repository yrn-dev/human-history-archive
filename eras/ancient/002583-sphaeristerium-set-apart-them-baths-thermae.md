# Sphaeristerium

The *sphaeristerium* was a specialized area set apart within the Roman baths (*thermae*) specifically for the playing of ball games. In Roman society, ball games were viewed as an adjunct to the bathing experience, with the activities being graduated according to the health and age of the bathers.

## Ball Games in Antiquity
Ball games, utilizing a rounded body with a smooth surface, are considered to have originated in the earliest times, as rolling objects appeal to human infants as well as animals. Evidence of ball games is found in various ancient cultures:
*   **Egypt:** Portrayals of ball games exist on Egyptian monuments.
*   **Greece:** In the works of Homer, Nausicaa is described as playing ball with her maidens. Additionally, Halios and Laodamas performed ball play accompanied by dancing before Odysseus and Alcinous.
*   **Primitive Societies:** Ball games are still played among the least advanced savage tribes.

Among the Greeks, games with balls (*sphairai*) were seen as useful subsidiaries to more violent athletic exercises. They were used to render the body graceful and keep it supple, though these games were generally left to girls and boys. In contrast, the scriptures of the Hebrews contain no mention of the ball, as they are described as the least athletic of races.

## Greek Ball Game Variations
While few regular rules for ball games have survived, the *Onomastikon* of Pollux of Naucratis provides names for various forms of play that suggest specific actions:
*   ***Aporraxis***: The act of putting the ball on the ground using an open hand.
*   ***Ourania***: A game where the ball is flung into the air to be caught by two or more players.
*   ***Phaininda***: A game of catch involving two or more players where feinting is employed to test the skill and quickness of the participants.

## The Roman Thermae
The *thermae*, or public baths, were central to Roman social and physical life. These establishments varied in scale and luxury. Seneca compared the baths of Scipio Africanus at his villa of Liternum to the public baths of Rome, which grew in magnificence over time, preceding the construction of the stately Thermae of Diocletian and Antoninus. Admission to these baths cost a *quadrans*, which was a quarter of an *as* (approximately one eighth of an English penny).

Architecturally, the *thermae* utilized advanced roofing. While the conical vault of the baths in Pompeii is not classified as a dome, the great hemicycles in the *thermae* were certainly roofed with semi-domes. The Laconicon in the Baths of Titus (A.D. 72) may have also been domed.

Examples of these structures are found across the Roman world. In the quarter of Dermeche, the imposing remains of the baths of Antoninus are preserved. In France, the town of Bagneres-de-Luchon is celebrated for its thermal springs and a bathing establishment known as the Thermes, which is identified with the *Onesiorum Thermae* of Strabo.
