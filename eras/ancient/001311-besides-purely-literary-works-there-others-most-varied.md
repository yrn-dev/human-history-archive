# Non-Literary and Varied Works of Human History

While literary productions often dominate historical records, the archives reveal a vast array of works of a varied nature that extend beyond purely literary pursuits. These include official and private communications, scientific treatises, technical records, and industrial developments.

## Babylonian Scientific and Administrative Records
In Babylonia, records existed that were not purely literary, including collections of private and partly official letters, most notably those of Khammurabi. A significant portion of Babylonian intellectual output was dedicated to astronomy and astrology. Astronomy had a long standing in the region; a standard work written from an astrological perspective was translated into Greek by Berossus and was believed to date back to the era of Sargon of Akkad.

Babylonian achievements in this field include the invention of the zodiac and the ability to foretell eclipses of both the moon and the sun. The state maintained observatories attached to temples, from which astronomers sent regular reports to the king. Early records show that stars were named and numbered, and the archives contain observations of the phases of Venus as well as tables of lunar longitudes. By the Parthian and Seleucid periods, these reports had become thoroughly scientific in character.

This astronomical progress was supported by advancements in mathematics. The Babylonians developed a simple method of ciphering and utilized a duodecimal system. Their numerical units included the *soss* (60), which corresponded to a degree of the equator, the *ner* (600), and the *sar* (3600). Physical evidence of this mathematical capability includes tablets of cubes and squares calculated from 1 to 60 found at Senkera. Furthermore, the use of the pulley, the lever, the clepsydra, and the sun-dial indicates a sophisticated knowledge of mechanics. At Nimrud, Layard discovered glass vases bearing the name of Sargon and a crystal lens turned on a lathe; such a lens may have been used for observing the heavens or to produce the excessive minuteness found in some Assyrian tablet writing.

## Industrial and Technical Developments
In other regions and eras, "varied works" manifested as industrial specialization. In the Vale of Leven and riverside towns east of Dumbarton, a "hive of industry" developed. This included the distinctive industry of Turkey-red dyeing, supported by the soft and pure water of the Leven. By the early 18th century, bleaching was established, followed by cotton-printing at Levenfield in 1768. Large-scale operations for printing and dyeing yarns, calicoes, and other cloths were conducted in towns such as Renton, Jamestown, Bonhill, and Alexandria.

Technical progress in this region also included the erection of the first power-loom mill at Milton. Other significant industrial works included the Singer sewing-machine works at Kilbowie, barge-building shops of the Clyde Trust at Dalmuir, and famous shipbuilding yards and engineering works at Clydebank and Dumbarton. Additionally, the area featured breweries and distilleries in Milngavie, Dumbarton, Bowling, and Duntocher, while Kirkintilloch and Cumbernauld became centers for the mining of ironstone and coal, as well as saw-mills and chemical works.

## Historical and Critical Analysis of Documents
The study of historical records often involves distinguishing between different types of texts. In the decipherment of cuneiform, scholars encountered thousands of names across business documents, votive inscriptions, historical records, and literary productions.

In the field of Biblical learning, De Wette advanced the practice of historical criticism. He moved beyond literary appreciation and analysis to determine the validity of evidence and the worth of documents as records. Applying these principles to the Old Testament, he analyzed the Pentateuch and the *Books of Chronicles*. De Wette argued that the *Books of Chronicles* were untrustworthy, a view he used to clarify the history and religion of the Israelites. He further asserted that *Deuteronomy* was a work of the 7th century B.C. and argued that the theory of the Mosaic origin of institutions in the Pentateuch was incompatible with the history of the Israelites as described in *Kings*, *Samuel*, and *Judges*.
