# BONA DEA

Bona Dea, meaning the "good goddess," was an ancient Roman deity associated with fruitfulness, specifically regarding women and the earth. In Roman tradition, she was identified as a Dryad and the wife of Faunus. Through later syncretism, she was also identified with Maia and Ops. Her association with Maia is attributed to the fact that the dedication day for her temple on the Aventine was May 1st.

## Cult and Rituals
The worship of Bona Dea was characterized by strict exclusivity and secrecy. The cult was attended and cared for by women only; no man was permitted to approach the festival or remain in the house during the celebration of the rites. According to Cicero, the real name of the goddess remained unknown to men.

The goddess was honored with an annual sacrifice in the house of a magistrate possessing *imperium*, such as a consul or the *Praetor Urbanus*. This sacrifice was performed by the magistrate's wife or mother, and took place in the presence of the Vestal Virgins. The rites were celebrated by night, with evening festivals featuring music and mirth.

Specific taboos and symbols defined the cult:
* **Taboos:** Wine and myrtle were forbidden.
* **Symbols:** Snakes, representing the medicinal art, and herbs with healing properties were kept in her temple.
* **Sacrifice:** The victim offered to the goddess was a *porca* (sow), referred to as *damium*.

## Greek and Foreign Influences
There are several theories regarding the origins and influences of the Bona Dea cult. Some sources suggest the goddess was a Phrygian deity and the mother of King Midas. The Greeks identified her as Gynæceia and considered her one of the mothers of Dionysus. In Greek tradition, the name of Dionysus must not be uttered, which explains the practice of covering tents with vine-leaves during the festival.

The Roman goddess was also linked to the Greek deity Damia. The names *damium* (the sacrifice), *Damia* (the goddess), and *damiatrix* (the priestess) are likely Greek. Damia was worshipped in Greece and at Tarentum, where a festival called *Dameia* was held. It is probable that the Greek cult of Damia was engrafted onto the original Roman goddess, possibly following the conquest of Tarentum in 272 B.C. Because of these overlapping elements, it is no longer possible to clearly distinguish the Greek components from the Roman ones.

## The Profanation by P. Clodius
The mysteries of Bona Dea became a matter of significant political and social noise in Rome due to an incident in 62 B.C. Publius Clodius, a man of Patrician rank known for his arrogance and impudence, profaned the mysteries. Clodius was in love with Pompeia, the wife of Julius Caesar. While Caesar's mother, Aurelia, kept a strict watch over Pompeia's apartment to prevent interviews between the lovers, Clodius managed to breach the restrictions of the festival.

The resulting scandal led to legal charges against Clodius. However, in 61 B.C., Quintus Fufius Calenus, acting as tribune of the people, was instrumental in securing Clodius's acquittal.
