# The Golden Eagle Is Cut To Pieces

## The Installation of the Golden Eagle
King Herod erected a large golden eagle of great value over the great gate of the temple, dedicating it to the structure. Herod noted that the building of the temple cost him a vast sum, asserting that the Asamoneans, during their one hundred and twenty-five years of government, had been unable to perform a work for the honor of God as great as this. However, the installation of the eagle was contrary to the law of the fathers, which forbids those who live according to it from erecting representations or images of any living creature.

## The Conspiracy of Judas and Matthias
Judas, the son of Saripheus, and Matthias, the son of Margalothus, were celebrated interpreters of the Jewish laws and eloquent men beloved by the people for their education of the youth. Upon discovering that Herod was suffering from an incurable and unusual distemper, they argued that such misfortunes were a result of the king's rashness in erecting works forbidden by the law. 

Judas and Matthias persuaded their scholars to pull down the golden eagle. They argued that the virtue of this action was more advantageous than the pleasures of life, as it would allow them to die for the preservation of the law of their fathers. They taught that such an act would secure them everlasting fame, commendation from the present generation, and serve as an example for posterity, thereby alleviating the common calamity of death.

## The Destruction of the Eagle and Aftermath
Acting on these teachings, a group of young men waited until rumors spread that the king was dying. At midday, while a great number of people were present in the temple, they used thick cords to lower themselves from the top of the temple and cut down the golden eagle with axes.

The king's captain of the temple responded by bringing a body of soldiers and capturing approximately forty of the young men. When brought before Herod, the men confessed to the act, stating they had done so by the command of the law of their country. When questioned about their joy in the face of death, they replied that they would enjoy greater happiness after they were dead.

## Public Mourning and Political Unrest
Following the executions, the men who had been slain were not granted funeral mourning due to the fear people held for Herod. Later, those desiring innovations gathered in crowds to mourn the men who perished for the temple and the laws of their country. These lamentations were solemn and loudly heard throughout the city.

The multitude demanded that punishment be inflicted upon those honored by Herod. Specifically, they called for the removal of the high priest whom Herod had appointed, requesting a replacement of greater purity and piety. When Archelaus later took power, he was pressured by the people to grant these requests as revenge. Although offended by their importunity and intending to travel to Rome for Caesar's determination regarding his government, Archelaus sent his general to persuade the people to remain quiet and avoid appearing seditious, promising to consult with them once he was established in government.

## The Final Days of Herod
In his final period, Herod became disturbed and passionate after discovering that letters forged against his sister Salome and others had been used to deceive him. He suspected that letters against Alexander were also forged and sought to punish Antipater for his crimes. However, a severe distemper restrained his pursuit.

Before his death, Herod altered his testament. He made Antipas king, neglecting Archelaus and Philip because Antipater had ruined their reputations with him. He bequeathed a thousand talents to Caesar, and approximately five hundred talents to his friends, freed-men, children, and wife. He also provided Salome with most splendid gifts and bequeathed large quantities of money and land to others. Herod eventually commanded that Antipater be slain; he survived Antipater by five days before he died.
