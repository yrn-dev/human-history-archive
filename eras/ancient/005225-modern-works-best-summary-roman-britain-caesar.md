# Roman Britain and the Invasions of Julius Caesar

## Modern Summaries and Authorities
For those seeking a modern summary of Roman Britain and the invasions led by Julius Caesar, the work *Ancient Britain* (1907) by T.R. Holmes is cited as the best summary, noting that the author references numerous authorities. Other relevant works for the study of early Britain include J. Rhys's *Celtic Britain* (3rd ed., 1904), Boyd Dawkins's *Early Man in Britain* (1880), and Sir John Evans's *Ancient British Coins* and *Stone Implements, Bronze Implements*. 

Additional specialized sources include A. Hadrian Allcroft's *Earthwork of England* (1909) regarding ancient earthworks, and the works of Arthur J. Evans in *Archaeologia* (vols. lii.-lv) and J.M. Kemble and A.W. Franks' *Horae Ferales* (1863) concerning late Celtic art.

## Pre-Roman British Society
By the time of Julius Caesar, the inhabitants of Britain—with the possible exception of some tribes in the far north—shared Celtic speech and customs. They were politically organized into separate, often warring tribes, each led by its own princes. Their dwellings varied from hill forts with stone or earth walls and villages of round huts sunk into the ground (similar to those in northern Gaul) to subterranean chambered houses or pile-dwellings in marshes.

In the south, town life and market centres began to emerge, alongside improved housing. These southern tribes utilized a gold coinage and a currency of iron bars or ingots. These ingots typically weighed 1-1/3 lb, though some weighed two-thirds of a pound or 2-2/3 lb.

The social and religious structure was heavily influenced by the Druids. This priesthood taught secret lore, wielded significant influence, and practiced magical arts and human sacrifice, though they generally did not participate in politics. In terms of dress, the British—excluding the wilder sorts of the fens and north—wore hats, cloaks, sleeved coats with skirts cut above the knee, and loose trousers similar to the Gauls. Red was the favorite color for striped and checkered coats and trousers, while cloaks were typically blue or black. Chiefs adorned themselves with golden arm-rings and torques, as well as pins and brooches made of ivory or metal. Druid bards wore blue vestments, while leeches and star-gazers wore green.

## Caesar's Expeditions to Britain
Julius Caesar's involvement with Britain followed his campaigns in Gaul. In 55 B.C., after dealing with the Tencteri and Usipetes tribes near the Rhine—where he constructed a famous bridge in ten days—Caesar launched his first expedition to Britain. This initial attempt involved only two legions and resulted in little more than a landing on the coast of Kent.

In 54 B.C., Caesar returned for a second crossing, this time with a larger force consisting of five legions and 2,000 cavalry, departing from Portus Itius (Wissant or Boulogne). During this expedition, Caesar penetrated into Middlesex and crossed the Thames. He was opposed by Cassivellaunus, a British chieftain and ruler of the region north of the Thames. Cassivellaunus utilized war-chariots to harass the Roman columns. After several indecisive engagements, Caesar captured the camp of Cassivellaunus.

## The Terms of Peace and Aftermath
Following the fall of his camp, Cassivellaunus was forced to make peace with Caesar. The conditions of this peace included the provision of hostages and the payment of a tribute. However, these promises were not kept, and the tribute was never paid. The physical evidence of this conflict persisted for centuries; according to Bede, the remains of the entrenchments of Cassivellaunus were still visible seven or eight centuries later.

## Caesar's Written Accounts
The primary records of these events are found in Caesar's own writings. The *Commentarii de bello Gallico* (Gallic War), consisting of seven books written in 51 B.C., narrates the Gallic campaigns. While some ancient figures, such as Asinius Pollio, and modern critics have questioned the veracity of his writings, the *Gallic War* is generally considered to have stood the test of criticism regarding its narrative accuracy. In contrast, his *De bello civili* (Civil War), which records the struggle between Caesar and Pompey (49-48 B.C.), is noted to contain some misrepresentations, particularly in its opening chapters where it conflicts with the correspondence of Cicero or geographical facts.
