# The Basilica

The term "basilica" is of Greek origin, derived from the adjective *basilike*, meaning "royal." In its original context, it was likely paired with a feminine substantive such as *stoa* or *domus*. An early example was the *basileios stoa* in Athens, where the court of the Areopagus sometimes assembled and the *archon basileus* conducted business. A similar name was applied to a grand structure built by Herod the Great at Jerusalem along the southern edge of the Temple platform, which featured a columned fabric with aisles, a nave, and clerestory lighting.

## Definition and Civil Use
In Latin literature and inscriptions, a basilica is defined as a large covered building capable of accommodating many people. Strictly, these were buildings located near a city's business center, designed for the use of litigants, merchants, and those in public service. However, the term was also used in a derived sense for various large structures, including:
*   Covered promenades or halls of audience in private palaces.
*   Riding schools (*basilica equestris exercitatoria*).
*   Markets or stores for goods, such as flowers (*basilica floscellaria*) or clothing (*basilica vestiaria*).
*   Meeting places for religious associations.

## Development in Rome and the Empire
The first basilica in Rome, the Basilica Porcia, was erected in 184 B.C. by the censor M. Porcius Cato, who had recently visited Athens. By the age of Augustus, at least five basilicas existed near the Roman forum, including the extensive Basilica Julia. Other significant Roman examples include the Basilica Fulvia-Aemilia, the Basilica Ulpia (built by Trajan around A.D. 112), and the vaulted basilica of Maxentius or Constantine on the Via Sacra from the early 4th century.

Public basilicas were nearly universal across the empire. In Italy, evidence of this exists as early as the death of Augustus; Suetonius records that the emperor's body rested in the basilica of every town (*in basilica cujusque oppidi*) as it was transported from Nola to Rome. Private basilicas used for recreation or business also existed, such as the one in the palace of Domitian on the Palatine.

## Architectural Forms
While there is variety in surviving plans, the "normal" form of the basilica was essentially a columned structure with a central space surrounded by galleries, often in two stories, and clerestory lighting. 
*   **Standard Schemes:** The basilicas at Pompeii, Julia, Ulpia, and Aemilia featured central spaces surrounded by piers or columns.
*   **Variations:** Some structures, such as the palace basilica of Domitian and examples in North Africa and Silchester, had colonnades on the long sides but not the ends. Others, like those at Timgad and Trier, had no interior divisions. The basilica of Maxentius was entirely vaulted, resembling the halls of Roman Thermae.
*   **Features:** Fixed tribunals are found at Silchester, Timgad, and Pompeii. In contrast, the Basilica Julia had no tribunal, despite being used for the centumviral court.

## Transition to Christian Use
In the age of Constantine and his successors, the term "basilica" was applied to large buildings used for Christian worship. While some private basilicas in mansions and palaces were granted to Christians—such as S. Maria Maggiore and S. Croce in Gerusalemme (originating from the Liberian and Sessorian palaces)—most existing Christian basilicas were built from the ground up for sacred purposes. A theological romance, *The Recognitions of Clement*, mentions that Theophilus of Antioch gave "the basilica of his house" for a church after being converted by St Peter.

Christian basilicas differed from normal civil basilicas in key ways: they rarely had galleries over side aisles and were characterized by a semi-dome apse terminating the central nave. There is no known instance of a vaulted apse in a normal columned civil basilica, with the possible exception of Silchester.

## Construction and Regional Examples
In Rome, early Christian basilicas were often constructed using materials from public buildings and desecrated temples. This led to an architectural decadence where columns of different diameters and heights were used in the same colonnade, and different orders (such as Composite, Ionic, and Corinthian) stood side by side, as seen at S. Maria in Trastevere. At S. Lorenzo fuori le Mura, pilasters were even used horizontally as entablatures. In contrast, the basilicas of Ravenna exhibit greater harmony and unity because they did not rely on such repurposed materials.

Other notable basilican churches include:
*   **The East:** St Demetrius at Thessalonica, St Philip at Athens, Bosra in Arabia, Xanthus in Lycia, and the monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai.
*   **North Africa:** Over 130 examples have been noted in Algeria.
*   **Europe:** A vast hall at Trier (possibly built by Constantine) and remains at Silchester, Lincoln, Chester, and Uriconium in England. Later examples include an unaltered basilica from the time of Charles the Great at Michelstadt in Germany and the 11th-century basilica of St Mauritius near Hildesheim.
