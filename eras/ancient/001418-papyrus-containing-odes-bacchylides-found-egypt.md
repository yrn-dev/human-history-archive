# The Bacchylides Papyrus

The Bacchylides papyrus is an Egyptian manuscript discovered by natives in 1896. It represents the oldest known Greek manuscript and dates to the age of Alexander the Great. Upon its arrival at the British Museum in the autumn of 1896, the document was in approximately 200 pieces. It was subsequently reconstructed by Mr. F. G. Kenyon, who served as the editor of the *editio princeps* in 1897.

## Physical Structure and Content
The reconstructed manuscript is organized into three distinct sections:
*   **First Section:** Contains 22 columns of writing, ending after the first eight verses of Ode xii.
*   **Second Section:** Comprises columns 23 through 29. Column 23 is largely missing, containing only the final letters of two words. This section includes the remains of Odes xiii and xiv, the latter being the final of the *epinikia*.
*   **Third Section:** Comprises columns 30 through 39. It begins with the mutilated opening of Ode xv (the first of the dithyrambs) and concludes after verse 11 of the final dithyramb, titled *Idas*.

The columns typically contain 34 or 35 lines, though the number varies between 32 and 36. The papyrus contains nineteen poems: thirteen or fourteen *epinicia* (odes of victory) and six odes dedicated to heroes or festivals of the gods.

## The Poetry of Bacchylides
Bacchylides of Ceos (c. 504-430) was the nephew of Simonides and the youngest of the three great lyric poets. Before the 1896 discovery, he was known only through scanty fragments. His work is categorized into two primary classes: *Odes of Victory* and *Dithyrambs*.

The *epinikion* (ode of victory) evolved from the *humnos* (song in praise of a deity). While Stesichorus first composed hymns for heroes, and Simonides became the earliest recorded writer of *epinikia*, Bacchylides continued this tradition. His victory odes often contain valuable information regarding legendary history and court life.

Bacchylides' style is characterized by simple and elegant writing, a love for picturesque detail, and "cabinet pictures." Unlike Pindar, who used terse and didactic *gnomai* (maxims), Bacchylides' moralizing is described as an utterance of quiet meditation. In his mythical narratives—such as the stories of Croesus in Ode iii, Heracles and Meleager in Ode v, and the Proetides in Ode x—he employs a gentle flow of simple epic narrative rather than focusing on isolated, forceful scenes. He is also noted for his Homeric development of similes, focusing on decorative value, such as comparing the shades of the departed to leaves fluttering on the headlands of Ida.

## Professional Life and Rivalries
Bacchylides was born in Iulis on the island of Ceos, likely to a father named Meidon and a mother who was the sister of Simonides. He was later banished from his native city and found a new home in Peloponnesus, a region where his genius ripened and where he gained intimate knowledge of local legends, such as those of the river Asopus and the Arcadian cult of Artemis Hemera.

Like Pindar and Simonides, Bacchylides visited the court of Hiero I of Syracuse. Evidence suggests he was a guest of the prince; Ode iii (468 B.C.) was possibly written at Syracuse and pays compliment to Hiero's taste in poetry. A scholium suggests that Hiero preferred Bacchylides' odes to those of Pindar. This preference may have fueled a rivalry; Alexandrian scholars interpreted some of Pindar's passages as hostile allusions to Bacchylides, suggesting Pindar viewed him as a jealous rival whose skill resulted from study rather than innate genius. Notably, in 468 B.C., Bacchylides alone was commissioned to celebrate Hiero's victories, though Pindar composed an ode for a different Syracusan victor that same year.

## Legacy and Reception
Bacchylides was included in the Alexandrian "canon" of the nine lyric poets. His work was studied by Horace and commented upon by the grammarian Didymus (c. 30 B.C.). His popularity persisted into the first four centuries of the common era, with the emperor Julian noted as an admirer of his work.

Ancient critics provided varying assessments of his merit. In the treatise *Peri Hupsous* (attributed to Cassius Longinus or a first-century writer), the author describes Bacchylides as a "faultless" poet of "perfect elegance and finish." However, the author places him in the "second class," arguing that while he avoids glaring faults, he does not reach the loftier heights of "greatness" achieved by Pindar.
