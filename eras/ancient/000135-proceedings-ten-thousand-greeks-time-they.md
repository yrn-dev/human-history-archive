# Proceedings of the Ten Thousand Greeks

## Overview of the Force
The army known as the Ten Thousand Greeks consisted of established citizens who had entered into a special service under Cyrus. Unlike permanent mercenary soldiers, these individuals intended to return to their homes and families following a year of lucrative enterprise. This gravitation toward their homes remained a steady operative force throughout their proceedings.

By the time the army was mustered at Kerasus, despite experiencing extreme sufferings and losses during their retreat, the force still numbered over ten thousand military persons. This total included eight thousand six hundred hoplites, as well as bowmen, slingers, peltasts (or targeteers), and other specialists. Such a military presence was unprecedented in the Euxine.

## Strategic Position and Capabilities
Due to their numbers, discipline, and acquired self-confidence, the Cyreian army possessed a strength that even the city of Sinôpê could not have matched in the field. Because the army did not belong to any specific city or receive orders from an established government, they were comparable to the mercenary armies of the fourteenth century in Italy led by Condottieri.

Their presence created several geopolitical possibilities:
* They could have served as formidable auxiliaries for Korylas the Paphlagonian or the inhabitants of Trapezus should they have wished to challenge the dominion of Sinôpê.
* They possessed the numbers necessary to establish a new colony on various tempting sites, which potentially could have surpassed Sinôpê in importance.

The primary factors restraining the army from pursuing such schemes were their general education and Hellenic sympathies.

## Geographical Observations
During their movements, the army encountered various landmarks. One such point of interest was the mountain summit Thêchê. From this eminence, the sea became visible in a manner that was sudden, impressive, and distant. 

There has been historical difficulty in reconciling the descriptions provided by the historian Xenophon with the geography of the region. While some suggest Thêchê may be the same as Karakaban, others argue that a person coming from the land-side would find the sea in a descending route rather than an ascending one. An alternative theory suggests that Thêchê was a peak separate from the main road, and a guide may have led the soldiers there specifically to show them the sea before returning them to their path. It is also possible that a gap in the mountains on Tekiah-Dagh allowed the sea to be visible.

## Timeline of Proceedings
The proceedings of the Ten Thousand Greeks are documented from the period when they reached Trapezus through to their eventual junction with the Lacedæmonian army in Asia Minor. This period followed their retreat and their subsequent muster at Kerasus.
