# The Turks

## Origins and Early History
The Turks first appear in history around A.D. 560, subjugating the country north of the Oxus. According to Chinese chronicles, the Turks were once in subjection to the Jwen-Jwen, as were the Ephthalites (originally a tribe of the Yue-Chi known as the Hoa or Hoa-tun). While the Ephthalites' customs resembled those of the Turks, the two groups remained distinct and fought one another.

Tradition suggests a common origin for the Turks and Mongols, specifically a shared tradition of descent from a wolf. In Turkish tradition, this figure is called Thou-men (or Bertezena). In A.D. 552, Thou-men took the title of Il Khan and Kha-Khan. The region of Great Bucharia, which includes the steppes at the foot of the Altai, is referred to as Turkistan, a name identical to the Turan of Persian poetic legend.

## Central Asian and Western Expansions
Between A.D. 560 and 580, the Turks expanded their influence, beating down opposition and seizing Bosporus in the Crimea. Their pressure in Asia precipitated the movement of the Avars toward the West. In western Asia, the Turks assisted the Sassanid king Chosroes Anushirwan in destroying the power of the Ephthalites in A.D. 557. Through the annihilation of the Ephthalites, the Turks completed the ruin of the "White Race" of the plains stretching from the Don to the Oxus.

The Turks also subjugated most of the small principalities of the Kushan or Tochari. Later, the Khazars emerged from the land of Barsilia and took rule over Bulgarian hordes that had been left masterless by the Turks, forcing some to migrate to the Danube in 641. By 625, Byzantine annals describe the Khazars as "Turks from the East," a powerful nation controlling the coasts of the Euxine and Caspian. The khakan of this nation provided 40,000 men to Heraclius for the Persian war, contributing to the victory over Chosroes at Nineveh.

## The Ottoman Turks and European Conflicts
The Ottoman Turks originated in Bithynia, establishing a monarchy in Asia and Europe under the reigns of Othman, Orchan, Amurath the First, and Bajazet the First. This expansion eventually posed a significant danger to the Greek Empire and Constantinople. In 1097, during the First Crusade, forces from Europe captured Nicæa and Antioch, though they left the Turks in Konia unconquered.

In later centuries, the Ottoman power clashed with the Austrian monarchy under Emperor Leopold I. Between 1662 and 1664, a war occurred due to Ottoman aggression in Transylvania, ending with a victory for Montecuculi at St Gothard on August 1, 1664. The resulting peace of Vasvar left the Turks in possession of the fortress of Ersekujvar and Nagyvarad. 

A subsequent conflict arose in 1683 when the Turks, invited by Hungarian malcontents and encouraged by Louis XIV, overran Hungary and reached the walls of Vienna. On September 12, 1683, John Sobieski gained a victory over the Turks, saving the Austrian capital. Subsequent campaigns between 1683 and 1699 drove Ottoman power out of Hungary.

## Culture and Religion
The term "Bairam" is a Perso-Turkish word meaning "festival," used to describe the two primary festivals of Islam. The "Lesser Festival" (_Kutshuk Bairam_ or _Sheker Bairam_), also known as _'Id al-Fitr_, occurs during the first three days of the month of Shawwal, following the fasting month of Ramadan. It is characterized by official receptions, private visits, the giving of presents, new clothes, and visits to the graves of relatives.

The "Greater Festival" (_Qurban Bairam_), known as _Al-'id al-kabir_ or _'Id al-adha_, falls on the tenth and following days of the month of Dhul-hijja. This festival commemorates the ransom of Ishmael with a ram; pilgrims slay a cow, camel, he-goat, or ram in the valley of Mina. Throughout the Muslim world, those who can afford it sacrifice a legal animal to consume or give to the poor. Both festivals follow a lunar calendar.
