# THE PELOPIDS

The Pelopids represent one of the most distinguished and poetically significant legendary genealogies in the Peloponnêsus. Characterized by splendor, pathos, and a recurring family curse that inflicted wounds across successive generations, the lineage is central to Grecian epic, lyric, and tragic poetry.

## Lineage and Early Figures
The family's eponymous figure is Pelops, who serves as the eponym of the Peloponnêsus. The lineage includes Tantalus and Niobê. Pelops is noted for his victory in a chariot race against Œnomaus, the king of Pisa, which led to his establishment of a principality at Pisa. The family is further defined by the deduction of the sceptre of Pelops and specific kingly attributes associated with the line.

Following Pelops, the genealogy includes Chrysippus, as well as Atreus and Thyestês. The history of the family is marked by "family horrors" and tragedies. This line continues through Agamemnôn and Menelaus, and extends to figures such as Ægisthus, Helen, Klytæmnêstra, Orestês, Elektra, and Hermionê.

## Political Influence and Decline
The Pelopids held significant ascendancy in the Peloponnêsus. Agamemnôn, a member of this house, held the chief command during the Trojan war. According to Dictys Cretensis, the principal heroes involved in that expedition were all kinsmen and Pelopids; they received a large sum of gold from Agamemnôn and swore an oath not to lay down their arms until Helen was recovered.

The city of Mykênæ held great legendary importance in connection with the goddess Hêrê and the Pelopids. However, Mykênæ eventually declined, a process that coincided with the rise of Sparta and Argos. Consequently, the figures of Agamemnôn and Orestês were transferred to Sparta in later accounts. Orestês, the son of Agamemnôn, is recorded as the figure who maintained the family's ascendancy in the peninsula.

## Interactions with the Herakleids
The Pelopids are often contrasted with the Perseids, the other distinguished mythical family of the region. The Herakleids (the sons of Hêrakleids) were expelled fugitives who sought to return to the peninsula. A significant conflict occurred when Hyllus, son of Hêrakleids, perished in single combat against Echemus of Tegea. Echemus was connected to the Pelopid line through his marriage to Timandra, the sister of Klytæmnêstra. 

As a condition of this duel, a solemn compact was established stating that no further invasions of the peninsula by the family of Hyllus would be attempted for one hundred years. After this period expired, the Herakleids returned as companions and leaders of the Dorians—a northerly Greek group—and successfully established themselves.
