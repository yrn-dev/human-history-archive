# Wanderers and Travelling Teachers

In various historical and cultural contexts, the figure of the wanderer—specifically the travelling teacher or ascetic—has played a significant role in the intellectual and spiritual life of societies. These individuals often abandoned conventional domesticity to pursue knowledge, spread doctrines, or practice rigorous self-discipline.

## The Wanderers of Ancient India
In ancient India, the figure of the hermit or wandering beggar was established long before the advent of Buddhism. These ascetics followed a path of strict renunciation. For young men entering this life, the only formal initiation required was a period of residence in the house of a teacher. Upon completing these duties, the student was expected to "go forth remaining chaste."

The lifestyle of these wandering teachers was characterized by extreme abstinence. According to the *Apastamba*, the ascetic lived without a house, fire, protection, or pleasures. They remained silent, speaking only during the daily recitation of the Veda, and survived by begging for only as much food as was necessary to sustain life. Their attire consisted of clothes discarded by others, and some practiced total nudity. By abandoning the Vedas, truth, falsehood, pleasure, and pain, these wanderers sought the Universal Soul to achieve eternal salvation.

## Siddhartha Gotama and the Intellectual Climate
The influence of these travelling teachers is evident in the early life of Siddhartha Gotama (later known as the Buddha). At the age of nineteen, Siddhartha married his cousin Yasodhar, the daughter of a Koliyan chief, and lived a life of luxury. However, in his twenty-ninth year, he experienced a series of profound visions while driving to his pleasure-grounds. He witnessed a man broken by age, a man suffering from a loathsome disease, and a decomposing corpse. His charioteer, Channa, informed him that such fates apply to all living beings.

Following these experiences, Siddhartha encountered an ascetic walking in a calm and dignified manner. Channa described to him the aims and character of the "Wanderers," the travelling teachers who were central to the intellectual life of that era. This encounter, along with his visions, stirred Siddhartha's mind, leading him to view earthly gains as vanity and to long for a life of meditation, abstinence, and self-surrender.

Later, at age 35, the Buddha sought to proclaim his new gospel. While travelling to Benares to address five former disciples, he met Upaka, a wandering sophist. During their encounter, the Buddha described his mission as "beating the drum of the Ambrosia" (setting up the light of the doctrine of Nirvana) in the darkness of the world. He identified himself as a conqueror because he had conquered "intoxications"—the mental intoxication stemming from craving after future life, sensuality, or ignorance—and because his evil dispositions had ceased.

## Other Forms of Travelling Instruction and Performance
Beyond the spiritual ascetics of India, the act of travelling to impart knowledge or entertainment appeared in other cultures:

*   **Japan:** The samurai Yoshimitsu, a skilled performer on the *sho* (a musical instrument), demonstrated the value of teacher-student transmission while travelling to a battlefield in the north. Fearing that the secrets taught to him by his master, Toyohara Tokimoto, would die with him, Yoshimitsu invited the master's son, Tokiaki, to accompany him. On moonlit nights during their journey, Yoshimitsu provided instruction in the art of music to Tokiaki.
*   **Persia:** The *temacha* (spectacle), a form of comedy or farce, was performed by wandering minstrels known as *loutys* (or *joculatores*). Accompanied by *bayaderes*, these performers travelled to provide improvised entertainments for spectators.
*   **France:** In the 17th century, Joseph Bejart was a strolling player who accompanied Moliere in his theatrical wanderings before returning permanently to Paris.

## Philosophical Perspectives on Wandering
Different philosophical schools viewed the concept of "wandering" through different lenses. Confucius focused strictly on the obligations of this life and dismissed speculation on the unknown. He contrasted his own path with that of certain individuals who believed life was a dream and death an awakening. Confucius described these people as those who "travel beyond the rule of life," whereas he travelled "within it." He characterized their state as strolling beyond the "dust and dirt of mortality" to wander in "realms of inaction," noting that such people ignore their passions and the conventionalities of the world.
