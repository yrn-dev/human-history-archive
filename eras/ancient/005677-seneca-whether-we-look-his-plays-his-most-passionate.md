# Seneca

L. Annaeus Seneca (4 B.C.–A.D. 65) was a famous and ill-fated author of the early Roman Empire. He is primarily recognized for leaving behind a series of works that exerted a paramount influence on the beginnings of modern tragedy.

## Dramatic Works and Style
Seneca derived his tragic themes from the poets Euripides and Sophocles. His plays are characterized by a strong predominance of the rhetorical element and an artificiality of style that differs significantly from those Greek predecessors. His work is noted for a "sensational" choice of themes and a "modern" quickness of treatment.

The metrification of Seneca's plays is very strict. While it is uncertain if they were designed for the stage, they were undoubtedly intended for recitation. In the broader context of Roman tragedy, Seneca's plays are not representative of the method and style of earlier writers, though they follow the general progress of Roman tragic composition. In such works, action was often made as full and complicated as possible because plays were performed singly. 

The structure of these dramas typically involved dialogue-scenes (*diverbia*) interspersed with musical passages (*cantica*). Unlike Greek choruses, the Roman chorus stood on the stage and occasionally participated in the action. While the division of action was initially three parts, the addition of a prologue and epilogue likely led to the invention of the fixed five-act structure.

## Philosophical and Social Observations
Beyond his dramas, Seneca's writings reflect specific observations on human nature. In one instance, he observes that a good man is as singular and uncommon as the phoenix—a sentiment echoed by Juvenal regarding the difficulty of finding an accomplished woman.

In historical records concerning the early Christian sect, it is noted that Seneca, along with Plutarch and the elder Pliny, totally unnoticed the new sect.

## Influence on Later Literature
Seneca's influence extended into the Elizabethan era of English drama. Thomas Kyd, an important dramatist who preceded Shakespeare, drew most freely from Seneca, though some critics, such as Nashe, suggested this indebtedness was exaggerated by Kyd's use of Thomas Newton's translation.

Additionally, the writer Brooke wrote two tragedies, *Alaham* and *Mustapha*, based on the Senecan model. In these works, the choruses function as philosophical dissertations, often maintaining only a slight connection to the rest of the drama. Charles Lamb characterized Brooke's work as being "nine parts Machiavel and Tacitus, for one of Sophocles and Seneca," noting that Brooke's plays and poetry were "frozen and made rigid with intellect."
