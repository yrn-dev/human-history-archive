# Mancipium and Debt Security

In certain historical legal systems, debt was secured directly on the person of the debtor. Under these codes, a debtor who was seized for debt had the option to nominate a hostage, known as a *mancipium*, to work off the debt. The individuals eligible to serve as *mancipium* included the debtor's slave, child, or wife.

## Regulations on the Holding of Mancipium
The laws governing the detention of a *mancipium* included specific time limits and restrictions based on the status of the person. While slaves could be held, the creditor was restricted to holding a child or a wife as *mancipium* for a period of only three years. 

Regarding the sale of these hostages, a creditor was permitted to sell a slave-hostage. However, an exception existed for slave-girls who had borne children for their master; such individuals could not be sold and had to be redeemed by their owner.

## Liability and Death of the Mancipium
The legal responsibility of the creditor varied depending on the cause of the *mancipium's* death while in their possession:

*   **Natural Death:** If the *mancipium* died a natural death under the creditor's hand, the creditor was considered "scot free," and no claim could lie against them.
*   **Death by Cruelty:** If the creditor caused the death of the *mancipium* through cruelty, they were subject to severe penalties. In such cases, the creditor was required to give "son for son" or pay for a slave.

This "son for son" penalty is described as a curious extension of *talio*. This principle of reciprocal death applied to other scenarios as well, such as the death of a builder's son if the builder's poor construction caused the death of a house-owner's son, or the death of a man's daughter if the father caused the death of another man's daughter.

## Related Legal Protections and Penalties
The legal framework surrounding debt and personal injury emphasized the importance of intention and protected certain essential assets. For example, the Code forbade distraint on a debtor's corn; if a creditor seized corn, they were required to return it and forfeited their entire claim. Similarly, the distraint of a working ox was fined, and unwarranted seizures for debt also incurred fines.

In broader legal contexts, the Code utilized fines as the most common penalty for breach of contract, damages to property, and corporal injuries to slaves or *muskinu* (paid to the master). Depending on the enormity of the offense, restoration of illegally bought or damaged goods could be multiple—ranging from double to thirtyfold.

## Roman Law and Mancipium
In Roman law, the term *mancipium* appeared in the context of *emancipatio*, the process by which the *patria potestas* (the father's power of life and death over his son) was extinguished. According to the law of the XII Tables, a father lost this power if he sold his child three times. This led to a formal procedure where a father sold his son into *mancipium* three times; after each sale, the fictitious vendee enfranchized the son through *manumissio vindicta* (laying a rod on the slave and claiming him as free). This process was confirmed by the *praetor* and his *addictor*, and the third manumission granted the son his freedom.
