# Earliest Forms and Origins

The study of earliest forms spans various disciplines within the archive, including linguistics, geography, paleontology, and the history of literature and law. These records trace the evolution of names, symbols, biological life, and cultural expressions from their primary stages.

## Linguistic and Symbolic Origins
In the history of alphabets, the earliest reachable form of the symbol for the sound *b* is the Hebrew *beth*. This form was closely akin to the Phoenician version and served as the basis for the Greek *beta*, which borrowed the symbol and added a vowel. 

Similarly, the letter *M*—the thirteenth letter of the Phoenician and Greek alphabets and the twelfth of the Latin—was originally written from right to left. Its earliest representations in Greek are characterized by a first limb of greater length. The Phoenician name for this symbol was *mem*, while the Greek name *mu* was formed by analogy with the name for *n*. The Latin abbreviation *M'* for the praenomen Manius is thought to have developed from this form when written from left to right, with an apostrophe representing the original letter's fifth stroke.

## Early Biological and Geological Forms
Geological records indicate that the earliest ages of the earth were characterized by a barren land without soil or vegetation, shaped by violent volcanic forces and frequent storms. During this period, the atmosphere was denser and often obscured by cloud masses. 

The archive notes that the oldest deposits are the most worn and distorted, leaving no certain trace of life. It is probable that the earliest forms of life were small and soft, which explains why they left no evidence of their existence. Fossil records only began when living things developed shells and skeletons made of lime or other hard materials.

## Early Epitaphs and Inscriptions
The earliest epitaphial inscriptions known to the archive are those of the ancient Egyptians, written on coffins and sarcophagi. These typically begin with a prayer to a deity, such as Anubis or Osiris, and specify the office, descent, and name of the deceased, though they do not attempt to describe individual character.

In Britain, the earliest existing epitaphs date to the Roman period and are written in Latin. These were initially commemorative of soldiers from the legions of occupation before native Britons adopted the Roman form. English epitaphs from the 11th and 12th centuries also remained in Latin, generally limited to the phrase "Hic jacet" followed by the rank and name of the deceased. Notable exceptions include the encomiastic leonine verse epitaphs of William de Warren, earl of Surrey (d. 1089), and Gundrada, daughter of the Conqueror (d. 1085).

## Early Literary and Poetic Forms
In Greek literature, the earliest elegists, Tyrtaeus and Callinus, utilized elegy to rouse warlike spirits. Later, Simonides of Ceos used this form for epitaphs for those who fell in the Persian Wars. In the context of English poetry, the absence of an envoi in the medieval English ballades suggests a relationship with the earliest French form in its imperfect state.

Regarding the history of the Bible, the earliest manuscripts of Jerome's revision are divided into groups by country, with the Northumbrian, Spanish, Anglo-Irish, and Irish being the most important. The earliest notable revisions of the text in France occurred in the 9th century, undertaken by Theodulf (787-821) and Alcuin in 801. The earliest printed Vulgate is the Mentz edition of 1452-1456, also known as the "42-line" or *Mazarin* Bible.

## Early Toponymy and Legal Theory
The name of the manor of Bolton appeared in its earliest forms as Botheltun or Bodleton. Later variations included Boltune, Bowelton, Bodeltown, Bolton-le-Moors, Bolton-in-ye-Moors, Bolton-super-Moras, and Botheltun-le-Moors. The manor was granted to Roger de Poictou by William I.

In the realm of political theory and reform, Buchanan is cited as the most celebrated or earliest of the reformers to justify the theory of resistance, as detailed in his *Dialogue de Jure Regni apud Scotos*.
