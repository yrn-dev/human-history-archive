# Archelaus Goes To Rome With A Great Number Of His Kindred

The journey of Archelaus to Rome was marked by intense familial conflict, political rivalry, and legal challenges regarding his right to rule. Accompanied by a large group of his kindred, Archelaus faced accusations before Caesar concerning his conduct and the legitimacy of his succession.

## Departure and Familial Tensions
Archelaus traveled to the sea-side to begin his journey to Rome, accompanied by his mother, his friends Ptolemy, Poplas, and Nicolaus, as well as his sons-in-law and brethren. Salome also joined the party with her sons. While these relatives appeared to offer assistance to secure Archelaus's succession, their true intent was to accuse him of breaching the laws through his actions at the temple. To manage his domestic affairs and the palace in his absence, Archelaus left Philip as his steward.

## Rivalry with Antipas
Simultaneously, Antipas traveled to Rome to strive for the kingdom. Antipas argued that a former testament naming him king remained valid over a subsequent testament that favored Archelaus. Antipas was supported by Salome and many of Archelaus's own kindred who had sailed with Archelaus. Antipas also brought his mother and Ptolemy (the brother of Nicolaus), a man of significant weight due to the great trust and honor previously bestowed upon him by Herod. 

Despite the fact that Archelaus was the elder brother and the second testament granted him the kingdom, Antipas relied on the authority of the orator Ireneus, rejecting those who advised him to yield. Upon arriving in Rome, the kindred of Archelaus, driven by hatred and envy, shifted their support to Antipas. However, the primary desire of many of these relatives was to live without a king entirely, preferring a Roman governor; Antipas was their second choice should that fail.

## Accusations and the Council of Caesar
Once in Rome, Archelaus faced further accusations brought by an embassage of the Jews. This group consisted of fifty ambassadors who had been permitted by Varus to petition for the liberty of their country to live by its own laws. They were supported by more than eight thousand Jews residing in Rome.

Caesar convened a council of principal Romans in the temple of Apollo, a structure he had built and adorned at vast expense. During this assembly, the multitude of Jews stood with the ambassadors, while Archelaus stood with his friends. Archelaus's kindred remained neutral; their hatred and envy prevented them from supporting Archelaus, yet they feared the disgrace of being seen by Caesar as accusers of their own kinsman. Also present was Archelaus's brother, Philip, who had been sent ahead from Syria by Varus. Varus's motives for sending Philip were twofold: to assist Archelaus and to ensure Philip might receive a share of Herod's possessions should Caesar redistribute them.

## The Defense of Nicolaus
The legal proceedings included a lengthy accusation by Antipater, who produced numerous members of Archelaus's kindred as witnesses to prove the charges. In response, Nicolaus stood to plead for Archelaus. 

Nicolaus argued that the slaughter at the temple had been unavoidable, asserting that those killed had become enemies not only to Archelaus's kingdom but to Caesar himself. He further claimed that the accusers had encouraged Archelaus to commit other acts for which he might be accused. Nicolaus insisted that the latter testament must be viewed as valid because Herod had appointed Caesar to confirm the succession. He argued that Herod, in showing the prudence to yield his power to the "lord of the world," could not have been mistaken in his choice of an heir or the arbitrator of that succession.

## Background of the Temple Conflict
The accusations regarding the temple stemmed from events during the feast of unleavened bread (Passover), a memorial of the deliverance out of Egypt. During this time, an innumerable multitude gathered to worship God and offer sacrifices. Seditious individuals gathered in the temple to lament the teachers of the law, Judas and Matthias, and supported themselves through begging.

Fearing the madness of these men, Archelaus first attempted to quiet them through persuasion, sending his general and others to the temple, but these envoys were driven away and stoned. He then sent a tribune and a cohort of soldiers to suppress the tumult. The multitude responded by stoning the soldiers and wounding the tribune. Believing the government could not be preserved without bloodshed, Archelaus deployed his entire army. Footmen entered via the city and horsemen arrived via the plain, attacking the people as they offered sacrifices. The horsemen killed approximately three thousand men, while the survivors fled to neighboring mountains.
