# The Development of the Fibula

Fibulae, which are brooches of the safety-pin type, were extensively utilized during antiquity, although their use was confined to specific times and places. They were unknown to the oriental nations that had not been touched by Greek influence, as well as to the Egyptians. Similarly, they do not appear in Hissarlik or Crete. While the exact place of origin is not yet determined, evidence suggests they emerged in central Europe toward the close of the Bronze Age, shortly before 1000 B.C.

## Early Forms and Distribution
The earliest fibula was little more than a pin that had been bent round for security, with the head catching the point. A subsequent simple form, closely resembling the modern safety-pin where a coiled spring forces the point against a catch, has been found in late deposits of the Mycenaean Age, including the lower city of Mycenae and Enkomi in Cyprus. Other early occurrences, though rare, include the Swiss lake-dwellings of the later Bronze Age, the "terramare" deposits of the Po valley, central Italy, Bosnia, and Hungary.

Toward the end of the Bronze Age, the safety-pin was arched into a bow to allow it to encompass a greater amount of material.

## Iron Age Evolution and Classification
During the older Iron Age, known as the Hallstatt period, the bow and its accessories were modified and thickened to provide more rigidity and to create surfaces or prominences for decoration. Montelius classified these chief types into four main groups:

*   **Type I:** The catch-plate wire is hammered into a flat disk where the pin rests.
*   **Type II:** The bow is either hollowed out underneath to create a "boat" form or thickened in the middle to assume a "leech" shape. The catch-plate in this type is elongated to mask the end of a long pin and is only slightly turned up.
*   **Type III:** This type features a flattened catch-plate similar to Type I, but with additional convolutions added to the bow.
*   **Type IV:** The bow is convoluted (sometimes represented by knobs), and the catch-plate develops as seen in Type II.

Special variations also existed, such as the fibulae of the Greek geometric age, which featured an exaggerated development of the vertical portion of the catch-plate.

## The La Tène Period and the Bilateral Spring
In the later Iron Age, or early La Tène period, the prolongation of the catch-plate developed a terminal knob ornament that was reflexed upwards—initially slightly, and later to a marked extent, turning back toward the bow. As the La Tène period progressed (300–1 B.C.), this reflection became so marked that it practically merged with the bow.

A significant structural improvement introduced within the La Tène culture was the transition from a unilateral spring (coiled on one side of the bow) to a bilateral spring. In the bilateral design, the wire is coiled on one side of the bow's axis, taken to the other side, and coiled again before forming the pin. This invention became almost universal and serves as the dividing line between older and younger groups of ancient fibulae.

## Later Developments and Regional Styles
The bilateral spring eventually developed into two marked projections on each side of the axis. To provide protection and strength, these were given a containing tube and a metal core. This change meant the pin no longer had to be a continuation of the bow; it became a separate member not actuated by its own spring, leading to the development of the T-shaped or "cross-bow" fibula. This form reached great importance and size during the first centuries of the Empire, with its highest development noted on the ivory diptych of Stilicho at Monza (c. A.D. 400).

Different cultures further adapted these forms:
*   **Teutonic and Frankish Tribes:** Between the 5th and 9th centuries, the T-shape's crossbar became a decorated semicircle, often featuring a chased surface and radial knobs. The base of the shaft was flattened and ornate. An example from A.D. 481, belonging to King Childeric, features a singularly complicated pin-fastening.
*   **Anglo-Saxons:** In England, animal forms, medallion-forms, and T-forms were found in graves. The medallion-form was predominant in Kent. These brooches were often gold with a central boss and decorated with filigree, dragonesque engravings, or geometric mosaics of mother of pearl, garnets on gold foil, and turquoises.
*   **Scandinavian (Viking Period):** Between A.D. 800 and 1050, brooches were convex and oval, resembling a tortoise. They evolved from a frog-like animal (itself a development of the Teutonic T-shaped type) into purely decorative lines. These featured a perforated upper shell of chased work over a gilt, impure bronze under shell, eventually decorated with massive projecting bosses and open crown-like ornament.
