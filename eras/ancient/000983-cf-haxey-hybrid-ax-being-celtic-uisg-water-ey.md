# Celtic Water Terminology and Toponymy

The study of Celtic linguistic roots reveals a widespread influence on the naming of geographical features, particularly rivers and settlements, across Europe and the British Islands. A recurring theme in these toponyms is the designation of water, which appears in various forms across different Celtic languages and their subsequent evolutions.

## The Root for Water in Celtic Languages
The Celtic word for water is found in numerous forms across different regions. In Welsh, it appears frequently as *afon*, while in Manx it is *aon*, and in Gaelic it is *abhuinn* (pronounced *avain*). This root is radically identical to the Latin *aqua* and *amnis*, as well as the Sanskrit *ap*.

This linguistic root is present, though sometimes disguised, in a vast number of river names throughout the Celtic area of Europe. Examples include:
*   **British Islands:** *Evan*, *Aune*, *Anne*, *Ive*, *Auney*, and *Inney*.
*   **France and Brittany:** *Aff*, *Aven*, *Avon*, and *Aune*.
*   **Italy:** *Avenza* and *Avens*.
*   **Spain:** *Avono*.
*   **Portugal:** *Avia*.

Additionally, the terminal syllable of many Latinized names for French rivers, such as the *Garumna*, *Matrona*, and *Sequana*, appears to have originally been derived from this same word. The root is also visible in names such as *Doab* and *Punjab*.

## Influence on English and European Place Names
In England, the name Avon is used for several principal rivers. Beyond river names, the Celtic root for water has influenced the names of various towns and colonies.

### Axholme and Haxey
The island of Axholme in north-west Lincolnshire, England, provides an example of a hybrid name. Properly termed *Axeyholm*, the name combines three different linguistic origins:
1.  **Ax**: Derived from the Celtic *uisg*, meaning water.
2.  **Ey**: The Anglo-Saxon word for island.
3.  **Holm**: The Norse word for island.

Axholme is located between the rivers Don, Idle, and Trent and comprises the parishes of Crowle, Epworth, Haxey, Luddington, Owston, Althorpe, and Belton.

### Roman and Celtic Settlements
The Romans often named places noted for medicinal or warm springs *Aquæ* (Waters). These Roman designations often overlapped with or transformed existing Celtic names. For example, the colony of Aquincum, founded by the Romans in the 2nd century A.D. on the site of a former Celtic settlement on the right bank of the Danube, was a transformation of the Celtic name *Ak-ink*, which meant "rich waters."

Similarly, the Roman word *Aquæ* is likely the same as the Celtic words *Ac* or *Acq*. This is seen in the naming of towns such as Aix-la-Chapelle (Aachen) in the Rhine Province of Prussia and Aix in Savoy. In France, the *Aquæ Sextiæ* (Aix) was founded by C. Sextius Calvinus in 120 B.C. Other examples include *Aquæ Bormonis* (the waters of the God Bormo) in France and *Aquæ Sulis* (the waters of the Goddess Sulis) in England, the latter of which is associated with the inscriptions found at Bath.

## Other Celtic Linguistic Derivatives
Beyond water-related terminology, other words are believed to have Celtic origins. The term "bilge"—referring to the widest part of a cask or the lowest interior part of a ship's hull—is a corruption of "bulge" (from the French *bouge* and Latin *bulga*, meaning bag), which is thought to derive from an original Celtic word.
