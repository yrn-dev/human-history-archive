# The Case of the 2nd Earl of Bristol

## Succession and Political Ambition
Digby succeeded to the peerage as the 2nd Earl of Bristol in January 1659 following the death of his father. During that same month, he was made K.G. Upon the restoration, Bristol returned to England, where he found himself relegated to a position of secondary importance and excluded from office due to his religion. Driven by a desire to make a brilliant figure, he engaged in restless and ambitious activity within parliament.

## Conflict with Clarendon and the Crown
Bristol adopted a stance of violent hostility toward Clarendon. His views on foreign affairs leaned strongly toward Spain, leading him to oppose the marriage of the king to Catherine of Portugal. Although Bristol persuaded Charles to send him to Italy to evaluate the Medici princesses, the treaty with Portugal and the royal marriage were finalized while he was away.

In June 1663, Bristol attempted to upset the management of the House of Commons by Clarendon. However, King Charles exposed this intrigue to parliament. Bristol was subsequently forced to attend the House to exonerate himself. During these proceedings, he confessed that he had "taken the liberty of enlarging," and his "comedian-like speech" excited general amusement.

## Escalation and Expulsion
Exasperated by these failures, Bristol engaged in a violent scene with the king in early July. He unleashed fierce and disrespectful reproaches, concluding with a threat that if Charles did not grant his requests within twenty-four hours, he would do something to "awaken him out of his slumbers, and make him look better to his own business."

Following this, on the 10th, Bristol impeached Clarendon in the Lords on charges of high treason. When the charge was dismissed, he renewed the accusation. As a result, he was expelled from the court and avoided a warrant for his apprehension by remaining concealed for two years.

## Religious Renunciation and Return
In January 1664, Bristol created a new sensation by appearing at his house at Wimbledon. Before witnesses, he publicly declared himself a Protestant and renounced Roman Catholicism. This action was likely motivated by a desire to secure immunity from a charge of recusancy that had been preferred against him.

Bristol was eventually welcomed back to court when the fall of Clarendon became desired. He resumed his seat in the Lords on July 29, 1667.
