# The Cimbri and Scandinavian Influence

## The Cimbri: Origins and Geography
The Cimbri (also referred to as Kimbroi) were a people located at the northern extremity of the Jutish peninsula, which Pliny described as the Cimbric Chersonese. According to Pliny, the Cimbri were situated above several other tribes, including the Charondes, Phoundousioi, Chaloi, Kobandoi, Sabaliggoi, and Sigoulones, with the Saxons located on the neck of the peninsula. Pliny also noted the Promontorium Cimbrorum, stating that the Sinus Codanus lay between this promontory and Mons Saevo. 

Pomponius Mela recorded that the Cimbri and Teutones dwelt on the Sinus Codanus, with the Teutones also residing in Scandinavia (or Sweden). Modern tracing suggests the Cimbri may have been located in the province of Aalborg, formerly known as Himmerland. Ptolemy also fixed the remnant of the Cimbri at the northern extremity of the peninsula.

## Conflicts with Rome
The Romans first became aware of the approach of the Cimbri and Teutones in 113 B.C. In Illyricum, the consul Cn. Papirius Carbo was defeated by these groups. By 109 B.C., the Cimbri defeated the consul M. Junius Silanus after the Roman Senate refused their demand for lands to settle in. 

In 105 B.C., the Cimbri inflicted immense slaughter upon the consul Cn. Manlius Maximus and the proconsul Q. Servilius Caepio, who lost both of their camps. Caepio, who had previously plundered the temple of the Celtic Apollo at Tolosa (a site that had joined the Cimbri), suffered a crushing defeat at Arausio on the Rhone.

The Cimbri later engaged with the Roman general Marius. Before their battle, the king of the Cimbri, Boeorix, challenged Marius to settle the claim to the country through combat. The two parties agreed to fight on the third day at the plain of Vercellæ. To prepare for this engagement, Marius altered the design of Roman spears; he replaced one of the two iron nails fastening the wooden shaft with a wooden peg. This ensured that upon striking a shield, the peg would break and the iron head would bend, causing the spear to drag along the ground and remain attached to the shield.

## Early Brewing Practices
In early brewing, prior to the widespread use of hops as an astringent and preservative, various bitter and aromatic plants were utilized. The Cimbri employed *Tamarix germanica* for this purpose. Other regional practices included the Scandinavians using the fruit of the sweet gale (*Myrica gale*), the Icelanders using yarrow (*Achillea millefolium*), and the Cauchi using the twigs and fruit of the chaste tree (*Vitex agrius castus*).

## Scandinavian Influence in England
Scandinavian influence is heavily evident in the district of the Danelagh, particularly in Yorkshire, Lincolnshire, Nottinghamshire, and Northern Northamptonshire. This influence is seen in several areas:

**Administrative and Legal Systems:** The division of Lincolnshire and Yorkshire into "ridings" is of Scandinavian origin, as is the use of "wapentakes" instead of "hundreds" in several counties, including Rutlandshire, Nottinghamshire, and Derbyshire. The Scandinavian legal system introduced "lawmen" in towns such as York, Lincoln, Chester, Stamford, and Cambridge; these officials are considered the ultimate source of the jury.

**Social and Linguistic Impact:** Domesday and other documents record social classes with Norse names, such as *thrall*, *bonde*, *karl*, *karlman*, *dreng*, *hold*, and *lysing*. The taxation system utilized *carucates* rather than virgates and hides, and a duodecimal system of reckoning was used instead of a decimal one. 

Linguistically, Scandinavian dialects were likely the primary language of the Danelagh until the end of the 10th century. This resulted in a high proportion of Danish place-names and the adoption of many words into English. For example, the verb "lug" (meaning to pull or drag) and the term "luggage" are likely Scandinavian in origin, derived from the Swedish *lugg* (forelock) and *lugga* (to pull).
