# Renunciation and Family Ties

The concept of relinquishing familial and material bonds appears across various historical, religious, and cultural contexts, ranging from voluntary spiritual renunciation to the involuntary separations described in sacred texts and the structured legalities of kinship.

## Spiritual Renunciation in Manichaeism
In the Manichaean tradition around the year 400, the "elect" practiced a rigorous form of renunciation based on the preaching and precepts of Christ, which they termed the gospel. This path required the total abandonment of familial ties and material wealth. An elect described this commitment as giving up father, mother, wife, children, and "all else that the gospel bids us." 

This renunciation extended to financial assets, including the casting away of gold, silver, and even copper. The elect lived content with daily bread, remaining unconcerned with the future or the means of clothing and feeding the body. This lifestyle was intended to embody the beatitudes of Christ, manifesting as a state of being pure of heart, gentle, a peacemaker, and a mourner, while enduring hatred and persecution for the sake of righteousness. In this vision, the abandonment of delights, pleasures, eating, and drinking served as a sufficient answer to whether one had accepted the gospel.

## Familial Separation in the Qur'an
The Qur'an presents a vision of a future event characterized by the "Deafening Noise" (also described as the "Shout" or "deafening cry"). On this Day, the archive's sources describe a total collapse of earthly familial bonds. The text teaches that a man will flee from his own brother, as well as from his father, mother, wife, and children. This separation occurs because every individual on that day will be occupied by their own affair, leaving them indifferent or heedless of others. The text contrasts the states of people on this day, noting that some faces will be beaming, laughing, and rejoicing, while other faces will be stained with dust.

## Legal and Social Structures of Kinship
In contrast to spiritual renunciation, various historical codes and customs established strict rules for family and inheritance.

### Roman and Ancient Customs
In certain ancient codes, monogamy was the standard, though specific provisions existed for childless wives. A wife could provide her husband with a maid to bear children; these children were reckoned as the wife's, and she remained the maid's mistress. While the husband could take a concubine if the wife refused to provide a maid, a concubine—though of a lower rank—was a free woman whose children were legitimate. 

Regarding parental authority, fathers maintained control over children until marriage, including the right to their labor, the right to hire them out for wages, or even the right to sell them. In the absence of a father, mothers or elder brothers held these same rights.

### Tartar Customs
The marriage customs of the Tartars allowed a man to take up to a hundred wives, provided he could support them. The first wife and her sons were held in the highest honor and regarded as the most legitimate. Marriage involved a payment from the husband to the wife's mother. Tartar custom permitted the marriage of cousins and allowed the eldest son to marry any of his father's wives after the father's death, with the exception of his own mother. Additionally, a man could marry his brother's wife following the brother's death.

## Conflict and Kinship in Historical Accounts
Historical records illustrate that familial bonds were often strained by political ambition or personal conflict. In the case of Herod, natural affection for his children diminished as his favor toward Antipater grew. This alienation was exacerbated by Antipater's mother, who acted more harshly than a step-mother and hated the queen's sons. Similarly, the account of Tyrô describes a daughter tortured and imprisoned by her father due to the cruel counsels of his second wife, Sidêrô. Tyrô was forced to expose her twin sons, Pelias and Nêleus, in a boat on the river Enipeus to save them, though they later rescued her and killed Sidêrô.

In the laws attributed to Moses, strict prohibitions were placed on familial relations to ensure the legitimacy of children and the stability of families. Adultery was forbidden, and "abominable wickedness" was identified in relations between parents and children, or between a man and his aunts, sisters, or sons' wives.
