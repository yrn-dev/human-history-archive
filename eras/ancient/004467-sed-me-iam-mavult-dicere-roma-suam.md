# Sed me iam mavult dicere Roma suam

The phrase "Sed me iam mavult dicere Roma suam" appears in the context of linguistic and etymological discussions regarding the origin of the word "basket."

## Linguistic Context and Etymology
The term "basket" refers to a vessel used for carrying, protecting, or holding commodities, constructed by interweaving materials such as cane, rushes, or twigs. This craft is considered one of the most ancient industries and is viewed as the probable origin of all textile arts. The influence of basketmaking is evident in various historical artistic forms, including the basket capitals of Byzantine architecture, the willow pattern found on old china, and decorative designs in old ceramic ware, which were created using basket moulds prior to the invention of the potter's wheel.

In analyzing the etymology of the word "basket," some have identified it with the Latin term *bascauda*. This connection is highlighted by a reference to Martial (xiv. 99), who wrote: "Barbara de pictis veni bascauda Britannis: Sed me iam mavult dicere Roma suam." 

However, this etymological link is contested. The *New English Dictionary* asserts that there is no evidence to connect "basket" with *bascauda*, noting that the latter term refers more specifically to a brazen vessel, a tray, or a tub.

## The Nature of the Craft
Basketry is characterized as a primitive craft. Its relative importance to a society is observed to be in inverse ratio to that society's level of industrial development. Because the process of interweaving leaves, rushes, or twigs is practiced among the rudest nations of the world, it is regarded as a universal art.
