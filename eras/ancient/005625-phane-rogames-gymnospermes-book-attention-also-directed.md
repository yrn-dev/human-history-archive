# Phanérogames gymnospermes

The term *Phanérogames gymnospermes* refers to a botanical group established within the classification of plants. This group was created to incorporate the Cycadeae and Coniferae, based on the discoveries made by Robert Brown.

## Contribution of Brongniart
The classification of *Phanérogames gymnospermes* was featured in the "Prodrome," a small but significant work contributed to the *Grand Dictionnaire d'Hist. Nat.* in 1828. This work was written by Brongniart, an investigator and writer who is recognized as the "father of palaeobotany." The "Prodrome" served to organize the study of fossil plants by arranging them alongside their closest living allies, providing a foundation for subsequent progress in the field.

Brongniart's broader research into the relationship between existing and extinct plant forms culminated in the *Histoire des végétaux fossiles*. While the publication of this work began in 1828, it remained a "colossal fragment"; only the first volume was completed by 1837, followed by three parts of a second volume.

## Geological Succession of Plant Forms
In the "Prodrome," Brongniart directed attention to the succession of plant forms across different geological periods. This research led to the conclusion that different groups of plants predominated during specific eras:
*   **Palaeozoic period:** Pteridophyta were found to predominate.
*   **Mesozoic period:** Gymnosperms were the predominant form.
*   **Cainozoic period:** Angiosperms predominated.

These findings were later stated more fully in his 1849 work, "Tableau des genres de végétaux fossiles," published in D'Orbigny's *Dict. Univ. d'Hist. Nat.*

## Characteristics of Gymnosperms
Within the gymnosperms, certain species exhibit a condition known as being *polycotyledonous*, meaning they possess more than two cotyledons. These cotyledons are typically arranged in a whorl. This characteristic is specifically observed in the Coniferae, including the larch, spruce, fir, and pine. In these plants, observations have recorded the presence of six, nine, twelve, and even fifteen cotyledons. These structures are linear in form and resemble the mode of development and appearance of the fasciculated or clustered leaves found in the larch.
