# The Trinity and the Church of Rome

The doctrine of the Trinity has been a central point of theological discourse, ritual practice, and institutional conflict within the history of the Christian church, particularly concerning the church of Rome and its relations with other ecclesiastical bodies.

## Trinitarian Baptismal Formulas
The application of the trinitarian formula in baptism was not uniform from the beginning. While the *Teaching of the Apostles* prescribes baptism in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, it also references baptism into the name of the Lord, which was the normal formula of the New Testament. In the 3rd century, baptism in the name of Christ remained widespread; Pope Stephen declared such baptisms valid in opposition to Cyprian of Carthage.

However, discrepancies in these formulas led to institutional conflict. Pope Zachariah recorded that Celtic missionaries omitted one or more persons of the Trinity during baptism, which served as one of the reasons why the church of Rome anathematized them. Conversely, Pope Nicholas (858-867) allowed baptism to be valid *tantum in nomine Christi* (only in the name of Christ), as seen in the Acts. Other perspectives existed, such as those of the Armenian fathers, who specified that baptism "into the death of Christ" alone was essential, while Basil condemned baptism into the Lord alone as insufficient.

## The Trinitarian Controversy and Arianism
The "Trinitarian controversy" is described as a high and mysterious argument derived from the abuse of philosophy, which penetrated every part of the Christian world. This differed from the schism of the Donatists, which was an accidental quarrel confined to Africa. From the era of Constantine to the time of Theodoric and Clovis, the theological disputes of Arianism deeply involved the temporal interests of both Barbarians and Romans. These disputes are linked to the school of Plato and contributed to the decline and fall of the empire.

Later scholarship sought to examine these origins. George Bull (1634-1710), in his work *Defensio Fidei Nicenae* (1685), attempted to demonstrate that the doctrine of the Trinity was held by the ante-Nicene fathers of the church through an examination of early church literature.

## The Great Schism of 1054
The doctrine of the Holy Trinity played a decisive role in the final rupture between the Latin and Greek churches. Long-standing dissensions existed between the Pope of Rome—who claimed to be the head of the Christian community everywhere and the successor of St. Peter—and the patriarch and emperor in Constantinople, who did not acknowledge this claim. This rivalry was further fueled by the distinction between Greek-speaking and Latin-speaking Christianity. A dispute regarding a "fine point" in the doctrine of the Holy Trinity eventually consummated these dissensions, leading to the final rupture in 1054. Following this, the Latin and Greek churches remained distinct and antagonistic.

## Visions and Fasting
In various traditions, fasting was used as a means of evoking or consulting the spirit world. The Bogomils, for instance, practiced fasting until they saw the Trinity face to face. Within the church, fasting was practiced for similar reasons, with some saints receiving glimpses of the beatific vision as a reward for their asceticism.

## Liturgical Observance
In the Anglican Church, Trinity Sunday is observed as a great festival, similar to the observances of Christmas, Easter, Whitsunday, and Ascension Day, for which a special preface to the consecration prayer in the communion service is provided.
