# Roman Occupation of Scotland and Related Antiquities

The study of the Roman occupation of Scotland and the broader Roman presence in Britain involves the analysis of defensive structures, archaeological excavations, and the study of inscribed stones.

## Defensive Works and Hadrian's Wall
The north of Britain was defended by Hadrian's wall, a complex system of works. The most prominent feature is a stone wall, measuring 6 to 8 feet in thickness and originally reaching a height of perhaps 14 feet. This stone wall is accompanied by a deep ditch in front and a connecting road, turrets, forts, and "mile castles" behind it. In the high moors between Gilsland and Chollerford, the wall's traces remain plain as it winds along precipices and climbs from hill to hill.

Archaeological evidence indicates that the stone wall was preceded by a "turf wall" made of sods laid in regular courses with a ditch in front. Because ancient writers identify two wall-builders—Hadrian and Septimius Severus—it is inferred that Hadrian constructed the original turf wall and Severus later reconstructed it in stone. This reconstruction generally followed the line of the original to utilize the existing ditch, though deviations occurred in places such as Birdoswald, where the older work survived.

Additionally, there is a feature known as the "Vallum." Rather than a wall, this is a broad, flat-bottomed ditch with earth cast up on either side into continuous mounds resembling ramparts. While its purpose is debated—with some 19th-century authorities like Bruce and John Hodgson suggesting it defended the wall from southern insurgents—it is confirmed to be a Roman work no older than Hadrian. It was not intended for military defense and likely marked a limit of the civil province of Britain.

## Scottish Excavations and Research
Research into the Roman remains of Scotland is documented in several key works and publications. J. Macdonald has contributed significantly to this field through the publication of *Roman Stones in Hunterian Mus.* (1897) and *Bar Hill* (reprinted in 1906). Other essential sources include Rob. Stuart's *Caledonia Romana* (1852), the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* (vol. vii), and the volumes of the Scottish Antiquaries Society.

Specific sites of interest include:
* **Bar Hill:** Detailed in the work of J. Macdonald.
* **Burghead:** Discussed in the *Proceedings of Scottish Antiquaries* (xxv., xxvii.) by H.W. Young and in the *Transactions of the Glasgow Archaeological Society* by J. Macdonald.
* **Antonine Wall:** Research includes the *Antonine Wall Report* (1899) by F. Haverfield.

## Other Roman Forts and Settlements
Beyond the major walls, the Roman presence in Britain included detached forts and connecting roads. R.S. Ferguson provided accounts of Hardknott in the *Cumberland Archaeological Society Transactions* (xii), and J. Ward published on the Roman Fort of Gellygaer in 1903. 

In other regions of Roman Britain, excavations have revealed various settlement types. General Pitt-Rivers conducted excavations in Cranborne Chase (1887-1908) focusing on villages. Other significant sites include Silchester, Caerwent, and London, the latter of which was documented by Charles Roach Smith in *Roman London* (1859).
