# Cestius, Nero, and the People of Damascus

## The Conflict Between Florus and the Jews
During the twelfth year of the reign of the Roman emperor Nero, a conflict broke out involving Cestius Gallus, the president of the province of Syria. Prior to this, Florus had sought to provoke a war with the Jews to conceal his own "enormities," fearing that if peace continued, the Jews would accuse him before Caesar. Florus augmented the calamities of the people to induce a rebellion and falsely accused the Jews of revolting from the Roman government, claiming they were the authors of a disturbance in which they were actually the sufferers.

In response, the governors of Jerusalem and Bernice wrote to Cestius regarding the illegal practices of Florus. The Jewish multitude also besought Cestius to commiserate with their national calamities, identifying Florus as the "bane of their country." While Cestius quieted the multitude and assured them that Florus would treat them more gently, Florus deluded him. Cestius initially sent a tribune named Neopolitanus to investigate the state of affairs and the intentions of the Jews, who met king Agrippa at Jamnia.

## The Defeat of Cestius Gallus
Cestius eventually marched toward Jerusalem, but he later found himself in a position where he desired to flee. He attempted a deceptive retreat by leaving four hundred courageous soldiers at his fortifications to maintain the appearance that the entire army remained. However, when the Jews discovered the camp was empty, they slew the four hundred soldiers and pursued Cestius.

The Roman soldiers, gripped by fear and astonishment, abandoned their siege engines and instruments of war. The Jews pursued the Romans as far as Antipatris. In this engagement, which occurred on the eighth day of the month Dius (Marchesvan) in the twelfth year of Nero, the Jews slew 5,300 Roman footmen and 380 horsemen, while suffering only a few losses themselves.

## Ambassadors to Nero
Following the calamity that befell Cestius, several eminent individuals—including Philip, the son of Jacimus (commander of king Agrippa's forces), and two brothers, Saul and Costobarus—fled the city and went to Cestius. At their own request, Cestius sent Saul and his friends to Nero in Achaia. Their mission was to inform the emperor of their great distress and to lay the blame for kindling the war upon Florus, in hopes of alleviating Cestius's own danger by provoking Nero's indignation against Florus.

This was not the only instance of embassies being sent to Nero. Previously, when the procurator Festus ordered the demolition of a wall the Jews had built to prevent the royal dining room of king Agrippa from overlooking the temple sacrifices, the Jews petitioned to send an embassage to Nero. Ten principal men, along with Helcias (keeper of the sacred treasure) and Ismael the high priest, were sent. Nero allowed the wall to stand to gratify his religious wife, Poppea, though he retained Ismael and Helcias as hostages.

## The People of Damascus
The archive contains separate historical accounts regarding the people of Damascus across different eras. In one account, king Ahaz, having been beaten by the Israelites, sought assistance from Tiglath-Pileser, king of the Assyrians. Tiglath-Pileser made war upon the Syrians, laid their country waste, and took Damascus by force. He slew their king, Rezin, and transplanted the people of Damascus into Upper Media, subsequently planting a colony of Assyrians in Damascus.

In another account, Benhadad, the king of the Syrians and of Damascus, assembled an army and thirty-two auxiliary kings from beyond the Euphrates to campaign against Ahab. Benhadad besieged Samaria and sent ambassadors to Ahab, demanding his riches, children, and wives. When Ahab refused, Benhadad sent a second embassage, demanding the right to search the palace and the houses of Ahab's kindred to take whatever was excellent.
