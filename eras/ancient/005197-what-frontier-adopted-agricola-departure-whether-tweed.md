# The Roman Frontier After Agricola

The determination of the specific frontier adopted immediately following the departure of the general Julius Agricola remains unknown to historians. While some sources suggest the frontier may have been the Tweed or the Cheviot, the exact boundary is not established. Following Agricola's recall, the military history of Britain is described as a blank for a period of thirty years, spanning from A.D. 85 to 115.

## The Campaigns and Recall of Agricola
Julius Agricola moved to conquer the farther north in A.D. 80. During his campaigns, he established a frontier intended to be permanent between the Clyde and the Forth, which was guarded by a line of forts, including sites at Bar Hill and Camelon. Agricola advanced further into Caledonia, achieving a "famous victory" at Mons Graupius, likely located near the confluence of the Isla and the Tay. Evidence of this period includes the Roman encampment at Inchtuthill, the most northerly ascertained Roman encampment in Scotland.

Despite these successes, the Roman home government abruptly recalled Agricola. The reasons for this removal are attributed to several factors: a possible jealousy of a general who had become too brilliant, a desire to avoid the expense of campaigns in "barren hills," and the need for the Legio II Adiutrix for duties elsewhere. Consequently, his northern conquests—all those beyond the Cheviot, if not all beyond the Tweed—were abandoned.

## The Hadrianic Frontier
Knowledge of Britain's military situation resumes around A.D. 115 or 120, when northern Britons rose in revolt and destroyed the Ninth Legion stationed at York. In A.D. 122, the Emperor Hadrian visited Britain, replacing the Ninth Legion with the Sixth Legion and implementing a new frontier policy. 

Hadrian constructed a continuous rampart, likely made of turf rather than stone, extending over 70 miles from Wallsend to Bowness (from the Tyne to the Solway). This system included:
* A ditch positioned in front of the rampart.
* A series of small forts along the line.
* One or two outposts located a few miles to the north.
* Detached forts guarding the Cumberland coast, such as the one on the hill above Maryport.

This structure was intended to serve as a definite limit of the Roman world, signaling that the era of conquest had ended.

## The Antonine Wall
The frontier shifted again around A.D. 140–142. Under the Emperor Antoninus Pius and his general Lollius Urbicus, Roman forces advanced from the Tyne and Solway frontier to the narrower isthmus between the Forth and the Clyde. This isthmus, approximately 36 miles across, had been previously fortified by Agricola.

In this location, the Romans built another continuous rampart with a ditch and a connecting road. This wall was constructed of regularly laid sods. The frontier was supported by approximately a dozen fair-sized forts. Excavations have identified several of these:
* **Bar Hill:** A three-acre site with turf ramparts, wooden barrack-rooms, and stone buildings including a storehouse, bath, and headquarters. It was nearly five times the size of Agricola's earlier fort on the same site.
* **Castle Cary:** A site of nearly four acres featuring massive, well-dressed masonry.
* **Rough Castle:** A smaller fort noted for its defensive pits and strong earthen and turf-built ramparts.

By A.D. 140, the district up to the Firth of Forth was definitely annexed. However, this conquest was stubbornly disputed. After several risings, the land north of Cheviot appears to have been lost between A.D. 180 and 185.

## Imperial Policy and Military Administration
The shift from expansion to frontier maintenance reflected a broader Imperial trend. The early Caesars often avoided showing themselves to the provinces or armies, fearing that the military fame of a lieutenant might be seen as an invasion of the Imperial prerogative. Consequently, Roman generals were encouraged to guard entrusted frontiers without aspiring to further conquests.

During the first two centuries of Imperial history, army commands were typically filled by men with liberal educations and a succession of civil and military honors. This changed later when the "rougher trade of arms" was abandoned to barbarians and peasants of the frontiers. These later soldiers, who knew no science but war and no country but their camp, frequently subverted the throne they were meant to guard.
