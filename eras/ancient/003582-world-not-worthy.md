# The World Was Not Worthy

The concept of worthiness in relation to the world, the divine, and human existence appears across various historical and sacred contexts, ranging from the perceived value of a cause during global conflict to the spiritual transition of an individual from the earthly realm.

## Worthiness in Global Conflict and Humanity
In the context of the Great War, the world experienced a transformation where no area remained immune to the effects of the contest. The conflict saw factories in America and Europe operating continuously to produce materials for strife, while the economic problems of five continents were altered. The consequences were severe: Armenia lost half its population, the people of North Syria suffered famine, and plague blotted out African tribes and Indian villages. Some countries ceased to exist as anything more than geographical names.

Despite the colossal price paid in a world that had become too expert in destruction, the cause of the Allied victory was described as worthy because it delivered the world's freedom from a deadly danger. The vindication of the essential greatness of common humanity and the impulse toward international brotherhood—born from a revulsion toward the horrors of international strife—were seen as the positive fruits of the war. In this struggle, victory was attributed more to the faithfulness of the ordinary man than to the genius of a few.

## Divine Worthiness and Messengers
In the sacred texts of the Qur'an, worthiness is associated with the faithfulness of messengers sent to various peoples. Hud, sent to the 'Ad, Lut sent to the people of Lut, and Shu'aib sent to the dwellers of the thicket (or Companions of the Wood), each identified himself as a messenger worthy of all trust. These messengers called upon their respective peoples to fear Allah, obey them, and guard against evil. 

The messengers emphasized that they sought no reward or wage from the people they addressed, stating that their reward was only with the Lord of the Worlds, who is described as the Mighty and the Merciful. The texts record that these peoples—the 'Ad, the people of Lut, and the dwellers of the wood—rejected the messengers and gave them the lie. In the case of the people of Lut, the messenger Lut specifically condemned them as a people transgressing all limits for approaching males instead of the mates Allah had created for them.

## Spiritual Transition and the Earthly World
In the account of Abraham and his son, the act of sacrifice is presented as a means of exiting the world in a manner that transcends common death. Abraham suggests to his son that God considers him worthy to leave the world without the usual severe paths of departure, such as war or disease. Instead, the son is to be sent to God, the Father of all men, through a sacrifice accompanied by holy offices of religion and prayers. This transition is viewed as a way for the soul to be received by God and placed near Him.

## Worthiness in Thought and Expression
The concept of worthiness also extends to intellectual and artistic expressions. In the study of ancient poetry and art, certain conceptions of the divine are viewed as more worthy than popular ones. For instance, a vision of the gods living in a world of their own, characterized by eternal peace and exemption from passion, is presented as a higher conception. Conversely, unworthy conceptions of the divine nature are said to render a person incapable of visiting temples in a calm spirit or receiving emanations of divine peace.

In the realm of scholarship, the pursuit of a comprehensive and just point of view is seen as necessary for a work to be set forth in a manner worthy to meet the public eye. This requires continuous and exclusive labor to rectify erroneous statements of fact and properly illustrate complicated subjects. Similarly, in historical orations, such as those given by the Grecian sophists, the style of speech is sometimes characterized as being worthy of that specific professional class.
