# Antigonus Besieges Those That Were In Masada

## The Siege of Masada
During a period of conflict in Judea, Antigonus conducted a siege against those stationed at Masada, which is described as the strongest of all fortresses. The besieged party possessed a sufficient quantity of all other necessary supplies but suffered from a critical lack of water. 

This shortage of water led Joseph, the brother of Herod, to contemplate fleeing the fortress with two hundred of his dependents to seek refuge with the Arabians. Joseph's desire to leave was further motivated by reports that Malichus had repented of offenses committed against Herod. However, Joseph was prevented from escaping when a great deal of rain fell during the night, filling the reservoirs and cisterns. The besieged viewed this abundance of water as a mark of Divine Providence, which restored their courage. Consequently, they launched an irruption against the forces of Antigonus, engaging in both open battles and private ambushes, and succeeded in slaying a great number of Antigonus's soldiers, although they were occasionally beaten and forced to retreat.

## Herod’s Return and Military Campaign
Herod, after sailing from Italy to Ptolemais, assembled a significant army consisting of both foreigners and his own countrymen. He marched through Galilee against Antigonus, receiving assistance from Dellius (who had been sent by Antony) as well as Ventidius and Silo. Ventidius was tasked with resolving disturbances caused by the Parthians in the cities, while Silo was stationed in Judea, though he had been corrupted by bribes from Antigonus.

As Herod progressed, his forces grew daily, and most of Galilee joined him. Herod identified the delivery of his relations from the siege at Masada as his most necessary enterprise. However, his path was hindered by the city of Joppa, which was at variance with him. Herod determined that Joppa must be taken first to ensure that no enemy stronghold remained behind him as he advanced toward Jerusalem.

## The Liberation of Masada and Ressa
During the campaign to reach Masada, Silo used the situation as a pretext to withdraw his forces from Jerusalem. When the Jews pursued Silo, Herod intervened with a small body of men, putting the pursuers to flight and saving Silo. After successfully taking Joppa, Herod hastened to Masada.

Despite Antigonus laying snares and ambushes in the passes during Herod's march, these efforts caused little to no damage. Herod successfully recovered his relations from Masada and also took the fortress of Ressa. Following these victories, Herod moved toward Jerusalem. As he marched, he continued to attract followers—some due to friendship with his father, some due to his own reputation, some to repay benefits received from both, and the majority in hopes of gaining from him once he was established in the kingdom.

## Aftermath and Consolidation of Power
Upon reaching Jerusalem, Herod pitched his camp on the west side of the city. He was joined by the soldiers who had been with Silo, as well as many citizens who feared his power. Herod proclaimed that he came for the preservation of the city and the good of the people, stating he bore no grudges against his enemies. Antigonus countered this by arguing that the kingdom should not be given to Herod, whom he characterized as a private man and an Idumean (a half Jew), but should instead go to a member of the royal and sacerdotal family.

Once Herod had the government of all Judea, he promoted private citizens who had supported him and punished those who had sided with his enemies. He honored Pollio the Pharisee and his disciple Sameas for advising citizens to receive him during the siege of Jerusalem. Additionally, Herod secured Idumea with four hundred horsemen and two thousand footmen, sending his brother Joseph there to prevent any innovations by Antigonus. He also moved his mother and all his relations who had been in Masada to Samaria to ensure their security before marching to drive Antigonus's garrisons out of the remaining parts of Galilee.
