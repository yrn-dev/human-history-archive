# The Hanging Gardens of Babylon: Myth or Reality?

The Hanging Gardens of Babylon stand as one of the most enduring enigmas of the ancient world. Listed as one of the Seven Wonders of the Ancient World, they are described as a lush, ascending series of tiered gardens featuring a diverse array of flora, irrigated by a sophisticated system of pumps and canals to mimic a mountain landscape in the arid plains of Mesopotamia. However, unlike the Pyramids of Giza, the Gardens leave behind a frustrating lack of physical evidence.

## Overview
The traditional narrative attributes the gardens to King Nebuchadnezzar II (r. 605–562 BCE), who allegedly built them for his Median wife, Amytis, to cure her homesickness for the green hills of her homeland. While Greek and Roman historians—such as Berossus and Diodorus Siculus—wrote extensively about the gardens, these accounts were penned centuries after the events they describe.

The central historical conflict is the "silence of Babylon." Despite the vast amount of cuneiform tablets recovered from the Neo-Babylonian Empire, none explicitly mention "Hanging Gardens." This discrepancy has led modern historians to two primary theories: either the gardens were a poetic exaggeration (a myth), or they existed in a different location entirely.

A compelling alternative theory proposed by Dr. Stephanie Dalley of Oxford University suggests the gardens were actually located in Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire, built by King Sennacherib (r. 704–681 BCE). Dalley argues that misidentifications by later historians conflated Nineveh with Babylon. Archaeological evidence at Nineveh reveals a complex irrigation system and reliefs depicting lush gardens, providing a physical basis that Babylon lacks.

## Key Facts
*   **Architectural Design:** Described as "hanging" not because they were suspended, but because they were built on vaulted terraces (*kremastos* in Greek).
*   **Engineering:** If they existed, they required advanced hydraulic engineering to lift water from the Euphrates to the highest tiers.
*   **The "Nineveh Hypothesis":** This theory suggests the gardens were a misattributed Assyrian achievement rather than a Babylonian one.

## Did You Know?
*   **Missing Records:** Nebuchadnezzar II left detailed records of his palace and walls, but never mentioned the gardens.
*   **Botanical Diversity:** Ancient texts claim the gardens contained exotic plants brought from all over the known world.
*   **Literary Tropes:** Some historians believe the gardens were a "literary trope" used by Greeks to depict the exoticism and luxury of the East.
