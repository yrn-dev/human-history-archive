# Contents of Historical and Sacred Texts

The organization and description of a book's contents vary significantly depending on whether the work is a modern publication, a historical record, or a sacred text. In archival practice, a standard description of a book typically includes a literal transcript of the title-page and colophon, details regarding the physical size, form, and number of leaves, a statement of the literary contents and their starting points, and any necessary additional information.

## Structure of Sacred Texts

The arrangement of sacred texts often reflects specific theological or traditional canons. In the Hebrew Bible, the Old Testament is divided into three primary parts:

*   **The Torah (Law):** This section consists of five books, corresponding to the Pentateuch.
*   **The Prophets:** This division contains eight books, split into the "Former Prophets" (Joshua, Judges, Samuel, and Kings) and the "Latter Prophets" (Isaiah, Jeremiah, Ezekiel, and the Minor Prophets, the latter of which are counted as one book called "the Twelve").
*   **The Writings (Hagiographa):** This section comprises eleven books divided into three groups: poetical books (Psalms, Proverbs, Job); the five *Megilloth* or "Rolls" (Song of Songs, Ruth, Lamentations, Ecclesiastes, and Esther); and the remaining books (Daniel, Ezra and Nehemiah—which form one book—and Chronicles).

Other sacred works exhibit more complex or fragmented structures. The *Book of Baruch*, a deutero-canonical book of the Old Testament, is placed between Jeremiah and Lamentations in the LXX, and after Lamentations in the Vulgate. Its contents are divided into a historical preface (i. 1-14) and a confession of sin used by the Palestinian Remnant (i. 15-ii. 5). This confession, sent from Babylon to Jerusalem, acknowledges national sin and views the Exile as a righteous judgment.

The *Book of Enoch* is divided into five parts (i.-xxxvi., xxxvii.-lxxi., lxxii.-lxxxii., lxxxiii.-xc., and xci-cviii). Critical analysis by Dillmann suggests these consist of a "groundwork" written during the time of John Hyrcanus, sections written before 64 B.C., Noachic fragments, and a final part from a later hand.

## Historical and Scholarly Works

Historical records often emerge from the compilation of disparate sources. In Japan, during the Kwanei era (1621-1643), Ieyasu established the Momijiyama Bunko library in the castle of Yedo to store records copied from noblemen's houses and Buddhist temples. This led to the creation of two significant works: the *Kwanei Shoke Keizu-den*, a 372-volume work of genealogical tables, and the *Honcho Tsugan* (originally *Honcho Hennen-roku*). The latter, concluded by Harukatsu in 1635, consists of 300 volumes covering the period from the age of the Gods to the year 1610.

In the realm of biblical scholarship, J.G. Eichhorn's *Einleitung* (Introduction to the Old Testament), published between 1780 and 1783, treated the Old Testament as Oriental literature. His three-volume work applied textual and literary criticism to the scriptures, leading to conclusions regarding the late origin of *Ecclesiastes* (placed in the Persian Period, 538-332 B.C.) and the composite nature of the book of *Isaiah*.

## Linguistic and Legal Compendiums

Some works are structured to serve as reference tools. David Kimhi (c. 1160-1235) authored the *Sefer Miklol* ("Book of Completeness"), which is divided into a grammar and a lexicon (*Sefer Hashorashim*). Kimhi also wrote *Et Sofer* ("Pen of the Writer"), a grammatical compendium consisting largely of extracts from the *Miklol* to guide the punctuation of biblical manuscripts.

In legal history, the referencing of the Code, the Pandects, and the Institutes was historically conducted by mentioning the law number and the first words of the title. However, some scholars, such as Gibbon, adopted a more rational method of numbering the book, the title, and the law.
