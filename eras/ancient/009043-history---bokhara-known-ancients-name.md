# Bokhara

## Ancient and Classical History
In antiquity, Bokhara was known by the name of Sogdiana. The foundation of its capital is attributed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab. Due to its eastern location, it was never brought under the dominion of Rome, though it was deeply involved in the various bloody revolutions of Asia. Following the conquests of Alexander the Great, Sogdiana became part of the Seleucidae empire and shared the fortunes of Bactria.

Later, the nomad Yue-chi migrated from the east into the valley of the Oxus, establishing themselves as a settled territorial power. Under King Kadphises I, who is believed to have ascended the throne around A.D. 45, the dominions extended from Bokhara to the Indus. The district was subsequently reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty. By the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand.

## Islamic Period and the Middle Ages
Islam was introduced following the Arab conquest of Persia (640-642) and quickly became the dominant faith. During the early centuries of Mahommedan rule, the region—then called Sughd—was one of the most flourishing and celebrated districts of central Asia. It contained two primary cities: Samarkand, which generally served as the seat of government, and Bokhara, which earned a high reputation as a center of learning and religion. During the early middle ages, this region was also known as Ma wara 'l Nahr (or Ma-vera-un-nahr), a term translated as Transoxiana.

At the end of the 11th century, Malik Shah, the third of the Persian Seljuk dynasty, crossed the Oxus and subdued the entire country watered by the Jaxartes and the Oxus. In 1216, Bokhara was subdued by Mahommed Shah Khwarizm, but this conquest was seized by Jenghiz Khan in 1220. While the savage conqueror wasted the country, prosperity partially returned under his son, Ogdai Khan, who is described as benevolent and humane. His descendants held possession until 1369, when Timur (Tamerlane) established his capital at Samarkand.

## Intellectual and Religious Significance
Bokhara has served for ages as a center of religious life and learning. During the middle ages, Persian mysticism spread to the city. When Mongol invasions of the 13th century devastated other Moslem cities and Samarkand, Bokhara remained independent and continued to be a primary seat of Islamitic learning. 

Although many rich *madrasa* libraries were lost to conflagrations, confiscated by emirs, or scattered, treasures of literature remain in private libraries. Consequently, bibliophiles from Turkey, Armenia, Persia, and Afghanistan still visit Bokhara to purchase rare books, making the city the principal book-market of central Asia.

## Trade and Economy
Bokhara is the most important trading town in central Asia. Its bazaars specialize in the manufacture and sale of carpets, metal wares (particularly copper), silk stuffs, and Kara-kul (astrakhan) lamb-skins. Additionally, the chiefs of Bokhara highly esteem white hawks or falcons from the mountains of Wakhán, which are celebrated for their swiftness.

In the modern era, a Russian town called New Bokhara, or Kagan, has grown rapidly near the railway station, located 8 miles from the city on territory ceded by the emir.

## Political Relations and Conflict
Bokhara's history is marked by territorial disputes, particularly with the khanate of Khokand. The town of Khojent, located on the road between Bokhara and Khokand, was a frequent bone of contention. In 1864, the amir of Bokhara took possession of Khojent after assisting Khudayar Khan in regaining his throne. However, the Russians stormed Khojent in 1866.

The khanate of Khokand, which grew in the 18th century, experienced periods of instability. Between 1822 and 1867, wars occurred between the nomad Kipchaks and the settled Sarts. During the period of 1858-1866, Khokand fell under the suzerainty of Bokhara, which supported the Kipchak representative, Khudayar-khan. In 1866, following the occupation of Jizakh and Ura-tyube, the khanate of Khokand was separated from Bokhara.

## Demographics and Geography
The population of Bokhara is subject to varying estimates; Russian travellers suggest it does not exceed 50,000 or 60,000, while other estimates place it between 75,000 and 100,000. The population includes a large and ancient colony of Jews. Geographically, the city is separated from Khwarizm by a desert; according to Ibn Batuta, this desert stretch is an 18-day journey, while the total journey from Ukek to Bokhara would take at least 60 days.
