# Tiglath-pileser III and the New Assyrian Empire

Tiglath-pileser III (also known as Pulu) was an Assyrian monarch who rose to power in 745 B.C. His reign marked the foundation of what historians identify as the New Assyrian Empire, a period characterized by a shift toward political centralization and the creation of a professional military machine.

## Political and Administrative Reforms
Under Tiglath-pileser III, the Assyrian state transitioned from a system supported by feudal nobility to one managed by an elaborate bureaucracy. He introduced the concept of centralization into politics, organizing conquered provinces into districts. Each of these districts was required to provide a military contingent and pay a fixed tribute to the king. 

The Assyrian military was transformed into a standing army through careful discipline and successive improvements. This "irresistible fighting machine" was used to implement a policy of "blood and iron," with the ultimate goal of reducing the entire civilized world into a single empire to secure the world's trade and wealth for Assyria.

## Conquests and Expansion
Tiglath-pileser III directed Assyrian expansion south-westward, fighting across the territories of the Arameans (whose chief city was Damascus). To secure the high-roads of commerce leading to the Mediterranean and the Phoenician seaports, he broke the power of the Hittites and terrorized the Medes and Armenia.

A primary objective for Tiglath-pileser III and subsequent Assyrian kings was the possession of Babylon. In the ancient world, supremacy in Asia was not recognized *de jure* until a ruler had "taken the hands" of Bel-Merodach at Babylon, becoming the adopted son and inheritor of the old Babylonian empire. By conquering Babylon, Tiglath-pileser III sought to legitimize his power. He attained the summit of this ambition in 729 B.C., when he was invested with the sovereignty of Asia in the holy city of Babylon.

## Interactions with the Israelites
Tiglath-pileser III is identified in the Bible as "Pul." He was the first Assyrian monarch to appear on the Hebrew horizon. In 738 B.C., Menahem, the king of Israel, bought him off with a thousand talents of silver. However, Tiglath-pileser III later returned to the region. While he directed the transfer of the Israelites to Media—the "Lost Ten Tribes"—some records suggest that the actual deportation of these tribes was carried out by his successor, Sargon II, in 721 B.C.

## Successors and the Decline of Assyria
Tiglath-pileser III died in Tebet 727 B.C. He was succeeded by his son, Shalmaneser IV (also known as Ulul), who continued his father's policies. Shalmaneser IV died suddenly in 722 B.C. during the siege of Samaria. He was succeeded by a usurper named Sargon II, who took an ancient Akkadian Sumerian name to flatter Babylonian sensibilities. Sargon II is credited with arming the Assyrian forces with iron weapons for the first time, a technology communicated to the Assyrians by the Hittites.

Following Sargon II, the empire saw several other notable rulers:
* **Sennacherib:** He led an army toward Egypt, but his forces were destroyed by pestilence (plague) in 701 B.C. Unlike his predecessors, Sennacherib failed to secure the support of the Babylonian priesthood and never underwent the ceremony of "taking the hands" of Bel. In 689 B.C., he razed Babylon's walls and temples to the ground, an act that shocked the religious conscience of western Asia.
* **Esarhaddon:** He rebuilt Babylon to receive his crown and conquered Egypt, taking Thebes and overthrowing the Ethiopian XXVth Dynasty.
* **Assurbanipal (Sardanapalus):** He conquered Egypt in 670 B.C., replacing the Ethiopian dynasty. However, by his age, the Assyrian state was drained of its fighting population.

The Assyrian Empire eventually collapsed in 606 B.C. when Nineveh was captured by the Chaldeans and Medes, leading to the foundation of the Chaldean Empire.

## Comparison with Babylonia
The archive notes a fundamental difference in character between the two sister-states. Babylonia was a land of agriculturists and merchants, where priests were often raised to the throne by revolution and the king remained a priest under a powerful hierarchy. In contrast, Assyria was described as an "organized camp." Its dynasties were founded by successful generals, and the Assyrian king functioned as an autocratic general whose palace was more sumptuous than the temples of the gods.
