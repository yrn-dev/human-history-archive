# Antiquaries and Historical Interpretations

In the study of history and archaeology, the role of antiquaries—those who study and collect artifacts and ancient records—has often been central to the naming and categorization of historical sites and systems. However, archival records indicate that some classifications and names attributed to the past by antiquaries have been challenged or corrected by later research.

## Roman Britain and Road Networks
In the context of Roman Britain, the concept of "Four Great Roads" is identified as a category likely invented by antiquaries. This classification is noted as being unconnected with the actual layout of Roman Britain. Instead, the road system is more accurately described as four main groups of roads radiating from London, with a fifth running obliquely.

These groups include:
*   A south-east route to the Kentish ports and Canterbury, with Richborough (Rutupiae) being the most frequented.
*   A western route to Silchester, branching toward Bath, Exeter, Winchester, Gloucester, and South Wales.
*   A route known to the English as Watling Street, which ran to Chester and Wroxeter via Lichfield (Letocetum) and St Albans Wall, with branches to Lincoln and Leicester.
*   A route serving York, Lincoln, the eastern counties, and Colchester.
*   A route known as the Fosse, connecting Leicester and Lincoln with Bath, Exeter, and Cirencester.

Other less prominent roads include Akeman Street, providing access from London to Bath via Alchester, and a road connecting the Humber with the lower Severn, passing through Birmingham and Derby. Additionally, Icknield Street is a prehistoric "Ridgeway" (not Roman) along the Chilterns and Berkshire downs. While antiquaries suggested this road extended to Norfolk, Newmarket, and Royston, and linked it to the Celtic Iceni tribe, early documents do not support this, and the name is not connected to the Iceni.

## Geographic Naming and Military Campaigns
The influence of antiquaries extends to the naming of geographic features. Julius Agricola, a Roman general, campaigned in the farther north around A.D. 80. He established a frontier between the Forth and the Clyde, guarded by forts such as those at Bar Hill and Camelon. Agricola achieved a "famous victory" at Mons Graupius, likely near the Isla and Tay confluence, where the Inchtuthill encampment is located. 

Although the battlefield was misspelt as "Grampius," this error was adopted by antiquaries. Through their work, this misspelling provided the modern world with the name of the Grampian Hills.

## Roman Topography and Numismatics
In Rome, antiquaries have engaged in disputes regarding the summit of the Capitoline hill. It has been determined by some that the summit nearest the river is the Arx, or Mons Tarpeius, while the other summit—occupied by the barefoot friars of St. Francis—contains the temple of Jupiter. However, these conclusions have been impugned and the questions revived by M. Bunsen and Niebuhr.

In the field of numismatics, the division of noble and baser metals between the senate and the emperor is not presented as a positive fact, but rather as the probable opinion held by the best antiquaries. This view is supported by the *Science des Medailles* of Père Joubert and the work of Dr. Cardwell.
