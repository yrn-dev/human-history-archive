# Ecbatana and Asia Minor

## The City of Ecbatana
Ecbatana was a major city located in Media. It served as a significant center of power across various eras, functioning as a residence for the dominion of Western Persia and as one of the great cities of the Persian Empire, alongside Susa and Persepolis.

The origins of the city are associated with different figures depending on the source. One account identifies Dejoces as the founder of the city, while a passage in Judith suggests that Arphaxad built it; however, a Greek text of Judith indicates that Arphaxad instead added new buildings to the existing city. In the context of Scythian power, which grew in the old kingdom of Ellip to the east of Assyria, Ecbatana was built by a "Manda" prince.

The city later became a focal point of political transition. Astyages, the king of the Scythians (or "the Manda"), ruled from Ecbatana. In either 549 B.C. or 553 B.C., Cyrus, the king of Anshan in Elam, revolted against Astyages. After the army of Astyages betrayed their king to Cyrus, Cyrus established himself at Ecbatana. This event marked the end of the Scythian empire, which Greek writers referred to as the empire of the Medes due to a confusion between the terms "Manda" and "Mad" (Medes).

## Regional Infestations in Asia Minor
Asia Minor experienced various periods of instability and infestation by different groups throughout history. During the era of the Assyrian Empire, the region was infested by the Cimmerians, a Scythian tribe. The death of the Scythian leader Dugdamm (known as Lygdamis to Strabo) was viewed by the Assyrian ruler Assur-bani-pal as a sign of divine favor.

In later periods, other groups caused disruption in the region. The Isaurians were known to infest the roads of Asia Minor during the winter, extending their reach as far as the neighborhood of Constantinople. Additionally, pirates used Asia Minor as a base for their operations; specifically, a town of Lycia in Asia Minor served as one of the headquarters for pirates who carried their ravages from the coast of Asia Minor to the shores of Greece, the Ionian Sea, and the Italian shores.

## Political Transitions in Asia Minor
The peninsula of Asia Minor saw numerous shifts in governance. Following the death of Alexander, the region was given to Seleucus, though no Seleucid king managed to rule the entire peninsula. The Seleucids established several cities in southern Asia Minor to serve as centers of commerce, including Attalia, Apamea, Antioch, the Seleuceias, and the Laodiceas. These cities contributed to the spread of Christianity and the Hellenization of the area.

Other independent powers emerged in the region:
* **Pergamum:** An independent kingdom founded in 283 B.C. that lasted until 133 B.C., when Attalus III made the Romans his heirs.
* **Bithynia:** Established as an independent monarchy.
* **Cappadocia and Paphlagonia:** These functioned as tributary provinces under native princes.
* **Galatia:** In 278-277 B.C., Gallic tribes crossed the Hellespont and Bosporus to establish a Celtic power in central Asia Minor. They were eventually confined to a district on the Halys and Sangarius by Attalus I of Pergamum around 232 B.C., and later subjected to the suzerainty of Pergamum in 189 B.C. after a defeat by Manlius.

## The Persian Empire and Regional Geography
Under Darius I, the Persian Empire became the largest the world had seen, encompassing all of Syria and Asia Minor, as well as the old Babylonian and Assyrian empires, Egypt, the Caucasus, the Caspian regions, Media, and Persia, extending as far as the Indus in India. This vast administration was supported by the use of coined money and a system of great arterial roads with post horses for official messengers.

Geographically, the region was divided into several provinces. Upper Asia began at the river Indus and included provinces such as Bactriana (capital: Bactra), Persia (cities: Elymais and Persepolis), and Susiana (city: Susa). Asia between the Caspian Sea and the Pontus Euxinus included Colchis, Iberia, Albania, and the greater Armenia. The latter was separated from Assyria by mount Niphates and from Mesopotamia by mount Taurus, with the river Araxes running through it and the cities of Tigranocerta and Artaxata located within its borders.
