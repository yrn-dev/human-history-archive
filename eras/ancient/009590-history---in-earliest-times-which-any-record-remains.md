# Early Human and Earth History

The study of the earliest periods of existence and human civilization relies on a variety of sources, ranging from geological formations and ancient artifacts to dynastic records and linguistic fragments.

## The Record of Rocks
Knowledge of life preceding human memory and tradition is derived from the "Record of the Rocks." This consists of markings and fossils—including bones, shells, stems, fruits, fibers, and footmarks—preserved in sandstone, limestone, slate, and shale. These fossils exist alongside pittings from the earliest rainfalls and ripple marks from the earliest tides. Because sedimentary rocks have been distorted, bent, and crumpled over time, the record has required lifetimes of work to be read and ordered. The total compass of time represented by this geological record is estimated at 1,600,000,000 years.

## Early Populations and Migrations
In southern Asia, the earliest known stratum of population is represented by semi-barbarous races, including various tribes in the Malay Archipelago and China, as well as the Veddahs of Ceylon. Some of these groups offer analogies to Australians, a connection that must be very ancient as it dates to a time when the distribution of water and land differed from the present. In northern Asia, aborigines such as the hyperborean races (including Chukchis) and the Ainus of Japan are found, though materials for their history are currently unavailable.

Records indicate that later races were superimposed on these aborigines. The Persians and Hindus arrived from the north-west, the Chinese from the west, and the Siamese and Burmese from the north. While the historical movements of the Turks and Mongols from central Asia are documented, it is unknown if they had an earlier home.

## Early Chinese Civilization
The earliest civilization of China may have been a brunette civilization, potentially linked to the earliest Sumerian, Dravidian, and Egyptian civilizations, rather than being Mongolian. By 1750 B.C., China consisted of a vast system of city-states and little kingdoms. These entities paid feudal dues and acknowledged a loose allegiance to a great priest emperor known as the "Son of Heaven."

The Shang dynasty ended in 1125 B.C., succeeded by the Chow dynasty. The Chow maintained a relaxing unity until the era of the Ptolemies in Egypt and Asoka in India. However, China eventually fragmented during the Chow period as local rulers stopped paying tribute and Hunnish peoples established principalities. By the sixth century B.C., one authority records that there were five or six thousand practically independent states, a period known as the "Age of Confusion."

Historically, the Chinese practice of dividing landed possessions among all sons prevented the rise of a class of great land-owners. Instead, land was kept in small, intensively cultivated freeholds. This system created masses of town populations who owned almost no property and were neither slaves nor serfs; these populations provided the gang labor for building walls and canals, as well as the soldiers for the government. In the ages before the Christian era, the slave class and war captives played a smaller role in China than in any more westerly record.

## Ancient Records and Symbols
Ancient records provide insight into early social structures and cultural practices:

*   **Egypt:** A hieroglyphic bee found on the sarcophagus of Mykerinos, dating to 3633 B.C., represents the king of Lower Egypt. This serves as an illustration of how a bee colony was viewed as an embodiment of government by a ruler.
*   **Persia:** The *Shah Nameh* (Book of Kings), composed by the poet Dukiki and completed by Ferdusi under the patronage of Mahmood of Ghazni, sought to preserve original Persian traditions. It mentions four dynasties from the earliest ages until the Saracenic invasion, beginning with the "dark and fabulous" period of Kaiomors and followed by the "heroic and poetical" Kaianian dynasty.
*   **Bohemia:** The earliest genuine documents of the Bohemian language include legends (such as those of St Dorothy and St Catherine) and hymns. The rhymed chronicle of Dalimil, written likely during the reign of King John (1310-1346), records Bohemian history from the earliest period up to the reign of King Henry of Carinthia.
*   **Japan:** Records of the Hojo rulers between 1224 and 1284 indicate a period of justice and clemency. In Kamakura, the first half of every month was dedicated to judicial proceedings, and a bell was hung at the Record Office gate for petitioners to signal for immediate attention.
