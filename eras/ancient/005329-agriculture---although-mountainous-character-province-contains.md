# Agriculture

Agricultural practices and productivity vary significantly across different global regions, often dictated by geography, soil quality, and climate. In many mountainous or rugged areas, farming is restricted to specific zones such as valleys or coastal deltas, while other regions rely more heavily on stock-breeding or specialized crops.

## North American Provinces
In British Columbia, despite its mountainous character, there are many tracts of good farming land. These are located on the deltas of rivers running to the coast, on the lower slopes of mountains, and within the long valleys between interior mountain ranges. Vancouver Island also possesses much good farming land. The region is generally best suited for mixed farming, with hay, potatoes, oats, and wheat serving as the chief crops. 

Certain areas are specifically suited for sheep and cattle raising, including the country around Kamloops and the Yale district. Fruit raising has received significant attention, particularly in the Okanagan valley. The region produces excellent quality cherries, plums, apples, apricots, peaches, grapes, and various small fruits, most notably strawberries. Hops are also cultivated. These fruits find a large market in the growing provinces of Saskatchewan and Alberta.

## Spanish Provinces
In the province of Burgos, agriculture is limited by poor soil and a lack of summer rain, thriving primarily in the valleys, especially that of the Ebro. While other industries are undeveloped, Burgos is one of the richest Spanish provinces regarding livestock. The peasantry relies mainly on sheep-farming, with more than 500,000 head of sheep recorded at the beginning of the 20th century, grazing on vast ranges of almost uninhabited upland. Other animals bred in considerable numbers include asses, mules, horses, cattle, goats, and pigs. The city of Burgos serves as a center for the sale of agricultural produce.

The province of Avila is divided into two sections with different social economies and productions. The northern portion is generally level with indifferent soil—rocky in the Sierra de Avila valleys and strong and marly in a few places—and its population is mainly agricultural. The southern division consists of rugged granitic sierras with sheltered, well-watered valleys that abound with rich vegetation. In this southern region, stock-breeding is an important industry. The city of Avila maintains a considerable trade in agricultural products.

## Asian and Other Regions
In China's Kwei-chow province, agricultural products are very limited. The province is largely mountainous, with the exception of plains near Tsun-i Fu, Ta-ting Fu, and Kwei-yang Fu. While the province's chief wealth is mineral, wild silk is a valuable export, manufactured primarily in the prefecture of Tsun-i Fu. In another region described by Marco Polo, good wheat and barley without husk are grown. This area produces no olive oil, instead utilizing oil made from walnuts and sesamé.

In Japan, historical agricultural developments include the digging of a large canal in Yamashiro province and the construction of nine reservoirs for irrigation. Additionally, a Korean prince once attempted, unsuccessfully, to domesticate bees on a Japanese mountain.

## South American Plains
In Bolivia, while the country is perceived as rugged and mountainous, three-fifths of it consists of forest regions, great swamps, low alluvial plains, and flooded bottomlands. In eastern Chuquisaca and southern Santa Cruz, low-lying plains and extensive swamps are of little value for pastoral or agricultural purposes due to periodical inundations. However, some areas above the floods provide rich grazing lands. The llanos of Mojos and Guarayos contain extensive open plains and rich agricultural districts where cattle-raising has been successfully practiced since the era of the Jesuit missions.

## Historical Roman Britain
Geographically, the Roman province of Britain was divided into two distinct areas regarding agriculture. The south, east, and midlands consisted of comparatively flat lowlands that were suitable to agriculture. In contrast, the uplands—including northern England, Wales, and the hills of Cornwall and Devon—were mountainous in character, scarred with deep valleys and gorges. These upland regions were considered ill-fitted to the peaceful pursuits of agriculture and were instead characterized as an extensive military frontier devoid of farms.
