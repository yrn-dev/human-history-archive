# The Hamadryas Baboon of North Africa and Arabia

The Hamadryas baboon (*P. hamadryas*) is a primate species native to North Africa and Arabia. In the context of ancient history, this animal was dedicated by the ancient Egyptians to the god Thoth.

## Biological Characteristics and Classification
The baboon is a large terrestrial monkey characterized by a long, naked, dog-like muzzle with nostrils pierced at the truncated extremity. They typically possess short or medium-sized tails and short, thick limbs that are better adapted for walking than for climbing. Other physical features include large cheek-pouches and large, often brightly colored, naked callosities on the buttocks.

Scientifically, the baboon is categorized within the genus *Papio* (formerly known as *Cynocephalus*). While the genus was once extended into India, it is now confined to Africa and Arabia. The *P. hamadryas* specifically typifies the group *Hamadryas*. Other members of the genus include the yellow baboon (*P. cynocephalus*), the anubis baboon (*P. anubis*), the South Arabian baboon (*P. arabicus*), and the chacma (*P. porcarius*).

## Behavior and Habitat
Baboons generally frequent barren rocky districts, where they live in large droves. They are described as being exceedingly fierce and dangerous to approach. Their diet is opportunistic, consisting of practically everything eatable that they are able to kill or capture.

## Relation to Ancient Egypt
The ancient Egyptians had a specific relationship with various baboon species. While the Hamadryas baboon was dedicated to the god Thoth, the anubis baboons (*P. anubis*) were tamed by the Egyptians. According to frescoes, these animals were trained to pluck sycamore-figs from trees. The term "baboon" itself is derived from the French *babuin*, which originates from *Babon*, the Egyptian deity to whom the animal was sacred.

## Regional Context: North Africa and Arabia
The regions inhabited by the Hamadryas baboon are characterized by diverse geography and history. In Arabia, the peninsula is largely a gaunt, sand-strewn and lava-covered landscape, though the south-western and southern regions—historically referred to by the Romans as Arabia Felix—were once more favored provinces with high mountains and rich forests.

The flora of these regions includes the date palm (*Phoenix dactylifera*), which ranges from the Canary Islands through Northern Africa to India. In Arabia, the date palm is the primary source of national wealth and its fruit is a staple food. In Northern Africa and the oases of the Sahara, dates are often pressed and pounded into cakes for consumption.

The fauna of the "Ethiopian Subregion," which encompasses Arabia and the whole of Africa (excluding the Barbary States), also includes the *Struthio* (ratitae). Additionally, the deserts and borders of North Africa and Arabia are inhabited by the *C. githagineus*, a species of bird allied to the pine-grosbeak.
