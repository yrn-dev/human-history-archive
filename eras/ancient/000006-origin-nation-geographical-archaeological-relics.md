# ORIGIN OF THE NATION: GEOGRAPHICAL AND ARCHAEOLOGICAL RELICS

The study of the origin of the Japanese nation relies upon four primary guides: linguistic affinities, physical features, archaeological relics, and written annals. While the annals—consisting of the Chronicles and the Records—identify six distinct peoples, historical evidence suggests the nation is composed of four elements: the Yamato, the Sushen, the Kumaso (or Hayato), and the Yemishi. Regarding the Sushen, there is no conclusive evidence that they immigrated in significant numbers. The Yemishi are identified as the modern Ainu.

## Archaeological Evidence of Early Cultures

Archaeological research reveals the existence of two distinct cultures in Japan, with traces of a third. These cultures are distinguished by the types of relics they left behind and the locations where they are found.

The older culture is characterized by a neolithic stage. Its relics are primarily found embedded in the soil or within shell-heaps, with more than four thousand residential sites and shell-heaps attesting to its existence. This culture is defined by the total absence of metallic objects and the use of pottery that was not turned on a wheel. Tools and weapons from this period were made of bone, horn, or stone that was roughly hewn, finely chipped, or polished. This culture is identified as belonging to the aboriginal inhabitants, the Yemishi.

The second culture is associated with the Yamato race, or the Japanese proper. Its remains are found mainly in caves or sepulchral chambers. Unlike the neolithic culture, the Yamato culture utilized hard pottery molded on a wheel and iron weapons. While they produced models of stone articles and imitations of arrow-heads, swords, and sheath-knives, they did not use stone weapons. Copper was used primarily for the work of the silversmith and goldsmith, and was also found in mirrors, jingle-bells, and arrow-heads.

## The Bronze Culture and Continental Connections

Evidence suggests a third, intervening bronze culture existed in the south between the iron and stone phases. The presence of specific swords, which have also been discovered in Shantung, suggests that this bronze culture originated in eastern and central Asia. This hypothesis is supported by the fact that bronze vestiges are completely absent in the southern provinces of Kyushu, specifically Satsuma and Osumi.

A significant aspect of this archaeological record is the discovery of bronze bells. These bells have been found in twenty-four provinces, though they never appear at the same sites as bronze hand-weapons. In the main island, they extend as far east as Totomi—where they are numerous along with in neighboring Mikawa—and are most abundant in Omi. In Kyushu, they have only been seen in Higo.

These bells range in height from one foot four inches to four and a half feet. Their specialized shape is only cognate with bells used in China during the Chou dynasty (1122-225 B.C.) for military signaling. Furthermore, the decorative designs on these bells—consisting of spirals, triangles, and circles—are identical to motives found on Chinese drums of the Han dynasty (202 B.C.–A.D. 220). Because these circles and triangles also appear in the sepulchral pottery of Yamato sites, and because the bells are abundant in areas where the Yamato were strongly established, these relics are attributed to the Yamato culture.

## Historical and Methodological Context

The reconstruction of this history has been aided by the efforts of Japanese students over the last three decades who collected and collated disjointed fragments of the country's history. This labor provided the materials necessary for joint interpretation by historiographers.

It is noted, however, that historical evidence may not be exhaustive. The annalists of Japan did not appear to give special attention to racial questions. While the written annals name six groups—including Izanagi and his fellow Kami (identified as original immigrants), Jimmu and his followers (who re-conquered the islands), the Yemishi, the Kumaso, the Sushen, and the Tsuchi-gumo (earth-spiders)—it is not necessarily implied that these six represented different races.
