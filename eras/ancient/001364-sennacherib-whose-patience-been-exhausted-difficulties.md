# Sennacherib

Sennacherib was a king of Assyria and the son of Sargon II. He ascended to power in 705 B.C. and is noted for his military campaigns in the Euphrates valley, the Levant, and Egypt, as well as his actions toward the city of Babylon.

## Relations with Babylonia
In the Babylonian and Assyrian religions, the pantheons were structured with a chief deity—Marduk in the south and Assur in the north. While Assyrian rulers generally recognized the religious predominance of Babylon and the status of Marduk, they maintained absolute political control over the region. Assyrian kings often appointed their own brothers or sons as governors of Babylonia and used specific titles to denote their mastery over the district. The ceremony of "taking the hand of Bel-Marduk" was regarded by both Babylonians and Assyrians as an essential preliminary for exercising authority in the Euphrates valley.

However, Marduk and Assur became rivals when Babylonia caused difficulties for the Assyrians. In 689 B.C., Sennacherib, whose patience had been exhausted by these difficulties, destroyed Babylon.

## Campaign Against Hezekiah
In the fourteenth year of the reign of Hezekiah, king of the two tribes, Sennacherib launched an expedition against him with a great army. During this campaign, Sennacherib took all the cities of the tribes of Benjamin and Judah by force. As he prepared to march on Jerusalem, Hezekiah sent ambassadors to promise submission and pay any tribute the Assyrian king appointed.

Sennacherib initially agreed to depart in a friendly manner if he received thirty talents of gold and three hundred talents of silver. He provided an oath of security to the ambassadors. Hezekiah complied, emptying his own coffers and the treasures of the temple to pay the sum. Despite these treaties and oaths, Sennacherib continued the war. He pushed his conquests forward, taking all the strong places of Judah except for Jerusalem, which was reduced to the utmost extremity.

## Conflict with Egypt and Ethiopia
While Jerusalem was besieged, Sennacherib received word that Tirhakah, the king of Ethiopia, had joined forces with the king of Egypt to assist the city. This foreign assistance had been requested by the chief men of Jerusalem despite the remonstrances of Hezekiah and the prophet Isaiah.

Sennacherib wrote a letter to Hezekiah containing blasphemy against the God of Israel, boasting that he would vanquish Him just as he had vanquished the gods of other nations. Sennacherib then marched to meet the approaching enemy. He discomfited the Egyptians and pursued them into their own country, which he ravaged before returning with spoil.

## The Destruction of the Assyrian Army
Following his actions in Egypt, Sennacherib returned toward Jerusalem. However, his army was smitten by a pestilence. According to the Second Book of Kings, the angel of the Lord went out at night and smote 185,000 Assyrians, leaving them as dead corpses by morning. 

Upon finding his army destroyed, Sennacherib departed and returned to dwell at Nineveh. Following these events, two of Sennacherib's sons fled to Armenia, where they became the founders of two prominent families: the Genunii and the Arzerunii.
