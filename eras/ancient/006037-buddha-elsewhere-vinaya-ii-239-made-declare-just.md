# The Buddha and Early Buddhist Doctrine

The Buddha was a teacher who, at the age of 35, sought to proclaim a new gospel. After discovering that his former teachers, Udraka and Alara, had died, he traveled to the Deer-forest near Benares to address his first five disciples. During this journey, the Buddha encountered a wandering sophist named Upaka. When questioned about the nature of his religion, the Buddha stated that he was traveling to Benares to "beat the drum of the Ambrosia" (setting up the light of the doctrine of Nirvana) within the darkness of the world. He identified himself as the Buddha who knows and knows no teacher. When Upaka asked if he was a conqueror and an Arahat, the Buddha replied that conquerors are those who have conquered intoxications—specifically those arising from craving after future life, sensuality, or ignorance—and that he was a conqueror because evil dispositions had ceased within him.

## Concepts of Suffering and Emancipation

The core of the Buddha's teaching concerns the passing away of pain or suffering, which depends upon emancipation. In the *Vinaya* (ii. 239), the Buddha compares his doctrine and discipline to the great ocean, stating that just as the ocean has only one taste—the taste of salt—his teachings have only one flavor: the flavor of emancipation. 

This emancipation is described as a habit of mind characterized by freedom from a specific type of craving, which is identified as the origin of certain types of pain. The Buddha describes the state of a "brother" who has personally realized and continues to abide in this emancipation of the heart and mind, known as Arahatship, as a condition that is sweeter and higher still, serving as the purpose for which brethren lead a religious life under him.

## The Origin of Sorrow

The Buddha's description of suffering is presented as a series of truisms. A central tenet is that the *Upadana Skandhas*—the five groups of constituent parts of every individual—involve pain. This implies that the very conditions required to create an individual are the same conditions that necessarily produce sorrow. 

According to this view, as soon as an individual becomes separate, they are subject to decay and disease. Individuality leads to limitation, which in turn leads to ignorance, the ultimate source of sorrow. The results of this individuality include unsatisfied craving, separation from the pleasant, and union with the unpleasant. These statements are presented as facts rather than explanations of the origin of evil, serving as a protest against contemporary pessimistic beliefs that salvation could only be found through rebirth in heaven rather than on earth.

## Canonical Authorities and Texts

The life and teachings of the Buddha are preserved in several ancient authorities. The Canonical Pali texts, which reached their current form before the 4th century B.C., include:
*   ***Majjhima Nikaya*** and ***Anguttara Nikaya***: Containing accounts of the Buddha's birth and youth.
*   ***Sutta Nipata***: Containing ballads regarding the adoration of the babe and the "going forth," as well as fifty-five poems.
*   ***Digha Nikaya***: Including prose accounts of the "going forth" and the *Mahaparinibbana Suttanta*, which details the Buddha's last days and final journey.
*   ***Vinaya***: A five-volume work edited by Oldenberg that provides a complete statement of the rules of the order founded by the Buddha.

Other significant texts include the *Jataka* book, a collection of 550 folk-tales where the Buddha is identified as the hero in previous births. Buddhist Sanskrit texts also exist, such as the *Mahavastu* (likely 2nd century B.C.) and the *Lalita Vistara* (likely 1st century B.C.).

## The Buddhist Order

The Buddha founded a society or order with specific rules of conduct. The early retreats for the mendicants in this order were simple huts built in groves of palm-trees to serve as shelters during the rainy season. Over time, these structures increased in magnificence before the eventual decay of Buddhism. The Buddha consistently used the title "Arahat" for himself; he did not refer to himself as "the Buddha," nor did his followers address him by that title.
