# The Azores

The Azores (Portuguese: *Acores*), also known as the Western Islands, are an archipelago located in the Atlantic Ocean and belong to the kingdom of Portugal. The islands are positioned in an oblique line from the northwest to the southeast, situated between 36° 55' and 39° 55' N. latitude and between 25° and 31° 16' W. longitude. Rising from depths exceeding 2.5 miles, the archipelago is divided into three widely separated groups:
*   **The South-eastern group:** Comprising St Michael's (Sao Miguel), St Mary (Santa Maria), and Formigas.
*   **The Central group:** Comprising Fayal (Faial), Pico, St George (Sao Jorge), Terceira, and Graciosa.
*   **The North-western group:** Comprising Flores and Corvo.

The Azores are the most distant Atlantic island group from any continent. The nearest continental land is Cape da Roca on the Portuguese coast (830 miles east of St Michael's), followed by Cape Cantin on the African mainland (over 900 miles away) and Cape Race in Newfoundland (over 1,000 miles away). Despite this isolation, they are generally regarded as part of Europe due to their European flora and climate. As of 1900, the archipelago had an area of 922 square miles and a population of 256,291.

## History
Ancient Greeks and Romans appear to have had no knowledge of the islands, though the discovery of Carthaginian coins on Corvo suggests the islands may have been visited by Carthaginians. Arabian geographers, including Edrisi in the 12th century and Ibn-al-Wardi in the 14th, described nine islands in the Western Ocean north of the Canaries. These writers depicted the islands as having been populous with cities of some magnitude, though they noted that inhabitants had been reduced by intestine warfare.

The islands first appeared distinctly on a map in 1351, where the southern group was called the Goat Islands (*Cabreras*), the middle group the Wind or Dove Islands (*De Ventura sive de Columbis*), and the western group the Brazil Island (*De Brazi*), using the term "Brazil" to refer to a red dye-stuff. A 1375 Catalan map identifies St George as *San Zorze*, Corvo as *Corvi Marini*, and Flores as *Li Conigi*.

While some have conjectured that the discoverers were Genoese, evidence is insufficient. The "Flemish discovery" by van der Berg in 1432 is considered secondary; according to one account, he was driven to the islands by chance, which prompted interest in the court of Lisbon. Alternatively, it is suggested that Prince Henry the Navigator possessed a map of the islands and dispatched the navigator Gonzalo Velho Cabral to prosecute the discovery.

## Government and Infrastructure
The Azores are organized into three administrative districts named after their chief towns:
*   **Ponta Delgada:** The capital of St Michael's, which also includes St Mary.
*   **Angra (Angra do Heroismo):** The capital of Terceira, which also includes St George and Graciosa.
*   **Horta:** The capital of Fayal, which also includes Pico, Flores, and Corvo.

The districts return members to the parliament in Lisbon, with Ponta Delgada returning four members and the other two districts returning two each. Roman Catholicism is the majority creed, and Angra serves as an episcopal see. Military administration is split into two commands headquartered at Ponta Delgada and Angra.

The principal seaports are Ponta Delgada (population 17,620 in 1900), Angra (10,788), and Horta (6,574). Ponta Delgada is noted as one of the most frequented and important coaling stations. Horta is recognized as a major center for the transatlantic cable system. Communication is maintained via regular mail services and cables; a cable connects Lisbon to Villa Franca do Campo in St Michael's, extending to the central group. Additionally, Fayal was connected to Waterville, Ireland, by a cable laid in 1901. Meteorological stations are located at Ponta Delgada and Angra.

## Flora and Fauna
The Azores, along with Madeira and the Canary Islands, are classified within the Mediterranean province. The avifauna of these islands is the result of colonization occurring over a long period. Insular birds generally differ from their continental counterparts, typically possessing stronger bills and legs and darker plumage.

Specific examples of this variation include the bullfinch of the western islands (*Pyrrhula murina*), which has a mouse-colored breast instead of the ruddy breast found in *P. vulgaris*. The Azores, Madeira, and the Canaries all share a specific chaffinch (*F. tintillon*) that is not found elsewhere.
