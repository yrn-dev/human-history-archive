# The Indus and the Geography of Western India

The Indus is the primary river of western India. It enters the Bombay Presidency from the north of Sind and follows a tortuous course southward before emptying into the Arabian Sea. The river reaches the sea through several mouths, including the Khori, Sir, Bhitiara, Wari, Mall, Vatho, Hajamro, Sisa, Pitiani, Khudi, and Ghizri creeks.

## Hydrology and Seasonal Changes
The Indus exhibits significant seasonal variations in its bed and flow. During the dry season, the width of the riverbed varies between 480 and 1,600 yards. The flood season occurs from March through September, during which the average depth increases from 9 to 24 feet and the current's velocity rises from 3 to 7 miles per hour.

In the district of Dera Ghazi Khan, the Indus is not bridged and is navigable by native boats. Similarly, in the district of Bannu, the river is navigable for native boats throughout its 76-mile course. In the lower valley of Sind, the river's water can reach "blood heat" during the six hottest months of the year.

## Regional Geography and Lakes
The lower valley of the Indus, known as Sind, is characterized by very flat terrain with scanty vegetation, making its productivity entirely dependent on irrigation. On the right bank of the Indus is situated Manchar Lake, which reaches a length of 20 miles and a breadth of 10 miles during inundations, covering an estimated total area of 180 square miles.

To the east of the Indus lies a desert characterized by ridges of low sandhills. Further west, the region includes the Runn or Lake of Cutch. This feature is geologically peculiar, functioning as a salt marsh, an inland lake, or an arm of the sea depending on the season, with an area of 8,000 square miles. During the rains, the Runn floods and unites the Gulfs of Cambay and Cutch, effectively turning the territory of Cutch into an island.

## Geology and Mountain Systems
The geological composition of the region varies significantly across the presidency:

*   **Cutch and Kathiawar:** These areas feature isolated hills that constitute the western extremity of the Aravalli range. Geologically, they are covered by horizontal lava flows of the Deccan Trap series. Beneath these flows lie Neocomian beds (containing shales and sandstones with plant remains identical to the upper Gondwana system) and marine Jurassic beds containing numerous Ammonites. Tertiary shales, sandstones, and limestones overlie the Deccan Trap in Cutch.
*   **Sind and Eastern Gujarat:** These plains are covered by wind-blown sand and alluvium.
*   **Northern Boundaries:** On the right bank of the Indus in the north of the presidency, the Hala mountains—a continuation of the Suleiman range—serve as the boundary between British India and the dominions of the khan of Kalat.

## Climate and Environment
The climate of the Indus region is marked by extreme heat and aridity. Upper Sind is described as resembling the sultry deserts of Africa due to its sandy soil and extreme dryness. In Lower Sind, the mean maximum temperature in the shade during the six hottest months is 98 degrees Fahrenheit; however, in Upper Sind, temperatures have been recorded as high as 130 degrees in the shade.

While the south-west monsoon brings heavy rains to the coast and the Konkan region starting in June, it exerts little influence over Sind.
