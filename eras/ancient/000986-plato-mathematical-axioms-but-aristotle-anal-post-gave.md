# Axiom

An axiom (from the Greek *axioma*) is a general principle or proposition that is accepted as self-evident. This acceptance may be absolute or limited to a specific sphere of thought. Within the structure of special sciences, each possesses its own axioms—referred to in Aristotelian terms as *archai* or "first principles"—though these may sometimes be proven within the context of a wider science.

## Evolution of the Term in Greek Philosophy
The use of the term "axiom" evolved significantly between the eras of Plato and Aristotle. It is probable that Plato confined the use of the word to mathematical axioms. Aristotle, however, expanded the significance of the term in *Anal. Post.* i. 2. He applied it to the ultimate principles of thought that underpin all special sciences, such as the principle of contradiction. According to Aristotle, these ultimate principles are apprehended solely by the mind, although the mind may be led toward them through an inductive process.

## Post-Aristotelian and Early Modern Usage
Following Aristotle, the term was adopted by the school of Ramus and the Stoics, who used it to refer to a proposition simply. Francis Bacon further broadened the definition in *Nov. Organ.* i. 7, applying the term to any general proposition. 

In the development of modern philosophy, the term was likely reintroduced by René Descartes or his followers. Seeking a definite, self-evident principle to serve as the foundation for a new philosophy, Descartes looked to the science of mathematics. Consequently, the axiom of Cartesianism is the *Cogito ergo sum*. Later, Immanuel Kant narrowed the definition again, limiting axioms to intuitive, self-evident synthetic propositions regarding time and space.

## Axioms in Scientific and Mathematical Method
The application of axioms varies across different intellectual disciplines:

*   **Mathematics and Geometry:** The geometrical method utilizes axioms as a basis for proof. For example, Spinoza adopted a mathematical approach in his *Ethics*, following the manner of Euclid. He placed definitions, postulates, and axioms at the start of each book to form an intuitively certain basis for his system; his work contains twenty axioms, twenty-seven definitions, and eight postulates.
*   **Physical Science:** Francis Bacon addressed the testing of axioms in *N. O.* i. 106. He argued that when establishing axioms through induction, one must examine whether the axiom is framed to the measure of the particulars from which it is derived, or if it is "larger and wider." If it is wider, it must be observed whether new particulars confirm this wideness as a "collateral security" to avoid grasping at abstract forms or shadows rather than things realized in matter. Bacon also noted that while the syllogism is incompetent for principal things, there is no reason why it should not be employed in mathematics. In physics, he argued that the syllogism can be used after the axioms and notions of the first class have been well-defined by induction.

## Philosophical Critiques and Logic
The nature of axiomatic certainty remains a fundamental problem in metaphysics and logic. Those who reject the possibility of non-empirical knowledge maintain that every axiom is ultimately based on observation. 

Additionally, some critiques have been leveled at the deductive methods of Greek philosophers. It has been argued that Plato, Aristotle, and other Greeks were at fault when applying deductive methods to physical subjects, not necessarily because of the amount of argumentation used, but because they too hastily admitted inaccurate or false axioms without dispute.
