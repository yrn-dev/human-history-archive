# Delegation and Representative Assemblies

The concept of delegation and the use of delegates vary significantly across different historical political systems, ranging from consultative bodies and judicial commissions to the formal administration of dual monarchies.

## Delegation in Classical Antiquity

In the Roman Republic, the archive indicates that representative government was non-existent. The Popular Assembly did not function as a representative body, and there was no practice of electing delegates to represent the will of the citizens. Furthermore, Roman constitutional principles established that a general could not formally make a treaty; such agreements required the sanction of the senate or, in earlier periods, the patrician body in their assembly. This reflects a principle where the power a sovereign state delegates to its ministers does not authorize those individuals to perform acts inconsistent with the state's fundamental principles or constitution.

In Athens, Periklês is credited with introducing the formalities of law-making through specially delegated *nomothetæ*. To prevent the public assembly and these delegates from making decisions contrary to existing law, the *Graphê Paranomôn* (indictment for informality or illegality) was established. This allowed any Athenian citizen to bring a prosecution before the *dikastery* against a proposer of a law or *psephism* who failed to ensure their measure did not contradict pre-existing laws or failed to provide formal notice of such contradictions.

During the conflict involving the Four Hundred and the heavy infantry in Piraeus, delegates from the Four Hundred were used to negotiate with the infantry. These delegates reasoned with the moderate members of the infantry to prevent the ruin of the state, proposing that the Four Hundred be chosen from the Five Thousand in rotation.

## Religious and Judicial Commissions

Delegation has also been employed in ecclesiastical contexts to gather evidence. During a conflict involving the Arians, a synod appointed an episcopal commission consisting of six delegates to collect evidence on the spot. This measure was vigorously opposed by Egyptian bishops and led to scenes of perjury and violence. Following the return of these deputies from Alexandria, the council pronounced a sentence of exile and degradation against the primate of Egypt.

## The Austro-Hungarian Delegation

Under the principle of dualism, a specific system of delegation was established to manage common institutions between Hungary and the other territories of the king. A "Delegation" of sixty members was elected from both the Hungarian and Austrian parliaments. These Delegations were responsible for the common ministers—specifically those overseeing finance, war, and foreign affairs—and reviewed estimates for joint services.

To ensure the Delegations did not overshadow the parliaments, they were required to meet separately and communicate via written documents translated into German or Magyar. A common session was only convened if three exchanges of notes failed to produce an agreement; in such sessions, voting was done in silence by simple majority. The Delegations possessed no legislative power; for example, laws regarding army recruitment had to be voted on separately in each parliament, and the common budget required funding from the separate parliaments.

In Austria, the Delegation was chosen by representatives of separate territories rather than by the House as a whole. This structure meant the Delegation was not necessarily representative of the majority of the chamber of deputies but included representatives from various opposing groups. This allowed opposition to continue within the Delegation, as seen in 1869 when Poles and Tirolese voted in favor of a Hungarian budget proposal that Austria had rejected.

## Delegation in Imperial China

In the early 20th century, China established a National Assembly composed of approximately one hundred members. This body included delegates from the provinces alongside high officials and distinguished scholars appointed by the Throne. This assembly functioned as a consultative rather than an executive body, discussing matters such as the annual budget, taxation, and law amendments. All such subjects required the emperor's approval before being submitted to the assembly. Resolutions required the backing of at least thirty members and, if passed, were submitted as a memorial to the Throne. The emperor maintained the power to dissolve the assembly by imperial edict if resolutions were deemed objectionable.
