# The Buccina

The buccina was an ancient musical instrument that served as the technical and acoustic ancestor to both the trombone and the trumpet. Its historical development and influence are evidenced by linguistic derivations and various artistic and literary records.

## Technical Evolution and Influence
The relationship between the buccina and later brass instruments is reflected in the etymology of the words *Posaune* (the German term for trombone) and *Sackbut*, both of which are derived from *buccina*. This connection was recognized in Germany during the 15th and 16th centuries. Two specific translations of Vegetius illustrate this understanding: a 1470 publication at Ulm stated that the "bucina das ist die trumet oder pusan" (the bucina is the trumpet or trombone), and a 1534 publication at Augsburg described the buccina as the "trummet die wirt ausz und eingezogen" (the trumpet which is drawn out and in).

In a 1488 French translation by Jean de Meung, a distinction was made between the *trompe*, described as long and straight, and the *buisine*, which was described as short and reflected upon itself, similar to a part of a circle.

## Artistic and Archaeological Evidence
The buccina is distinguishable from other instruments, such as the *tuba* and the *cornu*, on Trajan's column. Further visual evidence of the instrument is found in the work of François Mazois, specifically in *Les Ruines de Pompéi* (Paris, 1824-1838), pt. iv, pl. xlviii. fig. 1. Additionally, J.N. von Wilmowsky's *Eine römische Villa zu Nennig* (Bonn, 1865), pl. xii., contains mosaics depicting a *buccinator* being accompanied on the *hydraulus*.

## The Buccina Marina
The military version of the buccina was a more advanced instrument than its prototype, the *buccina marina*. The *buccina marina* was a primitive trumpet fashioned from a conical shell, which often featured a spiral twist. In poetic contexts, this instrument was frequently referred to as the *concha*. It is often depicted in the hands of sailors—such as on a terra-cotta lamp shown by G.P. Bellori in *Lucernae veterum sepulcrales iconicae* (1702)—or in the hands of Tritons, as noted by Macrobius.

## Other Historical References
The apocryphal letter of St Jerome to Dardanus mentions the buccina in relation to Semitic races, claiming that the tuba is called *bucca* among the Hebreos, and through diminution, it is called *buccina*.

Following the collapse of the Roman Empire, the specific art of bending metal tubes was gradually lost. While the buccina continued to exist in Europe during the Middle Ages in terms of its name and basic construction principles, it permanently lost the characteristic "C" shaped curve that it had previously shared with the *cornu*.
