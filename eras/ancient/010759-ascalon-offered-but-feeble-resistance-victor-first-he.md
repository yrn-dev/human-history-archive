# Ascalon

Ascalon (modern Askalan) was one of the five chief cities of the Philistines, situated on the Mediterranean coast 12 miles north of Gaza. A well-fortified town, it served as the seat of worship for the fish-goddess Derketo.

## Ancient and Classical History
The city appears several times in the Tell el-Amarna correspondence. Its early history was marked by two separate revolts against Egypt, both of which ended in the city being reconquered; a sculpture at Thebes depicts the storming of the city. While Ascalon was located within the nominal territory of the tribe of Judah, it was never held by the Israelites for any significant length of time. The Bible records an incident of spoliation by Samson in the city, though this may have occurred at a different location of the same name in the hill country of Judaea.

In the ancient world, the Philistines of Ascalon took the city of Sidon, an event that led many Sidonian inhabitants to flee by ship and found the city of Tyre. Later, in 701 B.C., the city was taken by Sennacherib. The subsequent conquest by Alexander led to the hellenization of Ascalon's civilization. Following Alexander's era, the city became a tributary to Egypt and Syria alternately.

## Roman and Byzantine Eras
Herod the Great, a native of Ascalon, contributed significantly to the beauty of the city. However, the city suffered severe damage during the later wars involving the Romans and the Jews. In one instance, a large multitude of hardy soldiers—led by John the Essene, Silas of Babylon, and Niger (called the Persite)—marched from Jerusalem (a distance of 520 furlongs) to attack Ascalon. At the time, the city was strongly walled but lacked significant assistance, relying on a garrison consisting of one troop of horsemen and one cohort of footmen under the captain Antonius.

By the 4th century, Ascalon rose to importance once more. Until the 7th century, it served as a center of learning and the seat of a bishopric.

## Medieval Period and Crusades
The city was conquered by the Moslems in the 7th century. During the first crusade, Christians achieved a signal victory in the neighboring plain on August 15, 1099, but the city itself remained under the control of the caliphs until 1157. At that time, Baldwin III, King of Jerusalem, captured the city after a five-month siege. In 1178, Baldwin IV gave the city to his sister, Sibylla, upon her marriage to William of Montferrat.

In 1187, Saladin nearly annihilated the Christian army in the plain of Tiberias. Ascalon offered only a feeble resistance to Saladin, who initially strengthened and repaired the city's fortifications. However, after Richard Coeur de Lion captured St Jean d'Acre (Acre) in 1191, Saladin ordered Ascalon to be dismantled. The English king restored the city the following year, but it was subsequently abandoned.

## Decline
Following its abandonment, Ascalon lost much of its importance. In 1270, Sultan Bibars almost totally destroyed the city's fortifications and filled its port with stones.
