# The Growth of Christianity After the Time of Constantine

## Population and Proportions of the Faithful
Before the conversion of Constantine, the number of Christians was relatively small compared to the general population of the empire. According to the testimony of Origen, the proportion of the faithful was very inconsiderable when measured against the multitude of an unbelieving world. Calculations based on examples from Rome and Antioch suggest that no more than a twentieth part of the empire's subjects had joined the faith prior to Constantine's conversion. In the beginning of the fourth century, Christians still represented an inadequate proportion of the empire's inhabitants.

However, the actual strength of the group often appeared more formidable than their numbers suggested due to their habits of union, zeal, and faith. Following the time of Constantine, the numbers of the faithful increased enormously.

## Factors Contributing to Expansion
The growth of the faith was accelerated by several political and social factors. Constantine's edicts of toleration removed temporal disadvantages that had previously hindered progress, allowing ministers of the faith to recommend the truths of revelation freely. 

The conversion of the emperor himself served as a powerful example. The "venal and obsequious crowds" of the palace were influenced by the emperor's exhortations, his smiles, and the hope of attaining wealth and honors. This trend extended to the lower ranks of society, where dependent multitudes followed the conversion of those with power, riches, or eminence of birth. In some instances, the salvation of the common people was facilitated by material incentives; it is reported that the emperor promised twenty pieces of gold and a white garment to every convert, and that in one year, twelve thousand men—along with a proportional number of women and children—were baptized at Rome.

Additionally, the faith spread through the military. Constantine chose ministers and generals in whom he had confidence, and through these dignified missionaries, the faith multiplied within the army and the court. The German Barbarians who filled the legion ranks generally acquiesced to the religion of their commander.

## Architectural Evolution of Worship
The increase in the number of the faithful necessitated changes in the buildings used for worship. In the 2nd or 3rd century A.D., early Christian buildings likely consisted of an oblong interior ending in an apse. After the time of Constantine, as the population of the faithful grew enormously, side aisles were added to these structures. This modification gave the buildings an appearance similar to that of the civil basilica. Evidence for this evolution has been found at S. Saba on the Aventine in Rome, where a small, early church without aisles was discovered beneath the floor of a later basilica.

## Constantine's Relationship with the Church
The process of Constantine's own conversion is viewed by different sources in various ways. Lactantius claims the sovereign acknowledged the true and only God in the first moments of his reign, while Eusebius attributes his faith to a miraculous sign in the heavens during the preparation for the Italian expedition. Conversely, Zosimus asserts that Constantine only renounced the gods of Rome after killing his eldest son.

In a strict ecclesiastical sense, Constantine was not a member of the faithful until his last illness, when he received the imposition of hands as a catechumen and was admitted through baptism. However, he functioned as a protector and proselyte of the church long before this. He was granted a tacit dispensation from severe disciplinary rules, allowing him to pray with the faithful, dispute with bishops, and celebrate the vigil of Easter before he had contracted the formal obligations of a Christian. He eventually publicly disclaimed the superstition of his ancestors, refusing to offer vows to the Jupiter of the Capitoline Hill and declaring that his image should no longer appear in idolatrous temples.

## International Influence and Political Alliances
The success of the new faith extended beyond the Roman borders. In Armenia, King Tiridates' conversion was likely a matter of policy to fortify his realm against the Sassanids of Persia and draw closer to the West. Eusebius notes that the Armenians were ancient allies of the Roman empire and ardent Christians by the time Maximin attacked them around 308. Tiridates sent Aristaces to the council of Nice in 325, and Gregory, who later became a hermit on Mount Sebuh around 332, is said to have added a clause to the creed brought back by Aristaces.
