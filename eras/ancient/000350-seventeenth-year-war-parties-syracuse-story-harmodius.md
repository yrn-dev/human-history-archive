# Seventeenth Year of the War and the Story of Harmodius and Aristogiton

## Parties at Syracuse
During the seventeenth year of the war, news of the Athenian expedition reached Syracuse from various sources, though it was initially met with disbelief. An assembly was convened where different orators delivered speeches, some contradicting the reports of the expedition and others believing them. Among these was Hermocrates, son of Hermon, who provided counsel based on his conviction that he knew the truth of the matter.

The political climate in Syracuse was marked by internal divisions. Athenagoras, who was averse to war, denounced the push for military conflict as the interested policy of the oligarchical party. He expressed apprehension that the oligarchical youth were promoting military organization for their own separate interests. This concern was mirrored by historical precedents, such as in Argos, where a select regiment of one thousand hoplites chosen from the oligarchical youth subverted the democratical constitution within three years. Following the Athenian invasion, the ascendency of Hermocrates increased, while Athenagoras ceased to appear.

## Athenian Military Movements in Sicily
The Athenian generals divided their armament into two parts by lot. They sailed toward Selinus and Egesta to investigate a quarrel between the two cities and to determine if the Egestaeans would provide money. During this voyage, they touched at Himera, the only Hellenic city in that region, but were refused admission. They subsequently captured Hyccara, a petty Sicanian seaport at war with Egesta, enslaved the inhabitants, and gave the town to the Egestaeans.

Nicias sailed directly from Hyccara to Egesta, where he transacted business and received thirty talents. The Athenian forces later sold their slaves for one hundred and twenty talents. While urging Sicel allies to send troops, the Athenians attempted to take the hostile town of Hybla in the territory of Gela but were unsuccessful. Later, Nicias was suspected of knowing more than he revealed regarding his strategy; Thucydides suggests Nicias may have been misled by a secret correspondence with a party in Syracuse—which included some Leontines incorporated into Syracusan citizenship—who urged him to remain, claiming Syracuse could not endure much longer.

## The Conspiracy of Harmodius and Aristogiton
The Athenian historical record includes the daring action of Harmodius and Aristogiton, which occurred in 514 B.C. during the thirteenth year of the reign of Hippias. Pisistratus had died in possession of the tyranny and was succeeded by his eldest son, Hippias (though it is vulgarly believed that Hipparchus succeeded him). 

The conspiracy was sparked by a love affair. Harmodius, a youth of great beauty, was the lover of Aristogiton, a citizen of middle rank. Hipparchus, the son of Pisistratus, solicited Harmodius without success. Fearing that the powerful Hipparchus might use force to take Harmodius, Aristogiton formed a design to overthrow the tyranny.

Following a second failed solicitation, Hipparchus sought to insult Harmodius covertly. He invited Harmodius's sister, a young girl, to serve as a basket carrier (kanêphoræ) in a religious procession, only to reject her upon arrival on the grounds that she was unworthy and that the summons had never been issued. This public humiliation filled Harmodius with indignation and further exasperated Aristogiton.

## The Assassination and Aftermath
The conspirators, who were few in number to ensure security, planned their attack for the festival of the Great Panathenaea. This was the only day citizens could gather in arms without suspicion. While they appeared as part of the armed procession, they carried concealed daggers. Their hope was that a few daring spirits would inspire others to use their arms to recover their liberty.

During the festival, while Hippias was outside the city in the Ceramicus arranging the procession, Harmodius and Aristogiton feared they had been discovered after seeing an accomplice talking with Hippias. In a rush to take revenge, they entered the gates and encountered Hipparchus at the Leocorium. They fell upon him and slew him. Harmodius was killed on the spot, and while Aristogiton initially escaped through the crowd, he was later captured and executed.

Despite the failure of the conspiracy to immediately end the tyranny—as Hippias's reign lasted four more years until 510 B.C.—the Athenian public later mistakenly believed the two friends had liberated the city. They were celebrated in songs as the authors of liberty who "slew the despot and gave to Athens equal laws." Following the final expulsion of the Peisistratids, statues were erected in their honor, and their descendants were granted immunity from public burdens and taxes.

## The Nature of the Peisistratid Tyranny
The government of the tyrants was generally not considered odious or grievous to the multitude. The tyrants cultivated virtue and wisdom and adorned the city splendidly without exacting more than a twentieth of the Athenians' income. They provided sacrifices for temples and left existing laws in place, though they ensured that family members held key offices. For example, Pisistratus, son of Hippias, held the yearly archonship and dedicated altars to Apollo in the Pythian precinct and to the twelve gods in the market-place.
