# Second Assyrian Empire

The Second Assyrian Empire, also referred to as the New Assyrian Empire, was established in 745 B.C. by Tiglath-pileser III. This era marked a significant departure from the first Assyrian empire through its greater consolidation and the introduction of political centralization. Under this new system, conquered provinces were organized via an elaborate bureaucracy led by the king, with each district required to provide a military contingent and pay a fixed tribute.

## Military Expansion and Innovation
The Second Assyrian Empire was characterized by its transformation into an "organized camp" and the first power to employ the "doctrine of blood and iron." The Assyrian forces were developed into a disciplined standing army and an "irresistible fighting machine." Key military advancements included the replacement of chariots with cavalry, the introduction of saddles and high boots for riders by Tiglath-pileser III, and the creation of a corps of slingers by Sennacherib. The army utilized battering-rams, baggage-carts, and tents during marches, with the commander-in-chief, known as the *tartan*, ranking immediately below the king.

Technologically, the Assyrians adopted the use of iron weapons, a skill communicated to them by the Hittites. The usurper Sargon II is credited with arming the Assyrian forces with iron for the first time.

## Territorial Conquests
The primary objective of Assyrian policy was to reduce the known civilized world into a single empire to control its wealth and trade. Tiglath-pileser III began this process by breaking the power of the Hittites and terrorizing the Medes and Armenia. He secured the Phoenician seaports and the high-roads of commerce leading to the Mediterranean. In 745 B.C., he conquered Babylon, and by 729 B.C., he was invested with the sovereignty of Asia in the holy city of Babylon.

Following Tiglath-pileser III, his successor Shalmaneser IV continued these policies but died in 722 B.C. during the siege of Samaria. This led to a period of instability where a general named Sargon (Sargon II) seized the throne. During this transition, the Babylonian prince Merodach-baladan entered Babylon and was crowned king, resisting the Assyrians for twelve years before fleeing to the marshes of southern Babylonia. Sargon II eventually crushed the northern nations, took the Hittite stronghold of Carchemish in 717 B.C., and annexed the future kingdom of Ecbatana.

## Relations with the Israelites and Egypt
The empire expanded south-westward, fighting across the territories of the Arameans (whose chief city was Damascus). In 738 B.C., Menahem, the king of Israel, bought off Tiglath-pileser III with a thousand talents of silver. However, the Assyrians continued their push toward Egypt. Sargon II is credited with the deportation of the Israelites (the "Ten Tribes") to Media in 721 B.C.

Later, Sennacherib led an army toward Egypt in 701 B.C., but his forces were destroyed by a plague (pestilence) rather than military strength. The Assyrian conquest of Egypt was eventually realized by Esarhaddon, who took Thebes and overthrew the Ethiopian XXVth Dynasty. Later, in 670 B.C., Assurbanipal (also known as Sardanapalus) conquered Egypt, replacing the Ethiopian dynasty with Assyrian rule.

## Social and Political Structure
Assyrian society differed fundamentally from that of Babylonia. While Babylonia was a land of merchants, agriculturists, and a powerful priesthood, Assyria was dominated by a military aristocracy. The Assyrian king functioned as an autocratic general, and his palace was more sumptuous than the temples of the gods, from which it remained separate.

Socially, the empire utilized a large body of slaves. Above them were the commercial and agricultural classes, though these were less numerous in Assyria than in Babylonia. Scribes held a more important social position in Assyria. Unlike Babylonia, where the king remained a priest under a hierarchy, the Assyrian state was driven by its military apparatus.

## Decline and Fall
The empire's reliance on its fighting population proved a vulnerability. The state eventually collapsed after being drained of its military strength during the age of Assurbanipal. Pressure increased from the north as the Persians, Medes, and Scythians advanced, while Babylon remained in a state of insurrection. The empire came to a definitive end in 606 B.C. when the Chaldeans and Medes captured Nineveh, leading to the foundation of the Chaldean Empire.
