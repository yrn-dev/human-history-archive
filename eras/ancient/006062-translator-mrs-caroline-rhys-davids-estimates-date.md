# Buddhist Psychology and the Works of Mrs. Caroline Rhys Davids

## Buddhist Psychology and the Dhamma Sangani
Within the canonical books of Buddhism, there are seven works on *Abhidhamma*, which provide a more classified and elaborate exposition of the doctrine (Dhamma) than that found in the *Nikayas*. These works are considered later additions to the canon. Of these seven, only one has been translated: the *Dhamma Sangani*.

This work was translated by Mrs. Caroline Rhys Davids and published under the title *Buddhist Psychology* in 1900. The translation includes an introduction that provides the most comprehensive account available of the psychological conceptions that serve as the basis for Buddhist ethics. Mrs. Caroline Rhys Davids estimates that this ancient manual, intended for Buddhist students, dates back to the 4th century B.C.

## Contributions of T.W. Rhys Davids
The archive records an extensive body of translation and scholarly work by Rhys Davids. His translations include:
*   *Vinaya Texts* (with Oldenberg, 1881-1885)
*   *Questions of King Milinda* (1890-1894)
*   *Buddhist Suttas* (1881), which includes the translation of the *Mahaparinibbana Suttanta* regarding the last days of the Buddha.
*   *Dialogues of the Buddha* (1899), which contains translations of the *Mahali Suttanta* and the *Digha Nikaya* (i. 71-74), as well as recurring paragraphs on the most ancient forms of ecstatic meditation exercises.
*   *Jataka* (vol. i.), published as *Buddhist Birth Stories* (1880).

In addition to translations, Rhys Davids authored several manuals and monographs, including *Buddhism* (1903), *American Lectures, Buddhism* (1896), and *Buddhist India* (1903). He also edited the *Yogavacaras Manual of Indian Mysticism as practised by Buddhists* for the Pali Text Society in 1896, a work based on a unique manuscript.

## Scholarly Research and Chronology
Rhys Davids contributed to various historical and critical analyses of Buddhist texts and figures. He wrote an article on "Buddhist Schools of Thought" for the *J.R.A.S.* in 1892 and conducted research into the identity of the stories of Buddha and St. Josaphat.

In his work *Ancient Coins and Measures of Ceylon* (1877), Professor Rhys Davids discussed the chronological questions surrounding the date of the Buddha. The generally accepted European date is derived by combining the date of Asoka's accession to the throne (approximately 270 B.C., based on mentions of Greek kings in his edicts) with the interval between that accession and the Buddha's death (218 years according to the Ceylon Chronicles). This calculation places the Buddha's death at 488 B.C. and his birth at 568 B.C., given that he was eighty years old at death. This differs from dates accepted in Siam, Burma, and Ceylon, which are roughly half a century earlier (543 and 623 B.C.).

## Related Buddhist Concepts
The archive's sources note that the Buddha always used the title "Arahat" for himself; he did not call himself "the Buddha," nor did his followers address him by that name. 

Regarding spiritual practice, the records indicate that ecstatic meditation was a practice followed by the Buddha's teachers and was admitted as part of the training for the Buddhist Bhikshu. These "Conditions of Bliss" are viewed as useful for removing mental obstacles to achieving Arahatship. However, seeking Arahatship through ecstasy alone is regarded as a "deadly heresy," as it is a means to an end rather than the end itself. Arahatship consists of thirty-seven constituent parts, and the states of Rapture comprise one group of four within those parts.
