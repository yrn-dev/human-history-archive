# Basilides

Basilides was a prominent exponent of Gnosticism who lived in Alexandria, likely during the first few decades of the 2nd century. While Eusebius's *Chronicle* dates his first appearance to A.D. 133, other evidence suggests his activity may have begun earlier; Agrippa Castor, who lived under the reign of Hadrian (117-138), wrote a polemic against him.

## Writings and Sources
Basilides authored an exegetical work consisting of twenty-four books based on a gospel of his own, though the specific identity of this gospel remains unknown. Other related texts include *Ethika*, *Exegetika* on the prophet Parchor, and writings by his son, Isidorus, titled *Peri prosphuous psuches*. 

Much of the knowledge regarding Basilides' system comes from the accounts of the Fathers of the Church, though these sources vary. Irenaeus provides a sketch of his school of thought, which is closely related to the *Syntagma* of Hippolytus (preserved in Philaster, Epiphanius, and Pseudo-Tertullian). These accounts are supplemented by notices in the *Stromateis* of Clemens Alexandrinus. A different account with a monistic tendency is found in the *Philosophumena* of Hippolytus, though recent opinion tends to favor the accounts of Irenaeus and the *Syntagma*.

## Cosmological System
The system of Basilides involves a supreme God, also referred to as the *innatus pater*. From this deity emanated 365 heavens and their accompanying spirits, a concept corresponding to astronomical daily aspects. The supreme God is associated with the name Abraxas (or Abrasax), which carries the numerical value of 365.

The *Pater innatus* is surrounded by a series of Hypostases. According to Irenaeus, these include *Nous*, *Logos*, *Phronesis*, *Sophia*, and *Dunamis*; Clemens suggests that *Dikaiosune* and *Eirene* may also be included. Furthermore, the world originates from seven powers—the lowest emanations of the supreme God—who create and rule the world.

Unlike many other Gnostic sects, Basilides' system is distinguished by the total absence of the idea of a fall within the godhead or the figure of the fallen female god, Sophia Achamoth. His doctrines are noted for their closeness to Persian dualism and the system of Saturninus.

## The Saviour and Jesus of Nazareth
The concept of salvation is introduced when the supreme God sends his *Nous* (also called Christ) into the world to address the rising pitch of worldly confusion. Clemens refers to this Saviour as *diakonos* or *pneuma diakonoumenon*.

The relationship between this Saviour and the historical Jesus of Nazareth is a subject of varying accounts:
* **The Earthly Jesus:** Basilides himself recognized an earthly Jesus but denied the principle of his sinlessness. Some views within the Egyptian school held that Jesus was a mere mortal and the son of Joseph and Mary, selected as an instrument to restore the worship of the supreme Deity.
* **The Descent of the Christ:** According to the *Excerpta ex Theodoto*, the *Diakonos* descended upon Jesus during his baptism in the form of a dove. This event led followers of Basilides to celebrate the day of baptism (*epiphaneia*) as a high festival.
* **The Nature of the Body:** Irenaeus reports that the Saviour appeared only as a phantasm. Consequently, Basilides is associated with the Docetes, a group that taught the non-reality of the material body of Christ.
* **The Passion:** Some interpretations suggest that the immortal Christ forsook the earthly body of Jesus before his death, leaving the mortal man to suffer and expire. Other modifications suggest Jesus was endowed with a miraculous apathy that rendered him insensible to suffering, or that his passion served to expiate venial transgressions.

## Redemption and Magic
In the system of Basilides, salvation is defined as the separation of that which was combined for evil, leading to the restoration and fulfillment of all things. This process begins with the mission of the Saviour and the "great separation of the sexes."

Among later followers of Basilides, magic became a central element. They believed that knowing the secret names of the rulers of the various heavens would allow a soul to pass through those heavens to the supreme God after death. In this context, Christ was believed to possess a mystic name (*Caulacau*) that enabled his descent to earth and subsequent ascent to the Father. Consequently, redemption was viewed as the revelation of these mystic names, a belief linked to the use of Abraxas gems.
