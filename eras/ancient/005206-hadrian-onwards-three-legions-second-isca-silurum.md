# Roman Military Presence in Britain from Hadrian Onwards

From the time of the Emperor Hadrian, the Roman army tasked with guarding or coercing the province of Britain consisted of three legions and a large, though uncertain, number of auxiliary troops. The three legions provided a total of approximately 15,000 heavy infantry. These were stationed at three primary legionary fortresses: the Second Legion at Isca Silurum (Caerleon-on-Usk), the Ninth Legion at Eburacum (York), and the Twentieth Legion at Deva (Chester).

## The Second Legion at Isca Silurum
The fortress of Isca Silurum, located on the west bank of the Usk in Monmouthshire, was established to coerce the wild Silures. While some sources suggest it was founded around A.D. 50, coin-finds indicate it may have been established between A.D. 74 and 78 during the governorship of Julius Frontinus. The Legio II Augusta garrisoned the site from its foundation until near the end of Roman rule in Britain.

Unlike Eburacum, Isca Silurum remained purely military, and no municipality or civil life appears to have developed outside its walls. The site contains visible remains of the Roman period, including part of the ramparts, the site of an amphitheatre, and various sculptured stones and inscriptions. The name Caerleon is believed to be derived from the Latin *Castra legionum*.

## Legionary and Auxiliary Fortifications
Legionary fortresses were large rectangular enclosures covering 50 to 60 acres, protected by strong walls. Traces of these walls are still visible on the south side of Caerleon, in the abbey gardens at York, and in the lower courses of the north and east town-walls of Chester.

In addition to the legions, the province employed auxiliaries—troops of the second grade organized into cavalry *alae* or infantry cohorts of 500 or 1,000 men. These auxiliaries were stationed in *castella* (forts) located closer to the frontiers than the legionary bases. These *castella* were significantly smaller, generally ranging from three to six acres. Over 70 such forts are known in England and some 20 in Scotland. Notable excavated examples include Housesteads on Hadrian's wall, Hardknott on a Cumberland moor, and Gellygaer between Cardiff and Brecon.

Internal building materials varied by date; forts built before A.D. 150 used wood freely, with stone reserved for a few principal buildings, whereas later forts featured internal buildings made entirely of stone.

## Hadrian's Frontier Policy
Upon his arrival in Britain in A.D. 122, Emperor Hadrian introduced a new frontier policy, signaling that the era of Roman conquest had ended. This shift followed a period of instability; between A.D. 115 and 120, northern Britons rose in revolt and destroyed the Ninth Legion at York. To replace them, Hadrian brought the Sixth Legion.

Hadrian constructed a continuous rampart—likely made of turf rather than stone—extending over 70 miles from Wallsend (Tyne) to Bowness (Solway). This system included a ditch in front of the rampart, several small forts along its length, one or two outposts to the north, and detached forts guarding the Cumberland coast, such as the one on the hill above Maryport. This wall served as a definite limit of the Roman world.

## Imperial Strategy and Administration
The military approach of Hadrian and the two Antonines was to maintain the dignity and existing limits of the empire without attempting further enlargement. They sought the friendship of barbarians and promoted a vision of Roman power actuated by justice and order. This policy resulted in a long period of relative peace, with only slight hostilities occurring to keep the frontier legions exercised.

Across the broader empire, the peace establishment under Hadrian consisted of thirty brigades. Each legion comprised 6,831 Romans and, including auxiliaries, totaled about 12,500 men. While three legions were sufficient for Britain, the empire's principal strength was concentrated on the Rhine and Danube (sixteen legions) and the Euphrates (eight legions).
