# Pious Ejaculations and Religious Devotion

In the study of human history and religious practice, the term "pious ejaculations"—referring to short, sudden prayers or exclamations of devotion—appears across various cultures, eras, and literary contexts. These expressions often serve as markers of spiritual intensity, ritual obedience, or the internal state of the believer.

## Ancient and Mystery Cults
During the first centuries, pious ejaculations were common expressions rising from the lips of thousands of *mystae* across Italy, Asia Minor, Egypt, and other regions. These expressions were linked to a prevailing atmosphere of re-birth, which served as the keynote for various solemn mysteries and initiations, including the Eleusinian, Orphic, and Mythraic traditions. Through these processes, repentant pagans sought eternal bliss and pardon. While these practices existed alongside the early church, there is limited evidence that the church directly borrowed its interpretations or ceremonies from these outside sources; instead, many originated among believers, and it is possible that outside cults borrowed from the church.

## Zoroastrianism
In the religion of Zoroaster, the use of specific prayers, genuflections, and ejaculations was central to the life of the faithful. Upon reaching puberty, a Persian was invested with a mysterious girdle as a badge of divine protection. From that point forward, all actions—including the most necessary or indifferent—were sanctified by these peculiar devotional acts. The omission of these prayers or ejaculations was considered a grievous sin, viewed as no less guilty than the violation of moral duties. These practices, combined with moral duties such as liberality, mercy, and justice, were required for the disciple who wished to avoid the persecution of Ahriman and live in a blissful eternity with Ormusd, where felicity is proportioned to one's piety and virtue.

## The Gospel of Barnabas
The *Gospel of Barnabas* is an apocryphal work mentioned in antiquity, such as in the *Decretum Gelasii*. A longish Italian manuscript of this gospel was discovered in Amsterdam by the Deist John Toland and later held in the imperial library at Vienna. Written from a Mahommedan standpoint and potentially containing Gnostic materials, the text is described as highly imaginative and occasionally grotesque, though it is pervaded by high ethical enthusiasm. Lonsdale and Laura Ragg, who edited the work with an English translation in 1907, suggest it was written between the 13th and 16th centuries by a Christian renegade to Mahommedanism. The manuscript contains Arabic marginal notes that are described as being partly notes for Arabic students and partly pious ejaculations.

## Literary and Historical Representations
The concept of pious ejaculations also appears in historical narratives and literature to characterize specific environments or emotional states.

In the poem *Vert Vert* by the French poet Jean Baptiste Louis Gresset (1709-1777), the author depicts a convent where the nuns' conversation consists entirely of prayers and pious ejaculations. The plot follows a parrot that, after being moved to another convent, forgets this religious language and instead shocks the sisters with profane swearing.

In the historical accounts of Herodotus regarding the invasion of Greece by Xerxes, the text records the "emphatic ejaculations" of terror-stricken spectators from Abydos. One such spectator exclaimed, questioning why Zeus, appearing as a Persian man named Xerxes, would gather the human race for the ruin of Greece.
