# Babylon

## Etymology and Names
The city of Babylon, located on the left bank of the Euphrates approximately 70 miles south of Bagdad, has been known by several names throughout history. The name "Babylon" is the Greek form of *Babel* or *Bab-ili*, which translates from Semitic as "the gate of the god" (though it is sometimes incorrectly written as "of the gods"). This name is a Semitic translation of the original Sumerian name, *Ka-dimirra*.

Alternative etymologies and names exist in historical records. In an inscription by the Kassite conqueror Gaddas, the city is referred to as *Ba-ba-lam*, which appears to derive from the Assyrian *babalu*, meaning "to bring." Another foreign *Volksetymologie* is found in Genesis xi. 9, deriving the name from *balbal*, meaning "to confound." 

Other titles and names associated with the city include:
*   **Din-tir**: An ancient name favored by poets, signifying "the life of the forest" in Sumerian, although a native lexicon translates it as "seat of life."
*   **Su-anna**: A second name that may have originally referred to a specific quarter or separate village. In later inscriptions, this is often represented ideographically as *E-ki*, though the meaning and pronunciation of this term are uncertain.
*   **Uru-azagga**: Meaning "the holy city," a title applied to Babylon as well as other cities in Babylonia.

## Religious Center and Origins
The name *Ka-dimirra* (or *Bab-ili*) likely originally referred to the *E-Saggila*, the "house of the lofty head." This was the temple dedicated to Bel-Merodach, the divine patron of the city. The temple was founded in pre-Semitic times, and the city of Babylon grew around this sanctuary. Because Merodach was the son of Ea—the culture god of Eridu near Ur on the Persian Gulf—it is possible that Babylon began as a colony of Eridu. The town of Borsippa was adjacent to Babylon.

## Political History and the Monarchy
In the history of the Babylonian and Assyrian empires, the legitimacy of a monarch's power in Babylon was closely tied to the priesthood of "Bel." A ruler was not considered secure in his tenure until he had "taken the hand of Bel," meaning he had been adopted by the priesthood as the god's representative and son. Political stability often depended on the balance of power between the monarchy and the wealthy priesthoods.

The monarchy frequently relied on mercenary armies composed of foreigners, who were prone to mutiny if they lacked plunder or pay. A notable conflict occurred during the reign of Sennacherib, son of Sargon II. Sennacherib engaged in a violent quarrel with the Babylonian priesthood and refused to "take the hand of Bel." In 691 B.C., he destroyed the holy part of Babylon and moved the statue of Bel-Marduk to Assyria. Following Sennacherib's assassination by one of his sons, his successor, Esarhaddon, rebuilt the temple and restored Bel-Marduk to make peace with the deity.

## Assyrian and Regional Influence
Babylon was eventually conquered and ruled by Tiglath Pileser III, who founded the New Assyrian Empire. He is also noted for directing the transfer of the Israelites (Bani Israel) to Media. His successor, Shalmaneser IV, died during the siege of Samaria. He was followed by a usurper who adopted the ancient Akkadian Sumerian name Sargon II, likely to flatter Babylonian sensibilities. Sargon II is believed to have been the monarch who carried out the deportation of the Ten Tribes and is credited with arming Assyrian forces with iron weapons for the first time.

In later periods, Babylon remained a significant power in regional diplomacy. For example, Crœsus, after a battle with the forces of Cyrus in the territory of the Syrians or Assyrians of Kappadokia, sent envoys to Labynêtus, the king of Babylon, requesting auxiliaries.
