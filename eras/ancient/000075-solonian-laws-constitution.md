# SOLONIAN LAWS AND CONSTITUTION

The Solonian laws and constitution, promulgated in 594 B.C., represent the first known example of a disinterested and genuine constitutional reform in Grecian history. Established during the archonship of the eupatrid Solon, these reforms served as the foundation-stone for the system that would later evolve into the Athenian democracy, although the institutions Solon himself created were not democratical in nature.

## Social and Economic Context
Immediately preceding Solon's legislation, Athens was characterized by internal dissension and the misery of its poorer population. The state suffered from the rapacity and injustice of the rich, and a general mutiny had become necessary to force large-scale reform. A primary source of conflict was the law of debtor and creditor, which led to the slavery of debtors. 

Upon being made archon and invested with full legislative powers, Solon refused to establish himself as a despot. Instead, he introduced the *seisachtheia*, a relief-law for poorer debtors. This measure, combined with a debasing of the money standard, aimed to resolve the crisis of debt. While there was initial partial dissatisfaction, the measure eventually gained general popularity.

## The Seisachtheia and Contract Law
The *seisachtheia* settled the long-standing disputes regarding debtor and creditor laws, and no subsequent complaints regarding private debts were recorded. Solon rescinded all existing mortgages on land; however, the law continued to allow creditors to seize the property of a debtor while removing the power to seize the debtor's person. 

This shift eliminated "noxious contracts" that had previously threatened the liberty of poor freemen and their children. In their place, a system of money-lending emerged based on prospective earnings and property, which proved useful to both parties. This established a lasting Athenian sentiment of high respect for the sanctity of contracts. Under the subsequent democracy, the *hêliæa* (the popular judicial body of hêliastic jurors) took a solemn annual oath to repudiate any proposals for the redivision of lands or the abrogation of debts.

## Political Constitution and Timocracy
Solon was empowered to modify the political constitution, resulting in the creation of a timocracy—a system where political rights and franchises were measured according to wealth. He established a census consisting of four scales of property:

*   **The Three Richest Classes:** These classes were subject to a graduated liability for income tax.
*   **The Fourth (Poorest) Class:** Members of this class exercised their power only within the assembly, where they chose magistrates and held them accountable.

Solon also established a pro-bouleutic Senate of Four Hundred to serve as a pre-considering body and enlarged the powers of the Senate of Areopagus.

## Documentation and Preservation
The Solonian laws were recorded in a style of writing called *boustrophêdon*, where lines alternated direction from left to right and right to left. These were inscribed on:
*   **Kyrbeis:** Triangular tablets used primarily for laws regarding sacrifices and sacred rites.
*   **Rollers (or pillars):** At least sixteen wooden rollers used for regulations concerning profane matters.

These records were kept first in the acropolis and later in the prytaneium. While some fragments remain—such as references to the sixteenth *ἄξων* (roller) and the eighth law on the thirteenth *ἄξων*—much of the original legislation is lost.

## Legacy and Evolution
Although Solon's laws regarding inheritance, adoption, and penal offenses remained largely in force for centuries, and his quadripartite census persisted for financial purposes until 377 B.C., his political and judicial arrangements underwent a complete revolution. 

Later Athenian orators often used Solon's name loosely to refer to any legislator, and later generations frequently confused Solonian institutions with post-Solonian ones. By the time of Periklês and Ephialtês, a party of resistance attempted to maintain the "orbit marked out by Solon" against the advancing democracy. The poet Kratinus later satirized the decline of these original laws, claiming the wooden tablets of Solon and Drako were being used by people to roast barley. Despite these changes, the Solonian reforms are credited with laying the essential groundwork for the eventual development of the Athenian democratic state.
