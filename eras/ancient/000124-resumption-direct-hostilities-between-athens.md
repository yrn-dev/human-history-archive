# From the Resumption of Direct Hostilities Between Athens and Sparta

## The Rekindling of General War
The resumption of direct hostilities between Athens and Sparta transformed the Syracusan war from an isolated event into a component of a general war that rekindled throughout Greece. The winter of 414-413 B.C. was characterized by extensive and active military preparations, a period that Thucydidês identifies as the nineteenth spring of the Peloponnesian war, while other historians refer to it as the beginning of the Dekeleian war.

During this midwinter period, Eurymedon traveled to Syracuse with ten triremes. Simultaneously, Demosthenês worked throughout the winter to organize a second armament for the early spring. To prevent Corinthian reinforcements from sailing out of the Corinthian gulf, Athens sent twenty triremes around the Peloponnesus to the station of Naupaktus. In response, the Corinthians prepared twenty-five fresh triremes to act as a convoy for transports carrying hoplites.

## Mobilization and Strategic Activity
Military levies of hoplites took place in Lacedæmon, Bœotia, Sikyôn, and Corinth to support the armament for Syracuse. These efforts occurred alongside preparations for the occupation of Dekeleia. In Sicily, Gylippus worked actively to encourage the region to take a more decisive role in the struggle of the coming year.

Prior to this resumption of war, the Isthmian festival had taken place. Because the festival was considered half Athenian—having been established or revived by Theseus—and because the Athenian theôry held a privileged place (προεδρία) at the games, the habit of excluding Athens from the Corinthian herald’s proclamation had not yet been renewed at that time.

## Relations with the Peloponnesian Alliance
The Lacedæmonians maintained a position of strength that allowed them to punish allies who had incurred their displeasure, most notably the Eleians. The Eleians, led by Thrasydæus under a democratical government, had a history of conflict with Sparta dating back to 420 B.C., when they used their administration of the Olympic festival to exclude Sparta. 

Following the resumption of hostilities in 414 B.C., the Eleians refused to provide aid in the war against Athens. They further excluded King Agis from the Olympic temple when he sought to offer sacrifice and consult the oracle of Zeus Olympius; this was based on the temple's ancient canon prohibiting consultations regarding hostilities of Greek against Greek. In response, Sparta demanded that the Eleians pay unpaid arrears of their war quota and relinquish authority over their dependent townships (Periœki) in the region of Triphylia, south of the river Alpheus and north of the Neda, to make them autonomous. This included Lepreum, whose autonomy Lacedæmon had previously vindicated in 420 B.C.

## Diplomatic Maneuvers and Treaty Violations
The relationship between Athens and Sparta often existed in a state of contradiction, where they remained in name as allies and in peace regarding direct operations on each other's territory, while engaging in hostile maneuvering as allies of third parties.

An example of this occurred when the Lacedæmonians sent a detachment of three hundred hoplites under Agesippidas to reinforce the Epidaurians by sea in September, bypassing Athenian knowledge. The Argeians complained to Athens, arguing that the Athenians had violated a treaty between Athens, Argos, Elis, and Mantineia. This treaty stipulated that no party should allow the passage of troops through its territory without joint consent; the Argeians contended that the sea was part of Athenian territory. 

Under the persuasion of Alkibiadês, the Athenians compensated the Argeians by carrying the Helots and Messenians from Kephallenia back to Pylos to ravage Laconia. To mark this, the Athenians inscribed on the pillar recording their alliance with Sparta that the Lacedæmonians had not observed their oaths, though Athens did not formally break the treaty.
