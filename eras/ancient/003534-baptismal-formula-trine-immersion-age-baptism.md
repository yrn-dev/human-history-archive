# Baptismal Formula and the Age of Baptism

## The Baptismal Formula
The formulas used during baptism were not uniform from the beginning, and the trinitarian formula did not always coincide with trine immersion. The *Teaching of the Apostles* prescribes baptism in the name of the Father, Son, and Holy Ghost, yet also mentions baptism into the name of the Lord, which is described as the normal formula of the New Testament. In the apostolic age, common uses included "of Jesus Christ," "of the Lord Jesus Christ," "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ," or "into Christ Jesus." Paul interpreted these as being equivalent to baptism "into the death of Christ Jesus," symbolizing the believer being raised from death into everlasting life.

Throughout history, the validity of various formulas was debated. In the 3rd century, baptism in the name of Christ was widespread enough that Pope Stephen declared it valid, opposing Cyprian of Carthage. Similarly, Pope Nicholas (858-867) allowed baptism to be valid *tantum in nomine Christi* (only in the name of Christ), as seen in the Acts. Other figures, such as the African monk Ursinus, Hilary, and the synod of Nemours (A.D. 1284), also asserted the validity of baptism into the name of Christ alone. Conversely, Basil, in his work *On the Holy Spirit*, condemned "baptism into the Lord alone" as insufficient. Armenian fathers often specified that baptism "into the death of Christ" was the only essential element.

Current regional variations include the formula of Rome ("I baptize thee in the name of Father and Son and Holy Spirit") and the Eastern formula ("so-and-so, the servant of God, is baptized"), where the Greeks add *Amen* after each person and conclude with "Now and ever and to aeons of aeons, amen."

## Trine Immersion
Trine immersion refers to the practice of dipping the candidate thrice. Tertullian provides an early explanation, stating that candidates are dipped thrice for the several names and persons. Jerome added that this was done so the "one sacrament of the Trinity may be shown forth." Other figures, including Athanasius, Gregory of Nyssa, Leo of Rome, Cyril of Jerusalem, and Theophylactus, viewed trine immersion as symbolic of Christ's three days of entombment. This interpretation is enjoined in the Armenian baptismal rubric and an epistle from Macarius of Jerusalem (c. 330), where it is linked to the idea of baptism into the death of Christ.

Basil confessed ignorance regarding the origin of trine immersion, suggesting that the rival explanations were aetiological inventions to explain an existing custom adopted from paganism. In Greek and Latin antiquity, pagan lustrations were normally threefold; authors such as Virgil, Ovid, Horace, and Persius mention these trine lustrations. A scholiast on Horace notes that people plunged themselves thrice when expiating sins.

## Modes of Administration
While immersion was a rule during the period of large adult catechumenate baptisms—facilitated by the use of circular baptisteries with fonts—other modes existed. In some rites, candidates stepped into the font but were not immersed; instead, the priest poured water over their heads three times. Some modern Baptists, such as the Mennonites, continue to practice baptism by pouring or sprinkling. However, "modern" Baptists are distinguished by their use of immersion. Immersion is also still practiced exclusively by a large portion of paedobaptist Christendom, such as the Orthodox Eastern Church.

## The Age of Baptism
The age at which baptism was administered has varied by tradition and denomination. In the oldest Syrian, Armenian, and Greek rites, a service existed for sealing a child on its eighth day or giving a Christian name to a non-pagan child. In this rite, the priest blessed the infant and—except in Armenia—sealed the forehead with the sign of the cross, praying that the child enter the holy Catholic church at the "proper time" or "in due season." This practice was viewed as an analogue to the circumcision of Christ.

In contrast, the Baptist tradition holds the distinctive view that baptism should be administered to believers only. Both General Baptists (influenced by Mennonite Anabaptists) and Particular Baptists (springing from Independents) utterly rejected the baptism of infants.
