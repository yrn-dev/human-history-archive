# Art and Architecture of Babylonia and Assyria

The cultures of Babylonia and Assyria, which developed on the banks of the Euphrates and Tigris, were primarily literary in nature. While they lacked the specific artistic spirit found in Greece or Egypt, they developed distinct architectural and artistic traditions based on their geography and mutual influence.

## Babylonian Architecture and Art
Due to a lack of stone and an abundance of clay, Babylonia relied heavily on the use of brick. Babylonian temples are described as massive, shapeless structures of crude brick supported by buttresses. To manage rainfall, these structures utilized drains; one such drain discovered at Ur was constructed of lead.

The reliance on brick fostered several early developments in design and decoration:
*   **Structural Elements:** The use of brick led to the early development of the column and the pilaster.
*   **Wall Decoration:** Walls were brilliantly colored and decorated with frescoes and enamelled tiles. In some instances, walls were plated with gold or bronze. Additionally, painted terra-cotta cones were embedded into the plaster.
*   **Sculpture:** Unlike the bas-reliefs of the north, Babylonia utilized figures in the round. Early examples from Tello are characterized as realistic, though somewhat clumsy.
*   **Gem-Cutting:** Because stone was scarce and every pebble was precious, the Babylonians achieved a high level of perfection in gem-cutting. Notable examples include two seal-cylinders from the age of Sargon of Akkad.

## Assyrian Architecture and Art
Assyria began as an offshoot of Babylonia and was initially a "servile pupil" regarding its culture and arts. Early Assyrian palaces and temples were built of brick, even though stone was the natural building material of the region. They even maintained the use of brick platforms, which were necessary for the marshy soil of Babylonia but served little purpose in the north.

Over time, Assyrian architects moved away from Babylonian influence and began incorporating stone. A primary distinction in their decoration was the use of sculptured and coloured stone slabs to line the walls of palaces, rather than the painting used in Chaldaea.

The art of Assyrian bas-reliefs is categorized into three distinct periods:
1.  **Assur-nazir-pal III:** Characterized as simple but vigorous.
2.  **Sargon:** Characterized as realistic and careful.
3.  **Assur-bani-pal:** Characterized as refined, though lacking in boldness.

## Metallurgy and Craftsmanship
While few remarkable specimens of early metallurgic art have been found—with the possible exception of the silver vase of Entemena—later periods saw great excellence in gold jewelry, specifically in the creation of bracelets and ear-rings. Copper was also worked with skill.

## Cultural Interdependence
Historically and geographically, Assyria and Babylon were closely intertwined. The arts, sciences, literature, and religion of the Assyrians were derived from their southern neighbors in Babylonia. While the Assyrians shared the same language and race as the Babylonians, they eventually developed an individuality that manifested in their religion and art.

The decline of these states began in the 7th century B.C. Following the death of Assur-bani-pal, the Assyrian empire began to break up. Later constructions, such as a palace erected among the ruins of a city after the Scythians burned Calah, exhibit a small size and architectural poverty, reflecting the depleted resources of the empire. The Assyrian empire ended with the capture and destruction of Nineveh by a Scythian army aiding the Babylonians.
