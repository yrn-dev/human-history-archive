# Ptolemy, Cassius Dio, and the Antonine Itinerary

The study of early Britain and the broader classical world relies upon a specific set of authorities, including the works of the geographer Ptolemy, the historian Cassius Dio, and the records found in the Antonine Itinerary. These sources provide critical data regarding geography, imperial history, and the administration of the Roman Empire.

## Ptolemy and Classical Geography
Ptolemy is categorized among the foremost names in the field of geography, alongside other figures such as Strabo, Hipparchus of Nicaea, and Pausanias. His work is essential for understanding the scientific development of cartography during the classical period. 

Ptolemy's contributions are closely linked to the earlier work of Marinus of Tyre (c. A.D. 120), who sought to return to the scientific principles established by Eratosthenes and Hipparchus. Marinus was the first to project the positions of places using latitude and longitude, though his method was rudimentary, utilizing straight lines that intersected at right angles. This caused distortion as the distance from the central parallel of Rhodes increased. Furthermore, Marinus overestimated distances by assuming a degree equaled 500 stadia.

Ptolemy refined these calculations. For example, where Marinus estimated the distance from the first meridian (through the Fortunate islands) to Sera, the capital of China, as 225°, Ptolemy reduced this to 177°. Similarly, Ptolemy reduced the latitude of Agisymba from Marinus's 24° south of the equator to 18°. Ptolemy's work also serves as a primary source for the lost maps and descriptive accounts of Marinus.

## Cassius Dio as a Historical Source
Cassius Dio is recognized as a principal representative of history in the manifold prose works of his period, grouped with other historians such as Josephus, Appian, Arrian, Herodian, Eusebius, and Zosimus. His writings are frequently cited as authorities on various historical events and figures.

In the context of the Roman Empire, Dio Cassius provides accounts of the reign of Marcus Aurelius. This includes the "Miracle of the Thundering Legion," a victory over the Quadi in 174. While Christians attributed the victory to prayer, pagan writers, including Dio Cassius, ascribed the event to the magic arts of an Egyptian named Arnuphis, who influenced Mercury and other gods.

Dio also provides narratives regarding the revolt of Avidius Cassius, the commander of Roman troops in Asia, who proclaimed himself emperor in 175. Additionally, Dio Cassius and Capitolinus are noted for charging the emperor's wife, Faustina, with "shameless infidelity," though these claims are regarded by some as lacking trustworthy evidence.

## The Antonine Itinerary and Early Britain
The Antonine Itinerary, dated probably between A.D. 210 and 230, is one of the principal references used to study early Britain. It is listed alongside other key authorities such as the writings of Tacitus, Julius Caesar, Strabo, Diodorus, and the elder Pliny. 

The lists within the Antonine Itinerary provide essential data for the historical reconstruction of the region, complementing other administrative and geographical records such as the *Notitia Dignitatum* (c. A.D. 400) and the 7th-century Ravennas. These sources, along with Roman inscriptions collected by scholars like Hübner and Haverfield, form the basis for the archival understanding of Britain's classical period.
