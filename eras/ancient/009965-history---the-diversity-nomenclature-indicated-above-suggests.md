# History and Nomenclature

The study of history and the physical world is frequently complicated by diversity in nomenclature, chronological systems, and the interpretation of terminology. These variations appear across various disciplines, from the recording of ancient civilizations and the administration of land to the naming of geographical features and the classification of medical conditions.

## Chronological and Narrative Challenges
In the study of Babylonia and Assyria, readers without specialized knowledge often face perplexity due to the extreme divergence in chronological schemes employed by different writers. This diversity of opinion stems from the underlying principles of the chief suggested schemes of chronology. Researchers attempt to resolve these problems by examining later historical periods before pushing inquiries back into the earlier periods of Sumerian and Babylonian history.

In contrast to factual historical records, some ancient texts use the form of history for satire. Lucian's *True History*, a two-book narrative, was written as a diversion and a satire targeting logographers and poets—such as Homer and Ctesias—who wrote marvelous tales. Lucian wittily asserts that the only true statement in his work is that it contains nothing but lies from beginning to end.

## Land Administration and Social Titles in Japan
Historical records of Japan reveal a perplexing diversity of nomenclature regarding land ownership and administration. Holders of manors were referred to by various titles, including *honjo*, *shoya*, *ryoke*, or *ryoshu*. Similarly, intendants were termed *yoryudo*, *betto*, *kengyo*, *shoji*, or *shocho*, and were also sometimes called *nanushi-shoku*. 

The naming of land often reflected its origin; reclaimed lands were called *myoden* (name-land) after the person who reclaimed them. Those who owned large tracts of these lands were designated *daimyo* (great name), while smaller holders were called *shomyo*. This system contributed to a decline in lands paying taxes to the Central Government. By the reign of Emperor Toba (1108-1123), State domains administered by provincial governors accounted for only one per cent of the provincial area. Consequently, governors often remained in Kyoto and sent deputies to their posts, which reduced the authority of the Crown.

## Terminology in Japanese Mythology and Archaeology
A significant difficulty for interpreters of Japanese mythology is the term *Kami*. While foreign writers often translate *Kami no yo* as the "Age of Gods," the word *Kami* does not necessarily have a divine import. Historically, *Kami* could refer to the head of a household, as each of the hundreds of families in Japanese society had its own *Kami*. For example, the Government was commonly called *O Kami* (the Honourable Head), and feudatories were often titled *Kami* of a specific locality.

In Japanese archaeology, nomenclature regarding primitive implements is also disputed. The identity of the "hoe" is contested because there is no proof that agriculture was practiced by primitive people, nor have grain traces been found at neolithic sites. However, the modern Ainu, believed to be the ancient population, practice the worship of the first cakes made from the season's millet, which may indicate agricultural pursuits unless the rite was borrowed from the Yamato.

## Geographical and Territorial Nomenclature
Geographical nomenclature often reflects the political and physical history of a region. While coast-lines provide convenient boundaries for nomenclature, variations in elevation above sea level are equally important. The relative position of high and low lands modifies atmospheric phenomena, rain, and wind, making the study of mountain ranges as essential as the study of a continent's extent or its relation to the poles and equator.

The naming of the islands known as Ogasawara-jima illustrates how nomenclature suggests doubtful ownership. Japanese annals state they were discovered near the end of the 16th century and added to the fief of Daimyo Ogasawa Sadayori. They were also called *Bunin-jima* (corrupted to Bonin), meaning "without inhabitants." After communications ceased in 1635 due to a government veto on sea-going vessels, fitful contact returned in 1728, followed by an unsuccessful attempt to establish a colony at Port Lloyd in 1861.

During this period, Captain Beechey visited and named some of the islands. In 1830, a party of Americans, British subjects, and a Dane settled on Peel Island (Chichi-jima), hoisting the British flag. By 1853, Commodore Perry found thirty-one inhabitants on Peel Island, including English, American, and Portuguese citizens, as well as natives of the Ladrones and the Sandwich Islands. By 1875, the population grew to sixty-nine, though visitor Russell Robertson noted they lacked laws, religion, education, and government. Although the settlers spoke English and viewed themselves as a British colony, the British government renounced all claims in 1861, recognizing Japan's right of possession.

## Specialized Nomenclature in Other Fields
Nomenclature is also used in medicine to honor pioneers, even when the understanding of a condition evolves. "Bright's disease" is a generic term for a class of kidney diseases (acute and chronic nephritis) characterized by dropsy and the presence of albumen in the urine. First described in 1827 by Dr. Richard Bright, it was later discovered that these symptoms result from various morbid conditions rather than a single disease. The term is retained in medical nomenclature in honor of Dr. Bright.
