# Pompey

Pompey was a prominent Roman military and political figure whose career was marked by extensive commands in the East, a strategic coalition with other powerful Romans, and a eventual role as the champion of the senate during a period of civil strife.

## Military Campaigns in the East and Judea
Pompey was granted almost unlimited powers for a mission to the East through the Manilian and Gabinian laws of 66 and 67 B.C. During his operations in Judea, he pursued Aristobulus, who attempted to avoid a siege. While in the fruitful country of Judea—noted for its balsam trees and vast numbers of palm trees—Pompey pitched camp for one night before hastening toward Jerusalem. 

Upon Pompey's approach, Aristobulus sought to mitigate Pompey's anger through supplication, promising money and the delivery of both himself and the city. However, Aristobulus failed to fulfill these conditions, and his party refused to admit Gabinius, who had been sent to collect the promised funds. Inside Jerusalem, a sedition arose: the party of Hyrcanus favored opening the gates to Pompey, while Aristobulus's party wished to fight to liberate their king. The party of Hyrcanus, impressed by the order of the Roman soldiers, prevailed. Aristobulus's supporters retreated into the temple and destroyed the bridge connecting the temple to the city to maintain their opposition. Pompey subsequently sent one of his great officers, Piso, with an army to occupy the palace and distribute a garrison throughout the city, utilizing the counsel and assistance of Hyrcanus's party.

## The First Triumvirate and Political Alliances
Pompey was a central figure in a coalition with Julius Caesar and Crassus. This partnership was reinforced by familial ties, as Pompey married Caesar's daughter, Julia. The coalition's program was advanced through the use of Pompey's veterans, who were mustered by Caesar to drive the consul M. Bibulus from the forum. In exchange for this support, Pompey received the ratification of his acts in Asia and the assignment of Campanian state domains for his veterans.

Pompey also exerted influence over other figures in Rome. He conferred Roman citizenship upon Lucius Cornelius Balbus (called Major) and his family as a reward for Balbus's services against Sertorius in Spain. When Balbus was later prosecuted in 56 B.C. for illegally assuming the rights of a Roman citizen, Pompey, along with Cicero and Crassus, spoke on his behalf, leading to Balbus's acquittal.

## Decline of the Coalition and Civil War
The stability of the coalition began to erode following the death of Julia in 54 B.C. and the death of Crassus at Carrhae in 53 B.C. Although the three men had renewed their agreement at the conference of Luca in 56 B.C.—which extended Caesar's command in Gaul—Pompey eventually drifted away from Caesar and became the champion of the senate.

In 52 B.C., Pompey passed a law *de jure magistratuum*. This legislation allowed for a successor to be provided for the Gallic provinces before the end of 49 B.C., which threatened to make Caesar a private person and leave him liable for his unconstitutional acts. Despite attempts by the tribune C. Scribonius Curio to negotiate a settlement where both Pompey and Caesar would resign their *imperium*, negotiations failed. In January 49 B.C., after martial law was proclaimed, Caesar crossed the Rubicon. Following this, Pompey and the senate fled beyond the Ionian Sea.

## Later Influence and Legacy
Even after his flight from Rome, Pompey's influence persisted through his supporters. When Caesar released Aristobulus from his bonds and sent him to Syria with two legions, Aristobulus was killed by poison administered by those of Pompey's party. Additionally, Pompey commanded Scipio to slay Alexander, the son of Aristobulus, at Antioch; Scipio carried out the order by beheading him following accusations of offenses committed against the Romans.
