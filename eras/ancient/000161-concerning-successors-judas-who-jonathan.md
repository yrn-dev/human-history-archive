# The Successors of Judas: Jonathan and Simon

Following the death of Judas Maccabeus, the leadership of the Israelites passed to his brothers, Jonathan and Simon. This period was characterized by a transition from the purely military genius of Judas to the diplomatic strategies of Jonathan and the subsequent leadership of Simon.

## The Succession of Jonathan
Judas Maccabeus was slain in a heroic struggle against superior numbers under Bacchides at Elasa in 161. Following his death, Judea faced significant distress, including a famine and the rise of those who transgressed the laws of their forefathers. Bacchides gathered Jews who had apostatized from the traditional way of living and entrusted the care of the country to them. These individuals captured and delivered the friends and partisans of Judas to Bacchides, who tortured and killed them.

In response to this calamity, the remaining companions of Judas approached his brother Jonathan, urging him to imitate the care Judas had shown his countrymen and to serve as a governor. Jonathan, declaring his readiness to die for his people and stating he esteemed himself no inferior to his brother, was appointed general of the Jewish army.

## Leadership and Diplomacy of Jonathan
While Jonathan was not considered as brilliant a soldier as Judas, he was an astute diplomat who leveraged the internal conflicts of Syria for the benefit of his people. He maintained a friendship with the Romans and entered into a league with Antiochus the son.

During the struggle for the Syrian throne between Demetrius I and Alexander Balas, Jonathan supported Alexander. In 153, Alexander honored Jonathan in an extraordinary manner, appointing him High Priest and conferring upon him the title of "King's Friend." Later, after the accession of Demetrius II in 145, Jonathan won his favor and assisted him in crushing a rebellion in Antioch. In exchange, Jonathan sought the withdrawal of Syrian garrisons from Judea, though Demetrius failed to keep this promise.

Jonathan's military career included a notable engagement in the plain of Asor. After being ambushed by the men of Demetrius, the majority of Jonathan's army fled in haste. However, Jonathan and approximately fifty men—including commanders Mattathias the son of Absalom and Judas the son of Chapseus—fought boldly and put the enemy to flight, pursuing them as far as Cadesh.

## The Fall of Jonathan and the Rise of Simon
Jonathan eventually transferred his allegiance to Antiochus VI, who had been crowned by the tyrant Trypho. Jonathan routed the generals of Demetrius on the plain of Hazor and subjugated the territory between Jerusalem and Damascus. This success aroused the jealousy of Trypho, the guardian of Antiochus the son.

Trypho plotted against Jonathan and his friends. He captured Jonathan through a wile while Jonathan was traveling to Ptolemais with a small company. Jonathan was put in bonds, and Trypho launched an expedition against the Israelites. When Trypho was subsequently driven away by Simon, Jonathan's brother, the enraged tyrant put Jonathan to death.

## The Leadership of Simon
Upon the death of Jonathan, the Israelites appointed Simon as their general and High Priest. Simon performed several courageous actions, particularly against Trypho. He formed a confederation with Antiochus Pius and waged war against Trypho and Cendebeus, the general of Antiochus's army. 

The leadership of Simon ended in treachery; he was murdered by his son-in-law, Ptolemy.

## Family Background
The leadership of these brothers originated with their father, Mattathias, the son of Asamoneus. Mattathias was a priest of the order of Joarib and a citizen of Jerusalem. He had five sons: John (called Gaddis), Simon (called Matthes), Judas (called Maccabeus), Eleazar (called Auran), and Jonathan (called Apphus). Mattathias had encouraged his children to die for the laws of their country rather than live ingloriously under the prohibitions of the king.
