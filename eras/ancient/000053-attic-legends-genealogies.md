# ATTIC LEGENDS AND GENEALOGIES

The legends and genealogies of Attica are characterized by a complex structure, often originating from diverse roots. Historically, each dême maintained its own specific traditions, a fact that was largely overlooked by the old epic poets. These mythical pedigrees generally served to unite specific clans or gentes rather than the broader community.

## The Æakid Genealogy
The Æakids were a family renowned for their military excellence, with a genealogy that connected various eminent gentes across Salamis, Ægina, Cyprus, Achaia Phthiôtis, and among the Epirotic Molossians. The lineage began with Æakus, the son of Zeus and Ægina. Æakus was known for prayers that procured relief for Greece, and he was honored with prayer and sacrifice at the Æakeion in Ægina, a site that maintained its dignity into the time of Pausanias.

The offspring of Æakus included Phôkus, Telamôn, and Pêleus. Phôkus was killed by his brothers, Pêleus and Telamôn. Following this event, Telamôn was banished and traveled to Salamis. Pêleus went to Phthia, where he married Thetis. Other notable figures in this lineage include Neoptolemus and Ajax, whose son Philæus became the eponymous hero of a dême in Attica. Additionally, Teukros, who was banished, settled in Cyprus; in Attic archaeology, the legend of Teukros is linked to the specific formalities and functions of the judicature.

## Autochthonous and Royal Lineages
Erechtheus is described as autochthonous. According to legend, he gained victory over Eumolpus, though Poseidôn eventually ended the life and reign of Erechtheus, who was slain in battle. He was succeeded by his son, Kekrops II, and subsequently by his grandson, Pandiôn II. These two figures are viewed by genealogizers as duplications of the earlier Kekrops and Pandiôn, inserted to fill a perceived chronological chasm.

The daughters of Erechtheus include Prokris, Kreüsa, and Oreithyia. Oreithyia became the wife of Boreas, and the Athenians offered prayers to Boreas for his gracious help during times of danger. The legends also record the voluntary self-sacrifice of Erechtheus's three daughters, as well as the relationship between Kreüsa and Iôn.

Pandiôn's children include the daughters Proknê and Philomêla—associated with the legend of Têreus—and sons including Ægeus and Thêseus. The legendary character of Thêseus underwent refinement over time, a process noted by Plutarch in his handling of legendary matters.

## The Amazons and Other Traditions
The legend of the Amazons is characterized by its antiquity and prevalence, being universally accepted as a part of the Greek past. The Amazons are credited with glorious achievements and were described as ubiquitous. While historical critics faced a conflict between faith and reason regarding these accounts, the historians of Alexander presented the Amazons as a present reality.

In the realm of Attic drama, the great tragedians frequently drew subjects from the legendary world. Euripidês, for example, composed dramas concerning the adventures of Têmenus and his daughter Hyrnethô, the valor of Archelaus (son of Têmenus) in Macedonia, and the family misfortunes of Meropê and Kresphontês. The Dorian Herakleids, such as these figures, occupy the lowest position in the descending genealogical series, which is seen as a sign of the transition toward genuine history.
