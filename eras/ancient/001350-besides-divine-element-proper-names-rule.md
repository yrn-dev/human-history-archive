# Proper Names in the Babylonian-Assyrian Periods

In the Babylonian-Assyrian periods, the formation of proper names followed specific structural rules, typically involving a combination of divine, verbal, and objective elements. While these names could be complex, they also underwent processes of reduction and simplification over time.

## Structure of Full Names
As a general rule, proper names during these periods consisted of three elements. Beyond the initial divine element, the name typically included a verbal form attached to a third element representing an object. 

The interpretation of these names often presents challenges for scholars due to the nature of the writing. Even when a sign indicative of a verb is recognized, the specific intended form of the verb may remain undetermined. For example, the sign KUR, which corresponds to *na[s.][=a]ru* ("protect"), could be read as an imperative (*u[s.][s.]ur*), an active participle (*n[=a]sir*), or a third person perfect (*i[s.][s.]ur*). Similarly, the sign MU can signify "name" or the Sumerian word for "give." This creates ambiguity in names where a deity's name is followed by MU-MU, as it is unclear if the first MU is a form of the verb *nad[=a]nu* ("give") and the second is the noun *shumu* ("name"), or vice versa.

## Reduction and Simplification
While full names containing three or more elements existed, an early trend toward reduction emerged. This occurred in two primary ways:
1. Combining the name of a deity with only a verbal form.
2. Omitting the name of the deity entirely.

Further simplification led to names consisting of a single element. These single-element names are frequently characterized by the addition of "hypocoristic affixes," which function similarly to modern pet-names. Examples of these endings include *-tum* (feminine), *[=a]n*, *[=a]*, *um*, *atum*, *atija*, and *sha*.

## Methodology of Interpretation
The majority of Babylonian-Assyrian proper names are derived from business documents where individuals appear as witnesses, scribes, or interested parties. These documents are particularly valuable because they often provide variant readings of the same name—writing it phonetically in one instance and ideographically in another.

Names from the Khammurabi period, where phonetic writing was fashionable, have helped resolve doubts regarding ideographic readings. For instance, the names *Sin-na-di-in-shu-mi* ("Sin is the giver of a name") and *Bel-na-di-in-shu-mi* ("Bel is the giver of a name") serve as models for names beginning with a deity followed by MU-MU.

Historical texts also provide variant readings that allow for the decoding of complex names. The name of King Nebuchadrezzar appears in several forms, including *Na-bi-um-ku-du-ur-ri-u-[s.]u-ur* and PA-GAR-DU-SHES. Through these variations, it is determined that PA or AK (with the deity determinative AN) equals *Na-bi-um* (Nebo), GAR-DU or DU equals *kudurri* ("boundary" or "territory"), and SHES equals *u[s.][s.]ur* (the imperative of *nasaru*, "protect"). Consequently, the name signifies, "O, Nebo! protect my boundary" (or "my territory").

## Challenges with Divine Names
Determining the exact phonetic reading of divine names was historically difficult, often relying on conjectural readings until scholars discovered parallel passages or explanatory lists created by Babylonian and Assyrian scribes. 

Some phonetic readings remain unknown or uncertain, such as that of the deity Ninib. These uncertainties usually involve minor deities or old local gods who were assimilated into more powerful gods. In many instances, these names may actually be descriptive epithets rather than genuine proper names. 

A specific difficulty exists regarding the god of storms, written as IM. In Babylonia, he was generally known as Ramman ("the thunderer"), while in Assyria he was designated as Adad. Because the god had multiple designations, the correct reading of the sign IM is often a point of doubt.
