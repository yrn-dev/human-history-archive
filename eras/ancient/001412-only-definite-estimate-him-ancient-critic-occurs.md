# Bacchylides

Bacchylides was an ancient lyric poet whose work remained popular for at least the first four centuries of the current era. His standing in antiquity is evidenced by his inclusion in the "canon" of the nine lyric poets compiled by Alexandrian scholars, where he was listed alongside Alcaeus, Sappho, Anacreon, Simonides, Pindar, Stesichorus, Ibycus, and Alcman.

## Ancient Reception and Influence
The poet's works were studied and referenced by a wide array of ancient writers and scholars. The Alexandrian grammarian Didymus, writing around 30 B.C., produced a commentary on the epinikian odes of Bacchylides. Additionally, the poet Horace is noted by Porphyrion to have been a student of Bacchylides' works; Horace imitated him specifically in *Odes* i. 15, a section in which Nereus predicts the destruction of Troy.

References to or quotations from Bacchylides appear in the writings of several figures, including:
*   Plutarch
*   Strabo
*   Dionysius of Halicarnassus
*   Aulus Gellius
*   Athenaeus
*   Stobaeus
*   Zenobius
*   Hephaestion
*   Clement of Alexandria
*   Various scholiasts and grammarians

Furthermore, Ammianus Marcellinus records that the emperor Julian enjoyed reading the poet's work. While Bacchylides is not mentioned in the extant works of any Attic writer, this absence is not considered a fair basis for an adverse inference regarding his reputation.

## Critical Estimate
The only definite estimate of Bacchylides provided by an ancient critic is found in the treatise *Peri Hupsous* (commonly translated as "On the Sublime," though it refers to "On the Sources of Elevation in Style"). The authorship of this work is ambiguous; while it is ascribed to Cassius Longinus (circa A.D. 260), it is more likely the work of a writer from the first century of the era.

In chapter xxxiii of the treatise, the author discusses whether "greatness" in literature should be preferred over flawless merit that exists on a lower level, concluding that greatness is preferable. To illustrate this point, the author compares poets and playwrights:
*   In tragedy, the author asks who would prefer Ion of Chios over Sophocles.
*   In lyric poetry, the author asks who would prefer Bacchylides over Pindar.

The critic describes Bacchylides and Ion as being "faultless" and possessing a style characterized by "perfect elegance and finish." However, the essayist ultimately regards Bacchylides as a poet of the second class. In this view, while Bacchylides never commits glaring faults, he also never reaches "loftier heights."
