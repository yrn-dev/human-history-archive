# Roman Forum and Associated Architecture

The Roman Forum and the architectural styles of the Roman period are documented through various archaeological studies and historical analyses, including the work of Huelsen in *The Roman Forum* (1906). These sources provide insight into the structural variety of public buildings, specifically the basilicas, and the design of Roman portals.

## The Roman Basilica
Basilicas in the Roman world exhibited considerable variety in their architectural plans. A standard scheme involved a central space surrounded by galleries, which were supported by either piers or columns. This layout is evident in the basilicas of Pompeii, Julia, and Ulpia. The Basilica Aemilia, located to the north of the Roman forum, also conforms to this general design.

Other variations of the basilica existed across the empire:
* **Colonnaded Structures:** In some North African examples, as well as at Silchester and in the palace basilica of Domitian, colonnades were placed along the long sides of the building but were absent from the ends.
* **Open Interiors:** Examples found at Timgad and Trier [?] lacked interior divisions entirely.
* **Vaulted Structures:** The Basilica of Maxentius was entirely a vaulted structure, sharing a form similar to the great halls of the Roman Thermae.

## Tribunals and Legal Use
The presence and nature of tribunals—raised platforms—varied by location. Fixed tribunals have been identified at Silchester, Timgad, and Pompeii. In the Basilica of Maxentius, vaulted apses are present, which may have contained tribunals. 

In contrast, the Basilica Julia had no tribunal, despite the fact that it was regularly used for the centumviral court. Similarly, the Basilica Ulpia did not feature a vaulted apse; while the Capitoline-plan shows a semicircular projection at the end, this feature was distinct from the basilica itself.

## Roman Doors and Metalwork
Roman doors were categorized into three primary types: single doors (*unifores* or *monothyrai*), double doors (*geminae* or *bifores*), and folding doors (*valvae* or *ptyches*). Folding doors featured leaves that were hinged to fold back over one another. Evidence of these hinges has been discovered at Pompeii. Additionally, a painting in the portico of Eumachia at Pompeii depicts a door with three leaves, where the two outer leaves were likely hung and the inner leaf folded.

High-quality Roman metalwork is preserved in several bronze doors:
* **SS. Cosmas and Damiano:** These doors in Rome consist of two leaves, each containing two panels, framed in bronze.
* **The Pantheon:** These doors follow a similar design to those of SS. Cosmas and Damiano but include additional narrow horizontal panels at the middle, top, and bottom.
* **Lateran Basilica:** This site contains two other bronze doors from the Roman period.
