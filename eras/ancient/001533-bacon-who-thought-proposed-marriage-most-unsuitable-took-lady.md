# Bacon and the Marriage of Sir John Villiers

## Intervention in the Villiers Marriage
During a period of political tension, Coke sought to reconcile himself with Buckingham. The proposed method for this reconciliation was the marriage of Coke's daughter, who possessed an ample fortune, to Sir John Villiers. Sir John was the brother of the marquess and was described as being nearly penniless or penniless. 

This proposed match was viewed as distasteful by both Lady Hatton and her daughter, leading to a violent quarrel. Bacon intervened in the matter, taking the side of Lady Hatton because he considered the proposed marriage to be most unsuitable. Bacon explained his reasons for this disapproval to both Buckingham and the king.

## Consequences and Resolution
Bacon's opposition to the marriage resulted in an unexpected reaction from the king and Buckingham, whose indignation was strongly roused. Both sent Bacon bitter letters of reproof. During this time, rumors circulated that Bacon would be disgraced. It was further reported that Buckingham compared Bacon's current conduct to his previous unfaithfulness to Essex.

Despite these reactions, Bacon appears to have acted out of a simple desire to serve the best interests of Buckingham. Upon realizing the severity of the situation, Bacon changed his course. He used every means in his power to advance the marriage match and offered a humble apology to appease the indignation he had excited. Following this "sharp lesson," Bacon's affairs prospered and things proceeded smoothly.

## Other Personal and Professional Affairs
Bacon's life was marked by various fluctuations in fortune and status. In 1597, he increased his reputation through the publication of his *Essays*, as well as the *Meditationes Sacrae* and *Colours of Good and Evil*. However, his private finances were in poor condition, and he was arrested for debt in 1598. During this period, a scheme to improve his position through marriage to the wealthy widow Lady Elizabeth Hatton failed.

Professionally, Bacon gradually gained favor with the queen, beginning around 1594. He served in crown affairs as one of the learned counsel, though he lacked a salary, warrant, or commission. His relationship with Essex also deteriorated during this time due to an estrangement caused by the earl's dislike of Bacon's advice.

## Political Career and the King's Service
Bacon later served under King James, consistently upholding the royal prerogative during the fourth parliament of 1610. Following the death of Salisbury, Bacon hoped to utilize his sagacity and political skill as secretary, but the king undertook those duties himself. Bacon also made two unsuccessful attempts to obtain the mastership of the wards—first upon the death of Salisbury and later upon the death of Sir George Carey.

In 1613, Bacon proposed that Coke be transferred from the court of common pleas to the chief justiceship of the king's bench to fill a vacancy left by Sir Thomas Fleming. Bacon's primary motive was likely to reduce the risk of Coke colliding with the king on questions of prerogative. Following Coke's promotion, Bacon became the attorney-general.

In 1614, Bacon sat for Cambridge University in a parliament that was dissolved in June after a stormy session. During this session, the House objected to Bacon serving as a member while holding the office of attorney-general. While an exception was made for him, the House resolved that no future attorney-general would be eligible for a seat in parliament.

## Intellectual Contributions
Beyond his political and legal career, Bacon sought to reorganize the sciences. He aimed to expose a new method that would allow the human mind to proceed with certainty and security toward the true end of all human thought and action.
