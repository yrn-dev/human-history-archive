# Baptism

Baptism, derived from the Greek words *baptismos* and *baptisma* meaning "ceremonial washing," is a rite of purification and initiation. While the verb *baptizo* refers to this ritual, the shorter form *bapto* simply means to "dip" without ritual significance.

## Origins and Early Influences
The practice of ritual washing to remove sin existed in religions prior to Christianity. Tertullian noted that the mysteries of Apollo and Eleusis, as well as the votaries of Mithras and Isis, utilized a font (*per lavacrum*) for initiation. Additionally, ancient traditions included seeking specific waters to purge the guilt of homicide.

Within the context of early Jewish practice, the "proselyte's bath" served as a purification for those joining the faith. For publicans and soldiers seeking out John in the wilderness, John's baptism functioned as a purification from the past, similar to the proselyte's bath, but further advanced them by raising them as children unto Abraham and assuring them of a place in the kingdom of God without requiring circumcision. For Jews already practicing the faith, John's baptism aligned with a practice used by those wishing to consecrate their lives through stricter study and practice of the law, leading Epictetus to refer to the "baptized Jew."

The concept of spiritual rebirth was also present in the teachings of the Rabbi Baraciba, who taught that the inner man rising at the fourteenth year after puberty (age 29) is superior to the man born from the mother's womb. Furthermore, the cure of Naaman in the Jordan (2 Kings v. 10) was viewed as a prefiguration of Christian baptism.

## Development of the Christian Rite
In the apostolic age, the baptism performed by John—which was in the name of the coming Messiah—was identified with Jesus of Nazareth. Common formulas included "into Christ Jesus," "in the name of the Lord Jesus Christ," or "of Jesus Christ." Paul explained these as being equivalent to being baptized "into the death of Christ Jesus," suggesting the faithful were raised from death into everlasting life. This likeness to Christ's death and resurrection was intended to ensure a real union between the believer and Christ.

By the late 2nd century, the rite was often called "Illumination" (*photismos*). Justin described the process as pronouncing the name of God the Father, the Lord of the universe, over the repentant person. The person was then washed in the name of Jesus Christ (crucified under Pontius Pilate) and in the name of the Holy Ghost. Justin argued that while Jewish rites of ablution cleansed only the flesh, Christian baptism purified the soul from hatred, envy, and wrath. He characterized this as a spiritual circumcision, similar to that of Enoch.

## Legal and Cultural Parallels
The developed ceremony of baptism, specifically its threefold renunciation, bears a resemblance to the Roman legal ceremony of *emancipatio*. In Roman law, a father could extinguish his *patria potestas* (power of life and death over his son) by selling the child three times into *mancipium*. After each sale, the son was enfranchized via *manumissio vindicta*, where a rod (*vindicta*) was laid on the slave to claim him as free. 

This legal process may have suggested the triple *abrenunciatio* of Satan, freeing the believer from one who held power of death over them by birth. Just as Roman manumission dissolved agnatic relationships, the baptized person gave up father and mother to join a spiritual society of brethren. Additionally, the concept of being adopted as a son and heir of God is believed to have been taken by Paul from Roman law.

Other parallels exist in non-Western cultures; for example, a Malay rite of purification involves turning to the west three times with a renunciation of the Evil One, then turning to the east.

## Theology of Grace and Repentance
Baptism was viewed as justifying the believer and constituting them as a saint whose past sins were abolished. However, sin committed after baptism excluded the individual from divine grace and the sacraments. To be restored, a rite called *exhomologesis* was devised, involving a period of strict repentance and austerities. This acted as a repetition of baptism, though the water of the font was omitted. 

Because this restoration could only be performed once, a second lapse resulted in perpetual exclusion from the sacraments. This fear led some individuals, including Constantine, to remain catechumens for their entire lives and receive baptism only on their deathbeds.

## Later Historical Developments
In the East, the commemoration of the Baptism of Christ was associated with the feast of Epiphany, also called the Theophany or the Day of Lights. In the Teutonic west, this became the Festival of the three kings (the Magi).

In later Russian history, the baptism of Olga marked a significant era of Russian Christianity. After sailing to Constantinople, Olga was received in a detailed ceremony and received the name of Empress Helena during the sacrament of baptism.

In England, two distinct groups of Baptists emerged: the General Baptists (Arminian, influenced by Mennonite Anabaptists) and the Particular Baptists (Calvinist, springing from the Independents). Both groups rejected infant baptism. While they were not initially committed to immersion, they eventually reached agreements on the proper administrator, subjects, and mode of baptism, leading to the 1644 Confession of Faith by the Particular Baptist churches of London.
