# Foundations of Buildings of Ordinary Weight

In the construction of buildings of ordinary weight, the foundation is primarily provided for through the application of "footings" to the walls. These footings serve a specific technical purpose: they allow a wall to obtain a bearing upon a breadth of ground that is significantly greater than the width or thickness of the wall above the footing. This expansion compensates for the difference between the ground's power to resist pressure and the wall's own power of resisting pressure.

## Influence of Soil Composition
The necessity and design of footings are determined by the nature of the ultimate foundation upon which the wall rests:

*   **Hard Rock:** If a building is erected on rock as hard as the main constituents of the walls, expanded footings are theoretically unnecessary.
*   **Standard Soils:** When building upon sand, clay, or strong or weak gravel and chalk, the footing must be expanded based on the resistance power of the structure used as the foundation.
*   **Loose Soil:** In cases of made ground or other imperfectly resisting, loose, or badly combined soil, a solid platform is required under the constructed footing. This platform must be wide enough to prevent sinking and must bear evenly over the ground. A layer of concrete is considered the most durable, familiar, and effectual material for this purpose.

## Use of Concrete in Foundations
Concrete is employed in foundations for several distinct reasons:

**Pressure and Stability:** When concrete is used to prevent soil from yielding to pressure, it must be spread widely. Its depth or thickness as a layer is determined by its own ability to transmit the weight of the wall to the ground without being crushed or breaking across.

**Meteorological Influences:** Certain soils, such as clays, are liable to shrink when dried and swell when wetted. To protect a building from derangement caused by these meteorological influences, concrete foundations are commonly interposed. Alternatively, concrete walls—which are less expensive than stone or brick—may be brought up from a level sufficiently below the ordinary ground surface.

**Structural Substitution:** When concrete serves as a substitute for a wall to carry it to a low level, it functions as a wall itself. In this capacity, its width is proportional to its comparative weakness due to the absence of manipulated bond in its construction, and it is encased by the surrounding soil. According to the London Building Act, if a concrete wall replaces brick, it requires an additional thickness of one-third. There are currently no regulations regarding the thickness of reinforced concrete.

## Specialized Foundation Engineering
In civil engineering, foundations in water-bearing strata, such as under rivers or in the sea, utilize caissons. These are enclosures made of iron or wood used to keep out water.

*   **Open-top Caissons:** These may have a water-tight bottom or be carried down into a water-tight stratum using a cutting edge and weights, often aided by internal excavation. An example is found in the quays of the Seine at Rouen, where open-timber caissons were sunk onto bearing-piles to a depth of 9¾ ft. below low-water.
*   **Bottomless Caissons:** These act as diving-bells where compressed air keeps out water. Men excavate the bottom to reach a firm foundation while building a pier or quay-wall on the roof as the caisson descends.
*   **Large Foundation Blocks:** In harbours such as Zeebrugge, Scheveningen, and Bilbao, large metal caissons were built inland, floated into position, and then sunk and filled with concrete to form foundation blocks for breakwaters.
*   **River Piers:** Open iron caissons are used for bridge piers to reach water-tight strata, allowing water to be pumped out so foundations can be laid in the open air, as seen with the piers of the Tower Bridge in London.

## Rituals and Historical Practices
The laying of foundation stones has been accompanied by various customs. Modern practices often involve placing newspapers or coins in a cavity beneath the stone as memorials. However, ancient customs were more severe; it was once believed that immuring live animals or humans in the foundation ensured "immovable stability." Evidence of this includes human skeletons found under two round towers in Ireland and a 15th-century instance where the wall of Holsworthy church was built over a living human being.
