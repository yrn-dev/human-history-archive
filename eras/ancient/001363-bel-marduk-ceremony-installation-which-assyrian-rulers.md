# Bel-Marduk and the Ceremony of Installation

In the religious and political landscape of ancient Mesopotamia, the deity Marduk served as the central figure of the Babylonian pantheon. As Babylon rose to political prominence as the center of a great empire, Marduk absorbed the attributes, titles, and prerogatives of other deities, most notably those of En-lil (the Bel of Nippur). Through this process, Marduk became known as "Bel," meaning "the lord." This elevation included the appropriation of ancient myths; for instance, the victory over the monster Tiamat, symbolizing primeval chaos, was ascribed to Marduk, establishing him as the creator of mankind and the world.

## The Role of Bel-Marduk in Assyrian Politics

While the Assyrian religion centered on its own head of the pantheon, Assur—originally the patron god of the city of Assur—the Assyrian rulers maintained a profound respect for the traditions of the south. Even when the kings of Assyria exercised absolute control over Babylonia, they typically conceded to the region the form of an independent district. 

To legitimize their authority in the Euphrates valley, Assyrian rulers recognized a specific religious requirement. The ceremony of "taking the hand of Bel-Marduk" was considered an essential preliminary to exercising power in Babylonia. This act served as a formal method of assuming the throne and signified that the monarch had been adopted by the priesthood of Bel as the god's son and representative. No monarch throughout the history of the Babylonian and Assyrian empires felt their tenure of power in Babylon was secure until this ceremony had been performed.

## Conflict Between Monarchy and Priesthood

The necessity of "taking the hand of Bel" highlights the tension between the growing power of the monarchy and the wealthy priesthoods. While kings relied on their armies—often composed of foreigners who were susceptible to bribery or mutiny—the priesthoods held significant spiritual and political leverage.

A notable breach of this tradition occurred under Sennacherib, the son of Sargon II. Following a violent quarrel with the priesthood of Babylon, Sennacherib refused to "take the hand of Bel." In 691 B.C., he struck at the priesthood's power by destroying the holy section of Babylon and removing the statue of Bel-Marduk to Assyria. This defiance ended in tragedy when Sennacherib was assassinated by one of his sons. His successor, Esarhaddon, found it politically expedient to make peace with the god, rebuilding the temple and restoring Bel-Marduk.

## Later Influence and the Fall of Babylon

The reconciliation between the Assyrian crown and the Babylonian priesthood continued under Esarhaddon's son, Assurbanipal (Sardanapalus). Assurbanipal received a Babylonian education rather than a military Assyrian one, reflecting the deep "Babylon-ization" of certain Assyrian rulers.

In the later Neo-Babylonian Empire, the influence of the priesthood of Bel-Marduk remained dominant. When the monarch Nabonidus attempted to centralize the empire by bringing various local gods to Babylon, he roused the jealousy of the priesthood of Bel-Marduk. Seeking an alternative ruler, the priests looked to Cyrus the Persian. In 538 B.C., Cyrus entered Babylon without a fight after a battle outside the walls. According to biblical accounts, the crown prince Belshazzar was killed the night the city fell. Despite the change in imperial rule, the services of Bel-Marduk continued without intermission under the Persians.

## Theological Context and the Pantheon

The Babylonian religion exhibited a strong tendency toward concentrating the attributes of all gods into Marduk. By the time of the Neo-Babylonian Empire, it appeared that other gods—such as Adad, Shamash, Nergal, and Ninib—were merely different forms of Marduk. This movement toward a monotheistic conception of divine government was mirrored in Assyria, though there the role of the head of the pantheon was transferred to Assur.

Marduk's consort was known as Sarpanit, who was designated as "Belit" (the lady) because Marduk had become the Bel. This title of Belit was also associated with Nin-lil, the consort of the older Bel of Nippur, and later with the mother-goddess Ishtar.
