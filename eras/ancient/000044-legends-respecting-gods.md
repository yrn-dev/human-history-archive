# Legends Respecting the Gods

The mythical world of the Greeks is structured as a descent from the gods, who are superior and anterior to humanity, moving down to heroes and finally to the human race. Alongside the gods exist various monstrous, ultra-human, and extra-human natures. While these beings—including the Gorgons, Harpies, Sirens, Cyclôpes, Centaurs, Sphinx, Chimæra, Scylla, Charybdis, Echidna, Pegasus, Chrysaor, and the Grææ—are not properly called gods, they share attributes of conscious agency, free will, and the capacity for pleasure and pain.

## The Rise of Zeus and the Divine Order
The early mythical cycle is characterized by the collision of terrific forces that are eventually brought under the orderly government of Zeus. Zeus supplanted less capable predecessors to achieve supremacy over both gods and men, though he remained subject to social restraints from other chief deities and the practice of consulting the divine agora.

The origins of the divine race involve Chaos, followed by Gæa and Uranos. After Uranos was disabled, Kronos and the Titans rose, but Kronos was eventually overreached. Following the birth and safety of Zeus and his brethren, Zeus led a victory over Kronos and the Titans, later overcoming Typhôeus.

Zeus is the foremost figure in the Grecian conception. He is depicted as a human type enlarged, with a vast array of functions. As a judicial and administrative authority, he is known by many surnames, including Horkios (guarantor of oaths), Xenios (enforcer of hospitality), Ktêsios (guardian of the family hoard), and Meilichios (granter of expiation). These roles generated a demand for legends to translate serious presentiments into communicable forms; for instance, terrific legends regarding Zeus Horkios served to discourage perjury. Furthermore, Zeus is ascribed more amours than any other god, as Grecian kings and chieftains sought to trace their lineage to him. These unions produced distinguished progeny, many of whom were persecuted by Hêrê.

## Variations in Theogony and Influence
Different sources provide varying accounts of the divine origins. The Hesiodic theogony is a primary authority, though it differs from the accounts found in Homer. Hesiodic myths are traceable to Delphi and Krête. In contrast, the Orphic theogony introduces Zeus and Phanês, as well as Zagreus.

Foreign religions also influenced Greek legends, particularly regarding Dionysos and Dêmêtêr. Ecstatic rites connected to Dionysos were introduced from Asia between 700 and 500 B.C., grafting Asiatic frenzy onto the existing joviality of the Grecian Dionysia. Melampus is noted as the earliest teacher of these rites, and the Orphic sect emerged as a variety of Dionysiac mystics. Thracian and Egyptian influences further encouraged mystic legends.

## Specific Deities and Their Legends
### Apollo and Artemis
The birth of Apollo and Artemis to Zeus and Lêtô is widely accepted, though variations exist. For example, Æschylus described Artemis as the daughter of Dêmêtêr, an innovation Herodotus attributes to the Egyptians, who claimed they were the children of Isis and Dionysos. 

Artemis is generally depicted as a huntress, though the Tauric and Ephesian versions of the goddess borrow attributes from the Lydian Great Mother and an indigenous Tauric Virgin. In Homer, Artemis and Apollo both use the far-striking bow. Legends often highlight the jealousy of the gods; for instance, Artemis sent the Kalydônian boar to Œneus because he failed to sacrifice to her while honoring other gods.

### Dêmêtêr and the Mysteries
Dêmêtêr is central to the Eleusinian mysteries. According to the Homeric Hymn to Dêmêtêr—which serves as a sacred record of divine service—Dêmêtêr ordered the building of the Temple of Eleusis for her residence and prescribed the ritual of the mysteries.

## The Nature of Mythical Narrative
The perception of these legends evolved over time. In ancient epics and popular legends, the proceedings of the gods were subjects of common talk and sympathy, inspiring both reverence and fear. However, later figures like Herodotus exhibited a more anxious religious feeling, often stopping his narrative or entreating forgiveness from gods and heroes to avoid giving offense by reciting sacred legends publicly.

The complexity of these myths is evidenced by the attempts of learned Greeks to reconcile discrepancies by suggesting the existence of multiple homonymous personages—such as six different Apollos, five Athênês, and three different Zeuses.

## Other Divine Lineages
The sons of the Titan Iapetus include Promêtheus, Epimêtheus, Menœtius, and Atlas. While Hesiod lists all four, Homer mentions only Atlas in the Odyssey, describing him as the one who knows the depths of the sea and holds the tall pillars that keep the heaven apart from the earth.
