# Our exiled fathers crossed the sea

The phrase "O God, beneath Thy guiding hand Our exiled fathers crossed the sea" is associated with Leonard Bacon, who served as the Pastor of the First Church in New Haven. While a formal biography of Bacon does not exist, biographical information is available in a commemorative volume published by his congregation in 1882, as well as a sketch in the 1901 work *Ten New England Leaders*.

## Historical Instances of Exile and Sea Crossings

The archive contains various accounts of individuals and groups facing exile or crossing seas under different circumstances. In the Roman era, Cæsar, after being chosen Dictator by the Senate, restored exiles and the children of those who had suffered during the time of Sulla, reinstating their civil rights. Following this, Cæsar embarked on an expedition during the winter solstice at the commencement of January. He crossed the Ionian gulf with five legions and six hundred picked horsemen to take Apollonia and Oricum.

In other instances of political displacement, Ptolemæus Auletes, the father of Cleopatra, lived as an exile at Ephesus. During this period of exile, his daughter Berenice reigned; however, following his restoration, Ptolemæus put her to death.

## Exile and Displacement in Asia

In Japanese history, the sovereign's chief confidant, Fujiwara no Suketomo, was exiled to Sado Island in 1325, where he was killed by instructions from Takatoki. His thirteen-year-old son, Kunimitsu, traveled from Kyoto to Sado to bid his father farewell. After an attempt to avenge his father by killing Homma Saburo, a member of the governor's family, Kunimitsu escaped the island by climbing a bamboo tree that bent over a deep moat, allowing him to return to Kyoto and eventually attain the post of chunagon.

Another account describes a youth who served as a "scape-goat" for a lord. The youth's father-in-law had previously received bounties from Michizané, but following Michizané's banishment, the father-in-law was forced to serve the enemy of his benefactor. The son, however, remained loyal to the grandsire's lord.

## Trade and Long-Term Exile

The archive records the travels of Nicolas and Maffeo Polo, who departed from Venice for Constantinople. In the year 1260, during the reign of Baldwin II, the two brothers decided to cross the "Greater Sea" for trade, traveling from Constantinople to Soldaia with a store of jewels. 

Maffeo and his nephew Mark later served Kúblái in various capacities, including Mark's three-year government of the city of Yang-chau and missions to the Indian Seas and other southern states of India. ## Maritime Fear and Military Movement

In the conflict involving Timoleon, Carthaginian fugitives reached Lilybæum but were gripped by a profound conviction that the gods were wrathful. This religious terror was so great that they were nearly unable to board ships to return to Carthage, fearing the gods would prevent them from reaching land if they were caught at sea. Simultaneously, Carthage feared that Timoleon would cross the sea to attack their own soil. To counter this, Giskon, the son of Hanno, was recalled from exile to organize a new army of foreign mercenaries.
