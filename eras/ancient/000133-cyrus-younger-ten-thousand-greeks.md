# Cyrus the Younger and the Ten Thousand Greeks

Cyrus the Younger was a Persian prince who sought the crown of the Persian Empire, leading a campaign against his brother, King Artaxerxes Mnemon. To achieve this goal, Cyrus leveraged his esteem for the Greeks and his connections with various Greek figures to assemble a formidable mercenary force known as the Ten Thousand.

## Assembly of the Greek Forces
Cyrus made secret preparations to attack his brother, utilizing a network of Greek officers and mercenaries. Among these was Klearchus, a Lacedæmonian of significant military experience and ability who had been banished from his country for extreme tyranny and abuse of authority while serving as Harmost at Byzantium. Cyrus provided Klearchus with ten thousand darics to levy an army of mercenaries, ostensibly for the defense of Greek cities in the Chersonese against Thracian tribes.

Other Greek figures were similarly empowered to collect soldiers. These included Proxenus, a Bœotian; Agias and Sophænetus, Arcadians; and Sokrates, an Achæan. Additionally, Aristippus and Menon—Thessalians of the Aleuadæ family of Larissa—received funds to maintain two thousand mercenaries for their own political purposes in Thessaly, remaining subject to Cyrus's call. Cyrus's stated objectives for these forces included the siege of Miletus and an expedition against the Pisidians, predatory mountaineers in south-east Asia Minor.

## The March to Babylonia
Cyrus collected his army at Sardis and began a march that took him through Kolossæ, Kelænæ, Peltæ, Keramôn-Agora, and Käystru-Pedion. During the journey, Cyrus faced financial distress and was supplied by Epyaxa. The army proceeded through Thymbrium, Tyriæum, Ikonium, Lykaonia, and Tyana before crossing the Taurus into Kilikia. In Kilikia, Syennesis assisted Cyrus with money despite acting with duplicity.

At Tarsus, the Greek soldiers mutinied and refused to march further. Klearchus first attempted to suppress the mutiny with severity, but failing that, he used persuasion and discourse. Eventually, through deceitful maneuvers and an increase in pay, Klearchus brought the soldiers back to Cyrus's side. The army then marched to Issus, passing through the Gates of Kilikia and Syria. Despite the desertion of Pasion and Xenias, Cyrus continued toward Thapsakus on the Euphrates. After fording the river, the army endured privations while crossing the desert, passing through Pylæ and Charmandê. During this phase, a dangerous dispute broke out between the soldiers of Menon and those of Klearchus.

## The Battle of Kunaxa
Upon entering Babylonia, Cyrus prepared for battle despite the treason of Orontes. He addressed his officers and soldiers, having formed a conception of the superiority of the Greeks. Before the engagement, Cyrus gave three thousand darics (ten Attic talents) to the Ambrakiotic prophet Silanus, who had correctly predicted that no action would occur within a specific ten-day window.

The forces were mustered at Kunaxa. Cyrus's army consisted of 10,400 Greek hoplites, 2,500 Greek peltasts, and 100,000 barbarian or Asiatic troops with twenty scythed chariots. In contrast, reports described the army of Artaxerxes as numbering 1,200,000 men, including 6,000 horse-guards under Artagerses and 200 scythed chariots under Abrokomas, Tissaphernes, and two others (though later accounts suggested this number was overestimated by one-fourth).

During the battle, the Greeks achieved an easy victory and pursued the Persians. Cyrus, riding an unmanageable horse named Pasakas, was confronted by Artagerses, the leader of the Kadousians. Artagerses insulted Cyrus, calling him wicked and foolish for bringing "base Greeks" to slay his brother and lord. Artagerses hurled a javelin at Cyrus; while the blow was resisted by Cyrus's breastplate, the violence caused him to reel in his saddle. Cyrus responded by killing Artagerses with a javelin to the throat.

## Aftermath and the Retreat
Following the battle, the Greek troops' initial triumph turned to dismay and sorrow upon learning of the death of Cyrus. The soldiers felt repentance for the venture they had been seduced into by Cyrus and Klearchus. Klearchus, despite his own lack of foresight during the battle, attempted to maintain a victorious tone. He sent messengers, Proklês and Glus, to Ariæus via Menon, claiming the Greeks were conquerors and offering to place Ariæus on the throne intended for Cyrus if he joined them.

In the immediate aftermath, the Greeks suffered from a lack of bread and were forced to kill baggage animals for food, using wooden Egyptian shields, arrows, and baggage carts as fuel for their fires.
