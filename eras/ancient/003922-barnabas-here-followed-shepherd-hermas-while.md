# Barnabas

Barnabas is a figure appearing in the New Testament, specifically identified in Acts iv. 36 as Joseph, a Levite born in Cyprus. The name Barnabas was given to him by the apostles; the Greek rendering of this Semitic name may be translated as "son of consolation" or "son of exhortation." While philological analogy has suggested the form Bar-Nebo, this is considered inconsistent with his identity as a Jew and a Levite. The name is best understood as an Aramaic form equivalent to *paraklesis*, signifying comfort or encouragement.

## Life and Ministry
Barnabas is characterized in the New Testament by his goodness of heart, fineness of perception, and insight into essentials. He is described as "a good man" (Acts xi. 24) and functioned as a "prophet," a gift that likely manifested as practically helpful and encouraging support.

His early recorded actions include providing generous help (Acts iv. 36 f.) and acting as a mediator for Saul (Paul). Barnabas braved the suspicions surrounding the former persecutor—possibly due to a prior acquaintance in Hellenist circles in Jerusalem—and introduced him to the older apostles (Acts ix. 27). In the broader context of early Christian thought, Barnabas occupied a mediating role between Pauline Christianity and Jewish Christianity, a position typical of Christian Hellenists. Because of this, he is spoken of with respect in the Clementines, whereas Paul is discountenanced for being a radical regarding the Law.

## Relationship with Mark and Paul
Barnabas was the cousin of John, known by the surname Mark. Mark accompanied Barnabas and Paul on a relief visit to Judaea around A.D. 46, and later served as their assistant on a joint mission beyond Syria. During this mission, after arriving at Perga in Pamphylia, Mark withdrew and returned to Jerusalem.

This event later caused a disagreement between Paul and Barnabas. When Paul proposed revisiting the sites of their joint labors after the Jerusalem council (Acts xv.), he refused to take Mark again, doubting his reliability. Barnabas, however, supported his younger kinsman, taking Mark with him to sail away to Cyprus. Beyond these accounts, tradition suggests Barnabas may have served as an evangelist in Alexandria.

## The Epistle of Barnabas
The *Epistle of Barnabas* is an apocryphal book of the New Testament that once held quasi-canonical authority, particularly in Alexandria. It appears as an appendix to the New Testament in the 4th-century *Codex Sinaiticus* (followed by the *Shepherd of Hermas*) and in an 11th-century manuscript alongside the *Didache*. Early church figures such as Origen and Clement cited or commented on the text in a manner similar to how they treated canonical scriptures.

Clement ascribed the work to "the prophet" or "the apostle" Barnabas, specifically referencing Paul's fellow-apostle. However, internal evidence suggests this ascription is impossible, as the epistle itself makes no such claim. Scholars suggest the work may have been written by an unknown namesake or attributed to the apostle by the Alexandrian Church due to the writing's popularity.

## Canonical Status
The status of the *Epistle of Barnabas* shifted over time. By the early 4th century, the historian Eusebius categorized it among works to be decidedly rejected, alongside the *Didache*, the *Shepherd of Hermas*, and the *Acts of Paul*. This stood in contrast to the "acknowledged" works (such as the Gospels and Paul's Epistles) and the "questioned" works (such as those of James and Jude). 

The final determination of the New Testament canon was largely influenced by Athanasius, who established a list that excluded the *Epistle of Barnabas*. This Athanasian Canon eventually prevailed through the influence of the Church of Rome and the triumph of Nicene Christianity.
