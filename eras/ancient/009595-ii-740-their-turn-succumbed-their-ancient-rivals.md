# Regional Power Shifts and Dynastic Transitions

The history of various global regions is marked by the rise and fall of dynasties, the movement of peoples, and the struggle for territorial mastery. These transitions often occurred through military conquest, the establishment of tributary relationships, or the gradual decline of existing powers.

## Dynastic Struggles in India

In the Indian subcontinent, the period following the Hun tyranny—which ended around 565 with the Turkish overthrow of the White Huns on the Oxus—saw the emergence of several native and foreign dynasties. In Kathiawar, a chief named Bhatarka, likely of foreign origin, established himself at Valabhi around 500 on the ruins of the Gupta power. His dynasty persisted until 770, when it was destroyed by Arab invaders from Sind.

Other regional powers included the Mauryas of Puri in the northern Konkan and the Kadambas of Vanavasi on the southerly coast. In the southern Deccan, the Chalukyas and Rashtrakutas competed for dominance. The Gurjaras, believed to be ancestors of the Gujar caste and likely arrivals with the White Huns, established power over Gujarat and overran north-eastern Kathiawar around 600, making the raja of Valabhi a tributary and establishing a branch at Broach between 585 and 740.

The empire of Harsha (who died in 647 or 648) briefly held sway over Kathiawar, Gujarat, and Malwa, with his kingdom extending south to the Nerbudda. South of this boundary, the Chalukyas absorbed smaller kingdoms after overcoming the Rashtrakutas and other rivals during the 7th century. By 740, Vikramaditya II of the Chalukyas had taken the capital of the Pallavas. However, the Chalukyas eventually succumbed to the Rashtrakutas, who took over the majority of their dominions, including Gujarat. For approximately two centuries (c. 750–950), a balance of power existed between the Rashtrakutas and the Gurjaras.

In the district of Belgaum (anciently Venugrama), the country was held by the Chalukyas from the mid-6th century until approximately 760, followed by the Rashtrakutas. After the Rashtrakuta power broke up, a portion survived in the Rattas (875–1250). The Rattas struggled with the Kadambas of Goa, eventually overthrowing them by 1208, before the Rattas themselves succumbed to the Yadavas of Devagiri in 1250.

## The Khazar State and Byzantium

The Khazars reached the peak of their power between the decline of the Mahommedan empire and the rise of Russia. Their capital was transferred from Semender to Itil, where merchants from Bagdad, Armenia, and Byzantium traded in honey, leather, furs, and wax from the Volga. The importance of this trade was such that in 834, Emperor Theophilus of Byzantium provided materials and workmen to build the Khazars a stone fortress called Sarkel (the White Abode) to protect against Petcheneg Turks.

The Khazar dynasty accepted Judaism around 740, though the state maintained tolerance for Christians and Mahommedans, allowing individuals to be judged by the official judges of their own faith. The Khazar khakan held high status at the Byzantine court. For example, Emperor Justinian Rhinotmetus married the khakan's daughter during his exile in 702, and in 775, the grandson of a Khazar sovereign, Leo IV, ascended the Byzantine throne.

## Ancient Near East and East Asian Transitions

In the ancient Near East, the relationship between Assyria and Babylonia was characterized by volatility. Tukulti-In-aristi ruled Babylon for seven years as "king of Sumer and Akkad" before a Babylonian revolt led to his murder by his son, Assur-nazir-pal I. Reconciliation between the two kingdoms occurred later during the reign of Tukulti-Assur. Following the end of the old royal line with Bel-kudur-uzur, a new dynasty was established at Assur by In-aristi-pileser. A later conqueror, Tiglath-pileser I, expanded Assyrian reach toward Cappadocia and Armenia. In 1107 B.C., he suffered a temporary defeat by Merodach-nadin-akhi of Babylonia, where a new line of kings had taken over after the Kassite dynasty succumbed to Elamite attacks.

In China, the state of Tsin (or Chin) formed a homogeneous empire around 220 B.C. The empire experienced periods of division and struggle with tribes often called Tatars. It became a major power under the Han dynasty (c. 200 B.C.–A.D. 220) and the T'ang dynasty (A.D. 618–906), the latter of which extended into northern India and central Asia before being dismembered by the Kitans. After the Sung dynasty (960–1127), the Mongol power under Kublai Khan conquered China in the 13th century. The succeeding native Ming dynasty reigned for nearly 300 years before succumbing to the Manchus in 1644.
