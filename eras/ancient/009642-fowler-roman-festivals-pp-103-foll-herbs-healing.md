# Bona Dea and the Healing Herbs of the Roman Cult

The Bona Dea, known as the "good goddess," was an ancient Roman deity associated with fruitfulness, specifically regarding the earth and women. In the Roman religious tradition, she was identified with Fauna and, through later syncretism, with Maia and Ops. The connection to Maia is attributed to the dedication day of the goddess's temple on the Aventine, which occurred on the 1st of May.

## Cult Practices and Restrictions
The worship of Bona Dea was characterized by strict exclusivity and specific taboos. The cult was attended and the temple on the Aventine cared for by women only. A second celebration took place at the beginning of December within the house of a magistrate who possessed *imperium*. This particular celebration became noted in history due to the political consequences following the profanation of these mysteries by P. Clodius in 62 B.C.

Within the cult of Bona Dea, both myrtle and wine were tabooed. Various myths developed over time to provide explanations for these specific prohibitions.

## Medicinal and Symbolic Elements
The temple of Bona Dea served as a repository for items associated with the medicinal arts. Specifically, herbs with healing properties were kept within the temple. Additionally, snakes were kept there, as they served as the usual symbol of the medicinal art.

## Rituals and Greek Influence
The victim offered to Bona Dea was a *porca* (a sow), a practice consistent with the cults of other deities of fertility. This victim was referred to as *damium*. Consequently, the goddess herself was known as Damia, and her priestess was titled *damiatrix*.

These names are almost certainly Greek in origin. Damia was worshipped in several locations in Greece as well as in Tarentum, where a festival called *Dameia* was held. It is highly probable that the Greek cult of Damia was engrafted onto the original Roman goddess, potentially following the conquest of Tarentum in 272 B.C. Due to this blending, it is no longer possible to clearly distinguish the Roman elements from the Greek elements in this cult, though it remains intelligible as the worship of an Earth-goddess with associated mysteries.
