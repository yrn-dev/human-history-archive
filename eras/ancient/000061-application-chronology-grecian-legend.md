# Application of Chronology to Grecian Legend

The attempt to apply a formal chronology to the legends of ancient Greece involves the effort to organize mythical narratives into a linear historical sequence. This process primarily concerns the period anterior to 776 B.C., the date of the first recorded Olympiad. While various ancient and modern scholars have attempted to fix dates for these events, the reliability of such systems is a subject of significant scholarly debate.

## The Nature of Grecian Mythes
To the early Greeks, mythes formed their entire mental stock. These narratives were not viewed as poetical fancies but as serious realities, characterized by a tendency toward universal personification and a "personifying faith" that supplied the absence of positive knowledge. In the Homeric age, this mythical faith and religious point of view were paramount.

The Greeks wove gods, heroes, and men—and similarly religion and patriotism—into an indivisible web. Consequently, the threads of reality were not intended to be distinguishable from the divine or heroic elements. Because of this, the mythes of Troy and Thebes are considered by some critics to be of the same nature as the legends of Zeus in Krete or Prometheus; they are not viewed as having a distinct historical base that can be objectively detected.

## Chronological Systems and Divergences
Despite the legendary nature of these accounts, they were chronologized in ancient times and continue to be so by some modern scholars. Various schemes have been employed, leading to important differences regarding the dates of principal events. For example, Eratosthenês dissented from the views of Herodotus, Phanias, and Kallimachus. In more recent scholarship, Larcher and Raoul Rochette follow Herodotus, while O. Müller and Mr. Fynes Clinton hold opposing views.

Mr. Clinton’s *Fasti Hellenici* provides a comparison of these schemes, including a double chronological table that places events in series from Phorôneus to the Olympiad of Corœbus in 776 B.C., contrasting the systems of Eratosthenês and Kallimachus. However, other critics argue that any chronological system applied to incidents before 776 B.C. is essentially illusory and uncertified.

## The Role of the Logographers and Early Historians
The prose logographers, such as Hellanikus and Pherekydês, were the chief collectors of Attic mythes. They authored works called *Atthides* (works on Attic archaeology), in which they interwove legends into a single chronological series. This process often combined disparate traditions; for instance, the religious and political legends of Eleusis were worked into a continuous sequence with those of the Erechtheids. Through this method, figures like Kekrops were placed in the mythical chronology at a higher point than the hero Erechtheus.

Later figures like Herodotus and Thucydidês also sanctioned the belief that chronologizing the mythical past was practicable. Thucydidês spoke of the deeds of Hellên and his sons with confidence, and Herodotus recited heroic genealogies from Kadmus and Danaus. Modern critics, however, note a radical distinction between these narratives and the evidence available after 776 B.C. While Mr. Clinton recognizes Hellên and his sons (Dôrus, Æolus, and Xuthus) as fictitious, he identifies 776 B.C. as the first date that can be fixed upon authentic evidence.

## Challenges in Chronological Application
Several difficulties arise when attempting to historicize Grecian legend. One such issue is the "intervening blank" between legend and history, which complicates the hypothesis of continuous tradition. Furthermore, some scholars have attempted to use etymology as a general scheme to explain the characters and events of Greek legend—a method notably pursued by Hermann—though others deprecate this approach, arguing that etymology cannot impart a genuine system to the general body of mythical narrative.

The precision of ancient chronologists is also questioned. Some, from Damastês and Hellanikus downward, claimed to fix the exact hour, day, and month of the capture of Troy. In contrast, modern scholars like Mr. Clinton suggest that the event can, at best, be determined within a fifty-year window.

## Transition to Historical Consciousness
The transition from the mythopœic age to a scientific point of view was marked by the expansive force of the Grecian intellect. This shift was influenced by the opening of Egypt to Grecian commerce around 660 B.C. and the subsequent progress in social, geographical, and historical understanding. The commencement of physical science—led by figures such as Thalês, Xenophanês, and Pythagoras—introduced an impersonal nature of study that opposed the religious feelings of the multitude.

By the age of Herodotus and Thucydidês, a striking advance in ethical and intellectual standards had occurred. The "obsolete enormities" of the heroic age—such as the castration of Uranus by Kronus or the cruelty and licentiousness attributed to the gods—provoked strong disapprobation. Xenophanês composed poems specifically to denounce such tales. This evolution shifted the role of the poet from an organ of the past to an organ of the present, leading to the development of iambic, elegiac, and lyric poetry.
