# Historical Greece

## Geography and Limits
The northern boundary of Greece is marked by Olympus, with the Scardus and Pindus mountains extending and disseminating through the Peloponnesus and southern Greece. Other notable geographical features include Ossa, Pelion, and the Cyclades. The region is characterized by geological irregularities, including rivers that run dry during the summer, frequent lakes, and marshes. Some rivers follow subterranean courses out of land-locked basins.

The configuration of the land creates significant difficulties for transport and land communication. However, the coastline is heavily indented, providing universal accessibility by sea. Consequently, sea communication is essential for the colonies and islands. This maritime nature led ancient philosophers to observe the influence of commerce and maritime habits on the inhabitants, noting a distinct difference between the sea-states and the land-states. These geographical factors influenced both the intellectual development and the political relations of the people. The limits of Greece include the islands in the Aegean and Greeks residing on the coast of Asia Minor, as well as the Epirots and Macedonians.

## Transition from Legendary to Historical Greece
The transition from legendary Greece to historical Greece is marked by a shift in governance and social values. In legendary Greece, the king held an overruling personal ascendency. While the *boulê* (council) and the *agora* (assembly) existed, they were subordinate to the king; the *agora* served primarily as a medium for the king to promulgate his intentions. This is evidenced by the *agora* summoned by Telemachus in Ithaka and the submission presented in the second book of the Iliad. Justice was administered by the chiefs or the king, though Hesiod recorded complaints of unjust judgment in his own case.

In historical Greece, the Council and Assembly evolved from being media for the king's actions into the paramount depositaries of power. An exception to this general rule was found in the Spartan kings, whose powers remained limited. Additionally, the use of public speaking as an engine of government emerged in the earliest times, stimulating intellectual development.

## Social and Moral Evolution
Sociality in legendary Greece was rooted in personal sympathies and omnipotent personal feelings toward individuals, the king, or the gods. Strong family ties existed, characterized by respect for the wife and bonds between kinsmen and brothers. Hospitality was a core value, specifically in the reception of the suppliant and the stranger.

However, legendary Greece also exhibited unrestrained, aggressive, and ferocious passions. Hesiod provides a dark picture of this era, which included the mutilation of dead bodies and the mistreatment of orphans. Homicide was handled through *poinè*, a valuable compensation paid to the kinsman of the murdered person. In contrast, historical Greece treated homicide as a crime against society.

The condition of the Homeric Greeks included various professions and classes, such as slaves and *thêtes*. Their commerce and navigation were limited, though they interacted with the Phoenicians, Taphians, and Kretans.

## Political Developments and Conflict
Historical Greece saw the rise of various city-states and political entities. The "Age of the Grecian Despots" included cities such as Megara, Sikyon, and Corinth. The heart of Greece is centered in Attica and the Peloponnesus.

The historical record includes significant military and diplomatic events, such as the Battle of Marathon and the subsequent march of Xerxes against Greece. Later periods were marked by the Peloponnesian War. A thirty-year truce existed fourteen years prior to the start of this war, and the year before the conflict began was marked by the blockade of Potidæa. In March 421 B.C., the Peace of Nikias was concluded between the Spartan confederacy and Athens, intended to last for fifty years.

## Theories of Migration and Origin
There has been historical debate regarding the "Dorian invasion." Some scholars, such as J. Beloch, questioned the historical value of these traditions, suggesting they were attempts to reconcile the political geography of Homer (8th-century Ionians describing 12th-century events) with that of historical Greece. Such legends may have been used to justify political ambitions or connect towns with similar names during rivalries between non-Dorian Athens and Thebes and Dorian Sparta. However, as earlier dates for Homer became more probable, some argue that the poems accurately represent a phase of prehistoric "Achaean" or "Homeric" culture that ended in a general catastrophe.
