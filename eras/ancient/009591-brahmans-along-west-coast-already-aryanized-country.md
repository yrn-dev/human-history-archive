# Aryanization of the West Coast of India

## Early Inhabitants and Trade
In the earliest recorded times, the majority of the west coast of India was occupied by Dravidian tribes. These populations lived in fortified villages under the leadership of their own kings and practiced simpler arts of life. Their faith was primarily centered on the propitiation of demons and spirits. 

Despite the simplicity of their internal social structure, these early inhabitants engaged in significant maritime commerce. Evidence indicates that an export trade existed with the Red Sea via East Africa as early as 1000 B.C. By 750 B.C., a similar trade route had been established with Babylon through the Persian Gulf. This specific trade route served as a conduit for cultural and technological imports; traders brought the legend of the Flood, the art of brick-making, and the Brahmi alphabet back to India.

## The Process of Aryanization
The demographic and cultural landscape of India was shaped by different strata of population. The Dravidians, who may represent the earliest northern invaders, were eventually driven toward the south and the center of the peninsula by a third stratum: the Aryans. These Aryan-speaking peoples, who may have originated in Asia Minor or southern Russia, migrated south-eastwards around 1500 B.C. (though some sources suggest a spread into India between 2000 and 1000 B.C.). Those who entered North India through western passes before 1000 B.C. spoke Sanskrit and interacted with the primordial brunette Dravidian civilization, learning much from it.

The "Aryanization" of the west coast specifically occurred through the settlement of Brahmans. The Brahmans were a hereditary sacerdotal class that wielded extraordinary influence, which tempered the power of absolute monarchies in settled states like those in the Ganges valley. This class was closely linked to the institution of caste, a system of population division based partly on racial distinctions. Through the settlement of Brahmans along the west coast, the region was Aryanized in its religion and, to a certain extent, in its language. This process was completed before the close of the 6th century B.C., prior to the Persian conquest of the Indus valley.

## Linguistic Expansion and the Konkan Coast
The linguistic evolution of the region involved two main groups of Indo-Aryan speech: the "Midland" language of the Gangetic Doab and the "Outer Band" languages. The Outer Band originally included areas such as Gujarat and Rajputana, extending to the basin of the river Nerbudda and the Vindhya hills. 

As the population of the Midland expanded, they occupied these tracts and reached the sea in Gujarat, pushing the speakers of the Outer Band languages further afield. These speakers crossed the Vindhyas to settle on the Konkan coast and the central plateau of the Deccan. Upon arriving on the Konkan coast, they encountered speakers of Dravidian languages. Due to the influence of their superior civilization, the Aryan speakers retained their tongue and imposed it upon the aborigines. Consequently, the inhabitants of this region became the ancestors of those who speak modern Marathi.

## Subsequent Political Shifts
Following the period of Aryanization and the brief Persian dominion, the march of Alexander the Great down the Indus facilitated the rise of the Maurya empire under Chandragupta. During this era, the city of Ujjain served as the seat of a prince of the imperial house acting as a viceroy, ruling over Gujarat, Malwa, and Kathiawar.

After the death of Asoka in 231 B.C., the Maurya empire collapsed. The western territories were inherited by the Andhra dynasty of the Satavahanas of Paithan. The Satavahanas were a Dravidian family whose dominion by 200 B.C. reached from the Western Ghats and Nasik to the deltas of the Kistna and Godavari. By approximately A.D. 210, Satavahana power in the west declined and was replaced by the Kshaharatas, the Saka satraps of Surashtra. These foreign rulers had mastered Gujarat and Ujjain by A.D. 120.

## Economic Decline
From approximately A.D. 40, the cities along the coast experienced significant enrichment through trade with the Roman empire, a commerce encouraged by both the Saka satraps and the Satavahanas. However, this prosperity ended following the extinction of the main Kshaharata dynasty around 300 A.D. and the fall of Palmyra in 273 A.D., leading to the decay of this maritime commerce.
