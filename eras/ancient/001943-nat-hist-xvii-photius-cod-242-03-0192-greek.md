# Baetylus

## Definition and Nature
A baetylus (derived from the Greek *baitulos* or *baitulion*) is a term of Semitic origin, corresponding to *bethel*, which refers to a sacred stone. These objects were regarded as fetish objects of worship and were believed to be endowed with life. According to the writings of Photius (*Cod.* 242) and Pliny (*Nat. Hist.* xvii. 9), baetyli were typically meteoric stones that were either dedicated to the gods or revered as symbols of the gods themselves.

## Greek Mythology and Cult Practices
In the context of Greek mythology, the term was specifically applied to a stone associated with Cronus. Fearing misfortune from his own children, Cronus swallowed this stone in mistake for his infant son, Zeus, who had been substituted for the stone by Gaea and Uranus, the parents of his wife. 

This particular stone was preserved at Delphi. The rituals associated with its care included anointing it with oil on a daily basis and covering it with raw wool during festal occasions.

## Phoenician and Roman Examples
In Phoenician mythology, the name Baetylus is attributed to one of the sons of Uranus. Another significant example of a baetylic object was the holy stone of Pessinus, which served as an effigy of Rhea Cybele. This stone was black and irregular in form. It was brought to Rome in 204 B.C. and placed within the mouth of the statue of the goddess.

## Forms and Variations
While many baetyli were originally shapeless stones, attempts were sometimes made to give them more regular forms. Examples of such modifications include:
* **Apollo Agyieus**: Represented by a conical pillar with a pointed end.
* **Zeus Meilichius**: Represented in the form of a pyramid.

Other notable baetylic idols were located in the temples of Zeus Teleios at Tegea and Zeus Casius at Seleucia. These idols maintained their significance even during the declining years of paganism, as evidenced by the attacks directed toward them by ecclesiastical writers.
