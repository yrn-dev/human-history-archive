# General Character of Military Conflict and Organization

The nature of warfare and military organization has varied significantly across different eras and civilizations, shifting based on technological advancements, the character of leadership, and the strategic goals of the combatants.

## Strategic Disparities and the 18th Century
In the wars spanning from 1740 to 1748, a notable characteristic was the extraordinary disparity between the means employed and the intended ends. While the French and other armies pursued grandiose political schemes, their execution often fell short due to the limitations of time and space. The small standing armies of the 18th century were capable of conquering territory by degrees but lacked the ability to deliver a decisive blow. 

Prussia emerged as a striking military power during this period. Its success was largely attributed to a stricter application of universally recognized principles of tactics and discipline, as well as a superior administrative system that proved almost decisive against Austria. Additionally, the personal ascendancy of Frederick—described as energetic and resolute—provided an advantage over opposing generals who were responsible to individual sovereigns. Frederick succeeded where others failed because he possessed a definite end and the proportionate means to achieve it. In contrast, the French obtained very little of their objectives despite later victories.

## Roman Military Evolution and Leadership
Roman military history demonstrates a transition from citizen-based forces to professionalization. Marius served as a great military reformer, replacing the citizen militia with a professional soldiery and establishing the cohort as the military unit instead of the maniple. By drawing cavalry and light-armed troops from foreign countries, Marius is considered the originator of the mercenary army, creating a class of soldiers whose only trade was war.

The effectiveness of Roman campaigns often depended on the specific traits of the general. In the war with Philip of Macedon, the Romans required a leader capable of using persuasion and diplomacy rather than relying solely on main force. This was essential because Philip relied on the cooperation of Greece to supply troops and resources; unless Greece was detached from his alliance, a single battle could not decide the war. Titus was suited for this role due to his affability, strict sense of justice, and high character. Similarly, the Lusitani sought out Sertorius to lead them against Rome because of his reputation for experience, his skill in stealthy maneuvers, rapid movements, and craft in the field.

## Ancient Greek and Japanese Warfare
In ancient Greece, the relationship between political orators and military leaders was often one of tension. Some argued that war tended to shift ascendency toward military officers and away from orators. For instance, Phokion, a straightforward military man, noted that while war gave him command over the orators, peace gave the orators command over him. In the conflict involving Korkyra, military operations were characterized by the activity of oligarchical exiles on Mount Istônê and the deployment of triremes by both the Peloponnesians and Athenians.

In Japan, the character of provincial warfare—often family against family—initially required little in the way of elaborate defenses. Because fighting was conducted hand-to-hand or with missiles like arrows, defenses were limited to watch-towers, wooden palisades, earthen walls, and ditches. Some buildings used outward-facing nails as a form of chevaux de frise. This changed in 1542 with the introduction of firearms, which led to the construction of massively built castles patterned after Western models.

## Military Administration and Support
Military organization extends beyond combat to administration and morale. In various nations, infantry regiments maintain bands to enhance the order and ease of marching. While British bandsmen are used as stretcher-bearers during action, continental European bands are kept in hand to play troops into action. Instruments vary by region: the saxophone is common in many countries but not favored by British authorities, while the "Jingling Johnny" (a frame of small bells) is universal in the Russian army. Scottish regiments are distinguished by the use of bagpipes.

## Other Historical Conflicts
Other conflicts have been characterized by stagnation or ideological disputes. In Asia Minor, a "bickering war" occurred between Byzantium and Bagdad in which neither side made permanent gains, though Moslems occasionally raided as far as the Bosphorus. In more modern contexts, the declaration of war by England against Germany on August 4 was framed as a pursuit of liberty and peace, though critics pointed to Britain's own widespread militarism and its supremacy of the sea as a counter-argument to the critique of German militarism.
