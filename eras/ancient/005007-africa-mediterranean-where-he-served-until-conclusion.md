# Africa and the Mediterranean

The relationship between Africa and the Mediterranean basin has been defined by prehistoric geological shifts, the rise and fall of maritime empires, and centuries of naval conflict and strategic transport.

## Prehistoric and Early Civilizations
In pre-history, a catastrophic event occurred between 30,000 and 10,000 B.C. involving the refilling of the Mediterranean. Ocean waters broke through westward hills, flooding a basin that had previously been an eastern Levantine lake. This salt flood submerged primitive settlements and rose until it reached the mountain cliffs of Africa and Arabia.

Early maritime activity in the region was led by Hamitic, Sumerian, and later Semitic peoples. The Phoenicians, a Semitic people, established independent harbor towns along the eastern Mediterranean, including Sidon, Tyre, and Acre. They eventually expanded their voyages westward to North Africa, where they founded the cities of Utica and Carthage.

## Classical and Roman Eras
During the Roman period, the province of Africa served as a base for military movements; for example, Cornelius Gallus advanced from Africa against L. Pinarius Carpus. Despite their territorial reach, the Romans are characterized as having left the Mediterranean seaways undeveloped. This lack of naval foresight meant that when barbarians pressed toward the warm waters, Rome could not swiftly transport armies from Africa, Asia, or Spain to rescue the Adriatic coasts or Italy. Consequently, the Vandals became masters of the western Mediterranean without engaging in a naval battle.

The Vandals later used the forests of Corsica to build fleets that terrorized the Mediterranean. This power persisted until Belisarius destroyed the Vandal power in Africa. Following this, the lieutenant Cyril conquered Corsica in 534, placing it under the exarchate of Africa as part of the East Roman empire.

## Medieval and Early Modern Transitions
From the northern coast of Africa, Mussulmans made their first descent upon Corsica in 713. Later, Moorish incursions from Spain continued to threaten the region. In 828, Boniface II, count of the Tuscan march, led a successful expedition against the African Mussulmans. His son, Adalbert, continued this war against the Saracens, though Mussulmans remained in possession of parts of Corsica until approximately 930.

In the thirteenth century, the Mongol conquests drove the Ottoman Turks into Asia Minor. By the sixteenth century, the Ottoman Sultans had conquered Egypt and most of North Africa. Their fleet established them as the masters of the Mediterranean. This dominance lasted until 1571, when the naval battle of Lepanto restored Christian ascendancy to the Mediterranean waters.

## Naval History and Strategic Operations
The Mediterranean remained a focal point for European naval powers into the modern era. In 1761, Captain Alexander Hood served in the Mediterranean aboard the "Africa" until a peace was concluded. Later, in the 19th century, Sir Geoffrey Thomas Phipps Hornby served as commander-in-chief in the Mediterranean starting in 1877, where he managed foreign relations during the Russian advance on Constantinople.

In the early 20th century, the Mediterranean was a critical theater for troop movements. During a period of crisis, the French intended to transport a full army corps of their best troops from North Africa across the Mediterranean to the main battle line. The French General Staff believed the French Fleet and the Austrian Fleet provided a guarantee for these transports. However, the presence of the German ship *Goeben*—which outstripped every vessel in the French Navy in speed—posed a significant threat. The *Goeben* had the potential to avoid French battle squadrons and sink the transports crammed with soldiers. To counter this, Admiral Boué de Lapeyrère implemented a system of convoys and delayed embarkation on August 4 to organize adequate escorts.

## Geography and Communication
Ancient efforts were made to link the Mediterranean with other bodies of water. Nekôs sought to establish the best communication between the Mediterranean and the Red Sea by digging a canal, a project later finished by Darius. While the circumnavigation of Africa was attempted—notably by the Achæmenid Sataspês as a penalty imposed by Xerxes—it was found to be impractical for traffic or communication given the resources of the time. Sataspês eventually returned without completing the circuit, claiming his vessel had stuck fast and could move no farther.
