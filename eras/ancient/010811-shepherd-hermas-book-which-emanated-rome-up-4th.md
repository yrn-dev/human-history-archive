# Shepherd of Hermas

The *Shepherd of Hermas* is an early Christian work that emanated from Rome. It is categorized as part of the broad fringe of primitive Christian literature that approximates the character of the New Testament, specifically grouped among the Sub-Apostolic Fathers alongside the *Didache*, the letters of Ignatius and Polycarp, the letters of Clement of Rome, and the *Epistle of Barnabas*.

## Canonical Status and Usage
The status of the *Shepherd of Hermas* varied significantly across different eras and regions of the early church. In the primitive church, it was one of the books read in church that were eventually excluded from the official canon. Up until the 4th century, it continued to be read in church settings.

Its authority was particularly noted in the following contexts:
* **Manuscript Appendices:** In the *Codex Sinaiticus* of the 4th century, the *Shepherd of Hermas* appears as an appendix to the New Testament, following the *Epistle of Barnabas*. It also appears in an 11th-century manuscript following the *Epistle of Barnabas* and the *Didache*.
* **Regional Usage:** The work enjoyed a level of quasi-canonical authority, a fact supported by the historian Eusebius.
* **Liturgical Use:** Along with the letters of Clement of Rome, it was read in the primitive church.

## Evolution of Acceptance and Rejection
The transition of the *Shepherd of Hermas* from a widely read text to a rejected one is documented through several historical lists and reviews:
* **The Muratorian Fragment:** This list, believed to be of Roman origin and dating from the end of the 2nd century, admitted the *Shepherd of Hermas* for private reading, but not for public reading, due to the date of its composition.
* **Eusebius's Classification:** Early in the 4th century, Eusebius categorized Christian works into three classes. He placed the *Shepherd of Hermas* in the third class, consisting of works to be decidedly rejected.
* **The Athanasian Canon:** The final canon established by Athanasius in the 4th century, which became the standard for the New Testament, did not include the work.

## Teachings and Content
The *Shepherd of Hermas* contains instructions on morality, marriage, and spiritual discipline.

**Marriage and Divorce**
The text addresses the laws of divorce. According to the *Mandate* (Book IV), the Christian law allowed an innocent wife to divorce a guilty husband, mirroring the allowance for an innocent husband to divorce a guilty wife in cases of adultery.

**Asceticism and Purity**
The work encourages certain rigors regarding purity and virginity. Specifically, it encourages the institution of *virgines subintroductae*, or "spiritual wives." This practice was based on the assumption that the grace of baptism could restore the original purity of life shared by Adam and Eve before the Fall.
