# Phasaelus Is Too Hard For Felix; Herod Also Overcomes

## Conflict with Felix
Following the departure of Cassius from Syria, disturbances and sedition arose in Judea. Felix, who remained at Jerusalem with an army, launched a sudden assault against Phasaelus. This attack was intended as revenge against Herod for the death of Malichus, with Felix targeting Herod's brother. During this conflict, the local people rose in arms.

Herod was present with Fabius, the governor (or prefect) of Damascus, and intended to travel to the assistance of his brother. However, he was detained by a sickness. In Herod's absence, Phasaelus proved to be "too hard" for Felix by himself. Phasaelus successfully defeated Felix and shut him up in a tower, eventually dismissing him based on certain conditions.

## Relations with Hyrcanus and the Fortresses
During this period, Phasaelus expressed complaints regarding Hyrcanus, accusing him of ingratitude. Phasaelus noted that despite receiving many benefits, Hyrcanus supported their enemies. Specifically, Phasaelus reproached Hyrcanus for the assistance he had provided to Malichus and for overlooking the actions of Malichus's brother. The brother of Malichus had caused many places to revolt and had established garrisons within them, including Masada, which is described as the strongest of all the fortresses.

## Herod's Recovery and Subsequent Actions
Once Herod recovered from his disease, he intervened in the conflict. He arrived and took back from Felix all the places Felix had acquired. Like Phasaelus before him, Herod dismissed Felix upon certain conditions.

## Political Ascent and the Tetrarchy
Following these events, Antony arrived in Syria. A group of one hundred potent Jews, utilizing men of great eloquence, attempted to accuse Herod and his associates. However, Messala spoke in defense of the young men. Antony consulted Hyrcanus, who was Herod's father-in-law, to ask who governed the nation best; Hyrcanus identified Herod and his friends.

Due to an old hospitable friendship with Herod's father, Antipater, Antony appointed both Herod and Phasaelus as tetrarchs and committed the public affairs of the Jews to them. While Antony bound fifteen of their adversaries and intended to kill them, Herod obtained their pardon.

## Later Conflicts and the Parthian Deception
In later years, during the festival of Pentecost, a large multitude gathered around the temple in Jerusalem. While Herod guarded the royal palace and led a courageous assault that put many ten thousands of the enemy to flight, Phasaelus was charged with guarding the wall.

During this time, Antigonus requested that Pacorus, the general of the Parthians, be admitted into the city as a reconciler. Phasaelus received Pacorus kindly and was persuaded by him to act as an ambassador to Barzapharnes. Herod strongly opposed this, warning Phasaelus of the perfidiousness of the barbarians and urging him to kill the plotter rather than expose himself to snares. Despite this, Phasaelus complied. Pacorus took Hyrcanus with him to avoid suspicion and conducted Phasaelus away. Subsequently, the Parthians bound both Hyrcanus and Phasaelus. Phasaelus greatly reproached the Parthians for their perjury.

## Family Ties and the Death of Phasaelus
Phasaelus was the brother of Herod. He married Salampsio, who was the daughter of Herod and Mariamne (the granddaughter of Hyrcanus), making them first cousins. Together, they had five children: three sons named Antipater, Herod, and Alexander, and two daughters named Alexandra and Cypros.

In a separate effort to save his brother, Herod traveled to Arabia to obtain money from King Malichus. Herod hoped to use the money to prevail upon the "covetous temper" of the barbarians to spare Phasaelus. He was prepared to offer three hundred talents and even brought his brother's seven-year-old son as a pledge. However, these efforts were in vain, as Phasaelus had already died. King Malichus subsequently ordered Herod to leave Arabia, using the Parthians as a pretense to avoid returning gifts or debts owed to Antipater.
