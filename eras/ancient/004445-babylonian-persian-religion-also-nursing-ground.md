# The Syncretism of Babylonian and Persian Religions

The interaction between Babylonian and Persian religious systems served as a significant intellectual and spiritual "nursing-ground" for later developments, most notably the rise of Gnosticism. This synthesis occurred as the influence of the Iranian religion, characterized by a stronger monotheism, encountered and eventually superseded the polytheism of the Euphrates Valley.

## Influence on Gnosticism
The syncretism of Persian and Babylonian beliefs is evident in the systems of early Gnosticism, such as that described by Basilides. Several specific parallels exist between Gnostic concepts and Persian religious figures:

*   **Numerical Symbolism:** The Gnostic supreme God is sometimes called Abraxas (or Abrasax), a name with a numerical value of 365. This corresponds to the astronomical conception of 365 heavens and their daily aspects. Notably, the Persian god Mithras (Meithras) was also known in antiquity to possess this same numerical value.
*   **Divine Hierarchies:** In the system of Basilides, the *Pater innatus* (supreme God) is surrounded by a series of Hypostases—ranging from five to seven, including *Nous, Logos, Phronesis, Sophia,* and *Dunamis* (with *Dikaiosune* and *Eirene* potentially added). This structure mirrors the *Ameshas-spentas* that surround the Persian deity Ahura-Mazda.
*   **World-Creating Powers:** Gnostic systems frequently describe seven world-creating angels or powers. These are identified as specifically oriental speculations, originating as the seven planetary deities of the later Babylonian religion. As the Iranian monotheistic religion gained dominance over the Babylonian system, these planetary gods were degraded into daemonic or semi-daemonic forces within Gnostic thought.

## Babylonian-Assyrian Religious Legacy
The religious influence of Babylonia and its offshoot, Assyria, extended far beyond its own borders. While the Assyrians shared a language with the Babylonians, they maintained a distinct individuality in their religion and art. Even after the Persian conquest—with Cyrus taking Babylon in 555 B.C.—Babylon continued to exert a cultural and religious influence for many centuries.

The astral theology of the Babylonian-Assyrian priests had a profound effect on the ancient world, including the Romans and Greeks. By the middle of the 4th century B.C., Babylonian astrology began a "triumphal march to the west," particularly impacting Egypt. Among the Greeks and Romans, "Chaldaean wisdom" became a synonym for divination via the stars and planets.

## Impact on Semitic and Israelite Traditions
The civilization of the Euphrates valley provided a significant impetus for the purification of old Semite religions, including those of the nomadic Arabs and the sons of Israel (Israelites). Traces of direct borrowing from Babylonia are found in the Old Testament, with indirect influences appearing in the Psalms, the prophetical books, and "Wisdom Literature."

Furthermore, the recrudescence of views found in apocalyptic literature is attributed by many scholars to the astral theology of Babylonian and Assyrian priests. These influences persisted into the period of the New Testament, appearing in modified and transformed forms within early Christian gnosticism.

## Persian Contributions to Angelology and Eschatology
While Babylonian influence was widespread, certain complex theological developments in the Israelite tradition are attributed specifically to Persian rather than Babylonian sources. 

The Book of Daniel presents a complete system of angelology, featuring a hierarchy of commanding angels—such as Gabriel and Michael—who manage human affairs. This concept was foreign to the primitive Israelitish belief in the indivisibility of Yahweh's power. Because the Babylonian system of attendant spirits was not as complete as the one found in Daniel, scholars conclude this doctrine resulted from prolonged Persian influence. Similarly, the doctrine of the resurrection of the dead, appearing for the first time in the Old Testament in the Book of Daniel, is believed to have originated among the Persians and was engrafted onto the Israelite mind following intercourse with the Zoroastrian religion.
