# Babylonian-Assyrian Proper Names

The study of proper names used by the Babylonians and Assyrians involves the analysis of historical records, business documents, literary productions, and votive inscriptions. While scholars have reached a general agreement regarding the principles of pronunciation and formation for thousands of these names, some minor differences in transliteration and spelling persist.

## Linguistic Origins and the Cuneiform System
The primary difficulty in reading Babylonian and Assyrian proper names stems from the "ideographic" method of writing. The cuneiform script was not originally Semitic; it was developed by the Sumerians (or Sumero-Akkadians), the non-Semitic settlers of the Euphrates valley. When the Babylonians settled in the region, they adopted this script and adapted it to their Semitic language.

This resulted in a double method of writing:
*   **Ideographic/Sumerian:** Words could be written using a sign or combination of signs representing an entire word. For example, the sign MU represents the Sumerian word for "name." In a Babylonian or Assyrian text, this sign is read as *shumu*, the Semitic equivalent. Similarly, the Sumerian word for "clothing" (SIG-BA) is read as *lubushtu* in Semitic texts.
*   **Phonetic:** Words could be spelled out in syllables. The Sumerian signs were used as phonetic syllables regardless of their original meaning. For instance, *lubushtu* could be written phonetically as *lu-bu-ush-tu*.

As this culture spread north to Assyria, it became the Babylonian-Assyrian syllabary. Over time, this system was modified, and Semitic equivalents for signs were sometimes abbreviated or distorted to create new phonetic values.

## Structure and Formation of Names
Proper names in the Babylonian-Assyrian periods typically followed specific structural patterns, though they evolved over time.

### Complex Names
Full names often consisted of three or more elements. Generally, these included:
1.  A divine element (the name of a deity).
2.  A verbal form.
3.  An object represented by a third element.

The interpretation of these names is often complicated by the ideographic system. For example, the sign KUR can be read as *na[s.][=a]ru* ("protect"), but scholars must determine if the intended form is an imperative (*u[s.][s.]ur*), an active participle (*n[=a][s.]ir*), or a third person perfect (*i[s.][s.]ur*). Similarly, the sign MU can mean "name" (*shumu*) or "give" (*nad[=a]nu*). If a name contains a deity's name followed by MU-MU, it is uncertain whether the first MU is a verb and the second a noun, or vice versa.

### Simplified and Hypocoristic Names
At an early stage, names began to be reduced from three elements to two, either by omitting the deity's name or by combining a deity's name with only a verbal form. Eventually, single-element names emerged. These often featured "hypocoristic affixes"—similar to modern pet-names—such as *-tum* (feminine), *[=a]n*, *[=a]*, *um*, *atum*, *atija*, and *sha*.

## Case Study: The Name Assur
The name of the city and deity Assur illustrates the complexities of Assyro-Babylonian philology and popular etymology. The city was likely founded by Semites from Babylonia. In the law-code of King Khammurabi (c. 2250 B.C.), the city is referred to as *A-usar* (*ki*), where *-ki* indicates a land territory or province more extensive than the city itself. *A-usar* is thought to mean "well watered region."

However, the name appears in several forms, leading to triple popular etymology:
1.  **A-usar:** The non-Semitic form.
2.  **An-sar:** In later literature, the name appears as *An-sar* or *An-sar* (*ki*). In the Creation tablet, *An-sar* means "host of heaven" (contrasted with *Ki-sar*, "host of earth"). This suggests Assyrian scribes attempted to identify the deity Asur with the Creation deity An-sar.
3.  **Asir/Ashir:** The epithet *Asir* ("overseer") was applied to several gods, including Asur, adding further confusion to the name's origin.

## Scholarly Resources
The formation of these names, particularly the difficulties surrounding Sumerian names, has been addressed in several specialized works:
*   Huber's *Die Personennamen in den Keilschrifturkunden aus der Zeit der Koenige von Ur und Nisin* (1907), focusing on the Sumerian period.

*   Tallqvist's *Neu-Babylonisches Namenbuch* (1905), focusing on the neo-Babylonian period.
