# Authorities on the Life of the Buddha

The historical and biographical accounts of the Buddha are preserved across various canonical and non-canonical texts. These sources range from early Pali episodes to later Buddhist Sanskrit texts, providing a fragmented but detailed record of his life, from birth to his final days.

## Canonical Pali Sources
The Canonical Pali texts reached their current form before the 4th century B.C. These records often present the Buddha's life as a series of episodes. Some of the primary accounts include:

*   **Birth and Early Life:** The account of the Buddha's birth is found in the *Majjhima Nikāya* and the *Anguttara Nikāya*. Additionally, an old ballad titled "Adoration of the babe" is preserved in the *Sutta Nipāta*.
*   **Youth and Departure:** The period of the Buddha's youth at home is documented in the *Anguttara Nikāya* (i. 145). His "going forth" is recorded both as an old ballad in the *Sutta Nipāta* and as a prose account in the *Dīgha Nikāya*.
*   **Attainment and Teaching:** A significant long episode in the *Majjhima Nikāya* covers the Buddha's years of study and penance, his attainment of Buddhahood and Nirvāna, and the conversion of his first five converts. Subsequent events, from the conversation with those five converts through the end of the first year of his teaching, are detailed in the *Vinaya*.
*   **Later Life:** A visit to Kapilavastu is recorded in the *Vinaya*. The final days of the Buddha are documented in the *Dīgha Nikāya* within the *Mahāparinibbāna Suttanta*.

## Buddhist Sanskrit Texts
Beyond the Pali canon, several Buddhist Sanskrit texts provide further biographical detail:

*   **Mahāvastu:** Likely dating to the 2nd century B.C., this text covers the Buddha's life up until the end of the first year of his teaching.
*   **Lalita Vistara:** Likely dating to the 1st century B.C., this work follows the Buddha's life up to the delivery of his first sermon.
*   **Buddha Carita:** This is listed among the Sanskrit authorities regarding the life of the Buddha.

## Structure of the Nikāyas
The Buddhist scriptures are organized into collections known as Nikāyas. The *Anguttara Nikāya* is the longest book in the Buddhist Bible, spanning 1,840 pages. It is characterized by a specific organizational method where Buddhist doctrine is expressed in classes or numbered lists—starting with units, then pairs, then trios, and so on. This system was designed as a memory aid for a time when the texts were learned by heart and had not yet been written. This collection encompasses much of the ethics and psychology of Buddhism.

Another collection, the *Samyutta*, organizes the Buddhist doctrine by subject. It consists of 55 *Samyuttas* (groups), where suttas are grouped by their topic or by the type of people to whom they were addressed.

## Terminology and Identity
In the canonical texts, the Buddha consistently refers to himself by the title "the Arahat." The sources indicate that he does not call himself "the Buddha," nor do his followers address him by that specific title.
