# BAAL

## Etymology and General Meaning
Baal is a Semitic word that primarily signifies "owner," "inhabitant," or "lord." In the context of Semitic family and religious relations, the term was specifically used to express the relationship between a husband and his wife, as well as the relationship between a deity and a worshipper. When used in a religious sense, it did not necessarily imply that the god was the lord of the worshipper, but rather that the deity was the ruler in, or the possessor of, a specific district or place.

The term also appears as a noun of relation in various contexts, such as "baal of hair" to describe a hairy man, "baal of wings" for a winged creature, "baals of arrows" for archers, and "baal of oath" for conspirators.

## Religious Conceptions and Worship
In the Old Testament, the term is regularly written with the article as "the Baal." Because the Baals of different sanctuaries or tribes were not always viewed as identical, the plural form "the Baalim" is frequently mentioned. The deity was often viewed as a local power; for example, place names such as Baal Hazor and Baal Hermon likely refer to the "House or Temple of the Baal" of those specific locations.

The cult of Baal often involved the worship of "givers of increase" on mountain summits and under green trees, where licentiousness was practiced in the belief that it would secure abundant crops. Practices denounced by Israelite prophets associated with the cult of Baal and Astarte include:
* Human sacrifice.
* The burning of incense.
* The preparation of sacred mystic cakes.
* Violent and ecstatic exercises.
* Ceremonial acts of kissing and bowing.

In some instances, the deity was represented by a shapeless cone of stone or a pillar, which served as an object of public pilgrimage and adoration, as the deity was held to be immanent within the stone. However, small portrait statues were also manufactured for private worship or to be carried by colonists.

## Baal in Israelite Context
The sons of Israel applied the title of Baal to Yahweh himself, as evidenced by names such as Be'aliah ("Yahweh is baal or lord"), Jerubbaal (Gideon), Eshbaal (a son of Saul), and Beeliada (a son of David). The name Beeliada appears as Eliada in other records, suggesting that El (God) was regarded as equivalent to Baal.

However, when the name Baal became exclusively associated with idolatrous worship, a marked abhorrence for the word developed. In compound proper names, the word *baal* was replaced with *bosheth* (meaning "shameful thing"), resulting in names such as Mephibosheth and Ishbosheth.

## Punic and Carthaginian Traditions
The Carthaginians, who were a colony of Tyre, shared the language, religion, and customs of the Tyrians, Canaanites, and Israelites. In Carthage, Baal-Ammon was venerated as a lord and associate deity to the "great lady" Tanit. Inscriptions on votive stelae describe Tanit as the "manifestation" or "face" of Baal.

The influence of Baal is also evident in Punic naming conventions, where the name of God was joined to personal names. For example, the name Hannibal signifies "Baal [or the Lord] has been gracious to me," while Asdrubal implies "the Lord will be our succour." Other similar names include Maharbal, Adherbal, and Mastanabal.

## Related Deities and Variations
Various forms of the deity appear across the Semitic world. In the Philistine city of Ekron, a god named Baalzebub (or Beelzebub) was worshipped; King Ahaziah of Israel once sought to inquire of this deity regarding his health. Another form is Baal-Shamem, meaning "Baal of the heavens."

In Phoenician tradition, Baal was sometimes viewed as a sun-god, serving as the male counterpart to Astarte. While some theories suggest a connection between the Semitic Baal and Celtic mythology—specifically the "beltane" fires—scientific philologists have repudiated this etymology, stating that the Gaelic form of beltane has no connection to the word for fire (*teine*).
