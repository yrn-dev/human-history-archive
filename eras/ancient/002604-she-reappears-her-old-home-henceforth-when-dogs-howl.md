# Dogs and Canine-Related Narratives

The archive's records contain diverse accounts regarding dogs, ranging from biological descriptions of semi-domesticated breeds to their roles in mythology, royal sport, and personal anecdotes.

## Biological Characteristics and Breeds
In the northern regions of both hemispheres, several semi-domesticated dog breeds exist that possess wolf-like characteristics, including long woolly hair and erect ears. The Eskimo dog is described as a reclaimed wolf, with some reports stating that the Eskimo maintain the strength and size of these dogs by crossing them with wolves. These dogs are characterized by a rough coat, a straight bushy tail, a moderately sharp muzzle, and small, upright ears. Notably, the Eskimo dog howls rather than barks. They are recognized as cunning, enduring, and skilled hunters that feed on meat, game, and fish. Despite their utility to Arctic explorers and owners, they are considered too rapacious to be trained to avoid attacking small domesticated animals, such as goats or sheep.

Other regional variations include the black wolfdog of Florida, which resembles the local black wolf, and the sheepdogs of Europe and Asia, which resemble the wolves of those respective areas. In India, the pariah dog is closely similar to the Indian wolf. Additionally, the Hare Indian dog, found near the Mackenzie river and Great Bear Lake, is more affectionate and gentle than the Eskimo dog, though it is impatient of restraint and, like the coyote, is practically unable to bark.

## Dogs in Mythology and Legend
Canines appear in several classical narratives as instruments of fate or trials. In one account, the goddess Artemis transformed Aktæôn—a devoted hunter and her favorite companion—into a stag. This transformation led to Aktæôn being set upon and devoured by his own dogs. The reasons for Artemis's displeasure vary by source: some suggest he saw her naked while she bathed, others that he courted Semelê, and Euripidês suggests he claimed to be her superior in the chase.

In another narrative, the king of the Molossians, Aidoneus, possessed a dog named Cerberus. Aidoneus required all suitors of his daughter, Kore, to fight this dog; the victor would then receive her hand in marriage. When Theseus and Peirithous arrived not as wooers but as ravishers, Aidoneus imprisoned them. Peirithous was killed by the dog, while Theseus was strictly guarded.

## Historical and Personal Accounts
Dogs have appeared in the lives of various historical figures as pets or symbols. The Roman general Paulus Aemilius experienced a perceived omen involving a small dog named Perseus. Upon returning home in triumph after being named general against Perseus, Aemilius found his young daughter, Tertia, in tears because her dog, Perseus, had died. Aemilius interpreted this as a sign of good luck for his command.

In contrast, Alkibiades is recorded as having owned a dog of exceptional beauty and size, for which he paid seventy minae. In an act of provocation, Alkibiades cut off the dog's fine tail. When friends criticized this action, he explained that he wished the Athenians to gossip about the dog so they would not say "something worse" about him.

## Dogs in Sport and Folklore
The Swedish King Olaf utilized dogs and falcons for hunting. In one instance, his falcons killed five black-cocks in two flights, and his dogs retrieved the birds from the ground. This success was noted by his followers, who believed no king had better luck in hunting.

In the realm of popular superstition, one account describes a step-mother who, following the reappearance of a figure in her old home, trembles and becomes kind to children whenever dogs howl in the night.
