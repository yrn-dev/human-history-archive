# Herod's Procuratorship of Syria and the Conflict with Malichus

## Appointment as Procurator
During the civil war between Cassius and Brutus on one side, and Antony and the younger Caesar (Augustus) on the other, Cassius and Marcus assembled an army in Syria. Because Herod was expected to be of significant assistance in providing necessary supplies, Cassius and Marcus appointed him procurator of all Syria. In this capacity, Herod was entrusted with the entire care of the army, serving as the general of the forces of Celesyria. He was provided with a fleet of ships as well as armies of horse and foot soldiers. As an incentive for his service, Cassius promised Herod that he would be made king of Judea once the war had concluded.

## The Rivalry Between Antipater and Malichus
Prior to Herod's appointment, a volatile relationship existed between Antipater and Malichus. After Cassius had departed from Judea, Malichus began plotting against Antipater, viewing him as an obstacle to his own wicked practices and believing that Antipater's death would secure the government of Hyrcanus. Antipater, fearing Malichus's cunning and power, retreated beyond the Jordan River to gather an army consisting of his own countrymen and Arabs for protection.

Malichus attempted to deceive Antipater's sons, Phasaelus—the guardian of Jerusalem—and Herod, who was in charge of the weapons of war. Through oaths and numerous excuses, Malichus persuaded the sons to reconcile him with their father. Antipater eventually forgave Malichus and even intervened on his behalf with Marcus, the president of Syria, dissuading Marcus from killing Malichus despite his attempts at innovation and the disturbances he caused in Judea.

## The Assassination of Antipater
Despite the reconciliation, Malichus remained afraid of Antipater and the rising power and hopes of Antipater's son. Malichus conspired to remove Antipater by offering money to the butler (or cup-bearer) of Hyrcanus. During a feast where both were present, the butler administered a poisoned potion to Antipater, resulting in his death. Antipater is described as a man of piety, justice, and love for his country who had previously recovered and preserved the government for Hyrcanus.

Following the murder, Malichus used armed men to settle the affairs of the city. When Herod and Phasaelus confronted him about the conspiracy, Malichus denied all knowledge of the crime.

## The Reaction of Herod and Phasaelus
The two sons of Antipater differed in their immediate response to the murder. Herod wished to seek immediate revenge by marching upon Malichus with an army. However, Phasaelus advised a more political approach to avoid the appearance of starting a civil war. Phasaelus pretended to believe Malichus's denials and instead erected a fine monument for their father.

During this period of dissimulation, Herod visited Samaria to compose differences and revive the spirits of those in distress. Later, when Herod entered the city with his soldiers for a festival, Malichus became affrighted and persuaded Hyrcanus to bar Herod's entry under the pretense that strangers should not enter while the multitude were purifying themselves. Herod ignored these messengers and entered the city at night. Despite his suspicions, Herod continued to maintain a facade of friendship toward Malichus, even loudly bewailling the death of Antipater to avoid alerting Malichus to his true intentions.

## The Death of Malichus
Herod eventually informed Cassius of his father's murder. Recognizing Malichus's poor morals, Cassius authorized Herod to seek revenge and privately ordered the commanders of his army at Tyre to assist him. 

While Cassius was at Laodicea, Herod planned a way to punish Malichus. Because his own son was a hostage at Tyre, Herod visited the city, intending to secretly remove the boy and march into Judea. However, Herod used a servant to deceive the army commanders, pretending the servant was preparing a supper for them. In reality, the servant persuaded the commanders to attack Malichus with their daggers. The commanders met Malichus on the seashore near the city and stabbed him to death.

When Hyrcanus learned that the killing was done by the command of Cassius, he commended the action, describing Malichus as a wicked man who had conspired against his own country.

## Subsequent Disturbances in Judea
Following the departure of Cassius from Syria, further sedition broke out in Jerusalem. A man named Felix, who had been left with an army, assaulted Phasaelus in an attempt to avenge the death of Malichus by attacking Herod's brother. Herod, who was with Fabius (the governor or prefect of Damascus), attempted to assist his brother but was delayed by a sickness. 

Phasaelus managed to defeat Felix on his own and shut him in a tower before dismissing him on certain conditions. During this conflict, Phasaelus reproached Hyrcanus for his ingratitude, noting that Hyrcanus had supported their enemies and overlooked the actions of Malichus's brother, who had seized several fortresses, including the strongest of all, Masada. Once recovered from his illness, Herod took all the places Felix had captured and also dismissed him under specific conditions.
