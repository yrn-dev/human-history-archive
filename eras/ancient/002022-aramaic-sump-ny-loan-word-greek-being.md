# Sump[=o]ny[=a]

The term *sump[=o]ny[=a]* appears in the Book of Daniel (iii. 5 and 15) as one of the names of musical instruments. While it has generally been rendered as "dulcimer," many scholars believe this translation is incorrect and that the word actually signifies a kind of bag-pipe.

## Etymological Theories
The theory that *sump[=o]ny[=a]* refers to a bag-pipe is based on the supposition that the word is a loan-word from the Greek, specifically a mispronunciation of *sumphonia*. However, this argument is considered exceedingly weak for several reasons:

*   **Dating and Interpolation:** The date of the Book of Daniel is a matter of controversy, and this controversy hinges partly on the derivation and significance of *sump[=o]ny[=a]*. Furthermore, it is possible that the word is a late interpolation.
*   **Alternative Derivations:** The exact form of the word is uncertain. In verse 10, the term *sipp[=o]ny[=a]* is used to describe the same instrument, which suggests the word may instead be derived from the Greek *siphon*, meaning a pipe or tube.
*   **Greek Usage:** Even if *sumphonia* is the source, there is little evidence it was used to describe a specific instrument. Its original natural sense in Greek is "a concordant interval" or "concord of sound." Evidence of its use for a particular instrument only appears in the 2nd century B.C., and even then, the evidence is very slight. One relevant passage in Polyb. xxvi. 10. 5 exists, but the translation depends on a context with an uncertain reading.

## Historical and Cultural Context
Despite the weaknesses in the Greek loan-word argument, there are curious linguistic parallels in later European history. In Spain and Italy—the two countries through which Eastern culture entered Europe—the bag-pipe was known by the names *sampogna* or *zampogna*. These terms strongly recall the Chaldaean *sump[=o]ny[=a]*. In these same countries, the word *sinfonia* coexisted with *zampogna*, retaining the classical Greek meaning of "a concord of sound."

Regarding the history of the instrument, a passage in Dion Chrysostom proves that the bag-pipe was known in Greece by A.D. 100. It is believed the Greeks received a type of bag-pipe from either Chaldaea or Egypt (where it was known as the *as-it*). In Greece, however, it remained a rustic instrument utilized only by peasants and shepherds.
