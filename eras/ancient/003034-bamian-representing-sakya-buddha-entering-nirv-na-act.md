# Bamian and the Recumbent Figure of Sakya Buddha

## The Recumbent Colossus of Bamian
Located approximately two miles east of Bamian was a massive recumbent figure representing Sakya Buddha entering *Nirvāna*, which is described as the act of death. This colossus was reported to be about 1,000 feet in length. According to historical accounts, this image may have been the largest of its kind, surpassing other known recumbent figures, such as a 69-foot statue in Burma or a 15-foot image known as "Bhim’s Baby" in the caves at Dhamnár in Málwa.

Despite its immense scale, modern travelers have not alluded to traces of the figure. It is suggested that the statue lacked durability because it was likely constructed from rubble and finished with plaster, a method similar to that used for *Nirvāna* figures found in Indo-China.

## Symbolism of the Recumbent Posture
The posture of a lying or recumbent Buddha is a common feature in Buddhist countries, including Ceylon, Burma, and Siam. These images symbolize Sakya Buddha entering *Nirvāna*. A prototype for such figures was observed by Hiuen Tsang in the 7th century in a Vihara located close to the Sál Grove at Kusinágara, the site where Sakya died and entered that state. While the stature of Buddha is said to have been 12 cubits, the creation of colossal images is viewed as a rude metaphor for the fact that gods such as Indra and Brahma tried in vain to compute his actual dimensions.

## History of Bamian
The city of Bamian possesses a very obscure history and cannot be identified with any city in classical geography, and it was certainly not *Alexandria ad Caucasum*. The first known mention of the city was made by Hsuan-Tsang. At that time, Bamian served as the head of one of the small states that emerged after the breakup of the empire of the White Huns.

Later, for a period of half a century ending in A.D. 1214, Bamian was the seat of a branch of the Ghori dynasty, which ruled over the basin of the Upper Oxus, also known as Tokharistan. In 1222, the city was besieged and annihilated by Jenghiz Khan. This destruction was prompted by the death of a favorite grandson of the Khan, who was killed by an arrow fired from the city walls. Although there is no further record of Bamian as a city, traditions and the nature of ruins at Ghulgulah suggest the city was rebuilt after the Mongol period before perishing again. In 1840, during the British occupation of Kabul, the site was the scene of a military action where Colonel William H. Dennie and a small force routed Dost Mahommed Khan and several Uzbeg chiefs.

## Concepts of Nirvāna
In the teachings of the Buddha, *Nirvāna* is presented as a state of rest attained through a change of heart. It is described as a "calm state of universal kindliness" and a "never-dying nectar" that allows those who reap it to destroy the "weeds of sorrow." The Buddha taught that the path to this state involves conquering the pride, anger, and lust of the heart to become free from doubt, ignorance, and wrong belief.

The Buddha described his own journey toward this state, noting that from the age of twenty-nine he strived for pure and perfect wisdom. He taught that while many paths are discussed by teachers, there is only one way to true wisdom: the path laid down in his system. This process of spiritual cultivation is metaphorically compared to farming, where faith serves as the seed, modesty as the guiding-shaft of the plough, and self-combat as the fertilizing rain.
