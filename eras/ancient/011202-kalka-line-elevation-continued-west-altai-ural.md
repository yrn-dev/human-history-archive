# Kalka

## Geography and Topography
Kalka is a region associated with the drainage systems of north-west Mongolia and Dzungaria. It is separated from the waters of the great rivers of western Siberia by the Altai mountains, which are located near the 50th parallel and reach altitudes of 10,000 to 12,000 feet.

Extending west from the Altai mountains toward the Ural Mountains is a continued line of elevation. This geographical feature does not reach considerable altitudes, but it serves as a hydrographic divide, separating the drainage of south-west Siberia from the great plains situated to the north east of the Aral Sea.

## Regional Context and Surroundings
The broader region of Northern Asia is characterized by a continental level that rises from the low flats of Siberia toward a great central water-parting. This divide stretches from the Black Sea through the Hindu Kush and Elburz to the Tian-shan mountains in the Pamir region, eventually reaching the Bering Strait. While some parts of this divide, such as the Altai, reach great heights, other portions remain below 5,000 feet.

To the south of this divide lies the central depression of the Gobi, a vast, almost waterless interior space where drainage is lost in swamps or deserts. The Gobi is bounded to the north by the Tian-shan (Celestial Mountains), which lie between 40 and 43 degrees North and 75 and 95 degrees East, with some summits exceeding 20,000 feet.

## Human and Political History
The steppes located between the Ural mountains and the Caspian Sea, extending toward the Altai mountains, were traversed by Grecian traders attracted by the rich gold deposits found in both the Ural and Altai regions. These areas were inhabited by various nomadic nations and tribes, including the Budini, the Thyssagetæ, the Jurkæ, and groups of Scythians. The Issêdones were the easternmost people known to the Greeks before the region transitioned into the realm of fable, involving creatures such as the gold-guarding Griffins and the one-eyed Arimaspians.

By the first century A.D., nomadic Mongolian peoples appeared on the eastern boundaries of Europe, mixing with Aryan nomads from the Caspian-Pamir region. Hunnish peoples were established between the Urals and the Caspian Sea, while the Alans—likely a Mongolian people with Aryan elements—were situated to their west.

In later periods, the region was associated with Mongol political structures. Records indicate that the Khans of the Kalkas, along with other Mongol dignitaries, provided annual tributes of white horses and white camels to the Kaan.
