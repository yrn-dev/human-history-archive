# Gospel of Barnabas

The Gospel of Barnabas is a work that appears in different forms across historical and apocryphal literature. References to an apocryphal Gospel of Barnabas exist in antiquity, such as in the *Decretum Gelasii*, though the contents of that specific ancient version are unknown.

## The Italian Manuscript
A longish gospel bearing this title exists in a single Italian manuscript. This version is written from a Mahommedan standpoint, although it likely incorporates materials of Gnostic origin and character. The manuscript was discovered in a private collection in Amsterdam by the Deist John Toland, who detailed it in his 1718 work *Nazarenus*. Following its discovery, the text passed to Prince Eugene of Savoy before being acquired by the imperial library at Vienna.

The text was edited and translated into English in 1907 by Laura and (Rev.) Lonsdale Ragg. These editors propose that the work was produced between the 13th and 16th centuries by a Christian renegade to Mahommedanism. While Toland and others previously believed the Italian text was a translation from Arabic, this view is considered demonstrably wrong. The manuscript contains Arabic marginal notes, which appear to be a combination of notes intended to assist Arabic students and pious ejaculations.

The content of the Italian manuscript is described as being highly imaginative and frequently grotesque, yet it is characterized by an unusually high level of ethical enthusiasm.

## Related Figures and Literature
The name Barnabas is associated with several other historical and literary contexts in the archive:

**The New Testament Barnabas**
In the New Testament, Barnabas is the surname given by the apostles to Joseph, a Levite born in Cyprus. He is described as a "good man" known for his goodness of heart, fineness of perception, and insight into essentials. He functioned as a prophet and an apostle, though he was not one of the Twelve. He played a mediating role between Pauline and Jewish Christianity and was instrumental in introducing Saul (Paul) to the older apostles.

**The Epistle of Barnabas**
Distinct from the Gospel, the *Epistle of Barnabas* is categorized as Sub-Apostolic literature. It is considered more eccentric than other writings of the Apostolic Fathers. Tradition has sometimes attributed this epistle to the Barnabas of Alexandria.

**The Acts of Barnabas**
The apocryphal *Acta Barnabae* (Acts of Barnabas) purports to have been written by Mark. In this text, Mark describes himself as having been baptized at Iconium and previously serving as a servant to Cyrillus, the high priest of Zeus.
