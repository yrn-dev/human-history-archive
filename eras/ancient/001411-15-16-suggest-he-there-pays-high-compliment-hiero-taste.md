# Hiero I of Syracuse

Hiero I was the brother and successor of Gelo as the ruler of Syracuse. His reign was characterized by a level of power that was not inferior to that of Gelo and was likely the greatest in the Grecian world and Sicily.

## Political Power and Territorial Expansion
Hiero's influence extended beyond the borders of Sicily. When the city of Cumæ on the coast of Italy was harassed by Tyrrhenian and Carthaginian fleets, Hiero provided a squadron that defeated and drove off the enemies. Following this, he established a Syracusan colony on the neighboring island of Pithekusa.

Hiero also exerted influence over other Sicilian and Italian leaders. When Anaxilaus, the despot of Messênê and Rhegium, attacked the Epizephyrian Lokrians, Hiero intervened. Through the envoy Chromius, Hiero issued menaces that compelled Anaxilaus to desist from his attack.

Hiero sought the heroic honors associated with the œkist (founder) of a new city. To achieve this, he founded the city of Ætna on the site of Katana. To establish this city, Hiero expelled the inhabitants of both Naxos and Katana, directing them to reside at Leontini. He populated Ætna with ten thousand new inhabitants: five thousand from Gela and Syracuse, and five thousand from the Peloponnesus. This population served as an auxiliary force to protect against potential discontents in Syracuse. Hiero further expanded the territory of Ætna at the expense of neighboring Sikel tribes. The city's social and religious customs were assimilated to the Dorian model, and joint administration of the city was handled by Hiero's friend and confidant, Chromius, and his son, Deinomenês.

## Relations and Character
Hiero's early rule was marked by conflict; he was described as harsh as a ruler and was involved in a quarrel with Thêro of Akragas and was jealous of his brother, Polyzêlus. However, he eventually reconciled with both.

His character is viewed differently across various sources. Pindar paid him direct compliments, describing him as a "father" to strangers and as one who was not envious of the good. Conversely, other interpretations of Pindar's work suggest that indirect hints and admonitions reveal Hiero's real character. Additionally, Hiero's constitutional position was described as ill-defined, which may have made him more exigent regarding the deference shown to him.

Hiero's status was such that he was sought after for political alliances. Themistokles, while fleeing from the Athenians and Lacedaemonians, sailed to Sicily to demand Hiero's daughter in marriage, promising that he would make all of Greece obey him. Hiero rejected these proposals.

## Patronage of the Arts
Hiero was a patron to several prominent poets, including Pindar, Simonides, and Bacchylides, who visited his court around the same time. Hiero commissioned odes in his honor from both Pindar and Bacchylides to celebrate his victories in 476 and 470 B.C. In 468 B.C., during the most important occasion, Bacchylides alone was commissioned for this task, although Pindar composed an ode for a different Syracusan victor at that same festival.

There was a tradition, supported by a scholium on *Pyth.* ii. 90, that Hiero preferred the odes of Bacchylides over those of Pindar. In his third Ode (468 B.C.), Bacchylides paid a high compliment to Hiero's taste in poetry. Some Alexandrian scholars suggested that Pindar viewed Bacchylides as a jealous rival who disparaged him to Hiero, and believed Bacchylides' skill resulted from study rather than genius.

## Public Recognition
Hiero and Chromius were both proclaimed as Ætnæans at the Nemean and Pythian games after their chariots achieved victories. These events served as the first occasion where the assembled crowds learned of the existence of the new Hellenic city of Ætna.
