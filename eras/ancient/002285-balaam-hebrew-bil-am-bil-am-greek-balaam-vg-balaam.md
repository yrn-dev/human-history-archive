# BALAAM

Balaam (Hebrew: *Bil'am*; Greek: *Balaam*) was a Gentile seer and prophet who appears in the biblical history of the Israelites during their sojourn in the plains of Moab, east of the Jordan. His narrative occurs at the end of the Forty Years' wandering, shortly before the crossing of the Jordan and the death of Moses.

## Narrative and Interaction with Balak
The primary account of Balaam is found in Numbers xxii-xxv. The story begins when Balak, the king of Moab, becomes alarmed after the Israelites conquer two kings of eastern Palestine: Og, king of Bashan, and Sihon, king of the Amorites. In response, Balak sends for Balaam to curse the Israelites.

Balaam's response is marked by hesitation. In one account, God appears to him in a dream and forbids him from going. However, after Balak sends princes to put further pressure on him, God permits him to go on the condition that he speaks only what God tells him. In another version of the narrative, the Midianites send ambassadors to Balaam; though God is initially displeased, he eventually instructs Balaam not to contradict the ambassadors.

## The Episode of the Speaking Ass
While traveling to meet Balak, Balaam is encountered by the Angel of Yahweh. Initially, the Angel is visible only to Balaam's ass, which attempts to avoid the Angel, prompting Balaam to strike the animal. By the will of God, the ass is miraculously enabled to speak with the voice of a man, complaining that Balaam is acting unjustly toward her. 

Once Yahweh enables Balaam to see the Angel, the Angel informs him that he would have slain Balaam had it not been for the ass. Although Balaam offers to return home, he is told to proceed with his journey, provided he declares only what is suggested to his mind.

## Oracles and Blessings
Upon meeting Balak, Balaam warns the king that he can only speak the words provided by God. Despite Balak's offers of sacrifice and attempts to secure a curse by moving Balaam to different locations—including the top of Pisgah to view the Israelites—Balaam is compelled by divine inspiration to bless the Israelites instead. 

The biblical narrative serves as a framework for seven oracular poems. The first four are of significant length, while the final three are brief. After these events, and following further remonstrances from Balak, Balaam departs.

## Later Actions and Death
According to the Priestly Code, Balaam later advises the Midianites on how to bring disaster upon the Israelites. He suggests that because the providence of God prevents the total destruction of the Hebrews through war, plague, or scarcity, the Midianites should instead use seduction. He instructs Balak and the Midianites to send their most beautiful daughters to the Israelite camp to entice the young men to abandon their laws and the worship of their God in favor of the gods of the Midianites. Following this counsel, Balaam goes his way. He is eventually slain in battle while fighting in the ranks of Midian.

## Interpretations and Traditions
Balaam has been a subject of various interpretations across different traditions:

*   **Scholarly Analysis:** Critics suggest the narratives in Numbers were compiled from two ancient documents (J and E) between 900 and 700 B.C. In these sources, Balaam's origin varies; he is described as an Edomite or Ammonite in J, and as an Aramaean or a native of southern Judah in E. The Priestly Code identifies him as a Midianite.
*   **Religious Traditions:** 
    *   **Jewish Traditions:** Rabbinical sources (Yalkut) have identified Balaam with Laban, the father-in-law of Jacob. Other traditions represent him as a typical sinner, with fables claiming he was blind in one eye or served as a governor in Ethiopia under Pharaoh. Josephus describes him as the best prophet of his time but one with a disposition unable to resist temptation. Philo characterizes him as a great magician or a "sophist" symbolizing warring opinions.
    *   **Islamic Traditions:** Some accounts describe Balaam as one of the Anakim (giants of Palestine) who read the books of Abraham to learn the name Yahweh, which allowed him to predict the future. It has been conjectured that the Arabic wise man Luqman is identical to Balaam, noting the similarity in their fathers' names and a popular etymology linking "Luqman" (meaning devourer or swallower) to Balaam.

## Significance
The narratives are noted for the recognition of the genuine inspiration of a non-Hebrew prophet, suggesting that Yahweh revealed his will to nations other than Israel. In a comparative sense, Balaam is viewed as a companion figure to Jonah; where Jonah ran away from his calling, Balaam sought to go where he was not originally sent.
