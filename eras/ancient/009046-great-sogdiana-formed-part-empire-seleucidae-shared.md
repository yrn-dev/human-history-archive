# Sogdiana

Sogdiana, known to the ancients as the region associated with Bokhara, was a significant district of central Asia. It is situated in the north, beyond the Oxus river, and was watered by both the Oxus and the Jaxartes. In the early middle ages, the region was also referred to as Sughd, or by the classical title Transoxiana (meaning *Ma wara 'l Nahr* or *Ma-vera-un-nahr*).

## Early History and Hellenistic Period
The foundation of the capital of Sogdiana is attributed to the great Persian hero Efrasiab. During the conquests of Alexander the Great, Sogdiana was the location where Alexander encountered strong resistance, in contrast to the easier conquest of Bactria. Following these conquests, Sogdiana became part of the empire of the Seleucidae, sharing its fortunes with the better-known region of Bactria. Within the Macedonian empire's structure, Bactria and Sogdiana were grouped together as one of the 22 provinces.

Under the rule of Seleucus, king of Asia, and his son Antiochus I, many Greek towns were established in eastern Iran, leading to a period where the Greek language was dominant. However, around 255 B.C., Diodotus, the satrap of Bactria, took advantage of the attacks of Ptolemy II and the difficulties facing the Seleucid kings to establish his independence and conquer Sogdiana. This act founded the Graeco-Bactrian kingdom.

## Transition to Nomad and Indo-Scythian Rule
The Greek dominance in the region was eventually undermined by internal dissensions and frequent usurpations among the kings. According to Chinese sources, Mongolian tribes known to the Greeks as Scythians—specifically the Tochari, who are identical to the Yue-chi—entered Sogdiana in 159 B.C. They subsequently conquered Bactria in 139 B.C., ending Greek rule in eastern Iran.

The Yue-chi gradually transitioned from nomads to a settled territorial power in Sogdiana and Bactria. By approximately A.D. 45, the dominions of their king, Kadphises I, extended from Bokhara to the Indus. This era evolved into the "Indo-Scythian" empire, ruled by the Kushan (or Kushana) dynasty. While the Kushans' principal seat was in Bactria, they maintained control over western India and eastern Iran until the dynasty began to decay in the 3rd century.

## Persian and Islamic Eras
Sogdiana was reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty. During this period, specifically by the 6th century, Nestorian Christians were present in Samarkand. Following the Arab conquest of Persia between 640 and 642, Islam was introduced to the region and quickly became the dominant faith.

Under early Mahommedan rule, Sogdiana became one of the most flourishing and celebrated districts of central Asia. It was characterized by two great cities: Samarkand and Bokhara. Samarkand generally served as the seat of government, while Bokhara earned a high reputation as a center of learning and religion.

## Later Conquests and Mongol Rule
At the end of the 11th century, Malik Shah, the third ruler of the Persian Seljuk dynasty, crossed the Oxus and subdued the lands watered by the Jaxartes and Oxus rivers. In 1216, Bokhara was subdued by Mahommed Shah Khwarizm, though this conquest was seized by Jenghiz Khan in 1220. While Jenghiz Khan's invasion wasted the country, prosperity partially returned under his son, Ogdai Khan, who is described as benevolent and humane.

The descendants of Ogdai Khan held possession until 1369, when Timur (Tamerlane) conquered the region and established his capital at Samarkand. Additionally, the House of Chaghatai, settled in the valley of the Jaxartes and the pastures of the Ili, ruled the wealthy cities of Sogdiana.
