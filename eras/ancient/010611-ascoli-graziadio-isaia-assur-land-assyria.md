# ASCOLI, GRAZIADIO ISAIA AND ASSUR

## Graziadio Isaia Ascoli
Graziadio Isaia Ascoli (1820–1907) was an Italian philologist born in Gorz to a family of Jewish descent. Demonstrating a strong linguistic talent from a young age, he published his *Studii orientali e linguistici* in 1854. By 1860, he had been appointed as a professor of philology in Milan.

Ascoli's scholarly work included contributions to the study of Semitic and Indo-European languages, as well as the gipsy language. However, he specialized primarily in Italian dialects. In 1873, he founded the *Archivio glottologico italiano*, where he published his *Saggi Ladini* and established the publication as a primary organ for original scholarship on the subject. He was widely regarded as the foremost authority on Italian linguistics, and his contribution to the 9th edition of the *Encyclopaedia Britannica* (revised for the 11th) is considered the classic English exposition on the topic.

## Assur: The Land and City
The term Assur is a Hebrew name appearing frequently in the Old Testament to refer to the dominion and land of Assyria. In Assyro-Babylonian literature, this region is known as *mat Assur* ("land of Assur"). The land derived its name from the ancient city of Assur, located at the southern extremity of Assyria proper. The territory of this early settlement was bounded by the lower Zab river to the south and the Zagros mountain range (in present-day Kurdistan) to the north.

The kingdom of Assyria grew from this primitive settlement through a gradual process of colonization within the rich vales of the middle Tigris region, an area watered by the Tigris and several tributaries, most notably the lower Zab. Some sources suggest the land was named after Assur, the son of Shem, who settled there with his family before being subjected to or driven out by the usurper Nimrod.

The city of Assur served as the primitive capital of Assyria and is currently represented by the mounds of Kaleh Sherghat (Qal'at Shergat) on the west bank of the Tigris. The city grew around a great temple whose foundation is attributed to the High-priest Uspia. For several centuries, the city and its surrounding district were governed by high-priests under Babylonian suzerainty. As Babylonian power declined, these high-priests became independent kings.

The city's defenses included a wall of crude brick on stone foundations, reinforced with burnt brick. Around 1270 B.C., Tukulti-Masu (or Tukulti-Inaristi) dug a deep moat outside the walls, and the city was further protected by an outwork known as a *salkhu*. In the 15th century B.C., the city expanded south to incorporate a "new town." The city featured several gates, including the "gate of the Tigris," the "gate of Assur," and the "gate of the Sun-god," as well as a quay made of limestone and burnt brick cemented with bitumen.

## The God Assur
Assur (also written Asur or Ashur) was the chief god of Assyria and the patron deity of the capital city. There is scholarly debate regarding whether the god took his name from the city or vice versa; however, it is probable that the god's name is older, or that the city itself was deified. An older form of the name, A-shir, means "leader."

The Assyrian pantheon was largely identical to that of the south, with the primary difference being the substitution of Assur for Marduk. Because of the warlike nature of the Assyrian people, their conception of the divine reflected this, viewing their gods as "little Assurs" alongside the "big Assur," the great protector of arms. Rituals, hymns, and myths from Babylonia were transferred to northern cities such as Nineveh, Arbela, Harran, Calah, and Assur, though they underwent some modifications.

## History and Character of Assyria
Assyria is described as an "organized camp," contrasting with Babylonia, which was a land of agriculturists and merchants. While Babylonian dynasties were often raised by priests, Assyrian dynasties were founded by successful generals. The Assyrian king functioned as an autocratic general of an army, supported by a feudal nobility and, from the time of Tiglath-pileser III, an elaborate bureaucracy. The royal palace was more sumptuous than the temples of the gods and remained separate from them. The population consisted primarily of soldiers.

The monarchy's foundation is attributed to Zulilu, who lived after Bel-kapkapi (1900 B.C.). Early rulers included Samsi-Hadad, who appears to have been a vassal-prince under Khammurabi. Assyria's power grew at the expense of Babylonia; for example, Assur-yuballidh of Assyria married his daughter to the Kassite king of Babylonia. Following the murder of the king by a Kassite faction, Assur-yuballidh intervened to place Burna-buryas on the throne.

By 1300 B.C., Shalmaneser I openly claimed supremacy in western Asia, founding Calah and settling colonists in Cappadocia. His son, Tukulti-In-aristi, eventually conquered Babylon and killed King Bitilyasu, establishing Assyria as the dominant power in the oriental world.

The empire began to break up following the death of Assur-bani-pal. Under his successor, Assur-etil-ilani, Scythians penetrated Assyria and burned Calah. The last king of Assyria was likely Sin-sar-iskun (possibly the Sarakos of Berossus). The empire finally ended when Nabopolassar of Babylonia and Cyaxares, the Scythian king of Ecbatana, captured and destroyed Nineveh.
