# The Nineveh Hypothesis: Relocating the Hanging Gardens

While traditional historical narratives place the Hanging Gardens within the walls of Babylon, a growing body of scholarly evidence suggests we may be looking for them in the wrong city. For centuries, the Gardens were attributed to King Nebuchadnezzar II, who allegedly built them to soothe his homesick wife, Amytis of Media. However, the silence of Babylonian records regarding such a feat has led modern historians to propose a provocative alternative: the Gardens were actually located in Nineveh, the capital of the Assyrian Empire.

This shift in perspective moves the Gardens from a romanticized Babylonian myth to a tangible piece of Assyrian engineering. By examining the records of the Assyrian King Sennacherib, historians are beginning to reconstruct a version of the gardens that aligns more closely with archaeological reality than legendary prose.

## The Silence of Babylon vs. the Boasts of Nineveh
The primary argument for the "Nineveh Hypothesis" is the glaring absence of the gardens in the native texts of Babylon. Nebuchadnezzar II left behind extensive records of his building projects—walls, temples, and palaces—yet he never mentions a tiered garden. In contrast, the records of the Assyrian King Sennacherib (who ruled from 705 to 681 BCE) describe a massive botanical project in Nineveh.

Sennacherib’s inscriptions detail the creation of a "palace without parallel," featuring a sophisticated system of canals and aqueducts that brought water from the mountains to the city. He specifically describes a garden that mirrored the mountains of Lebanon, utilizing advanced irrigation to sustain exotic flora in an arid climate.

## Engineering the Impossible
If the gardens were indeed at Nineveh, the "hanging" aspect becomes an engineering reality rather than a poetic exaggeration. Archaeological surveys have revealed the remains of a massive stone aqueduct—the Jerwan Aqueduct—which predates Roman engineering by centuries. 

This system allowed for the lifting of water to high terraces through a method likely involving Archimedes' screws or similar hoisting mechanisms. By transporting water upward, Sennacherib could create a lush, elevated forest that would appear to "hang" over the city walls, fulfilling the descriptions provided by later Greek historians who may have simply confused the two great Mesopotamian capitals.

## Key Facts
* **Traditional Attribution:** Historically credited to Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon (c. 605–562 BCE).
* **Alternative Theory:** Proposed by Dr. Stephanie Dalley, suggesting they were built by Sennacherib of Assyria (c. 705–681 BCE).
* **Location Shift:** Moves the site from Babylon (near modern Hillah) to Nineveh (near modern Mosul).
* **Technological Feat:** Required advanced hydraulic engineering to move water against gravity.
* **Scholarly Debate:** Some historians still argue the gardens were a "literary composite," a poetic ideal of the East rather than a single physical location.

## Did You Know?
* **The Missing Link:** No contemporary Babylonian archaeologist has ever found a physical trace of the gardens within Babylon's ruins.
* **Botanical Diversity:** The gardens were intended to house plants from all over the known world, serving as an ancient botanical garden.
* **Greek Influence:** Much of what we "know" about the gardens comes from Greek writers like Strabo and Diodorus Siculus, who wrote centuries after the gardens existed.
