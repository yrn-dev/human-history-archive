# The Cimmerians and the Scythian Invasions

The Cimmerians were a nomadic people who, during the seventh century B.C., occupied a vast territory between the Ister and the Palus Mæotis. They are described as the previous tenants of this region before being expelled by the Scythians. Some accounts suggest the Cimmerians may have been the northernmost portion of the Thracian people and were conterminous with the Getæ on the Danube.

## Expulsion from Europe
The displacement of the Cimmerians was the result of an invasion by a formidable army of Scythians originating from the neighborhood of the Palus Mæotis. When the Scythian host approached, the Cimmerians were divided on how to respond. While the majority of the population was dismayed and wished to evacuate their territory, the kings of the various tribes resolved to fight and perish in their homeland. These kings and their followers divided themselves into two equal bodies and fought a battle near the river Tyras, where they perished by each other's hands. The sepulchres of these kings remained visible during the time of Herodotus.

Following this conflict, the mass of the Cimmerians abandoned their country to the Scythians. The Scythian prince Madyês, son of Protothyês, pursued the fugitives across the Cimmerian Bosphorus from west to east.

## Migration and Settlement in Asia Minor
The Cimmerians escaped their pursuers by coasting along the east of the Euxine sea and passing to the west of Mount Caucasus. They first entered Kolchis and subsequently moved into Asia Minor. There, they established themselves on the northern coast peninsula near the site that would later become the Grecian city of Sinôpê.

The Scythian pursuers, however, mistook the route of the fugitives. Instead of following them into Asia Minor, the Scythians took a more circuitous route east of Mount Caucasus near the Caspian sea, which led them into Media. Consequently, both Media and Asia Minor were subjected to the ravages of northern nomads nearly simultaneously. Some historians, such as Niebuhr, have questioned this narrative, suggesting instead that the Cimmerians may have entered Asia Minor via the western side of the Euxine and across the Thracian Bosphorus.

## Conflict with Established Powers
The presence of the Cimmerians in Asia Minor created significant instability for the regional powers. Gyges of Lydia sought aid from Assyria to defend against his Cimmerian enemies, though these solicitations were unsuccessful. 

The broader movement of these tribes—the double invasion of Scythians and Cimmerians—laid waste to Asia between 630 and 610 B.C. While the Cimmerians infested Asia Minor, the Scythians (known to the Babylonians as the Manda) harassed the frontiers of an exhausted Assyria. The Assyrian Empire, drained of wealth and fighting population by struggles with Elam and Babylonia, was ill-prepared for these hordes.

## The Scythian Leader Dugdamm
During the period of Scythian harassment of the Assyrian frontiers, a Scythian leader named Dugdamm (referred to by Strabo as Lygdamis) emerged. The death of Dugdamm was viewed by the Assyrian ruler Assur-bani-pal as a special mark of divine favour.

## Origins of the Migration
Historical accounts differ regarding the cause of the Scythian propulsion that drove the Cimmerians from their lands. One tradition, found in the lost epic poem *Arimaspia* by Aristeas of Prokonnêsus, suggests the movement began in the extreme north when the Grypes (or Griffins) invaded the Arimaspians, creating a chain of pressure that eventually reached the Cimmerians. Another account, favored by Herodotus, suggests that the Scythians were originally occupants of Asia or regions east of the Caspian and were driven across the Araxês after an unsuccessful war with the Massagetæ, which precipitated their invasion of Cimmerian Europe.

## Legacy and Related Tribes
The Tauri, who dwelt in the southern portion of the Crimea (Tauric Chersonesus) and practiced human sacrifice to a virgin goddess, are sometimes identified as remnants of the expelled Cimmerians. Additionally, the Massagetæ of the Caspian plains were considered by some contemporaries of Herodotus to be of the same race as the Scythians.
