# From the End of the Persian to the Beginning of the Peloponnesian War

The period between the conclusion of the Persian Wars and the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War is characterized by the transition of Athens from a state of supremacy to the establishment of an empire. This era saw the shift of Greek power dynamics, as Athens evolved from an ordinary member of the Hellenic confederacy into a dominant force that eventually provoked the fear of the Lacedaemonians.

## The Aftermath of the Persian Wars
During the general Hellenic confederacy that fought against Persia under Spartan presidency, Athens initially held no ostensible rank above that of an ordinary member. Although it was adjudged the second dignity in the line at Plataea, this was only after a contending claim from Tegea. Despite this official ranking, Athens emerged from the struggle as a power transformed in the eyes of Greece. 

Athens had suffered more than its allies and contributed more at sea than all other allies combined. On land, at the battle of Plataea, Athenian hoplites demonstrated discipline, bravery, and efficiency against Persian cavalry that was superior even to that of the Spartans. Furthermore, while the Peloponnesians returned home immediately after the victory of Mykale, Athenian forces continued prolonged service to clear the Hellespont, acting as champions for the Asiatic Greeks.

The leadership of this era was marked by the talents of two Athenians: Themistoklês and Aristeidês. The Peloponnesians were viewed as having requited Athenian Pan-Hellenic patriotism ungenerously, having twice betrayed Attica to hostile ravage—once perhaps unavoidably, and a second time through culpable neglect when they postponed their march against Mardonius. Consequently, the Peloponnesians recognized that their own salvation at Salamis was due to the Athenian naval force and the dexterity of Themistoklês.

## The Rise of Athenian Naval Power and Hegemony
Directly following the repulse of the Persians, Athens began its intensive nautical training. Periklês later noted that the Peloponnesians could not easily acquire this maritime skill, as even the Athenians, who had studied it since the time of the Medes, had not yet fully mastered it.

Between 476 B.C. and 466 B.C., Athens undertook several key enterprises in its new capacity of hegemony. One of the first known actions was the conquest of Eion on the Strymon, where the Persian governor, Bogês, committed suicide with his family and effects after being starved out. Around 470 B.C., Athens acted against the Dryopes in Karystus, Eubœa, granting them a capitulation. They also targeted the Dolopes and Pelasgi in the island of Skyros. Following a complaint by Thessalian traders regarding piracy and cruelty, Kimon led the Athenian armament to conquer Skyros, expel its inhabitants, and replace them with Athenian settlers.

## The Path to the Peloponnesian War
The interval leading up to the Peloponnesian War was not one of undisturbed peace. For example, in the sixth year of the thirty years' truce, Athens faced a formidable revolt at Samos. There is also evidence of a project for a congress, possibly proposed by Periklês, though its date is debated; some place it before 460 B.C., while others suggest it occurred after the thirty years' truce but before the Peloponnesian War.

The tension between Athens and Sparta culminated when the Lacedaemonians voted that their treaty had been broken. The decision to declare war was driven less by the arguments of their allies and more by the Lacedaemonians' fear of the growth of Athenian power, as they observed that most of Hellas had already become subject to Athens.

## Persian Influence and the Later Conflict
The broader Persian history during this era spanned 117 years under six kings: Darius I, Xerxes I, Artaxerxes Longimanus, Xerxes II, Sogdianus, and Darius Nothus. The first ninety years of this period, ending in the forty-second year of Artaxerxes, coincided with the start of the Peloponnesian War.

During the conflict, Persian satraps maintained a strong interest in Greek affairs. The son of Darius Nothus, serving as satrap of Lydia, Phrygia the greater, and Kappadokia, arrived with an intense anti-Athenian sentiment. He recognized that for sixty years, Athens had been the efficient enemy that humbled the pride of Persian kings and emancipated continental Greeks on the coast. This Persian leadership sought to put down Athenian power, contrasting with the "treacherous balancing" of Tissaphernês and the enmity of Pharnabazus. Pharnabazus, who had supported the Peloponnesians for three years, eventually grew weary of his allies after finding his satrapy plundered and his revenues impaired by an all-powerful Athenian fleet in the Hellespont and Propontis.
