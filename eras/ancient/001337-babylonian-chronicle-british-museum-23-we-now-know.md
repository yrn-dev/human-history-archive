# Babylonian Chronicles and Records in the British Museum

The British Museum houses a vast collection of ancient records, including Babylonian chronicles, cuneiform tablets, and royal inscriptions. These materials have been instrumental in reconstructing the political chronology of ancient Mesopotamia and the relationship between the civilizations of Babylonia and Assyria.

## The Babylonian Chronicle and Dynastic History
A specific Babylonian chronicle held in the British Museum provides critical corrections to the "Kings' List." According to this text, Dynasty II of the Kings' List never held the throne of Babylon. Instead, these rulers governed only the extreme south of Babylonia, situated on the shores of the Persian Gulf. The chronicle clarifies that the kings of Dynasty II were contemporaries of both the earlier kings of Dynasty III and the later kings of Dynasty I.

The records further detail the fall of Dynasty I. During the reign of Samsu-ditana, the final king of this dynasty, Hittites from Cappadocia raided and captured Babylon. In its weakened state, the city was subsequently captured by the Kassites, who are identified as Dynasty III. The Kassites eventually captured southern Babylonia—which had been held by Dynasty II—and thereafter occupied the entirety of the Babylonian plain.

## Assyrian Connections and Chronology
The archive's records allow historians to trace the history of Assyria to a period preceding the rise of Babylon. The same Babylonian chronicle indicates that Su-abu, the founder of Dynasty I of the Kings' List, was a contemporary of Ilu-shuma, an early Assyrian patesi. 

Further linguistic and cultural analysis suggests that the Assyrian language is practically identical to the Babylonian language. The sources characterize the Assyrians and Babylonians as the same people, though they note the presence of some foreign admixtures.

## Persian Era Records
The British Museum's collection includes numerous Babylonian tablets from Nippur, which provide a chronological sequence for the Persian period. These tablets show that the dates of Darius II follow immediately after those of Artaxerxes I. This contradicts other accounts, as the reigns of Xerxes II and Secydianus do not appear in the Nippur tablets.

Other artifacts in the museum, such as the great Lycian stele from Xanthus, provide accounts of wars involving the Persian Empire. For instance, while the Athenian power was intact, Darius II did not interfere in Greek affairs; however, after Athenian power broke down before Syracuse in 413, Darius II ordered his satraps in Asia Minor, Pharnabazus and Tissaphernes, to wage war against Athens and collect overdue tributes.

## Archaeological Discoveries and Cuneiform Texts
The reconstruction of Babylonian and Assyrian life was greatly advanced by the discovery of the library of Assur-bani-pal by A. H. Layard. Subsequent expeditions by the British Museum (1877–1879) under Hormuzd Rassam led to further discoveries at Nineveh and its surroundings. 

At the mounds of Balawat (Imgur-Bel), Rassam discovered a palace restored by Shalmaneser II in 858 B.C., from which the British Museum acquired bronze gates featuring hammered reliefs. He also found a small temple dedicated to the god of dreams by Assur-nazir-pal III (883 B.C.), containing two rectangular alabaster inscribed tables within a stone coffer or ark.

The museum continues to publish series of cuneiform texts from Babylonian tablets. These include monographs and texts regarding Babylonian religion, mythology, magic, and sorcery, as well as reports from the astrologers and magicians of Babylon and Nineveh.

## Other Historical Manuscripts
Beyond ancient Mesopotamia, the British Museum preserves a wide array of historical autographs and religious texts. These include:
* **Medieval Manuscripts:** Documents in the hand of William of Wykeham and duplicates of charters attributed to Dunstan.
* **Biblical Texts:** The *Codex Alexandrinus*, a probable Northumbrian copy of the *Codex Amiatinus*, a 9th-century Hebrew Pentateuch, and various versions of the Bible ranging from the 13th-century Latin Bible to the first printed English Bible of 1535.
