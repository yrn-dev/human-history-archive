# Public Highways and Road Construction

The development of public highways has historically served as a primary means of communication, military movement, and trade across various civilizations and regions. From the structured networks of the Roman Empire to the specialized engineering of the 19th century, highways have evolved to connect large cities, facilitate the movement of armies, and integrate distant provinces.

## The Roman Highway System
In the Roman Empire, public highways formed a comprehensive chain of communication that connected all cities with the capital. Issuing from the Forum of Rome, these roads traversed Italy and extended through the provinces, terminating only at the empire's frontiers. One such great chain of communication, stretching from the wall of Antoninus in the north-west to Jerusalem in the south-east, measured 4,080 Roman miles.

The construction of these roads was characterized by a direct line between cities, often ignoring private property or natural obstacles. To achieve this, the Romans perforated mountains and constructed bold arches over rapid and broad streams. The roads were built with a raised middle terrace consisting of strata of cement, gravel, and sand, and were paved with large stones or granite near the capital. Mile-stones were used to accurately divide the distances.

While these highways allowed for easy intercourse between subjects of distant provinces, their primary purpose was to facilitate the marches of the legions; a country was not considered completely subdued until it was pervious to the authority and arms of the conqueror. To ensure the celerity of imperial orders and intelligence, a regular institution of posts was established. Houses were erected every five or six miles, each provided with forty horses, enabling travelers to cover one hundred miles in a day. While originally for public service, the use of these posts was occasionally extended to private citizens via Imperial mandate.

## Development in Asia and Europe
In Japan, ancient annals contain few references to road making, but the reign of Empress Gensho (715-723) is noted for the construction of the Nakasen-do, or Central Mountain road. This trunk highway runs from Nara to Kyoto and continues to modern Tokyo, traversing six provinces. It is probable that the project was commenced under Gensho and completed during the reign of Shomu (724-748) to facilitate the movement of troops against the rebellious Yemishi.

In Bulgaria, road communication has been described as unsatisfactory. The system is divided into three categories: "inter-village roads" maintained by communes, "district roads" maintained by district councils, and "state roads," which serve as the main highways maintained by the government. Maintenance and repairs on these roads are carried out through the *corvée* system using requisitions of material.

## Engineering and Innovations
The 19th century saw significant advancements in road-making science, most notably through the work of John Loudon McAdam (1756-1836). A Scottish inventor and road trustee, McAdam conducted experiments at Sauhrie and Falmouth to reform the poor condition of Great Britain's highways. His general conclusion was that roads should be constructed of broken stone. Following a parliamentary inquiry in 1823, his theories were adopted by public authorities, and he was appointed general surveyor of roads in 1827.

In other regions, the definition of a "highway" expanded to include rail and water. In India, before the arrival of railways, the Grand Trunk road and the Ganges were the only means of communication between the North-Western Provinces and Calcutta; however, the railroad subsequently became the "great highway" connecting Lower Bengal with Upper India. Similarly, in the approach to St Petersburg, the southern channel is referred to as the highway to the capital.

## Regional Infrastructure and Connectivity
In Bolivia, public highways have been constructed to connect large cities and certain points on the frontiers. These roads are often rough and nearly impassable at times, with dangerous and difficult river crossings. Some of these highways support subsidized stage coaches.

In other contexts, highways have influenced urban growth. In Rome, the city expanded beyond its early walls, with suburbs following the paths of the public highways. In modern engineering projects, such as the Quebec bridge over the St Lawrence, designs have incorporated highways alongside electric railways and rail lines between main trusses.
