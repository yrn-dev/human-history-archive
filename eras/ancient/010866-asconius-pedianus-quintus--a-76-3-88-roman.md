# ASCONIUS PEDIANUS, QUINTUS

Quintus Asconius Pedianus was a Roman historian and grammarian who lived during the early Roman Empire. His dates of birth and death are recorded as either 9 B.C.–A.D. 76 or A.D. 3–88. He was likely a native of Patavium (Padua), though he spent his later years residing in Rome. He died at the age of eighty-five, having been blind for the twelve years preceding his death.

## Literary Work and Methodology
During the reigns of the emperors Nero and Claudius, Asconius compiled historical commentaries on the speeches of Cicero. These works were intended for his sons and were synthesized from a wide array of sources. These included:
* The *Acta Publica* (Gazette).
* *Commentarii*, which were shorthand reports or "skeletons" of speeches by Cicero that remained unpublished.
* Letters and speeches written by contemporaries of Cicero.
* The life of Cicero written by Tiro.
* Various historical writers, including Atticus, Antias, Tuditanus, Varro, and Fenestella; the latter was a contemporary of Livy whom Asconius frequently criticized.

The resulting notes are written in good Latin and focus primarily on antiquarian, historical, and legal matters. While Asconius produced several commentaries, only five have been preserved in a mutilated condition: *in Pisonem*, *pro Scauro*, *pro Cornelio*, *pro Milone*, and *in toga Candida*. 

A separate commentary on Cicero's Verrine orations exists, but it is universally regarded as spurious due to its grammatical character and inferior Latinity. Other works attributed to Asconius include a treatise on long life and health (possibly a symposium imitating Plato), a defense of Virgil against detractors, and a life of Sallust.

## Chronology and Dating
The dating of Asconius's work can be narrowed through his specific references. In a note regarding the speech *pro Scauro*, Asconius refers to Longus Caecina (who died in A.D. 57) as still being alive. Simultaneously, the phrasing used suggests that the emperor Claudius (who died in A.D. 54) was no longer living. Consequently, this specific statement must have been written between A.D. 54 and A.D. 57.

## Historical and Legal Contributions
Asconius's fragments provide significant insight into the Roman legal system. He provides an account of the supplications and begging that occurred before the *judices* (jurors) at Rome when a sentence was about to be pronounced upon Scaurus, whom Cicero defended. Asconius notes that nine consulares praised Scaurus, including Pompeius (who praised him via tablets as he was absent). He further details how Scaurus, along with M. Glabrio, Paulus, P. Lentulus, L. Æmilius Buca, and C. Memmius, knelt before the judges to supplicate on one side, while Sylla Faustus, T. Annius Milo, T. Peducæus, C. Cato, and M. Octavius Lænas did so on the other.

Additionally, Asconius is cited as a source regarding the corruption and venality of the Roman *judices*—who were drawn from the equestrian and senatorial orders—during the period of the *quæstiones perpetuæ*.

## Transmission of Texts
The works of Asconius were rediscovered in 1416 by Poggio, who found them in a manuscript at St Gallen. Although the original manuscript is now lost, three transcripts were created:
1. A transcript by Poggio, currently located in Madrid (Matritensis x. 81).
2. A transcript by Zomini (Sozomenus) of Pistoia, located in the Forteguerri library at Pistoia (No. 37).
3. A transcript by Bartolommeo da Montpulciano, a copy of which is held in Florence (Laur. liv. 5).

Subsequent manuscripts of his work are derived from the copy made by Poggio.
