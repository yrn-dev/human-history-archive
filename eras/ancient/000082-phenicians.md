# PHENICIANS

The Phenicians were a people of the Semitic family who established a vast maritime and commercial network across the ancient world. Their influence extended from the Levant to the Atlantic coast, significantly impacting the development of early Greek civilization through trade, technology, and the introduction of writing.

## Geography and Major Cities
The Phenicians were centered in the south-eastern portion of the inhabited world. Key cities included Tyre, Sidon, and Byblus. Tyre, in particular, served as a dominant power, maintaining tributary dependence over regions such as Cyprus, which was subject to the Tyrians as early as the time of Solomon.

Beyond their homeland, the Phenicians established numerous colonies and settlements. In Africa, they founded Utica and Carthage, with Utica affirmed to have been established two hundred and eighty-seven years prior to Carthage. In Iberia, they founded Gadês, attributed to the founder Archaleus (a version of the name Hercules). Other settlements included Leptis Magna and Leptis Parva in Africa, as well as various outposts in Sicily and Cyprus.

## Commerce and Exploration
Phenician commerce flourished in the earlier periods of Greek history, characterized by a superior mercantile activity compared to the Greeks. Their trade networks were extensive:
*   **Maritime Trade:** Phenician ships were present in Grecian seas during Homeric times. From Gadês, they conducted commerce toward Britain on one side and Africa on the other. They also dominated trade in the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf, remaining without competitors in these regions even as their other powers waned. In the time of Herodotus, the distribution of frankincense and spices from Arabia was conducted exclusively by Phenician merchants.
*   **Land Trade:** The Phenicians carried on caravan-trade by land, including the exclusive conduct of land trade with Egypt prior to the reign of Psammetichus.
*   **Exploration:** The Phenicians are credited with the circumnavigation of Africa.

## Interaction with the Greeks
The relationship between the Phenicians and Greeks was marked by both cultural exchange and commercial competition. 

**Cultural Contributions:** The Phenicians introduced many arts to the Hellenes. Most notably, Phenicians who came with Cadmos settled in Boeotia and introduced letters (the alphabet), which did not exist among the Hellenes previously. The Ionians adopted these letters, changing their form slightly and continuing to call them "phenicians." Additionally, the Phenicians influenced the Greek mind regarding the scale of money and weight, the gnomon, and the division of the day. In Cyprus, Greeks partially embraced and diffused rites from the Phenician religion, which are described as sometimes voluptuous and sometimes cruel.

**Territorial Competition:** In Sicily, Phenicians initially held many outlying islets and promontories to facilitate trade with the interior Sikels and Sikans. However, after the arrival of Greek colonists (such as the Chalkidians at Naxos in 735 B.C. and the subsequent founding of Syracuse), the Phenicians were forced to relinquish these petty settlements. They concentrated their presence in Malta and three larger towns near Lilybæum: Motyê, Soloeis, and Panormus. A similar process occurred in Cyprus, where a considerable portion of trade and soil passed from Phenicians to Greeks.

## Political Relations and Decline
The political trajectory of the Phenicians was heavily influenced by the great empires of the East. The cities of Tyre and Sidon suffered a decline in power and traffic as they were reduced to a state of dependence by the arms of Nebuchadnezzar. Later, the Phenicians delivered themselves over to the Persian Empire of their own accord.

Under Persian rule, the Phenicians provided the essential naval force for the empire. This is evidenced by the account of Cambyses, who wished to send a fleet against Carthage. The Phenicians refused to participate, citing solemn vows that forbade them from making an expedition against their "own sons" (the Carthaginians). Because the entire naval force depended on the Phenicians, Cambyses did not compel them, and Carthage escaped Persian enslavement.

The general decline of Phenician dominance coincided with the growth of Grecian marine activity. By 550 B.C., Ionic ships were predominant in the Aegean, and Greek cities like Kyrênê and Barka had taken root in Libya. The conquest of Cyprus by Amasis further signaled the comparative decline of Tyre.

## Carthage and the Western Colonies
As the original Phenician cities declined, Carthage grew in power. Unlike the earlier Phenician settlements focused primarily on commerce, the establishments of the Carthaginians combined commercial views with views of empire. The Carthaginians and the Phenicians of Gadês were collectively referred to as "Pœnus." In some regions, such as Leptis Magna, the Phenician language was eventually lost and replaced by the language of the native Numidians, though these people continued to embrace Sidonian civilization and institutions.
