# Pāli Books

## Nature and Purpose
Pāli books are not books in the modern sense. Rather, they consist of verses or memorial sentences designed specifically to be learned by heart. Because the primary necessity of these texts is memorization, their entire method of arrangement and style are subordinated to this goal. Unlike Western writings, which are intended to be read, Pāli books are intended to be memorized.

## Structure and Organization
The organization of Pāli texts follows a specific hierarchy of length and content:
*   **Sutta:** A very short unit, usually occupying one or perhaps two pages, which contains a single proposition.
*   **Suttanta:** A collection of several suttas, typically those containing similar propositions, organized within the framework of a single dialogue. A suttanta is usually about a dozen pages long, though a few are longer.
*   **Book:** A collection of these suttantas may be referred to as a book. Such a work is not an essay or a narrative, but rather a string of passages formatted to assist the memory.

## The Nikāyas
The Nikāyas are collections of these suttantas. The first two are categorized by the length of the dialogues they contain:
*   **Dīgha Nikāya:** Known as "the Collection of Long Ones," it contains the longest suttantas.
*   **Majjhima Nikāya:** Known as the "Collection of the suttantas of Medium Length," these are shorter than those in the Dīgha Nikāya but longer than the ordinary suttas found in the subsequent two collections.

Together, these first two collections contain 186 dialogues. In these texts, the Buddha, or occasionally one of his leading disciples, is depicted engaging in conversations regarding ethical, philosophic, or religious points within the system now called Buddhism.

## Style and Comparison
The style of Pāli books is described as dignified and occasionally eloquent. While they differ from Western writing styles due to their purpose (memorization versus reading), they remind the reader of the dialogues of Plato in their earnest and elevated tone, their depth of philosophic insight, and their frequent use of Socratic questioning. These texts serve as evidence of the most cultured thought of their time and are revered by historical scholars.
