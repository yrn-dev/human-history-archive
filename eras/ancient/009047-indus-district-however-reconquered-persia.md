# Indus

The Indus is a major river system that serves as a central geographical and historical landmark in South Asia, acting as a boundary and a conduit for trade, migration, and political administration across various districts and provinces.

## Historical Migrations and Conquests
The Indus valley has been a focal point for the movement of peoples and the expansion of empires. According to Sanscrit epics, a fair, beef-eating people (who later became vegetarians) descended from Persia into the plains of North India, conquering their way toward the Indus before spreading further across India. These people, identified as the Eastern branch of the Indo-Europeans or Indo-Aryans, were settled in the valley of the Indus around 1000 B.C., having pushed through the Kabul valley.

In later antiquity, the dominions of King Kadphises I (approximately A.D. 45) extended from Bokhara to the Indus. However, this district was subsequently reconquered by Persia under the Sassanian dynasty.

## Regional Geography and Administration
The river defines the landscape and administrative boundaries of several districts:

**Dera Ismail Khan**
This district covers 3403 sq. m. and was formerly divided into two nearly equal portions by the Indus, which intersects the area from north to south. The region to the west of the river resembles Dera Ghazi Khan, while the east contains a wide tract known as the *Kachi*, which is exposed to river action. In 1901, the trans-Indus tract was allotted to the North-West Frontier Province, while the cis-Indus tract remained under Punjab jurisdiction. The population in 1901 was 252,379, consisting primarily of Baluch and Pathan Mahommedans. The Indus is navigable by native boats for 120 m. within this district.

**Dera Ghazi Khan**
Spanning 5306 sq. m., this district is a long, narrow strip 198 m. in length. It slopes from western hills down to the Indus on the east. The eastern portion is low enough to benefit from the floods of the Indus. The river is navigable by native boats but is not bridged anywhere within the district. The population in 1901 was 471,149, the majority being Baluch Mahommedans.

**Bannu**
Located north of the Indus, the district of Bannu contains 1680 sq. m. The Indus is navigable for native boats throughout its 76 m. course in this district, though it is not bridged here. The cis-Indus portions of Bannu and Dera Ismail Khan now form the Punjab district of Mianiwali.

**Kohat**
This district consists of a mountain region east of the Indus. The Teri Toi river runs eastward to join the Indus 12 m. north of Makhad, dividing the district into two main portions.

**Kotri**
Situated on the right bank of the Indus in the Karachi district of Sind, Kotri is a town that serves as a junction for branches of the North-Western railway. A railway bridge crosses the Indus here. Kotri previously served as the headquarters of the Indus steam flotilla and the station for Hyderabad, which is located across the river. Despite the development of railways, the town maintains a considerable general transit trade via the river.

## Baluchistan and the Western Frontiers
The Indus forms a significant boundary for Baluchistan, with 300 miles of mountain walls facing the river south of the railway to Quetta, and approximately 250 miles north of it. In the northern Pathan district, drainage systems either run toward the Gomal or burst eastwards through limestone hills to reach the Indus via the shortest transverse routes.

Scientific geographical mapping has further defined these frontiers. Professional surveys have produced maps extending from the Oxus to the Indus, and from the Indus to the Euphrates, involving the boundaries between Baluchistan and Persia.
