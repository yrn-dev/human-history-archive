# FROM THE SUBJUGATION OF OLYNTHUS BY THE LACEDÆMONIANS DOWN TO THE CONGRESS AT SPARTA

## Overview of the Period
The period following the subjugation of Olynthus and Phlius by the Lacedæmonians was characterized by a triumphant and seemingly unassailable position for Sparta. This era extends from the aftermath of these subjugations down to the Congress at Sparta and the partial peace of 371 B.C. The years succeeding the peace of Antalkidas (387-379 B.C.) are viewed by contemporaries as a time of significant concern regarding the state of Greece and the conduct of the Spartan state.

## The Peace of Antalkidas
The convention or peace known as the peace of Antalkidas is described as an incident of mournful and serious import in the history of Greece. The nature of this agreement is illustrated by an exchange involving the Spartan king Agesilaus. When told, "Alas for Hellas... when we see our Laconians *medising*!", Agesilaus replied that it should instead be said that the Medes (Persians) were "laconising." In historical texts, this agreement is referred to as *ἡ εἰρήνη* by Xenophon and *αἱ συνθῆκαι* by Isokrates.

## The Subjugation of Olynthus and Phlius
The surrender of Olynthus to Polybiades and the surrender of Phlius to Agesilaus occurred nearly at the same time. The matters regarding Phlius, which included the complaints of Phliasian exiles and a blockade, were concluded in one year and eight months, though the blockade itself likely lasted no more than one year. 

The governance of Phlius was handled by an "omnipotent Hekatontarchy" appointed through the partisan feelings of Agesilaus. The power and situation of this body were analogous to the Lysandrian Dekarchies and the Thirty at Athens, leading to unfavorable presumptions about their conduct.

## Spartan Hegemony and the Theban Conflict
Despite their dominant position, the Lacedæmonians faced condemnation for their actions. Xenophon notes that the Lacedæmonians had sworn to leave each city autonomous, but they violated these oaths by seizing the citadel of Thebes. 

This act led to their eventual punishment by the very people they had wronged. Furthermore, the Theban faction that had originally introduced the Spartans into the citadel—doing so with the intent that their city be enslaved to Sparta so they could rule despotically—were eventually put down by a group of no more than seven assailants from among the exiles they had banished.

## Military Engagements at Olynthus and Potidæa
In separate military movements, the Peloponnesians and Potidæans, under the general Aristeus, encamped on the isthmus facing Olynthus. Aristeus commanded the infantry, while Perdiccas, who had returned to the alliance of the Potidæans, commanded the cavalry. 

The strategic plan involved keeping the main force on the isthmus to await the Athenians, while placing two hundred cavalry from Perdiccas in Olynthus (located approximately seven miles from and in sight of Potidæa) to attack the Athenian rear. During the ensuing engagement, the wing led by Aristeus, including Corinthians and other picked troops, routed the opposing wing. However, the rest of the Peloponnesian and Potidæan army was defeated by the Athenians and forced to take refuge within fortifications. Aristeus eventually managed to force his way into Potidæa via the breakwater through the sea, though some of his men were lost.
