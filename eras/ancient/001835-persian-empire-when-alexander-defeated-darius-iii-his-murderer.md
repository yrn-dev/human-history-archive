# The Persian Empire

## Foundation and Early Expansion
The Persian Empire was founded in 539 B.C. when Cyrus took Babylon. Cyrus had previously succeeded Cyaxares the Mede in 550 B.C. and conquered Crœsus. Following the foundation of the empire, Cambyses, the son of Cyrus, subjugated Egypt, though he was later accidentally killed after going mad. He was succeeded by Darius I, the son of Hystaspes and a chief councillor to Cyrus. Under the rule of Darius I, the empire extended from the Hellespont to the Indus, and he conducted an expedition to Scythia.

## Conflicts with the Greeks
The empire engaged in a series of conflicts with the Greeks. Notable events included the Battle of Marathon in 490 B.C., followed by the Battles of Thermopylae and Salamis in 480 B.C. The repulse of Persia was completed in 479 B.C. through the battles of Platea and Mycale.

## Internal Politics and the Rise of Darius III
In the years preceding the Macedonian invasion, the Persian court experienced significant instability. A eunuch named Bagoas, who served as a confidential minister to Artaxerxes III, gained immense power and wealth. Bagoas became the real master of the kingdom, administering the upper satrapies and acquiring wealth by taking bribes from priests in exchange for returning confiscated sacred writings from Egyptian temples. Bagoas also imposed a new tax on the Israelites after the high priest of Jerusalem, Jesus, murdered his brother Johannes.

Bagoas exerted control over the throne through assassination; in 338 B.C., he killed Artaxerxes III and all his sons except the youngest, Arses, whom he raised as king. Two years later, Bagoas murdered Arses and placed a distant relative of the royal house on the throne. This new king, who had excelled in a war against the Cadusians and was known as Codomannus, adopted the name Darius III. When Darius III attempted to establish independence from Bagoas, the eunuch tried to poison him. However, Darius was warned and forced Bagoas to drink the poison himself.

## The Conquest by Alexander the Great
The Macedonian campaign against Persia began in 334 B.C. under Alexander, who had crossed into Asia after two years of consolidating his power in Greece and Macedonia. Alexander first defeated a Persian army at the Battle of the Granicus and captured several cities in Asia Minor. To prevent the Persians—who controlled the fleets of Sidon and Tyre—from raiding his communications, Alexander reduced and garrisoned coast towns. After storming Gaza and the city of Tyre, Alexander entered Egypt in 332 B.C., taking over its rule from the Persians.

In 333 B.C., Alexander encountered the forces of Darius III at the plain of Issus. The Persian army was a vast, ill-organized assembly of soldiers and contingents from every province, encumbered by a multitude of court officials, musicians, dancers, cooks, and the harem of Darius. Darius had moved this force over the mountains to the sea to cut Alexander off from Greece. Alexander's phalanx and cavalry charge routed the Persian host. Darius escaped on horseback, leaving his harem behind. Darius later attributed this defeat to the confined nature of the ground, which penned his forces between the mountains and the sea.

The Persian resistance continued until 331 B.C., when Alexander defeated Darius III again at the Battle of Arbela.

## The Fall of Darius III and Aftermath
Following his defeat at Arbela, Darius III fled east. He was eventually deposed and murdered in July 330 B.C. by Bessus, the satrap of Bactria. Other accounts describe Darius being overtaken at dawn in his chariot, dying after being murdered by his own people; he was still alive when the first Greeks reached him, but Alexander found him dead.

After the death of Darius III, Bessus attempted to organize a national resistance in the east, but Alexander conquered Bactria without much difficulty. Alexander then conducted a military parade of central Asia, founding Herat and traveling through Cabul and the Khyber Pass into India. There, he defeated an Indian king, Porus, in a battle involving elephants. Alexander eventually returned to Susa in 324 B.C.

In an effort to organize the vast empire he had won and win over his subjects, Alexander assumed the tiara and robes of a Persian monarch. He also arranged the "Marriage of the East and West," marrying Macedonian officers to Babylonian and Persian women. Alexander died of a fever in Babylon in 323 B.C.
