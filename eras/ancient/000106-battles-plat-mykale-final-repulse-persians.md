# BATTLES OF PLATÆA AND MYKALE: FINAL REPULSE OF THE PERSIANS

The battles of Platæa and Mykalê marked the final repulse of the Persian forces in Greece. Following the Persian defeat at Salamis, the Persians lost hope of achieving success through further maritime attacks on Greece, though they continued to anticipate a victory on land through the campaign led by Mardonius.

## The Persian Naval Position
After transporting the Persian monarch and his land-force across the Hellespont, the Persian fleet retired to winter at Samos and Kymê. During this period, the Persian leadership bestowed large rewards upon two Samian captains, Phylakus and Theomêstor, for their distinction in previous engagements; Theomêstor was specifically nominated as the despot of Samos under Persian protection. 

In the following spring, the fleet reassembled at the naval station of Samos, totaling four hundred sail. This force did not include the Phenicians and was intended primarily to maintain a watchful guard over Ionia, as the Persians did not expect the Greek fleet to venture an attack against them.

## The Battle of Mykalê
The Greek fleet eventually arrived, but the Persians fled from Samos, disappointing the Greeks' initial intention to fight. While some Greeks proposed returning home or turning toward the Hellespont, Ionian envoys persuaded them to pursue the enemy and offer battle at Mykalê. Upon arrival, the Greeks discovered that the Persians had abandoned the sea to fight exclusively on land.

Emboldened, the Greeks disembarked to attack the combined Persian land and sea forces. The Greek commander, Leotychidês, attempted to encourage the Ionians within the Persian ranks to revolt. Sailing close to the coast, he used a herald to make earnest appeals to the Ionians, calculating that even if they did not revolt, the act would make the Persians mistrust them.

The Persian generals, surprised by this daring and suspecting secret collusion between the Ionians and the Greeks, took preemptive measures. They ordered the Milesians to retire to the rear of the army to occupy mountain roads leading to the summit of Mykalê—territory with which the Milesians were familiar—and ordered the Samian contingent to be disarmed.

## The Campaign of Mardonius and the Battle of Platæa
While naval operations occurred at Mykalê, Mardonius led the land campaign. After wintering in Thessaly, Mardonius resumed operations in Bœotia in the spring, consulting Bœotian oracles. He attempted to negotiate peace with Athens, sending Alexander of Macedon to offer honorable terms. Despite the suffering of the city and the "selfish indifference" of Sparta and the Peloponnesians—who fortified the Isthmus but left Attica undefended—the Athenians resolutely refused Mardonius's offers. This led to Mardonius occupying Athens a second time and the Athenians migrating a second time to Salamis.

A large Spartan force eventually collected under Pausanias at the Isthmus. Pausanias marched over Kithæron into Bœotia, where he was harassed by Persian cavalry under Masistius. However, the Athenians proved superior against the cavalry, and Masistius was slain. The Greek army eventually took a position near Platæa along the Asôpus, where Mardonius posted himself on the opposite side of the river. Both armies were initially unwilling to attack, discouraged by prophets on both sides. Mardonius eventually met his "bitter reward" on the field of battle at Platæa.

## Aftermath and the Confederacy of Delos
The repulse of the Persians led to a significant shift in Greek geopolitics. Immediately following the battles of Platæa and Mykalê, the towns on the seaboard and the islands in the Ægean formed the confederacy of Delos. The purpose of this alliance was not only to expel the Persians from the Ægean but to ensure they were kept away permanently. 

Because Sparta was inadequate for the accomplishment of this objective, Athens took the lead. The archive notes that the permanent removal of the Persians was achieved through Athenian naval discipline, organization, military energy, and devotion to a Pan-Hellenic purpose. This formed the basis of the Athenian maritime empire.
