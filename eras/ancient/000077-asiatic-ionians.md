# Asiatic Ionians

The Asiatic Ionians were a group of Greek colonists who established several significant cities on or near the coast of Asia Minor. At the commencement of historical Greece in 776 B.C., there were twelve primary Ionian cities of note in this region, listed from south to north as Milêtus, Myûs, Priênê, Samos, Ephesus, Kolophôn, Lebedus, Teôs, Erythræ, Chios, Klazomenæ, and Phôkæa, along with several other less important settlements.

## Origins and Settlement
While legend attributes the foundation of these cities to a single "Ionic migration" occurring 140 years after the Trojan War, historical evidence suggests that these cities were founded by emigrants from European Greece through separate nativities and causes. For example, tradition states that in the 11th century B.C., Androclus, the son of the Athenian king Codrus, landed at Ephesus with his Ionians and a mixed body of colonists, marking the start of the history of Greek Ephesus. Prior to this, Greek writers assign the earliest inhabitants of Ephesus to the Leleges, Carians, Pelasgi, and "Amazons."

## Culture and Religion
In early Ionian times, the city of Ephesus was dedicated to the deity Artemis. She was viewed as a natural matronly figure, sometimes accompanied by a child, and was closely related to a primitive Asiatic goddess of nature whose cult existed at neighboring Ortygia before the Ionian migration. In this capacity, Artemis was the virgin-mother of all life, specifically wild life, and an embodiment of the earth's productive power.

The Asiatic Ionians maintained a strong association with the Ionic Greeks of the Aegean, including those in Attica and the Cyclades. This connection was exemplified by the early Ionic festival at Dêlos, which was characterized by great wealth and crowds of visitors. During this period of extensive association, the wealthy, such as the Milesians and Ephesians, wore tunics of linen and decorated their hair with golden ornaments. This style was also adopted by wealthy Athenians before they later shifted to the more sober woollen clothing of the Peloponnesus and Sparta.

## Political Subjugation and Persian Rule
The independence of the petty Ionian cities was eroded during the last half of the sixth century B.C. due to the rise of Ionian despots, such as Peisistratus of Athens and Polykratês of Samos, as well as the conquests of the Persians in Asia Minor. Because of intense separatism among the Greeks, the Ionians of Asia and the islands fell first under the domination of the kingdom of Lydia and subsequently under the Persians after Cyrus overthrew King Crœsus of Lydia.

Under Persian rule, the Asiatic Ionians were occasionally utilized for imperial military efforts. During Darius's march against Scythia, his naval force included Asiatic and insular Greeks. Darius ordered the Ionians to construct a bridge over the Danube; when his return was delayed, the Ionian despots maintained the bridge to ensure Darius could recross the river, thereby securing their own dominion at home. This period of subjugation contributed to a reputation for cowardice and effeminacy that was attributed to the Ionic name in the writings of Thucydidês and Herodotus.

## Revolts and Liberation
The Asiatic Ionians engaged in periods of rebellion against their Persian masters, though they were often reconquered. A significant turning point occurred following the defeat of the Persian army at Platæa in 479 B.C. On the same day, the Persian fleet and land army suffered a disaster at Mount Mycale on the Asiatic mainland, located between Miletus and Ephesus. Greek forces stormed a wall the Persians had built around their ships. These disasters encouraged the Ionian cities in Asia to revolt against the Persians for a second time.

Following these events, the commanders of the victorious Grecian fleet admitted the Samians, Chians, Lesbians, and other islanders who had been subjects of Persia into their alliance. While there was a proposal to transplant continental Ionians into European Greece to protect them from Xerxes, the Athenians rejected this, as they did not wish to abandon colonies they had originally planted. This disagreement led to Athens assuming separate duties and ascendency regarding the Asiatic Greeks, eventually resulting in the confederacy of Delos and the Athenian maritime empire.
