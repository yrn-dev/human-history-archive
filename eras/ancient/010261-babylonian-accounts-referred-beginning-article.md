# Babylonian Accounts and Their Influence

The Babylonian accounts preserved in the archive's records encompass a wide range of materials, from administrative record-keeping systems and legal codes to cosmogonies and royal chronicles. These texts provide significant insight into the culture of Babylonia and its profound influence on neighboring regions, particularly Canaan and the early traditions of the Israelites.

## Cosmogonies and Mythological Parallels
Babylonian traditions regarding the origin of the universe share striking similarities with the Hebrew cosmogony. The Babylonian epic describes a period of chaos characterized as a time when "the lands altogether were sea" and there were no trees or reeds. This corresponds to the description of chaos in the Hebrew account, which mentions the "great water" and a state of being "devoid of living things" (referred to as *tohu* and *bohu*). 

In the Babylonian epic, the creator is Marduk, a god of light. While the Hebrew account describes the creation of light as a specific act, the Babylonian tradition presupposes the existence of light apart from the sun. Furthermore, the Babylonian account describes the emergence of a "platform" or "firmament" of heaven from "earth-matter." The successive stages of creation in the Hebrew statement correspond so closely to those in the Babylonian epic that it is assumed one influenced the other. This is attributed to the pervasive influence of Babylonian culture and its mythic representations of the origin of things—including both Creation and Deluge stories—upon Canaan and other neighboring regions in remote times.

A notable difference exists regarding the "dragon of chaos and darkness." While this figure is a central element in the most important Babylonian traditions, it is conspicuously absent from the short account of creation translated by T. G. Pinches in 1890 and is unmentioned in the Hebrew cosmogony. However, the mythic figure of the dragon appears poetically in other biblical passages, such as those in Job, the Psalms, Isaiah, and the Apocalypse.

## Historical and Chronological Records
The archive contains extensive data on the chronology of Babylonian kings. The Babylonian List of Kings, published in 1884, serves as the primary framework for chronological systems, listing kings by dynasty from the First Dynasty of Babylon through the Neo-Babylonian period. Despite its importance, the list contains gaps, specifically regarding the lengths of Dynasties IV and VIII.

One of the most prominent figures in these records is Hammurabi (also referred to as Khammurabi), the sixth king of the first Babylonian dynasty. Historical dating for his reign has shifted over time; earlier estimates placed him between 2376–2333 B.C. or 2285–2242 B.C. However, more recent inscriptions showing that the second dynasty was partly contemporaneous with the first and third have lowered these dates. One adopted date for his reign is 2130–2088 B.C.

## Administrative and Legal Systems
Beyond mythology and royal lists, Babylonian accounts include practical records of governance and commerce. The archive notes that the Babylonians kept records upon movable tablets or slabs. This ancient method is cited as a precursor to the modern "slip system" used in accounting, where transactions are recorded on separate slips rather than being immovably bound in a ledger.

The legal framework of the society is exemplified by the Code of Hammurabi. Various scholarly editions of this code exist, including those by V. Scheil, H. Winckler, and R. F. Harper, the latter of whom dates Hammurabi to approximately 2250 B.C. These legal documents are categorized alongside other Babylonian and Assyrian laws, contracts, and letters.

## Cultural Intersections
The influence of Babylonian legends extended to various narratives found in the Pentateuch. The stories of the Flood, Adam and Eve, and the Creation of the World run closely parallel to Babylonian legends and were likely part of the common beliefs of Semitic peoples. Similarly, the stories of Samson and Moses have Babylonian and Sumerian parallels. The archive notes that Abraham, a patriarchal Semitic nomad, may have lived during the era of Hammurabi in Babylon.
