# Sennacherib and the Assyrian Chronology

Sennacherib was an Assyrian monarch and the son of Sargon. His reign is recorded in the archive's chronological lists as beginning in 705 B.C. (or 704 B.C. according to some records). He is noted for his military expeditions, his relationship with the kingdom of Judah, and his eventual death at the hands of his own sons.

## Military Campaigns and the Invasion of Judah
Sennacherib conducted an expedition against Hezekiah, the king of Judah. During this conflict, an official named Rabshakeh delivered threats to Hezekiah while Sennacherib moved against the Egyptians. Although Isaiah the prophet provided encouragement to Hezekiah, Sennacherib eventually returned to Jerusalem after failing to achieve success in Egypt. Upon his return, he discovered that his army had been destroyed, prompting his return home.

The invasion of Judah occurred in the fourteenth year of Hezekiah's reign, dated to 701 B.C. This event followed the fall of Samaria, which occurred in Hezekiah's sixth year (721 or 722 B.C.). While the book of Kings treats these two events as being separated by eight years, archive records indicate the actual interval was twenty-one years.

## Relations with Babylon and Later Reign
Sennacherib's interactions with Babylon were marked by conflict. In 689 B.C., Sennacherib destroyed Babylon. His reign also saw the rise of his son, Assur-nadin-sumi, who is listed as a ruler in 700 B.C. Sennacherib was succeeded by his son, Esarhaddon, in 681 B.C.

## Assyrian Chronological Systems
The chronology of the later Assyrian Empire is considered certain and precise from 911 B.C. to 666 B.C. This accuracy is due to the *limmi* system, where a special officer (archon) was appointed each year and gave his name to that year. Lists of these officers, discovered in the library of Nineveh, allow historians to fix dates with precision. These "canons" are further verified by the full annals of kings such as Asshur-nazir-abal, Shalmaneser II, Tiglath-pileser IV, Sargon, Sennacherib, Esarhaddon, and Assur-bani-pal.

In contrast, the Babylonians used a different system, dating years by the chief events determined by the government. Other ancient civilizations, such as those of the Old Empire in Egypt, also utilized this event-based dating system.

## Comparison with Earlier Monarchs
The Assyrian monarchy extended far back before the era of Sennacherib. For example, Shalmaneser I reigned approximately 600 years prior to the later period, with his reign dated to circa 1310 B.C. He is credited with building Calah. 

Other notable predecessors include:
* **Tiglath-pileser III (Pulu):** He established the second Assyrian empire, introducing political centralization and a standing army. He secured commerce routes to the Mediterranean and the Phoenician seaports before becoming master of Babylonia in 729 B.C.
* **Shalmaneser IV (Ulul):** The successor to Tiglath-pileser III, he continued the policy of empire expansion but died suddenly in 722 B.C. while besieging Samaria.
* **Shalmaneser II:** Reigned from 860 to 825 B.C. He is noted for shattering a confederacy of Syrian princes, including Ahab of Israel, in 853 B.C.

## The Fall of the Ten Tribes
Prior to Sennacherib's campaigns, the Northern Kingdom of Israel faced subjugation. Following the treachery of Hoshea against Pekah, Shalmaneser (IV) subdued the region. In 721 B.C., the kingdom was swept into captivity. Shalmaneser took Samaria by force and transplanted the ten tribes into Media, replacing them with the nation of the Cutheans.
