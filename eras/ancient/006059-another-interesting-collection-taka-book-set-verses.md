# The Jātaka Book

The *Jātaka* book is a collection of verses that are attributed to the Buddha, specifically those supposed to have been uttered during some of his previous births. The collection consists of 550 folk-tales that were current in India at the time the canon was being formed.

## Nature and Content
The Buddhist element of these stories is found in the identification of the Buddha as the hero of each individual tale. While the work is a set of verses, the prose elements of the collection have been preserved only within the commentary. It is regarded as the most authentic, complete, and oldest extant collection of folklore.

## Influence and Distribution
Many of the fables and stories contained within the *Jātaka* book migrated to Europe, where they appear in story-books, poems, and medieval homilies. A detailed account of this migration is provided in the work *Buddhist Birth Stories*. 

## Publication and Scholarship
A translation of the entire book has been published through the Cambridge University Press under the editorship of Professor Cowell.

## Related Early Buddhist Works
The *Jātaka* book is part of a broader body of early Buddhist literature used to understand the ethics and philosophy of the earliest Buddhists. Other notable works include:

*   **Sutta Nipāta**: A collection of fifty-five poems, most of which are short lyrics. These were translated by Professor Fausböll in the *Sacred Books of the East*. An ancient commentary on the majority of these poems exists as a separate work within the canon.
*   **Vinaya**: A complete statement regarding the rules of the order. This work was edited in five volumes by Professor Oldenberg, with three volumes of translations appearing in the *Sacred Books of the East*.

## Evolution of Buddhist Records
The development of early Buddhist records suggests a gradual build-up of material. Short suttas, which embodied doctrine in set phrases, came first. These were followed by episodes that embodied several suttas. These episodes were then woven together to form a suttanta, and eventually, suttantas were grouped into two Nikāyas. 

Parallel to the prose statements of doctrine, there was a strong tradition of poetry in the communities where Buddhism arose. Verses were utilized because they aided memory and allowed adherents to express feelings of ecstasy and enthusiasm inspired by reforming doctrines. The oldest of these verses are lyrics expressing deep thoughts or emotions. Because these were often difficult to understand, they were typically accompanied by prose comments explaining who uttered them and why. While the narrative framework in prose is generally preserved in old Buddhist literature, the latest books in the canon feature narrative parts in verse, resulting in collections of ballads.
