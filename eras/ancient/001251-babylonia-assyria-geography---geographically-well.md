# BABYLONIA AND ASSYRIA

## Geography
Geographically, ethnologically, and historically, the region located between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers in western Asia constitutes a single country. While ancient writers often referred to the entire district as Assyria, Babylonia would have been a more accurate designation. The region is naturally divided into two sections: a mountainous northern area and a flat, marshy southern area. These two divisions are further separated where the northern undulating plateau drops suddenly into the Babylonian alluvium.

The northern plateau, known as the modern El-Jezireh, spans approximately 250 miles in length. This vast flat is interrupted only by a limestone range branching from the Zagros mountains, known as Sinjar, Hamrin, and Sarazur. To the north of this plateau lies a well-watered, undulating belt of country featuring limestone hills—sometimes arid and sometimes covered in dwarf-oak—which enclose fertile valleys and rich plains. The Tigris and Euphrates rivers originate in the massive ridges of the Zagros and Niphates ranges, which serve as a geographic barrier between Assyria, Kurdistan, and Armenia.

Historically, the northern portion was part of Mesopotamia, becoming specifically identified as Assyria following the rise of the Assyrian monarchy. Most major cities, including Calah, Nineveh, and Arbela, were established on the left bank of the Tigris due to the abundant water supply, with the exception of the original capital, Assur.

## Political and Historical Development
The histories of Assyria and Babylon are closely intertwined, with the power of one often reflecting the weakness of the other. Although classical accounts frequently prioritize Assyria, deciphered inscriptions reveal that for most of their history—excluding some seven or eight centuries—Assyria was a province or dependency of Babylonia. Babylonia is described as the mother country, providing the foundation for Assyrian religion, culture, arts, sciences, and literature. The two peoples shared similar language and race.

In early Babylonian history, after a period of foreign influence involving Elamite invaders and Sumuabi of southern Arabia (or Canaan), the fortress of Babylon was built under Sumu-la-ilu, becoming the capital. Khammurabi (also written Ammurapi or Khammuram) eventually shook off Elamite supremacy. In 2340 B.C., in the thirtieth year of his reign, Khammurabi defeated the Elamite forces under Kudur-Lagamar and unified Babylonia into a single monarchy centered at Babylon. During this era, Assyria was a Babylonian dependency, as evidenced by a letter from Khammurabi ordering the dispatch of 240 soldiers from Situllum and Assyria.

Assyria's rise to supremacy began as it grew at the expense of Babylonia. After a period of complex relations involving the Kassite kings of Babylonia and Assur-yuballidh of Assyria, Shalmaneser I (1300 B.C.) openly claimed supremacy in western Asia. His son, Tukulti-In-aristi, later conquered Babylon and executed its king, Bitilyasu, establishing Assyria as the dominant power in the oriental world.

## Social and Political Structure
Babylonia and Assyria differed fundamentally in character. Babylonia was a land of agriculturists and merchants, governed by a powerful hierarchy of priests who had been raised to the throne by revolution; the Babylonian king remained a priest throughout his reign. In contrast, Assyria was essentially an organized camp. Its dynasties were founded by successful generals, and the Assyrian king functioned as an autocratic general. While early Assyrian kings were supported by a feudal nobility, Tiglath-pileser III introduced an elaborate bureaucracy.

Socially, neither country utilized a caste system. In Babylonia, the priesthood was divided into many classes, including doctors. In Assyria, the counterpart to the priesthood was a military aristocracy. Both societies relied on a large population of slaves, followed by commercial and agricultural classes. Scribes held a more prominent position in Assyria than in Babylonia.

The Assyrian military was characterized by conscription and the eventual organization of a standing army. Under the second Assyrian empire, cavalry replaced chariots. Tiglath-pileser III introduced high boots and saddles for riders, and Sennacherib established a corps of slingers. The army utilized battering-rams, baggage-carts, and tents on the march, led by a commander-in-chief known as the *tartan*.

## Art and Architecture
The culture of both regions was primarily literary rather than artistic. Due to a lack of stone and an abundance of clay, Babylonia utilized brick for its massive, shapeless temples. These structures featured buttresses, lead drains (as seen at Ur), pilasters, columns, and walls decorated with enamelled tiles, frescoes, and bronze or gold plating. Babylonian art is noted for figures in the round and a high perfection in gem-cutting, specifically seal-cylinders from the age of Sargon of Akkad.

Assyria initially followed Babylonian architectural styles, using brick platforms even in the north where they were unnecessary. However, later Assyrian architects began using stone. Assyrian palaces featured walls lined with colored and sculptured stone slabs. These bas-reliefs evolved through three stages: the simple and vigorous style of Assur-nazir-pal III, the realistic style of Sargon, and the refined, less bold style of Assur-bani-pal.

## The Fall of Assyria
The Assyrian empire began to break up following the death of Assur-bani-pal. Under Assur-etil-ilani, Scythians penetrated the territory, burning Calah. Though Nineveh's walls provided protection, the subsequent palace built there was small and poor, reflecting depleted resources. The final stages of the empire saw disputed rule between Assur-etil-ilani and his vizier, Assur-sum-lisir, followed by the reign of Sin-sar-iskun.

The end of the Assyrian empire came during the reign of Nabopolassar of Babylonia. Aided by the Scythian king of Ecbatana, Cyaxares, the Babylonians captured and destroyed Nineveh and other northern Babylonian cities that had sided with Assyria.
