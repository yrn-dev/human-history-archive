# Adaptation and Evolution in Religious Literature

## Babylonian Religious Adaptation
In the religious literature of Babylon, a process of adaptation can be traced with perfect definiteness. This process involved the re-editing of older incantations, which were originally associated with Ea, to instead grant Marduk supreme power over sorcerers, witches, and demons. Similarly, appeals and lamentations that had been composed for the cults of Adad, Shamash, and Bel were transformed into appeals and paeans dedicated to Marduk.

Ancient myths from various political and religious centers underwent a similar adaptation to meet changed conditions. Consequently, the original meanings of these myths were obscured by efforts to attribute all mighty acts and deeds—which were originally symbolical of occurrences in nature or the change of seasons—to the patron deity of Babylon, who served as the supreme head of the Babylonian pantheon.

## The Court of Marduk and Solar Deities
The court of Marduk was expanded over time by the addition of various minor deities, many of whom were patron gods of less important localities and often of a solar character. Simultaneously, the chief moon-god and the chief solar deity, Shamash of Sippara, tended to absorb lunar and solar deities from less important sites. 

This absorption led to a differentiation of the functions of Shamash across different times of the day and seasons of the year, distributed among the minor deities. For example, Ninib, whose chief seat appears to have been at Shirgulla (Lagash), became the sun-god of the morning and the springtime, bringing new life and joy to the earth. Conversely, Nergal of Kutha was regarded as the sun of the noonday heat and the summer solstice, acting as the harbinger of death and suffering.

## Religious Literature in the Low Countries
In the Low Countries, religious prose and drama played a significant role in the development of the language. A prose translation of the Old Testament and a *Life of Jesus* both date to approximately 1300. Among the mystical preachers, the Brussels friar Jan van Ruysbroec (1294-1381) is noted as the most important whose religious writings have survived.

Religious drama was prominent in medieval Holland. While the texts of the earliest mysteries are lost, records exist of plays such as *Our Lord's Resurrection* (The Hague, 1400), *Our Lady the Virgin* (Arnheim, 1452), and *The Three Kings* (Delft, 1498). A fragment of a *Limburg-Maastricht Passover Play* dates to about 1360, and the *Mystery of the Holy Sacrament*, composed by Smeken and performed at Breda in 1500, is the latest Dutch miracle play. Alongside these theological dramas, semi-religious companies performed "Sotternieen" and "Abelespelen," which were mundane farces and moralities performed outside of churches.

## Bulgarian Theological Works
Ancient Bulgarian literature was heavily influenced by Byzantine literature and predominantly religious in character from the conversion of Boris until the Turkish conquest. Originating in the works of their disciples and SS. Cyril and Methodius, this literature consisted largely of theological works translated from Greek, including:
* Collections of sermons and exegetic religious works.
* Translations of epistles and gospels.
* Lives of the saints.
* Translations of Greek chronicles and miscellanies, such as the *Sbornik* of St Sviatoslav.

During the time of Tsar Simeon, John the exarch wrote the *Shestodnev* (Hexameron), an account of creation. Later, the heresy of the Bogomils spurred controversial writing. Orthodoxy was championed by the monk Athanas of Jerusalem and St Kosmâs, while the Bogomils valued the *Questions of St Ivan Bogosloff*, which described the end and beginning of the world. Additionally, apocryphal works based on Scripture but embellished with imaginative Oriental legends became popular.

## Other Religious Literary Traditions
In Persia, while earlier Aryan literature shows no trace of drama, later national development saw the appearance of the popular comedy or farce and the religious drama or mystery. In the Basque region, much of the literature is translated and religious; the Protestant translation of the New Testament by Jean de Licarrague, printed in 1571 under the auspices of Jeanne d'Albret, is considered the standard of the language.

In India, the system of social organization was based on religious grounds rather than wealth or political power. It remains a problem in the literature to trace the gradual growth of this system, including the formation of new sections among the people and the extension of the institution to families of certain tribes, sects, or groups engaged in specific trades.
