# CENTRAL GREECE: THE ACCESSION OF PHILIP OF MACEDON TO THE BIRTH OF ALEXANDER

## State of Central Greece (360-359 B.C.)
During the period of 360-359 B.C., Central Greece was characterized by a lack of central action in the Peloponnesus. Sparta had suffered degradation, and cities such as Megalopolis and Messênê lived in fear of Sparta. While Athens remained in a comparatively good condition, Thebes held significant power; however, the Thebans had extinguished the free cities of Bœotia, an act that was repugnant to Grecian feeling. In Thessaly, the Pheræan dynasty experienced a loss of power following the reign of the cruel Alexander of Pheræ, who was assassinated, and the subsequent rule of the despot Tisiphonus.

## The Accession of Philip of Macedon
Philip of Macedon ascended to the throne in 360-359 B.C. following the death of his brother, Perdikkas, who had been slain in a battle with the Illyrians. At the time of his accession, Philip was an inexperienced youth whose situation appeared almost hopeless. He faced numerous enemies, rivals at home, and various embarrassments. At the time he mounted the throne, Macedonia passed wholly unnoticed by most Grecian cities, though it was considered an item of some moment in Athens, Thessaly, Thasus, and Olynthus.

Philip's future military ability had been founded during his youth, when he served as a hostage in Thebes and acquired ideas that would later inform his leadership. Through his own energy, perseverance, and genius—and aided by the dissensions of his enemies—he overcame his early difficulties.

## Early Military Campaigns and Diplomacy
Philip's early reign involved struggles against the Thracians and Athenians. In his dealings with Athens, he evacuated Amphipolis and defeated both the Athenians and Argæus. Despite his victory, Philip treated Athenian prisoners mildly and eventually made peace with Athens, renouncing his claim to Amphipolis. He further established his military reputation through victories over the Illyrians and Pæonians. Although Philip evacuated Amphipolis, the Athenians neglected the site.

## The Social War and Athenian Decline
The power of Athens was dealt a fatal shock by the Social War, which occurred the same year Athens recovered the Chersonese. This conflict saw the revolt of principal allies, including Byzantium, Rhodes, Kos, and Chios. The war was driven by the conduct of the Athenians, who acted more for their own separate interests than those of their allies, utilizing badly paid mercenaries who engaged in extortions.

Athens attempted to suppress the revolt, but the efforts were unsuccessful. An armament sent against Chios was repulsed, resulting in the death of Chabrias. Subsequent operations in the Hellespont led by Timotheus, Iphikrates, and Chares were also unsuccessful and marred by quarrels between the generals. Chares accused Iphikrates and Timotheus at Athens; while Iphikrates was acquitted, Timotheus was fined and retired from the city.

## The Rise of Macedonian Hegemony
As Athens struggled with the Social War and other conflicts—such as the victorious effort to stop a Theban-fomented revolt in Eubœa—Philip of Macedon emerged as a formidable threat. By occupying the defensible pass of Thermopylæ, Philip prevented the Athenians from meddling with the Southern states of Greece or crushing the Phokians. 

Philip's military power grew significantly after his decisive victory over Onomarchus. This battle demonstrated the efficiency of the Macedonian phalanx and the military maneuvers Philip had organized since his accession. By the time Demosthenes delivered his first Philippic and his oration against Aristokrates (between midsummer 352 B.C. and midsummer 351 B.C.), Philip had become the ascendent potentate of the region. Demosthenes noted that it was impossible for Athens to provide a land-force capable of contending against Philip in the field.

Philip's rise contributed to a general sinking of the condition of Central Greece. His ambitions created a climate of fear and hope across the Grecian cities, eventually leading to the destruction of Grecian independence.
