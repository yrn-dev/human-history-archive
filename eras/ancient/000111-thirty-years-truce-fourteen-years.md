# The Thirty Years’ Truce and the Prelude to the Peloponnesian War

The thirty years’ truce was a significant diplomatic agreement concluded fourteen years before the outbreak of the Peloponnesian War. This period served as a transitional epoch for Athens, marked by a shift in its international standing, internal political restructuring, and a complex relationship with Sparta and its allies.

## The Nature and Impact of the Truce
The conclusion of the thirty years’ truce represented a period of signal humiliation and a reduction of power for Athens. According to the archive's records, Athens lost very largely by the terms of this agreement and acquired nothing to compensate for these losses during the subsequent fourteen years leading up to the Korkyræan alliance. Specifically, the truce involved the loss of territories such as Nisæa, Pegæ, Trœzen, and Achaia.

There has been scholarly debate regarding the specific covenants of the truce. Some interpretations suggest the truce entitled the Æginetans to independence, as they may have argued that if Athens gave up Nisæa and Pegæ, she should also renounce Ægina. Other views suggest the Æginetans may have relied on an older alliance against the Persians ratified after the victory of Platæa, or perhaps a combination of both the ancient alliance and the thirty years’ truce to seek interference.

## Athens During the Fourteen-Year Interval
Despite the losses incurred at the start of the truce, the fourteen years that followed were a period of full maritime empire for Athens. While this empire faced partial resistance, it was never successfully overturned. This era was characterized by peace with cities outside of the Athenian empire and a period of architectural and sculptural splendor in the city, driven by the genius of Pheidias and others.

However, this period was not one of undisturbed peace. In the sixth year of this interval, Athens faced a formidable revolt from Samos. Later, the reconquest of Samos provided a surge of confidence and elation for the Athenian public.

## Internal Political Dynamics
During this time, Periklês emerged as the first citizen of the commonwealth. His influence grew even after the disastrous reverses that preceded the thirty years’ truce, as he had protested against the expedition of Tolmidês into Bœotia. 

As Periklês' personal influence increased, so did the strength and organization of the party opposed to him. This opposition found a leader in Thucydidês, son of Melêsias, a relative of Kimon. Unlike Kimon, who possessed exclusively military aptitudes, Thucydidês was a statesman and orator. Under his leadership, the aristocratical party—referring to themselves as the "honorable and respectable" citizens—organized themselves to sit together in the public assembly. This allowed them to coordinate their applause, dissent, and speaking parts more effectively to further their party purposes, separating themselves conspicuously from the Demos.

## The Path to the Peloponnesian War
The transition from the truce to open war was driven by a mixture of fear and hope. Sparta and the Peloponnesians hoped that a second combined effort could subdue Athens, while simultaneously fearing the marvelous increase of Athenian power that had occurred in the thirty-five years between the repulse of Xerxes and the thirty years’ truce.

The immediate causes of the conflict involved the revolt of Potidæa and the Megarian decree. While a minority in Athens opposed the Megarian decree—a position Sparta used to break Athenian unanimity and strengthen the party opposed to Periklês—Periklês argued that war was a matter of necessity. He maintained that the demands of the Spartan envoys were exorbitant and that conceding would be viewed as a sign of weakness and fear.

From the perspective of the archive's sources, Athens remained in the right regarding the truce. It was the enemies of Athens who broke the provisions of the truce by promising an invasion of Attica and encouraging the revolt of Potidæa. Athens had offered to submit disputed acts to the amicable arbitration prescribed by the truce itself.

## Related Historical Figures
The period was also marked by the exile of King Pleistoanax of Sparta. He had been banished fourteen years before the commencement of the Peloponnesian War, shortly before the thirty years’ truce, on charges of taking bribes from Athenians while invading Attica. He lived in banishment in Arcadia for over eighteen years. His eventual recall in the fourth or fifth year of the Peloponnesian War was prompted by the Pythian priestess at Delphi, who repeatedly commanded Sparta to bring back the "seed of (Hêraklês) the demi-god son of Zeus" or face the fate of ploughing with a silver ploughshare.
