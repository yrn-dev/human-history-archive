# AUTHORITIES FOR EARLY BRITAIN

The study of early Britain relies upon a variety of classical writers, ancient lists, inscriptions, and modern scholarly syntheses. These sources provide the primary framework for understanding the region's pre-Roman, Roman, and Anglo-Saxon periods.

## Classical Writers and Ancient Lists
The principal references to early Britain found in classical writers are contained in the works of Julius Caesar, Tacitus, Strabo, Diodorus, the elder Pliny, Ptolemy, and Cassius Dio. In addition to these narrative accounts, several ancient lists and itineraries provide critical data:
*   **The Antonine Itinerary:** Dated probably between A.D. 210 and 230.
*   **The Notitia Dignitatum:** Dated to approximately A.D. 400.
*   **The Ravennas:** A 7th-century *rechauffé*.

## Epigraphic and Linguistic Sources
Roman inscriptions serve as a significant source of evidence. These have been collected by Hübner in the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latin.* vii. (1873), as well as in supplements provided by Haverfield and Hübner in the periodical *Ephemeris epigraphica*. Other specialized epigraphic works include Hübner's *Inscript. Britann. Christianae* (1876) and the research of J. Rhys regarding Pictish and other inscriptions published in the *Proceedings Soc. Antiq. Scotland* (volumes xxvi and xxxii).

For those researching ancient authorities and linguistic data, A. Holder's *Altkeltischer Sprachschatz* (1896–1908) is a primary reference, collecting chief passages alphabetically.

## Modern Scholarly References
Various modern works provide summaries and specialized analyses of early British history and archaeology:
*   **General and Roman Britain:** T.R. Holmes' *Ancient Britain* (1907) is cited as the best summary for Caesar's invasions and Roman Britain. J. Rhys' *Celtic Britain* (3rd ed., 1904) is also a key reference.
*   **Archaeology and Material Culture:** Boyd Dawkins' *Early Man in Britain* (1880) and Sir John Evans' works on *Ancient British Coins* and *Stone and Bronze Implements* provide archaeological context.
*   **Art and Earthworks:** Late Celtic art is documented in *Horae Ferales* (1863) by J.M. Kemble and A.W. Franks, and by Arthur J. Evans in *Archaeologia* (vols. lii.–lv.). A. Hadrian Allcroft's *Earthwork of England* (1909) is the reference for ancient earthworks.
*   **General Reference:** The *Realencyclopädie* (Pauly-Wissowa, 1897) is also listed as a source for ancient authorities.

## Nomenclature and Early Evidence
The name "Britain" (Greek: *Pretanikai nêsoi*, *Brettania*; Latin: *Britannia*) is an anglicized form of the classical name for Scotland, Wales, and England, and is sometimes used to describe the British Isles as a whole (*Britannicae Insulae*). These Greek and Roman forms are considered attempts to reproduce a Celtic original, though the exact form remains disputed.

Physical evidence of early British workmanship has been unearthed in specific locales. For example, near the river Ancholme in Brigg, Lincolnshire, a boat hollowed from an oak trunk measuring 48 ft. 6 in. by approximately 5 ft. was discovered in 1885 and assigned to early British workmanship, alongside other prehistoric relics.
