# EUBOIC AND OLYNTHIAN WARS

The Euboic and Olynthian conflicts represent significant periods of instability and military engagement involving the powers of Athens, Philip of Macedon, and various regional confederacies. These conflicts were characterized by shifting alliances, the influence of oratorical persuasion in Athens, and the eventual expansion of Macedonian power.

## The Euboic Context and Early Influence
Eubœa was characterized by several towns, including Eretria and Chalkis. In early times, Chalkis and Eretria were among the foremost Ionic cities in Greece, surpassing Athens in maritime power and commercial relations. This influence is evidenced by the prevalence of the Euboic scale of weight and money across a large portion of Greece. 

The Euboic scale—consisting of a talent equal to sixty minæ, a mina equal to one hundred drachms, and a drachm equal to six obols—was of Eastern origin. It was the scale used by Dareius for gold tribute throughout the Persian empire. While it shared the same divisions as the Æginæan scale, the Euboic drachm contained only five-sixths of the silver weight of an Æginæan drachm. This scale prevailed in Athens before Solon introduced a debasement of approximately twenty-seven per cent, which created the distinct Attic scale.

## The Olynthian War (350–348 B.C.)
The Olynthian War began around 350 B.C. when Philip of Macedon opened hostilities against the Chalkidians and the Olynthian confederacy. The conflict was precipitated by unfriendly feelings from Philip toward Olynthus, which were exacerbated when the fugitive half-brothers of Philip found shelter in the city. Philip utilized corruption and the fomenting of intestine discord as means of intrigue within Olynthus.

Facing the Macedonian threat, the Olynthians sought an alliance with Athens. An embassy had been sent to Athens as early as 352 B.C. to make peace, and a second embassy in 350 B.C. successfully obtained Athenian help. Between 350 and 347 B.C., Philip worked toward the conquest and destruction of the Olynthian confederate towns.

## The Role of Demosthenes
The Athenian response to the Olynthian crisis was heavily influenced by the orations of Demosthenes, who acted as an opposition speaker. His "Olynthiac" speeches tracked the deteriorating situation:

*   **The Second Olynthiac:** The earliest of the series, this speech argued that Philip's strength was overrated and advocated for a vigorous, joint aggressive war carried on by Athens and Olynthus.
*   **The First Olynthiac:** Delivered after Athens failed to make significant exertions and bad news arrived from Thrace, this speech shifted from an offensive to a defensive tone. Demosthenes urged Athens to protect the Olynthians and their confederate cities, warning that their ruin would leave nothing to hinder Philip from marching into Attica.
*   **The Third Olynthiac:** Spoken in 348 B.C., this speech dealt with practical details of the war, arguing that the festival-fund (Theôric Fund) must be used for military purposes and that citizens must serve in person.

Despite these efforts, Athenian confidence was often exaggerated or misplaced. While some assistance was sent to Olynthus, resulting in partial success, the Athenians eventually lost sight of the danger.

## The Fall of Olynthus and Euboic Unrest
The war concluded with the total destruction of Olynthus and the thirty-two towns of its confederacy. The devastation was so complete that Demosthenes later remarked that men could walk over the sites without knowing that cities had once existed. This victory ensured that Philip could not be stopped outside of Greece.

Simultaneously, Athens faced the revolt of Eubœa. Philip engaged in intrigues within the region, leading Plutarch of Eretria to ask Athens for aid. Despite Demosthenes' dissuasion, aid was sent under Phokion. Although Plutarch proved treacherous, Phokion achieved a victory at Tamynæ. During this period (349–348 B.C.), Demosthenes himself served in Eubœa as a hoplite after being reproached for his absence from the battle of Tamynæ.

## Earlier Olynthian Conflicts
Separate from the war with Philip, Olynthus had previously faced the Lacedæmonians. In these earlier engagements, the Olynthian cavalry proved numerous and efficient. In one instance, they defeated Lacedæmonian and Theban cavalry near the city gates and nearly achieved a complete victory, only to be forced back by a diversion created by Derdas and his Macedonian cavalry. While the Lacedæmonian officer Teleutias ravaged Olynthian territory, the Olynthians retaliated with marauding expeditions against his allies.
