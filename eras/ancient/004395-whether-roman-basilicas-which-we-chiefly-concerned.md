# Roman Basilicas

The Roman basilica was a versatile architectural form used primarily as a place of business for merchants and as a court of law. Derived from a Greek word (*basilikê*), the structure evolved into various forms throughout the Roman Empire, eventually influencing the design of early Christian churches.

## Origins and Early Development
The precise origins of the Roman basilica are subject to debate. It is unclear whether they were derived directly from Athenian examples or mediately through similar structures in later Greek cities. While prototypes are sought in Greece, evidence of very early basilicas in those cities is lacking. The earliest known example is the basilica at Pompeii, which may date to the 2nd century B.C.

In Rome, basilicas appeared nearly at the beginning of the 2nd century B.C. The first was the Basilica Porcia, erected in 184 B.C. by the censor M. Porcius Cato. Because Cato had recently visited Athens and was struck by the city's beauty, it is considered possible that the architectural importation was direct. Other early examples include the Basilica Pauli, built by L. Æmilius Paulus.

## Architectural Characteristics
According to the descriptions of Vitruvius, the basilica was essentially a columned structure. While existing ruins show variety, a "normal" form is recognized: a central space surrounded by galleries, generally in two stories, featuring an arrangement for clerestory lighting.

Variations in the architectural scheme include:
*   **Standard Columned Form:** The Basilicas Julia, Ulpia, and Aemilia (north of the Roman forum), as well as the basilica at Pompeii, feature a central space surrounded by galleries supported on piers or columns.
*   **Modified Colonnades:** In some North African examples, the palace basilica of Domitian, and at Silchester, colonnades run down the long sides but do not cross the ends.
*   **Open Interiors:** Basilicas at Timgad and Trier possess no interior divisions.
*   **Vaulted Structures:** The Basilica of Maxentius is entirely vaulted, resembling the great halls of Roman Thermae.

The use of tribunals—fixed platforms—varied by site. They are found at Pompeii, Timgad, and Silchester. The Basilica of Maxentius contains vaulted apses that may have held tribunals. In contrast, the Basilica Julia had no tribunal despite its regular use for the centumviral court. Similarly, the Basilica Ulpia featured a semicircular projection that was distinct from the basilica and not a vaulted apse.

## Relation to Christian Churches
The similarity between the normal columned basilica and early Christian churches has led to theories that churches were simply secular basilicas converted for religious use. However, there is no evidence for this regarding public basilicas. Secular demands on such buildings would likely remain constant regardless of the empire's official religion.

Architecturally, Christian basilicas differ from the normal civil form in key ways. In the west, early Christian basilicas rarely had galleries over the side aisles. Most importantly, their interiors are dominated by a semi-dome apse terminating the central nave; conversely, there is no known instance of a vaulted apse in a normal columned civil basilica, with the possible exception of Silchester.

Evidence suggests Christian worship spaces began in the 2nd or 3rd century A.D. as oblong interiors terminated by an apse. After the time of Constantine, as the number of faithful grew, side aisles were added, causing the structures to resemble civil basilicas. This evolution is supported by a discovery at S. Saba on the Aventine, where a small, early church without aisles was found beneath the floor of the current basilica.

## Notable Examples and Later Developments
Several basilican structures provide insight into the transition from civil to religious architecture:
*   **S. Sabina (Aventine):** Dating from approximately A.D. 430, it preserves a primitive aspect with an open roof, antique Corinthian columns, and unique early Christian carved wooden doors.
*   **S. Lorenzo fuori le Mura:** Originally constructed in A.D. 432 with a short nave of six bays and an internal narthex. In the 13th century, Honorius III altered the church by pulling down the apse and adding a nave of twelve bays, which shifted the orientation and partially buried the original Corinthian columns.
*   **S. Agnese:** A small building similar to the original form of S. Lorenzo.

In the East, notable basilican churches include the monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai (built by Justinian), St Philip at Athens, and St Demetrius at Thessalonica. In North Africa, over 130 examples have been noted. Other examples include a basilica at Michelstadt in Germany from the time of Charles the Great and the 11th-century basilica of St Mauritius near Hildesheim. In Carthage, excavations have revealed a Christian basilica on the hill of the Petit Seminaire and the great Christian basilica of Damus-el-Karita.
