# Linguistic and Administrative Terms: Hau, Kau, Ori, and Kori

The terms *hau*, *kau*, *ori*, and *kori* appear across various contexts in the archive's records, ranging from the grammatical structures of the Basque language to administrative divisions in Japan and caste designations in India.

## Basque Pronouns and Demonstratives
In the Basque language, personal pronouns are categorized by person and number. The first person is represented by *ni* ("I") and *gu* ("we"), while the second person uses *hi* ("thou") and *zu* ("you"). In modern usage, *zu* has transitioned into a polite form of "thou," and a plural "you" (referring to more than one person) is created by adding the pluralizing sign *k--zuek*.

The third-person pronouns in Basque function as demonstratives. There are three primary forms:
*   *hura* or *kura*, meaning "that."
*   *hau* or *kau*, meaning "this."
*   *ori* or *kori*, meaning "this" or "that."

These demonstratives also serve as articles. Examples include *gazt-en-or* ("this younger one") and *andre-ori* ("this lady at some distance"). Additionally, the reflective "self" is expressed using the word *buru*, which means "head."

## Japanese Administrative Divisions
In the history of the Japanese empire, the term *kori* refers to a district. The empire was organized into provinces known as *kuni*, which were categorized into four classes: great, superior, medium, and inferior. Each *kuni* was further subdivided into *kori* (districts), which were divided into five classes: great, superior, medium, inferior, and small.

Historically, the *kori* was known as *agata* (literally "arable land"). The *agata* was further subdivided into *mura* (village) and *inaki* (granary). According to the systems established during the Daiho and Daika reforms, each *kori* was administered by a *cho*, or chief. The *agata* (or modern *kori* or *gun*) is described as the nearest English equivalent to a "district."

## Indian Castes and Languages
In Western India, the Kolis are a tribe or caste of uncertain origin. Some theories suggest the name derives from the Turki word *kuleh*, meaning "slave," which may have led to the term "cooly" for agricultural laborers. The Kolis constitute the primary inferior agricultural population of Gujarat and are also found in the Deccan and the Konkan. As of 1901, the number of Kolis in India was reported as nearly 3¾ million. This figure includes a distinct weaving caste in northern India referred to as Kolis or Koris.

Separately, in the study of regional languages, the term *kori* appears in the conjugation of the root *kar* (meaning "do"). In colloquial Bengali, the first-person present tense "I do" is expressed as *kori*.
