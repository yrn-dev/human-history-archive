# FROM THE BATTLE OF PLATÆA AND MYKALE DOWN TO THE DEATHS OF THEMISTOKLES AND ARISTEIDES

## The Battle of Mykalê
Following the flight of the enemy from Samos, the Greek fleet initially considered returning home or turning toward the Hellespont. However, Ionian envoys persuaded them to pursue the enemy fleet and engage in battle at Mykalê. Upon arrival, the Greeks discovered that the Persians had abandoned the sea and intended to fight exclusively on land.

The Greek forces, emboldened, decided to disembark and attack the combined Persian land and sea forces. Leotychidês, the commander at Mykalê in 479 B.C., employed a maneuver similar to one previously used by Themistoklês at the watering-places of Eubœa during the retreat from Artemisium. As he sailed close to the coast, Leotychidês used a herald to make earnest appeals to the Ionians within the enemy ranks to revolt. His goal was to incite a rebellion or, at the very least, create Persian mistrust of the Ionians.

In response to this daring display and suspicions of secret collusion, the Persian generals ordered the Samian contingent to be disarmed. The Milesians were ordered to the rear of the army to secure the various mountain roads leading to the summit of Mykalê, a territory with which they were familiar.

## Post-War Expeditions and Chronology
Following the battle of Platæa, an expedition was launched against the Thessalian Aleuadæ. This action was taken because the Aleuadæ had been hearty and ardent allies of Mardonius in Bœotia; it was believed the war could not be fully completed without defeating them and ensuring the predominance of the opposing party in Thessaly.

There are conflicting historical accounts regarding the timing of these events. Diodorus claims Leotychidês died in 476 B.C. However, Mr. Clinton argues that Diodorus is contradicted by other sources, including Plutarch regarding the accession of Archidamus and others regarding the Spartan revolt. Clinton places the banishment of Leotychidês and the expedition into Thessaly in 469 B.C. An alternative view suggests the expedition against the Aleuadæ occurred in the first or second year following the battle of Platæa.

## The Rise of the Athenian Empire
In the period following the battles of Platæa and Mykalê, the confederacy of Delos was formed by the towns on the seaboard and the islands in the Ægean. The primary purpose of this confederacy was to expel the Persians from the Ægean and ensure they remained away permanently.

Sparta was deemed altogether inadequate for the accomplishment of this objective. The success of the mission relied on Athens, which demonstrated a level of naval discipline, military energy, power of organization, and devotion to a Pan-Hellenic purpose previously unseen in Grecian history. Through these means, Athens acquired an empire that reached a maximum encompassing both inland and maritime dominion. The inland portion of this empire was later lost, a fact ratified by the Thirty Years Truce concluded with the Peloponnesian confederacy and Sparta in 445 B.C.

## Later Naval Conflicts at Mykalê
In a separate later engagement, the Athenian generals were divided by internal dissension; Leon and Diomedon opposed an oligarchical party that included Charmînus. Astyochus, sensing this disunity, sailed his fleet to the harbor of Samos to offer battle, but the Athenians were unable to leave the harbor.

Astyochus returned to Milêtus, where he remained inactive while awaiting Phenician ships. This inaction, combined with Tissaphernês' irregularity in providing pay and provisions, led to uncontrollable discontent among his troops, particularly the Syracusan contingent. Forced to appease them, Astyochus called a general assembly that resolved to fight immediately. He sailed a fleet of 112 triremes to the promontory of Mykalê, ordering Milesian hoplites to cross the promontory by land. The Athenian fleet, reduced to 82 sail due to the absence of Strombichidês, was moored near Glaukê on the mainland of Mykalê. Upon learning of the Peloponnesian decision to fight, the Athenians retired to Samos to avoid engaging with inferior numbers.
