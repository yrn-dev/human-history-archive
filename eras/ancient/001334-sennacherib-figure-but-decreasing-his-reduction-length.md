# Sennacherib

Sennacherib was a king of Assyria and the son of Sargon II. His reign was characterized by military expeditions and is a subject of chronological study regarding ancient inscriptions.

## Military Campaigns and the Expedition Against Hezekiah
Sennacherib led the Assyrian hosts to the borders of Egypt. During this expedition, his army was struck by a pestilence. According to the nineteenth chapter of the Second Book of Kings, the angel of the Lord smote 185,000 Assyrians in their camp in a single night, leaving them as dead corpses. Following this disaster, Sennacherib departed and returned to dwell at Nineveh.

In addition to the Egyptian campaign, Sennacherib made an expedition against Hezekiah. The first assault on Hezekiah occurred in the fourteenth year of Hezekiah's reign. While Sennacherib was gone against the Egyptians, the Rabshakeh made threats to Hezekiah, who was encouraged by the prophet Isaiah. The destruction of Sennacherib's army did not occur until the eighteenth year of Hezekiah's reign.

## Chronological Debates and the Bavian Inscription
Sennacherib's records have been used by various historians to attempt to resolve discrepancies in ancient chronological data. A specific point of contention involves an inscription engraved on the rock at Bavian regarding the date of Marduk-nadin-akh[=e]'s defeat of Tiglath-pileser I.

In 1897, the writer Rost suggested that the figure "418" engraved on the Bavian rock was a mistake and should instead be "478." This was part of an effort to counteract reductions made to the length of Dynasty III. However, by 1900, Rost abandoned this suggested emendation of Sennacherib's figure.

Similarly, in 1898, Lehmann-Haupt proposed a system to establish a low date for the close of Dynasty III. Unlike other writers who accepted the figures of the Kings' List, Lehmann-Haupt emended Sennacherib's figure in the Bavian inscription, reducing it by one hundred years.

## Legacy and Succession
Sennacherib had two sons who fled to Armenia, where they became the heads of two prominent families: the Genunii and the Arzerunii. His grandson, Assurbanipal (known to the Greeks as Sardanapalus), eventually succeeded in conquering and temporarily holding lower Egypt.
