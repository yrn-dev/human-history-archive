# GREECE UNDER THE LACEDÆMONIAN EMPIRE

## Military Organization and Training
The Lacedæmonian military system was characterized by a rigorous focus on fortitude and endurance (*karteria*). This system included violent contentions among the youth, involving biting, gouging out of eyes, and kicking. Additionally, scourging-matches (*diamastigosis*) were held before the altar of Artemis. These practices persisted into the closing days of Sparta and were observed by Pausanias, Plutarch, and Cicero, with Plutarch noting that some individuals died from the suffering.

The army was organized into specific units known as *mora* and *enômoty*. The size of the *enômoty* varied over time and was not necessarily equal across units. Suidas records the *enômoty* as consisting of twenty-five men, while Thucydides notes that during the first battle of Mantineia (418 B.C.), they consisted of approximately thirty-two men. By the battle of Leuktra, Xenophon indicates the number had risen to thirty-six men. Because of this disciplined structure, the Lacedæmonians were regarded as the only true technicians of war, while others were seen as merely improvisational in their military affairs.

## Governance and Imperial Conduct
The conduct of the Lacedæmonian empire varied significantly depending on the leadership of its kings. Polybius notes a sharp contrast between the proceedings of King Kleombrotus and King Agesilaus. Kleombrotus is described as having a generous regard for the feelings and interests of the empire's allies. In contrast, the proceedings of Agesilaus were of the opposite character. The two kings were frequently at variance and led opposing parties; this was especially evident between Agesilaus and Kleombrotus during the latter's life.

## Social and Moral Decline
Over time, the Lacedæmonian society experienced a shift in values. Previously, citizens preferred moderate households and the company of one another over the corruption associated with flattery and the pursuit of status in cities. They once feared appearing to possess gold, but later, some began to take pride in their wealth and adornments.

Similarly, the Lacedæmonians previously restricted travel abroad to prevent citizens from adopting the "easy" ways of foreigners. Eventually, however, those who considered themselves the most earnest began to spend their time flattering others while abroad. While they once strove to be worthy of leadership, they later focused more on the act of ruling than on whether they were worthy of doing so. Consequently, while Greeks had previously sought Lacedæmonian help to lead against those who committed injustices, they later urged one another to take measures to prevent the Lacedæmonians from exercising empire again. This decline is attributed to the fact that they no longer obeyed the laws of Lycurgus or the god.

## The Fall of the Empire
The decline of Lacedæmonian power was marked by two decisive defeats. A naval defeat at Knidus ended their maritime empire. Following this, a land defeat at Leuktra occurred. At the battle of Leuktra, Epaminondas achieved a famous victory through a unique strategy, employing a slanted phalanx and using the wing containing his elite troops to decide the battle. 

The interval between the defeat at Knidus and the defeat at Leuktra was a period where the recovery of the empire remained possible. However, the defeat at Leuktra excluded the Lacedæmonians from any possibility of recovering their empire. Cicero notes that the wealth of the Lacedæmonians collapsed after Kleombrotus, fearing envy, rashly clashed with Epaminondas.
