# Biblical and Historical Criticism

The study of ancient texts and historical records is characterized by the application of critical methods to determine the original meaning of writings, the reality of historical events, and the nature of ancient religions. In the context of the Old Testament, this process is known as biblical criticism.

## Nature and Goals of Old Testament Criticism
The primary objective of Old Testament criticism is to discover the actual meaning intended by the writers, the factual nature of events in Hebrew history, and the actual state of the religion of that time. This approach differs fundamentally from homiletic or dogmatic treatments of the text, which instead seek to find specific incentives for a particular type of life or a given body of dogma within the Scripture.

The growth of this field is largely a modern phenomenon. This expansion has been driven by the discovery of fresh evidence and the removal of conditions that previously hindered the critical study of evidence. While critical methods have become dominant, there have been "anticritica" responses. For instance, in his 1906 work, *The Problem of the Old Testament considered with reference to Recent Criticism*, Dr. James Orr sought to demonstrate that the fundamental positions of the dominant school of criticism are insecure.

## Historical and Archaeological Evidence
Historical criticism has benefited from independent evidence provided by Assyrian inscriptions. These records have supported Old Testament evidence regarding the relations between the Assyrians and certain Hebrew kings, specifically Ahaz, Jehu, and Ahab. Furthermore, these inscriptions have helped clarify the confused chronology of the Hebrews found in the books of *Kings*.

Archaeology has further expanded knowledge regarding the political powers that held Palestine in subjection and the nations surrounding Israel. This has allowed the history of Israel to be related to the history of the ancient East and has raised questions regarding the origin of Hebrew culture. A notable example is the discovery of the Code of Khammurabi, which bears remarkable resemblances to the Pentateuchal codes, prompting inquiries into the relationship between Babylonian and Hebrew law.

Despite these gains, archaeology has not provided information on the Hebrew nation prior to the Hebrew invasion, with the exception of the stele of Merenptah (c. 1270 B.C.), which states, "Ysirael is desolated, its seed is not," along with some vague allusions to particular tribes. Archaeology has not provided knowledge of any Hebrew individual from this period. Consequently, the historical elements underlying the stories of the Hebrew patriarchs in Genesis must be determined through the critical study of the Old Testament. While archaeology has made the historic reality of Chedorlaomer probable—a fact some critics had previously divined—it has not proved the historical reality of the patriarch Abraham or his role in the story.

## Textual Analysis and Dating
Critical analysis is often used to date specific passages and identify textual corruptions. In the study of certain *Poems* (xxiii. 1.-xxiv. 19), the first four are commonly viewed as ancient lyrics from the early monarchy, possibly from the time of Solomon or David, inserted by E and J. However, some recent critics suggest these belong to the post-exilic period, implying a late editor replaced earlier, less edifying oracles.

Specific textual examples illustrate these critical debates:
* **The First Poem (xxiii. 7-10):** Prophesies the countless numbers and unique exaltation of Israel.
* **The Second Poem (xxiii. 18-24):** Celebrates the monarchy, its conquests, and the moral virtue of Israel.
* **The Third Poem (xxiv. 3-9):** Also celebrates the conquests and glory of the monarchy.

Critics note that the mention of "Agag" in verse 7 is unlikely to refer to the Amalekite king of 1 Sam. xv. because Amalek was too obscure and small. Other versions, such as the Sam. Pent. and the Septuagint, use "Gog," which would suggest a post-exilic date. It is probable that both "Gog" and "Agag" are textual corruptions, though "Og" has been suggested as an alternative. Additionally, some suppose the name of the king of Edom, Bela, son of Beor, is a corruption of Balaam.

## Comparative Critical Methods
Beyond the Old Testament, critical methods are applied to various ancient texts. In the study of Thucydidês, recent editors have used manuscript authority to change the final words of certain passages, a move preferred by Dr. Arnold and other critics. Similarly, recent critics have corrected the mistake of attributing certain allegations regarding the number of citizens in Athenian democratical assemblies to Thucydidês, noting that such claims were actually made by the Four Hundred.

In the realm of the New Testament, Bengel (1734) established a critical apparatus for the Greek Testament. He utilized a canon stating, *"Proclivi scriptioni praestat ardua"* ("The difficult reading is to be preferred to that which is easy"), a principle recognized by subsequent critics. Bengel's method involved stating evidence both in favor of and against particular readings, using a five-letter Greek alphabet scale ([alpha] through [epsilon]) to denote the relative importance of various readings.
