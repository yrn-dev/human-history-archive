# Great Disturbances in the Family of Herod

The domestic life of Herod was marked by significant instability and "wild disorders," which often contrasted with his external successes. These disturbances were primarily driven by his marital choices and the subsequent preferential treatment of his children.

## Marital Shifts and Initial Family Conflict
The origins of Herod's domestic troubles are linked to his fondness for his wives. Upon ascending to government, Herod sent away Doris, a woman born at Jerusalem whom he had married while he was a private person. He subsequently married Mariamne, the daughter of Alexander and granddaughter of Hyrcanus.

This marriage triggered immediate family disturbances, which intensified following Herod's return from Rome. To favor his sons by Mariamne, Herod expelled Antipater, the son of Doris, from the city, permitting him to return only during festivals. Further instability arose when Herod executed his wife's grandfather, Hyrcanus. Although Hyrcanus had returned from Parthin—tempted by the marriage of his granddaughter—Herod slew him under the pretense of suspecting a plot. The archive notes that the true provocation was Herod's belief that it was "fitter" for Hyrcanus to be king than himself.

## The Preferring of Antipater
Significant disturbances arose within the family due to Herod's preference for his eldest son, Antipater, over his other children. This perceived injury was taken "very heinously" by Alexander. The tension between the siblings was exacerbated by the influence of others; Salome and Pheroras calumniated Alexander and Aristobulus upon their return from Rome, despite the fact that Herod provided wives for them.

The sedition within the family increased further following Herod's descent into the sepulcher of David. The conflict eventually led to a cycle of accusations and reconciliations. At one point, Herod brought Alexander and Aristobulus before Cæsar to accuse them, though Alexander later defended himself and was reconciled with his father. In another instance, Herod bound Alexander, but he was again reconciled to his father through the mediation of Archelaus, the King of Cappadocia.

## Accusations and the Role of the Eunuchs
The instability of the household continued through a series of accusations. Eurycles falsely accused Herod's sons, leading Herod to bind them and write to Cæsar regarding the matter. With Cæsar's permission, Herod later accused his sons before an assembly of judges at Berytus.

Further conflict was sparked by Herod's relationship with his eunuchs, whom he favored for their beauty. One eunuch managed the principal affairs of the government, while others handled the king's drink and supper. An informant told Herod that Alexander had corrupted these eunuchs with large sums of money. Under severe torture—which the archive notes was stretched to the utmost by the tormentors out of compliance with Antipater—the eunuchs confessed that Alexander harbored an "innate hatred" for his father. 

They claimed Alexander believed Herod despaired of living much longer and was concealing his age by coloring his hair black. According to these testimonies, Alexander asserted that the kingdom would come to no one else but him, and that he had already made preparations to take the kingdom as his birthright, claiming the support of many friends and rulers.

## Contrast in Herod's Justice
The archive highlights a distinct dichotomy in Herod's administration of justice. While he was viewed as "almost inexorable" in punishing crimes committed within his own family, he was noted for being "very generous" in remitting offenses committed by others. This is illustrated by his treatment of individuals delivered up by Agrippa, whom Herod let go without harm, contrasting sharply with the severity he showed his own children.
