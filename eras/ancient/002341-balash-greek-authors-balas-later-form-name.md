# BALASH

The term Balash (or Balas) appears in historical and geographical records referring to a Sassanian monarch, a claimant to the Seleucid throne, and a variety of gemstone associated with a specific region.

## Sassanian King Balash
Balash, known in Greek accounts as Balas and later identified as a form of the name Vologaeses, served as a Sassanian king from A.D. 484 to 488. He succeeded his brother, Peroz, who had been killed in a battle against the Hephthalites (also known as the White Huns) during their invasion of Persia from the east. 

During his four-year reign, Balash was characterized as a generous and mild monarch. He is noted for making concessions to Christians and for successfully suppressing a rebellion led by his brother, Zareh. However, his tenure ended when he was deposed and blinded due to his failure to take action against his enemies. Following his removal from power, his nephew, Kavadh I, was raised to the throne.

## Alexander Balas
In the records of Josephus, Alexander Balas is described as a man who claimed to be the son of Antiochus Epiphanes. While several historians viewed him as a counterfeit of no family, Josephus believed him to be the legitimate son of Antiochus. This view is supported by the author of the First Book of Maccabees, who refers to him by his father's name. Further evidence cited by Josephus includes the fact that Philometor, the king of Egypt, gave his daughter to Alexander Balas in marriage, an act unlikely to have occurred had the king believed him to be of mean birth or a counterfeit. Alexander Balas was the father of another Antiochus, who was referred to as "The God" (Epiphanes the Victorious) on his coins and was eventually killed by Trypho through the corruption of his surgeons.

## The Balas Ruby and Badakhshan
The term "Balas" is also associated with a specific type of gemstone. The Balas Ruby (or Balais Ruby) derives its name from the form *Balakhsh*, which refers to the region of Badakhshan. This geographical area is known by various names in historical texts, including *Badascian*, *Balacian*, *Balaxcen*, *Balaxia*, and *Baldassia*. Ibn Batuta notes that the mountains of Badakhshan gave their name to the Badakhshi Ruby, commonly called *Al Balaksh*.

In terms of quality, the Balas Ruby is considered an inferior kind of stone compared to the real Ruby of Ava, similar to the spinel. Historical records from the *Masálak al Absár* mention a triangular Balas ruby weighing 50 drachms that was presented to Malek ’Adil Ketboga in Damascus, described as the finest ever seen in Arab countries. The stone has also appeared in European literature; it is mentioned by Chaucer in "Court of Love" and by Dante in *Paradiso* (ix. 67), where it is referred to as *balascio*. Albertus Magnus described the *Balagius* as the female of the Ruby Proper or Carbuncle.
