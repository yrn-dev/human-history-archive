# The Beginning of the War Between the Jews and the Romans

The conflict between the Jews and the Roman Empire was the culmination of a long history of political instability, internal disputes, and shifting alliances. The war officially broke out in the twelfth year of the reign of Nero, following events involving Cestius and subsequent hostile assaults by the Jews.

## Historical Precedents and Roman Influence
The Roman presence in the region was established following a period of internal strife among the posterity of the sons of Asamoneus, who had previously ejected Antiochus Epiphanes from Jerusalem after he had held the city by force for three years and three months. These internal quarrels brought the Romans and Pompey into their settlement. 

Over time, Roman influence expanded through various leaders. Pompey besieged Jerusalem and took the city by force after the citizens shut their gates against him. Later, Crassus entered Judea and pillaged the Temple before marching against the Parthians. Other Roman figures, such as Gabinius, were active in the region, overcoming Alexander, the son of Aristobulus, and the Nabateans in battle. During the conflict between Caesar and Egypt, the Jews became confederates with Caesar, and Antipater established a friendship with him, leading to honors for the Jews from both the Romans and Athenians.

## Internal Instability and the Role of Herod
The political landscape was further complicated by the rule of Herod, the son of Antipater, who dissolved the existing government and brought Sosins upon the people. Following the death of Herod, a sedition occurred while Augustus was the Roman emperor and Quintilius Varus was present in the country. 

Later periods saw continued tension. Under the emperor Caius, Petronius was sent into Syria to make war against the Jews because they would not receive the emperor's statue. The region also experienced various leadership shifts, including the tetrarchy of Philip and the influence of King Agrippa, who was expert in the customs and questions of the Jews.

## The Immediate Causes of the War
The immediate escalation toward war involved grievances against Roman officials and acts of defiance. A significant point of contention was the conduct of Florus. While some among the people claimed they did not wish to fight the Romans themselves, but specifically against Florus due to the suffering he had caused, King Agrippa argued that their actions already constituted a war against Rome. 

Agrippa pointed to two specific acts of rebellion:
1. The failure to pay the tribute due to Caesar.
2. The cutting off of the temple cloisters from the tower Antonia.

Agrippa advised that the occasion for revolt could be prevented if the tribute were paid and the cloisters were joined together again, noting that the tribute was no longer being paid to Florus, nor did the citadel belong to him. Agrippa maintained the doctrine that the vast Roman empire was supported by Divine Providence, making any attempt to destroy it futile.

## The Nature of the Conflict
Once the war began, it was characterized by intense zeal and a disregard for death among the Jewish fighters. Some fighters, driven by the laws of their forefathers and a desire for liberty, viewed yielding to the Romans as an act of treason. They pressured leaders, such as Josephus, to maintain their reputation for manhood and wisdom by fighting to the end rather than accepting slavery.

As the conflict progressed, the Romans faced significant challenges. During the siege of the city, the Romans constructed banks to facilitate the capture of Jerusalem. However, they found the Jewish defenders to be invincible in their alacrity and courage, despite suffering from famine, sedition, and the miseries of war. The Romans observed that the Jews' courageous souls were superior to their afflictions, which led the Roman forces to increase their guards around their banks.
