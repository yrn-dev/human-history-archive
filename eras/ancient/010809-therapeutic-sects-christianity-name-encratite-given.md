# Therapeutic Sects and the Encratites

The term "therapeutic" appears in historical records across diverse contexts, ranging from ancient ascetic sects and early Christian movements to modern medical systems.

## Asceticism and the Encratites
In the context of early religious movements, the Therapeutic sects, along with the Essene sects of the Bani Israel, practiced the eschewing of property and marriage. Within Christianity, the name "Encratite" was applied to individuals who repudiated the use of wine and the practice of marriage. Rather than forming a distinct sect, the Encratite ideal represented a widespread impulse.

Early popular apocryphal histories, such as the *Acts of Thecla* (which likely dates to the first century), depict the apostles insisting that converts either avoid contracting wedlock or dissolve existing marital ties. This repudiation of marriage by wives or betrothed women often resulted in popular persecution and domestic friction.

## Practices and Beliefs of the Encratite Ideal
The drive toward virginity was accompanied by the institution of *virgines subintroductae*, or "spiritual wives." This practice was based on the assumption that the grace of baptism restored the original purity of life shared by Adam and Eve before the Fall. These rigors were encouraged in the *Shepherd of Hermas*, a text read in church until the 4th century and originating from Rome.

The practice of spiritual wives was common in several regions and periods:
* **African Churches:** These practices led to abuses that required the energy of a Cyprian to manage.
* **Antioch:** The practice remained rife in 260.
* **Celtic Church:** It was detected in the church of St Patrick and among Celtic elders in northern France as late as the 7th century.
* **Syriac Church:** Relations between the "daughters and Sons of the Resurrection" prevailed as late as 340. In Syriac churches into the 4th century, marriage was viewed as incompatible with the perfection of the initiated.
* **Middle Ages:** The Albigenses and other dissident sects continued the practice. For these groups, it served a dual purpose: elders could prove their chastity while eluding inquisitors. This was effective because inquisitors were less likely to suspect a man of catharism—a belief that viewed marriage as the "greater adultery" (*maius adulterium*)—if he appeared to be cohabiting with a woman.

Early councils generally condemned these customs, as well as the practice of self-emasculation. Eventually, within the Catholic church, those wishing to follow the Encratite ideal moved to monasteries. Additionally, renunciation of wedlock was imposed on the faithful during lifelong or lengthy terms of penance for sins. Later, as monkery replaced primitive baptism and rigorous penance, virginity and celibacy—along with the renunciation of money-making and property—were considered essential.

## Homoeopathy as a Therapeutic Sect
In the field of medicine, homoeopathy is described as a distinctive system of therapeutics. It is based on the law *similia similibus curentur*, a principle announced in 1796 by S. C. F. Hahnemann, a German native of Meissen who discovered the principle while experimenting with cinchona bark in 1790.

The essential tenets of homoeopathy, contrasted with "orthodox" therapeutics (or "allopathy"), are:
1. The cure of disease is achieved through drugs capable of producing symptoms in a healthy person similar to those of the disease being treated.
2. To determine a drug's curative virtues, it must be "proved" on healthy individuals of both sexes in gradually increasing doses.

The resulting record of "drug-diseases," verified by repetition on "provers," forms the homoeopathic materia medica. While this includes the uses and preparations of the orthodox pharmacopoeia, it adds these specific "provings."

Despite its contributions to the study of the physiological action of drugs and the use of simpler forms of powerful drugs like belladonna, nux vomica, and aconite, homoeopathy has been stigmatized as a "dissenting sect" within the medical profession. Some homoeopathists in the United States joined the prominent medical association by agreeing to remove the name "homoeopathy" from their hospitals, colleges, dispensaries, asylums, and periodicals.

In Denmark, homoeopathy was unknown until 1821, when it was adopted by medical practitioner Hans Christian Lund. Lund translated the works of Hahnemann and other members of the school into Danish, though some Danes had consulted Hahnemann prior to 1821.
