# Marriage and Procreation in Ancient Societies

The historical records of various ancient cultures reveal diverse legal, social, and personal approaches to marriage, the role of the wife, and the necessity of producing children. These practices ranged from strict monogamy and the use of handmaidens to the complexities of royal succession and the social expectations of noble birth.

## Legal Frameworks and Domestic Arrangements
In certain legal codes, monogamy served as the general rule. However, provisions existed for husbands whose wives were childless. A wife in this position might provide her husband with a maid—who was not a wife herself—to bear children. In such arrangements, the children were reckoned as the wife's, and the wife remained the mistress of the maid. While the wife could degrade the maid to slavery again for insolence, she was prohibited from selling the maid if the maid had borne children for the husband.

The law further stipulated that if a wife provided a maid for this purpose, the husband was not permitted to take a concubine. If she refused, however, he was free to do so. A concubine was considered a wife, albeit of a lower rank, and the first wife held no power over her. Concubines were free women, often dowered for marriage, and their children were legitimate. Divorce for a concubine was subject to the same conditions as for a wife.

Regarding the welfare of the wife, husbands were bound to maintain a wife who became a chronic invalid in their shared home, unless she chose to take her dowry and return to her father's house. In these instances, the husband was permitted to remarry, and all resulting children remained legal heirs and legitimate.

## Procreation and Royal Succession
The pressure to produce heirs was particularly acute among royalty. In Sparta, King Anaxandrides was urged by the Ephors to divorce his beloved wife because she bore him no children. The Ephors argued that the race of Eurysthenes must not become extinct and that marrying another would please the Spartans. Anaxandrides refused, stating that his wife had done him no wrong and that the counsel to send her away was not honorable.

Similarly, King Ariston of Sparta married two wives but remained childless. Believing he was not the cause of this barrenness, he married a third wife. This third wife had been the wife of a Spartan friend of Ariston; she was noted for being the fairest woman in Sparta, a transformation attributed to the intervention of the goddess Helen after her nurse prayed for her unshapeliness to be removed.

## Biblical and Hebrew Traditions
In the account of Abram, who dwelt near the oak called Ogyges in Canaan, Abram expressed unease at the barrenness of his wife, Sarai. Following a command from God, Sarai brought an Egyptian handmaiden to Abram's bed to obtain children. The handmaiden eventually bore a son named Ismael. However, this arrangement led to conflict; the handmaiden became arrogant toward Sarai, leading Sarai to punish her. The handmaiden fled into the wilderness but was commanded by a Divine Angel to return and submit to her mistress, after which she obtained forgiveness.

Other Hebrew traditions emphasized the importance of marrying free virgins born of good parents to ensure that children were liberal and virtuous. These guidelines discouraged free men from marrying slaves or harlots. Legal protections existed for wives accused of not being virgins at the time of marriage; if a husband brought a rash or impudent accusation that was proven false, he was punished with forty stripes (save one) and required to pay fifty shekels to the wife's father. If the woman was convicted of corruption, the punishment was stoning for commoners or being burnt alive for the daughter of a priest.

## Personal Conduct and Family Values
Different historical figures modeled varying approaches to the marital bond. One account describes a man who viewed his wife and children as the "holiest of things," asserting that beating them was a sacrilegious act. He valued being a good husband over being a great statesman and admired the patience shown by Sokrates the Philosopher toward an ill-tempered wife. This man also prioritized his presence during the birth and early care of his son, and his wife encouraged a brotherly regard for the children of their slaves by suckling them herself.

In other instances, marital dynamics involved tension over public behavior. Michal, the daughter of Saul and wife of David, criticized David for dancing in an "unseemly manner" and uncovering himself before servants and handmaidens. David responded that he was not ashamed to do what was acceptable to God. While Michal had no children with David, she later bore five children after being married to the man her father had originally given her to.

## Social and Political Proposals
The intersection of procreation and state interest is seen in the proposal made by Quintus Hortensius to Cato. Hortensius suggested that it was advantageous for the state if women in their youth did not "check their procreative power." He initially proposed that Cato's daughter, Porcia, be given to him to beget children, arguing that a community of offspring among worthy men would diffuse virtue among families. When Cato rejected this as strange, Hortensius instead asked for Cato's wife, Marcia, noting that Cato already had enough children.
