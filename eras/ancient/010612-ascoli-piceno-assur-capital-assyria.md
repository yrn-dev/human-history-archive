# ASCOLI PICENO AND ASSUR

## Ascoli Piceno
Ascoli Piceno (ancient *Ausculum*) is a town and episcopal see located in the Marches, a territorial division of Italy. It serves as the capital of the province of Ascoli Piceno. The town is situated 500 feet above sea level on the southern bank of the Tronto (ancient *Truentus*) river at its confluence with the Castellano. It is surrounded by lofty mountains and is located 17 miles west of Porto d'Ascoli and 53 miles south of Ancona. As of 1901, the town had a population of 12,256, while the commune totaled 28,608.

The town's architecture reflects various historical periods. Roman remains include the Porta Romana, a double-arched gate, and massive ancient city walls constructed from rectangular stone blocks 2 feet in height. Earlier fortifications have also been discovered at this site. The church of S. Gregorio was built into a Roman tetrastyle Corinthian temple, preserving the *cella* and two columns. Additionally, the church and convent of the Annunziata, which features two cloisters and a fresco by Cola d'Amatrice, are built upon large Roman concrete substructures. The site of a Roman theatre is also distinguishable.

The town features several pre-Renaissance buildings. The colonnaded market-place houses the original Palazzo del Comune (now the prefecture), which is Gothic with Renaissance additions, and the Gothic church of S. Francesco. The cathedral, originally Romanesque, underwent significant alterations and was restored in 1888 by Count Giuseppe Sacconi, with dome frescoes by Cesare Mariani. The cathedral's baptistery retains its ancient character, and the churches of SS. Vincenzo ed Anastasio and S. Vittore are also noted as good Romanesque buildings. Other landmarks include a castle with medieval fortifications and the fortress of the Malatesta, constructed in 1349.

## Assur: The Capital of Assyria
Assur (also spelled Asur or Ashur) was the primitive capital of Assyria. It is currently represented by the mounds of Kaleh Sherghat (Qal'at Shergat) on the west bank of the Tigris, positioned nearly midway between the Upper and Lower Zab. The city grew around the great temple of the god Assur, whose foundation is attributed to the High-priest Uspia.

For many centuries, the city and its surrounding district—which became known as the land of Assur or Assyria—were governed by high-priests under Babylonian suzerainty. As Babylonian power decayed, these high-priests became independent kings, and Assur became the capital of an important kingdom. The city was protected by a wall of crude brick on stone foundations, reinforced with burnt brick. Around 1270 B.C., Tukulti-Masu (or Tukulti-Inaristi) dug a deep moat outside the walls, and the land side was further defended by an outwork called a *salkhu*.

In the 15th century B.C., the city expanded south to include a "new town." The walls featured several entrances, including the gate of the Tigris, the gate of the Sun-god, and the gate of Assur. On the river side, a quay was constructed from limestone and burnt brick cemented with bitumen. The northern part of the city contained the temples and their lofty towers.

Assur remained the capital long after Assyria became the dominant power in western Asia, though it was eventually supplanted by other cities approximately 60 miles to the north: Dur-Sargina (Khorsabad), Calah (Nimrud), and Nineveh.

## The God Assur
Assur was the chief god of Assyria and the patron deity of the city of Assur. While the names of the god and city are identical, an older form, *A-shir* (meaning "leader"), suggests an early attempt to differentiate the two. It is probable that the god's name predates the city's, though some theories suggest the city was deified.

Originally a solar deity symbolized by the winged disk, Assur evolved into a god of war as Assyria became a military power; this transition is represented in art by adding a man with a bow to the winged disk. The worship of Assur eventually overshadowed other Babylonian deities transferred to Assyria, such as Adad, Ea, Sin, Shamash, Nergal, and Ninib, who took on Assur's warlike traits. Only Marduk retained significant independence due to Assyrian respect for southern traditions.

Assur presided over the fortunes of the state. Assyrian armies marched in his name and with his approval, carrying his symbol into battle. Victories were invariably ascribed to the help of Assur.

## History of the Assyrian State
The history of Assyria can be traced back to approximately 2500 B.C., though it achieved political prominence around 2000 B.C. Early rulers were called *patesis*. Under Khammurabi, a Samsi-Hadad served as a vassal-prince at Assur. The monarchy's foundation is attributed to Zulilu, who lived after Bel-kapkapi (1900 B.C.).

Assyria's power grew at the expense of Babylonia. Assur-yuballidh of Assyria corresponded with Amenophis IV of Egypt and intervened in Babylonian politics to place Burna-buryas on the throne. By 1300 B.C., Shalmaneser I, the great-great-grandson of Assur-yuballidh, openly claimed supremacy in western Asia, founding Calah and settling colonists in Cappadocia. His son, Tukulti-In-aristi, conquered Babylon and killed its king, Bitilyasu, establishing Assyria as the mistress of the oriental world.

In later periods, Assyria faced conflicts with Elam, which was eventually devastated by Assyrian forces despite the efforts of Assur-bani-pal to avoid the contest. However, these struggles left Assyria exhausted and drained of wealth and manpower. This vulnerability made it difficult for Assyria to defend its frontiers against the Scythians (or Manda), who harassed the borders and established power in the old kingdom of Ellip.
