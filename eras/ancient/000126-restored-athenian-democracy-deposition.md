# THE RESTORED ATHENIAN DEMOCRACY

The restoration of the Athenian democracy followed a period of significant political instability characterized by the rise and fall of oligarchical regimes. The most immediate precursor to the restoration was the rule of the Four Hundred, an oligarchy installed in the senate-house around February or March 411 B.C. This regime lasted for approximately four or five months, creating a state of danger and distraction that nearly brought Athens under the control of its enemies. The Four Hundred were deposed around July of that same year, leading to the re-establishment of the democracy.

## The Transition to Full Democracy
Following the deposition of the Four Hundred, there was a period of transition regarding the scope of political rights. While some historians, such as K. F. Hermann, suggest the old democracy was restored upon the return of Alkibiadês in 407 B.C., other evidence points to a gradual expansion of suffrage. Initially, there was a period involving the suffrage of a privileged group known as the Five Thousand. However, within a year of the Four Hundred's deposition, this restricted suffrage expanded to include all Athenians without exception.

By June 410 B.C., at the start of the archonship of Glaukippus, the full democracy was in action. During this time, the dikasts, the senate of Five Hundred, and other civil functionaries were renewed according to ancient democratical practices.

## The Decree of Demophantus
The intensity of the democratical feeling following the restoration was manifested in a remarkable decree proposed and carried by Demophantus. This decree prescribed a specific form of oath to be taken by all Athenians, swearing to maintain the democratical constitution. This act was intended as an emphatic proclamation of sentiment, similar to a solemn oath taken the previous year at Samos.

The decree was not only passed in the public assembly and senate but was engraved on a column located near the door of the senate-house. The nature of this oath indicates that the restored constitution granted political rights to all Athenians, rather than limiting them to the Five Thousand. This engraved column remained and its words were read for at least eighty years, remaining visible as late as the time of the orator Lykurgus. The decree eventually became invalid following the expulsion of the Thirty, when a resolution was passed to disregard laws enacted before the archonship of Eukleidês unless they were specifically re-enacted.

## Character of the Athenian People
The restoration after the Four Hundred is often analyzed alongside the later restoration following the overthrow of the Thirty. Historians have noted a "wonderful absence of all reactionary violence" by the Athenian people after the Four Hundred were overthrown, despite severe provocations. Thucydidês, though not a friend to the democracy, praised the moderate and gentle bearing of the people during this period.

This pattern of behavior—recovering democracy without vindictive impulse—is viewed as a permanent attribute of the Athenian popular character rather than a random occurrence. Such conduct serves as evidence against characterizations of the Athenian people as being habitually greedy, unjust, jealous, irascible, or giddy.

## The Oligarchical Conspiracy of 411 B.C.
The democracy's temporary loss in 411 B.C. was the result of a concerted conspiracy. While Pisander was the chief ostensible agent in putting down the democracy, the operation was orchestrated by Antiphon, described as one of the best men of his day in Athens. Antiphon used his talent for contriving measures and persuasion to prepare the way for the catastrophe, though he avoided the public eye due to the multitude's suspicion of his talent.

Other key figures in the subversion of the democracy included:
*   **Phrynichus:** Noted for his extreme zeal for the oligarchy and his distrust of Alkibiadês.
*   **Theramenês:** The son of Hagnon, who was considered as capable in debate as he was in council.

The conspiracy was facilitated by the "hetæries," or political clubs. The Athenian assembly eventually voted to relinquish their democracy, though this was done unwillingly and under the promise of Persian aid for the war. This event was a significant disruption of a freedom the Athenian people had enjoyed for almost a hundred years since the deposition of the tyrants.

## Chronological Notes
There are discrepancies in historical records regarding the dates of democratic restoration. Diodorus is noted for a chronological error, placing the restoration in 401 B.C., whereas the actual date was 403 B.C. This error incorrectly lengthens the interval between the surrender of Athens and the re-establishment of the democracy. Plutarch is also suggested to have perceived this interval as longer than it truly was.
