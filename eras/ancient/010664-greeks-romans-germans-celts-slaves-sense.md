# Greeks, Romans, Germans, Celts, and Slaves

## Linguistic and Ethnological Classifications
The term "Aryan" has been applied by various philologists in different ways. Max Muller used "Aryan" as a short term for the body of languages known as Indo-Germanic or Indo-European. According to Muller, "Aryas" are defined strictly as those who speak Aryan languages, regardless of their blood, color, hair, skull, or anatomical characteristics. In this linguistic sense, the term encompasses Hindus, Greeks, Romans, Germans, Celts, and Slaves. Muller asserted that even the darkest Hindus represent an earlier stage of Aryan speech and thought than the fairest Scandinavians, emphasizing that the label refers to the grammar of the language rather than hereditary race.

## The Greeks and the "Barbarians"
The Greeks divided the world into Greeks and "barbarians," further categorizing the latter into four primary classes: the Ethiopians, the Indians, the Scythians, and the Celts. The Greeks applied the name "Celts" to all inhabitants of Gaul. However, some sources suggest these were purely geographical divisions based on Greek cosmographical ignorance rather than true racial affiliation. For example, Gaul actually contained three distinct nations—the Gauls properly so called, the Aquitani, and the Belgæ—who differed in their laws, language, and institutions.

In ancient Greece, the concept of the "asylum" was a distinct feature. An asylum was an inviolable refuge where it was a religious crime to forcibly remove any person or thing under the protection of a deity. While many temples and altars were generally inviolable, absolute security was guaranteed at specific sanctuaries, such as those of Apollo at Delos, Poseidon in Calauria, and Zeus Lycaeus in Arcadia.

## The Romans and the Celts
The Romans encountered the Celts (Gauls) in various military conflicts. In one engagement, Roman cavalry achieved a victory of an "unparalleled and wonderful kind," routing large numbers of horse and foot. This led to the fall of Mediolanum, which the Cisalpine Gauls regarded as their metropolis. Following the defeat and death of their king, the Gæsatæ returned home, and the Celts surrendered their other cities to the Romans, receiving moderate peace terms.

The Romans also utilized mercenaries, including Cretans, in their army. In Rome, the social structure included a variety of slaves. Greek slaves were highly valued for their dexterity and superior acquirements, often filling higher positions within great families. In contrast, slaves from "barbarous nations" (those who were not Greek) were relegated to meaner purposes.

## Slavery in Roman Civilization
The status of slaves in Rome underwent significant changes over four hundred years. During the second century B.C., war-captives were abundant, and slaves possessed no rights, facing brutal treatment. By the first century A.D., the attitude of Roman civilization shifted. Slaves became more expensive as captives became less abundant, and owners realized that the comfort and profit derived from slaves increased with the slaves' own self-respect.

Influenced by the higher mentality of Greece, Roman harshness was qualified. New restrictions on cruelty were implemented: masters were forbidden from selling slaves to fight beasts, and a form of slave marriage was recognized. Slaves were granted wages as a stimulus and property rights through what was known as the *peculium*. In certain agricultural regions where gang work was not practical, slaves transitioned into serfs, working for their owners during specific seasons or paying a portion of their produce. Additionally, those who had been maltreated by their masters were among the classes of people who claimed the right of asylum.

## Germans and Celts in Comparison to Greeks
There are noted analogies between the Germans and Celts and the Greeks, though significant differences exist. While Grecian progress is described as self-operated, German progress was brought about by violent external influences. The primitive myths of the Germans were later operated upon by Christianity and Roman civilization, leading to the degradation of gods like Odin into men.

The Teutonic and Scandinavian epics share a heroic character and self-expanding subject with the Grecian epic. However, the epics of the Middle Ages were not as closely interwoven with religion or as solitary as the Grecian ones. In England, early history was conceived as beginning with Brute the Trojan, a belief held tenaciously until the seventeenth century.
