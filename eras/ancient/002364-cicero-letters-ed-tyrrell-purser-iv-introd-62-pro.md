# Cicero: Letters and Orations

The written works of Marcus Tullius Cicero, particularly his correspondence and legal speeches, serve as primary ancient authorities for the study of Roman history and the life of Julius Caesar. His surviving texts provide detailed insights into the political maneuvers, personal relationships, and legal disputes of the late Roman Republic.

## The Correspondence (Letters)
The letters of Cicero, notably those edited by Tyrrell and Purser, offer a window into the private and public affairs of his contemporaries. These include the *Ad Atticum* (letters to Atticus), *Ad Familiares* (letters to family and friends), and *Ad Diversos*.

The correspondence documents critical interactions between Cicero and the leading figures of his era. For instance, Cicero preserved copies of letters from Julius Caesar, including one in which Caesar begged him to stay at Rome (*Ad Attic.* ix. 16) and another urging him to keep quiet (*Ad Attic.* x. 8). Other letters reveal Cicero's interactions with various political figures, such as his correspondence with the orator and historian Lucius Lucceius. Cicero once earnestly requested that Lucceius write a separate history of Cicero's own consulship, suggesting that a panegyric by Lucceius would carry more weight than Cicero's own praises in Greek and Latin. Cicero even hinted that Lucceius did not need to sacrifice laudation for the sake of accuracy.

The letters also contain personal observations and social commentary. In *Ad Attic.* i. 18, Cicero alludes to Caius Memmius, a man of ability but dissolute habits, who had "initiated the wife of M. Lucullus in his own sacred rites," leading to a divorce. In other instances, Cicero's letters were used to make pointed remarks about a person's origins; for example, he once referenced a man's bored ears to imply he was not of genuine Italian stock.

## Legal Orations and Speeches
Cicero's speeches are essential for understanding the legal and political conflicts of the period. Among these is the *Pro Balbo*, which Orelli uses to identify a Memmius as a quaestor under Pompey during the Spanish campaign.

Another significant work is the *Pro Marcello*, delivered on behalf of Marcus Claudius Marcellus. Marcellus was a thorough aristocrat and an eloquent speaker who had been an inveterate opponent of Julius Caesar. After retiring to Mytilene following the Battle of Pharsalus to study philosophy and practice rhetoric, Marcellus was pardoned by Caesar following an appeal by the senate and his cousin. Cicero delivered the *Pro Marcello* speech on this occasion, though Marcellus was later murdered in the Peiraeus by an attendant named P. Magius Chilo.

Additionally, Cicero delivered the *Pro P. Sestio* in defense of Publius Sextius (or Sestius), a *tribunus plebis* who had worked to secure Cicero's own recall. Sextius was tried on charges of raising a tumult (*de vi*) during the popular meeting where Cicero's recall was proposed; Cicero later wrote to his brother Quintus regarding Sextius's acquittal.

## Historical Influence and Preservation
The works of Cicero were of such importance that later scholars sought to preserve and explain them. Quintus Asconius Pedianus, a Roman grammarian and historian active during the reigns of Claudius and Nero, compiled historical commentaries on five of Cicero's speeches: *in Pisonem*, *pro Scauro*, *pro Milone*, *pro Cornelio*, and *in toga Candida*. Asconius utilized various sources for these commentaries, including shorthand reports (*commentarii*) of Cicero's unpublished speeches, Tiro's life of Cicero, and the writings of contemporaries like Atticus and Varro.
