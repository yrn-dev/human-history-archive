# Gudea of Lagash

Gudea served as the *patesi*, or high-priest, of the city of Lagash. In the political structure of the Babylonian states, the *patesi* preceded the king; the state typically grew around a sanctuary where the god was the nominal ruler, and the human high-priest acted as the god's viceregent. Gudea's tenure is provisionally dated to 2700 B.C.

## Political Affiliations and Succession
Gudea was a vassal of Dungi, whose reign lasted more than 51 years. Dungi was the son and successor of Ur-Gur, a prolific builder who restored temples for Bel at Nippur, Ishtar at Erech, the Sun-god at Larsa, and the Moon-god at Ur. Following his own period of leadership, Gudea was succeeded by his son, Ur-Ningirsu. In terms of military achievements, Gudea claims to have conquered Anshan in Elam.

## Architectural and Artistic Contributions
Gudea is characterized as a great builder. To facilitate his construction projects and the creation of statues, he sourced materials from across western Asia:
* **Timber and Wood:** Cedar wood was brought from the Amanus mountains, and timber was sourced from Dilmun in the Persian Gulf.
* **Stone:** Quarried stones were obtained from Lebanon, and dolerite was brought from Magan, identified as the Sinaitic peninsula.
* **Precious Materials:** Gold and precious stones were acquired from the desert region between Egypt and Palestine, while copper was sourced from northern Arabia.

Several of Gudea's statues are currently housed in the Louvre. These works are carved from Sinaitic dolerite. One specific statue (identified as statue E) features a plan of Gudea's palace carved onto its lap, complete with an attached scale of measurement. Six of his statues were given special names and received offerings in a manner similar to the statues of gods.

## Archaeological Evidence at Tello
The ancient city of Lagash is identified as Tello. Excavations conducted there between 1877 and 1881 by the French consul de Sarzec brought to light monuments from the pre-Semitic age, including the aforementioned diorite statues of Gudea. Inscriptions on these statues confirm that the stone originated in Magan. 

Further excavations at Tello and its surrounding areas extended the known history of the city back to at least 4000 B.C. Among the discoveries was a collection of more than 30,000 tablets. These tablets were found arranged on shelves during the time of Gudea (c. 2700 B.C.).
