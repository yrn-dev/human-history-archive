# Euphrates

The Euphrates is a major river that serves as a significant geographical marker across various historical contexts, from the ancient Babylonian plains to the marches of the Greek army under Xenophon. It has historically functioned as a boundary between regions, such as separating the independent kingdom of Cappadocia from Armenia.

## Ancient Babylonia and Irrigation
In the Babylonian plain, the Euphrates was the primary source for an elaborate and skillfully planned network of irrigation canals. This engineering system reclaimed the region from an uninhabitable and pestiferous swamp, transforming it into one of the most fertile countries in the world. The science of irrigation in this region was highly advanced; according to Herodotus, wheat in this area commonly returned two hundred-fold to the sower, and occasionally three hundred-fold. Pliny noted that wheat was cut twice, and Berossus observed that various fruits and grains—including sesame, barley, palms, apples, and ochrys—grew wild, as wheat continues to do near Anah.

Three chief canals carried the waters of the Euphrates to the Tigris above Babylon:
*   **The Zabzallat canal (Nahr Sarsar):** Ran from Faluja to Ctesiphon.
*   **The Kutha canal:** Ran from Sippara to Madain, passing Tell Ibrahim or Kutha.
*   **The King's canal (Ar-Malcha):** Conducted from the Euphrates toward Upi (or Opis), which H. Winckler identified as being close to Seleucia on the western side of the Tigris.

Additionally, the Pallacopas (called Pallukkatu in Neo-Babylonian texts) started from Faluja and ran parallel to the western bank of the Euphrates as far as Iddaratu (possibly Teredon), supplying a large lake near Borsippa.

## Biblical and Regional Geography
The Euphrates is associated with the location of Pethor. According to the book of Deuteronomy (xxiii. 4), Pethor is placed in Mesopotamia. In other accounts, Balak, the king of Moab, sent princes to Balaam at Pethor on the Euphrates to request that he curse the Israelites (Bani Israel). While some scholars, such as A. Jeremias and Winckler, suggest the "River" mentioned in these contexts might refer to a "Ravine" (nahal) in Egypt or Musri on the southern frontier of Judea, it has been traditional to interpret the term in its ordinary sense as the Euphrates. In this tradition, Pethor is identified with a "Pitru" mentioned in an inscription of Shalmaneser II, located on a tributary of the Euphrates.

## The March of the Greeks
The Euphrates, specifically the Eastern Euphrates, was a critical objective for the Greek army described by Xenophon. The army sought to cross the river at the nearest available ford during mid-winter. There is scholarly debate regarding where the river remained fordable during this season:
*   **Professor Koch** argues the river was fordable a little above its confluence with the Tscharbahur (approximately latitude 39° 3′), a route that aligns with the pass from Mush to Erzerum.
*   **Mr. Ainsworth and Colonel Chesney** suggest it would not be fordable below the confluence with the river of Khanus (Khinnis).

Following their crossing, the Greeks passed a warm spring on their third or fourth day of travel. Professor Koch identifies this as the only warm spring south of the Bingöldagh mountains, located in the district of Wardo near the village of Bashkan.

The geography surrounding the river also presented significant challenges. Mr. Ainsworth describes the country on the left bank of the Euphrates before reaching Pylæ as being full of narrow valleys and hills, creating difficulties for the movement of an army. Regarding specific locations along the river, there is discussion concerning Charmandê; while some identify it as the modern town of Hit, others believe it was located lower down and nearly opposite Pylæ.

## Tributaries and Water-Sheds
The Eastern Euphrates is fed by several affluents. The Teleboas—described by Xenophon as beautiful but not of great size—is identified by Professor Koch and Mr. Ainsworth as the Kara-Su (or Black River). The Kara-Su flows through the plain of Mush into the Eastern Euphrates. The region contains a water-shed, specifically a saddle separating the Lake of Van from the mountain Ali-Dagh, which divides the affluents of the Tigris from those of the Eastern Euphrates.
