# Redemption and the Concept of the Father

## Gnostic Conceptions of Redemption
Among the later followers of Basilides, redemption was conceived as the revelation of mystic names. This belief was rooted in the conviction that the rulers of the several heavens possessed names that were kept as weighty secrets. It was believed that any individual who knew these names could pass through all the heavens after death to reach the supreme God.

In this framework, Christ was believed to possess a mystic name—identified as *Caulacau* (Hebrew: *QAW LAQAW*)—which provided the power necessary for him to descend through the heavens to earth and subsequently ascend to the Father. Basilides' form of Gnosticism is noted for its proximity to Persian dualism and its relationship to the doctrines of Satornil. Unlike many other Gnostic sects, Basilides' teachings lacked the idea of a fall within the godhead or the figure of the fallen female god, Sophia Achamoth.

## Financial Redemption Systems
In the context of 19th-century American banking, redemption referred to the process of exchanging bank notes for legal tender at par. In New England, a system emerged requiring the prompt redemption of notes, pioneered by the New England Bank in 1814. By 1824, the "Suffolk system" was implemented, wherein banks deposited a redemption fund into the Suffolk Bank in Boston. This bank then redeemed notes and sent them back to the originating banks for collection. This system operated successfully until 1858, with redemptions totaling $400,000,000 in that year alone, while the circulation of New England banks was less than $40,000,000. The primary merit of this system was its ability to keep the volume of circulation adjusted to business requirements. In Vermont, an act of 1842 provided legal sanction for this by remitting a 1% tax on bank capital for banks that maintained sufficient deposits in Boston to ensure their bills were uniformly redeemed at par.

Conversely, the national bank redemption system was described as defective. Under existing law, only a 5% reserve was kept in Washington for current redemption purposes. Because redemptions were not authorized in other locations, notes reached the treasury on average only once every two years.

## Territorial and Political Redemption
In the political history of France, the concept of redemption appeared in the Treaty of Arras (1435) between Charles VII and Philip the Good, the duke of Burgundy. As part of the agreement, the king gave the duke several countships, including Ponthieu, Auxerre, Bar-sur-Seine, and Mâcon. Additionally, the king granted the towns of the Somme (such as Péronne, Roye, and Montdidier), while specifically reserving the right of redemption for these towns.

### Theological and Philosophical Perspectives
In the Gnostic tradition of the followers of Basilides, the Father is the destination of the ascent of Christ and the ultimate goal for those who possess the secret names of the heavenly rulers.

In the philosophy of Descartes, the relationship between the creator and the created is defined by a strict separation. Descartes posits that neither mind nor matter can be conceived as existing apart from God, asserting that strictly speaking, God alone is a substance. However, Descartes' vision of God is one of monotheism rather than Christianity; he conceives of God as abstract will or self-consciousness. In this view, the material world is not a manifestation of God but a creation resulting from an act of bare volition. Consequently, the world remains a foreign matter to God, whom he acts upon from without, and in which God is not revealed.
