# Western Colonies of Greece — In Epirus, Italy, Sicily, and Gaul

The westward expansion of Grecian colonization, documented with authentic names and dates, began from the 11th Olympiad. While earlier attempts are considered probable, these likely consisted of detached bands of marauders or volunteer emigrants. Lacking official filiation from a known Grecian city's prytaneium, these early settlers often claimed descent from victorious heroes of the Trojan war to establish an honorable origin.

## Colonies in and near Epirus
Epirus is an ancient district of Northern Greece located along the Ionian Sea, extending from the Ambracian gulf in the south to the Acroceraunian promontory in the north. The region is characterized by mountainous terrain and the Pindus chain, and was historically celebrated for its horses and cattle rather than corn. In ancient times, the Epirots were divided into fourteen independent tribes, most notably the Molossi, the Thesproti, and the Chaones.

The states of Greece proper established several colonies on the coast of Epirus to serve as stepping-stones toward the West and the Adriatic. Corinth founded the flourishing colony of Ambracia, as well as Korkyra. Korkyra was a strategically important post that facilitated voyages from the Peloponnesus to Italy and traffic with Epirus, which was non-Hellenic at the time. Other settlements in the region include Leukas, Anaktorium, Apollonia, and Epidamnus. In Thesprotia, the colonies of Pandosia, Bucheta, and Elatria originated from Elis.

## Grecian Settlements in Italy and Sicily
The Greek settlements in the south of Italy and Sicily are referred to as Magna Græcia. In Sicily, authenticated colonization began in 735 B.C. with the foundation of Naxos by Theoklês. This was followed by a rapid multiplication of colonies, including Syracuse, Katana, Leontini, Gela, and Megara. Other Sicilian settlements included Zanklê (later Messênê), Agrigentum, Selinûs, and Himera, along with sub-colonies such as Kamarina, Kasmenæ, and Akræ.

In Italy, the colony of Cumæ in Campania was established earlier than the Sicilian colonies, though its exact date is unknown. Cumæ prospered between 700 and 500 B.C. before declining due to the despotism of Aristodêmus and invasions by Samnites and Tuscans. Other significant Italian colonies included Sybaris, Krotôn, and Rhêgium. The city of Tarentum was founded by Phalanthus the œkist and the Partheniæ.

The population of these regions was mixed. In Sicily, the Greeks encountered the Elymi, Sikans, and Sikels, as well as Phenicians. Over time, the Sikans and Sikels were gradually Hellenized. In Italy, the Greeks encountered the Iapygians and Messapians. Some Epirotic tribes were ethnically connected to those in southern Italy, and there are noted analogies between the Greek, Oscan, and Latin languages.

## Political Structure and Roman Conflict
The Greeks of Italy and Sicily were organized into separate city governments without a common policy or central rule, led primarily by Syracuse in Sicily and Tarentum in Italy. As Roman power expanded, these cities began to fear Rome and sought external assistance.

They found a potential protector in Pyrrhus, a kinsman of Alexander the Great and king of Epirus. Pyrrhus aimed to become the master-general of Magna Græcia. He commanded a modern army featuring an infantry phalanx, Thessalian cavalry, and twenty fighting elephants. Pyrrhus invaded Italy and defeated the Romans in two major battles: Heraclea in 280 B.C. and Ausculum in 279 B.C.

## The Carthaginian Intervention and Decline
After his initial victories over Rome, Pyrrhus turned his attention to the subjugation of Sicily. This move brought him into conflict with Carthage, the Phœnician trading city. Carthage viewed a strong power in Sicily as a threat and allied with Rome to oppose the invader. The Carthaginian fleet encouraged the Romans to continue the struggle and cut off the overseas communications of Pyrrhus.

Following a disastrous repulse at Beneventum, Pyrrhus was further pressured by Gauls who were raiding south through Illyria toward Macedonia and Epirus. Consequently, Pyrrhus abandoned his conquests and returned home in 275 B.C. This resulted in the extension of Roman power to the Straits of Messina.

## Relations with Gaul
To the north of the Roman power resided the Gauls, described as a hardy and warlike people. The Romans maintained a boundary against them using a line of fortified settlements and forts. While the Gauls had previously raided into Italy, the Roman frontier eventually became too formidable for them, leading the Gauls to redirect their raids through Illyria into Epirus and Macedonia.
