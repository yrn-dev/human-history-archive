# Layard and the Study of Ancient Nineveh

## Archaeological Discoveries and Hypotheses
Mr. Layard has conducted extensive research and discoveries at Nimroud, Kouyunjik, and Khorsabad. Based on the abundance of these finds, Layard is inclined to believe that all three sites are located within the circuit of ancient Nineveh. To support this hypothesis, he refers to the prodigious space alleged by Diodorus, citing Ktêsias, which describes a circuit of four hundred and eighty stadia, or approximately sixty English miles.

Within these sites, Layard observes a distinction in the quality of the remains. He considers the monuments found in the north-west portion of Nimroud to be more ancient than those in the south-west portion, as well as more superior in their execution and style than the monuments found at Khorsabad and Kouyunjik.

## Analysis of Ancient Fortifications
The scale of the walled space attributed to Nineveh is a point of historical consideration. While the testimony of Ktêsias is regarded as being of inferior value compared to that of Herodotus, the possibility of such a massive fortification is not considered incredible. This is based on the capacity of Assyrian kings to command vast amounts of human labor for simple and toilsome processes using inexhaustible and nearby materials.

Comparisons are drawn to other great fortifications to illustrate this possibility, such as the great wall of China and the walls built by the Romans in Britain, including the Picts Wall. These examples suggest that extensive fortifications could be achieved even under less favorable circumstances than those of the Assyrian kings. Furthermore, it is noted that while the walls of Nineveh and Babylon were larger than those of Paris in its current state, the walls of Paris represent a greater amount of work in terms of contrivance, elaboration, and costliness.

## Material Degradation
Layard has also noted the fragility of the materials used in Assyrian architecture. He remarks that edifices constructed from sunburnt bricks are prone to perishing when they are neglected, eventually crumbling away into earth and leaving little to no trace of their existence.
