# Reductions in the Dates of Early Babylonian Dynasties

The determination of the chronology of early Babylonian civilization has been a subject of significant scholarly debate, characterized by a movement toward the reduction of dates previously assigned to the earliest dynasties. These reductions directly impact the estimated age of Babylonian civilization, which some writers formerly placed as far back as 5000 or 6000 B.C.

## The Influence of Nabonidus and Early Chronology
Much of the early high dating was based on a statement by Nabonidus, who claimed that 3200 years separated his own era from that of Nar-am-Sin, the son of Sargon of Agade. Based on this single statement, a date of 3800 B.C. was usually assigned to Sargon. However, this figure has not been universally accepted. Winckler regarded the statement as an uncritical exaggeration, and Lehmann-Haupt suggested a textual emendation that reduced the number by a thousand years, proposing that 2200 years should be read instead of 3200.

Other theories suggest that the scribes of Nabonidus may have mistakenly reckoned several early dynasties as consecutive when they were actually contemporaneous. While archaeological and epigraphic evidence indicates that the empire of the Semitic kings of Agade was not separated by a very long interval from the kings of Sumer and Akkad (whose rule began with the Dynasty of Ur), a definite date for the early kings of Agade remains impossible to fix at present.

## Methodologies for Chronological Reduction
Scholars have employed various systems to resolve discrepancies between the Babylonian List of Kings, the chronological system of Berossus, and native inscriptions.

### Emendation of Figures
One group of researchers, including Peiser, Rost, Lehmann-Haupt, and Marquart, attempted to resolve data discrepancies by emending figures in inscriptions. In 1891, Peiser suggested that the length of Dynasty III in the Kings' List—recorded as "9 soss and 36 years" (576 years)—was a scribal error for "6 soss and 39 years." This reduced the length of Dynasty III by 177 years, which in turn reduced the dates for Dynasties I and II. Rost further reduced this figure in 1897, though he partially offset the effect by emending Sennacherib's date for Marduk-nadin-akh-e's defeat of Tiglath-pileser I from 418 to 478. Lehmann-Haupt, in 1898, reduced Sennacherib's figure in the Bavian inscription by a hundred years to obtain a low date for the close of Dynasty III.

### Structural Reorganization
Another group, led by Hommel and Niebuhr, focused on the treatment of Dynasty II to reduce the date of Dynasty I. In 1886, Hommel reversed the order of the first two dynasties of the Kings' List. By 1895 and 1898, he abandoned this, instead adopting Halevy's suggestion that Dynasty III followed Dynasty I immediately, viewing Dynasty II as either contemporaneous with Dynasty I or as an apocryphal "later historical construction." Niebuhr modified this by reducing the independent existence of Dynasty II to 143 years and overlapping it with Dynasty I by 225 years.

## The Babylonian List of Kings and Other Data
Since 1884, the Babylonian List of Kings has served as the primary framework for chronological systems. It lists Babylonian kings in dynasties from the First Dynasty down to the Neo-Babylonian period. However, gaps in the text—specifically regarding the lengths of Dynasties IV and VIII—create uncertainty. The omission of the length of Dynasty VIII is particularly problematic, as it prevents the combination of the Kings' List data with the evidence of the Ptolemaic Canon.

Other critical data sources include:
*   The chronological system of Berossus.
*   Synchronisms between the kings of Babylon and Assyria.
*   Early Babylonian date-lists.
*   Native inscriptions, such as Nabonidus's estimate of the period of Khammurabi.

## Recent Findings and Revised Estimates
New information has led to further refinements. The elimination of Dynasty II from the Babylonian throne suggests that Dynasty I rose around 2000 or 2050 B.C., a date that aligns with the chronological notices of Shalmaneser I. Additionally, the historical period of Berossus is now believed to begin in 2232 B.C. rather than 2506 B.C.

Discoveries regarding the period before the rise of Babylon have also emerged. A fragment of an early dynastic chronicle from Nippur details the Dynasty of Ur (five kings, 117 years) and the succeeding Dynasty of Isin (sixteen kings, 225 1/2 years). Because the capture of Isin by Rim-Sin occurred in the seventeenth year of Sin-muballit (Khammurabi's father), it is probable that the fall of the Dynasty of Isin coincides with this event. This suggests that later rulers of Isin were contemporaneous with earlier rulers of Dynasty I, placing the rise of the Dynasty of Ur at a date not much earlier than 2300 B.C.
