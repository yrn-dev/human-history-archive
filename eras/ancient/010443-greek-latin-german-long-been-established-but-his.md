# Greek, Latin, and German Languages

## Linguistic Origins and Relationships
Comparative philology has established a common parentage between the Greek, Latin, and German languages, as well as Persian and Sanskrit. The German philologist Franz Bopp, in his 1816 work *Uber das Conjugationssystem der Sanskritsprache in Vergleichung mit jenem der griechischen, lateinischen, persischen und germanischen Sprache*, sought to trace the common origin of the grammatical systems of these languages.

Further analysis of the Greek, Latin, and Oscan languages suggests that the first two, and likely the third, are sisters within the same Indo-European family. While Greek and Latin share a structural and radical analogy, comparative philology indicates that Latin departs less from the supposed mother-language and common type than Greek does. Latin contains a non-Grecian element and classes of words that suggest a confluence of different people with distinct tongues; this non-Grecian element is even more developed in the remains of the Oscan language.

## Regional Influence and the Pelasgic Element
In southern Italy and Sicily, Greek colonies acquired specific words through association with the Sikels. These words closely resemble Latin, indicating a resemblance between the language of Latium and that of the Sikels and Œnotrians prior to Greek establishment. The Oscan or Ausonian tribes, who appear to have been intruders and conquerors from the central mountains, occupied the intermediate country between these two extremities of the Sikel population. 

The affinity existing between the Latins, Œnotrians, and Sikels—and to some extent the Greeks—is referred to as "Pelasgic." This affinity does not extend to the Iapygians or the Opicians and Oscans. These linguistic analogies support the theories of Antiochus and Thucydidês that the Sikels once occupied a larger portion of southern Italy before migrating to Sicily due to Oscan invasions.

## Orthography and Philological Study
In the study of Greek names, there has been a historical tension between Latinized and Greek-approximated orthography. The common English practice of substituting "C" for the Greek "K" is viewed by some as incorrect, as the English "K" coincides precisely with the Greek "K" in both sound and appearance. While the Latin "C" originally coincided in sound with the Greek "K," the English "C" has deviated, becoming an "S" before letters such as *e*, *i*, *æ*, *œ*, and *y*. 

Because of this, German philologists universally employ "K" when writing Greek names. Some historians have adopted this practice to avoid the "multiplied sibilation" of English and to better reproduce the Greek language's euphony, though they may retain "C" for names that have become effectively Anglicised. Additionally, a circumflex is sometimes used to mark the long *o* (ω) and long *e* (η) when they appear in the penultimate or last syllable of a name.

In the United States, Francis Andrew March (1825–) promoted the philological method of teaching English "like Latin and Greek." In 1870, he published *A Comparative Grammar of the Anglo-Saxon Language*, which illustrated Anglo-Saxon forms using those of Old High German, Old Norse, Old Friesic, Old Saxon, Gothic, Latin, Greek, and Sanskrit.

## Historical and Political Intersections
The interaction between these linguistic and cultural groups is evident in the history of the Mediterranean and Europe. In the East, the Greeks recaptured Constantinople from Latin emperors in 1261. Michael VIII (Michael Palæologus), the founder of the new Greek dynasty, eventually broke away from Roman communion, marking the end of the eastward ascendancy of the Popes and the fall of Latin kingdoms in Asia.

In the Middle Ages, the center of Greek monachism shifted to Mount Athos. Between the 10th and 14th centuries, numerous monasteries were established there, including Greek, Slavonic, and one Latin monastery.

In later European politics, Germany and Austria sought to extend German influence eastward toward Asia Minor, a goal summarized by the phrase "Berlin to Bagdad." This ambition conflicted with Russian schemes for Slav ascendancy. During this period, Roumania and Italy were noted as being Latin in tradition and nominal allies of Germany.
