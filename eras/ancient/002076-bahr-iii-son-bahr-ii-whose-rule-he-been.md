# Bahram III

Bahram III was a Sassanid king and the son of Bahram II. Prior to his ascension to the throne, he served as the governor of Sejistan, a role that earned him the title "Saganshah," as recorded by Tabari and Agathias.

## Reign and Succession
The reign of Bahram III was notably brief, lasting only four months in the year 294. Following his short tenure, he was succeeded by a pretender named Narseh.

## Context of the Bahram Dynasty
Bahram III was one of five Sassanid kings to bear the name Bahram. The name is derived from *Varahran* (rendered in Greek as *Ouararanes* or *Ouraranes*), which is a younger form of *Verethragna*, the name of a Persian god known as "the killer of the dragon Verethra."

His immediate predecessor and father, Bahram II (who reigned from 277 to 294), saw his reign marked by an attack on the Persians by the emperor Carus, resulting in the conquest of Ctesiphon in 283, though Carus subsequently died of the plague. Theological inscriptions from the reign of Bahram II have been preserved.

Other monarchs of the same name include:
*   **Bahram I (274–277):** The son of Shapur I and successor to his brother Hormizd I. He is noted for ordering the cruel death of the prophet Mani, the founder of Manichaeism, at the instigation of the magians.
*   **Bahram IV (389–399):** The son and successor of Shapur III. He previously served as the governor of Kirman, for which he was called Kirmanshah. During his reign or that of his predecessor, Armenia was divided between the Persian and Roman empires. He was eventually killed by malcontents.
*   **Bahram V (420–439):** The son of Yazdegerd I. He ascended the throne with the assistance of al-Mondhir, the Arabic dynast of Hira, after the sudden death or assassination of his father. Bahram V initiated a systematic persecution of Christians, leading to war with the Roman empire, though the conflict ended in a treaty of mutual religious toleration for Zoroastrianism and Christianity. He converted the Persian part of Armenia into a province by deposing its vassal king. While Persian tradition remembers him as "Bahram Gor" ("the wild ass") and celebrates his beauty, valor, and victories over Indians, Turks, Romans, and Negroes, he is described as a weak monarch who favored the priests and grandees. He is also credited with building numerous villages, large gardens, and great fire-temples.
