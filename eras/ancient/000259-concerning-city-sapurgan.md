# CONCERNING THE CITY OF SAPURGAN

## Geography and Access
The city of Sapurgan is reached after a journey of six days from a preceding castle. The route to the city traverses fine plains, beautiful valleys, and hill-sides characterized by an abundance of fruits, other products, and excellent grass pasture. Due to this plenty, the region is a favored location for armies to establish their quarters.

The landscape encountered during this six-day journey includes a goodly number of villages and towns inhabited by worshippers of Mahommet. However, the path is occasionally interrupted by tracts of desert extending for 50 or 60 miles, or slightly less. These desert regions are devoid of water, requiring travelers to carry their own supplies; animals must survive without drink until they reach watering places beyond the desert tract.

## Economy and Local Products
Sapurgan is noted for having a great plenty of all necessities, but it is most distinguished for its melons, which are described as the very best in the world. The inhabitants employ a specific method of preservation for these melons, paring them into strips and drying them in the sun. Once dried, these melons are sweeter than honey and are transported for sale throughout the country. In addition to its agricultural output, the area around Sapurgan provides an abundance of game, including both beasts and birds.

## Identification and Location
Archive notes indicate that Sapurgan may closely express the pronunciation of the city referred to by old Arabic writers as *Sabúrḳán* or *Shabúrḳán*. In more modern terms, it is called *Shibrgân*. The city is situated in a richly cultivated plain on the verge of the desert, located approximately 90 miles west of Balkh. Records suggest the city has contained some 12,000 inhabitants.

## Textual Variations
There are discrepancies in the archival records regarding the travel time to Sapurgan. While some sources clearly state the journey takes six days, other texts—specifically the Pauthier text—mention "vi cités" (six cities). However, it is suspected that "cités" is a clerical error for "journées" (days), as this reading appears in one of the manuscripts.
