# Iranians and the Region of Bactria

The Iranians are a people of the Eastern branch of the Indo-Europeans. Scholars use the term "Indo-Aryan" to describe the portion of this Eastern branch that settled in India, while the term "Iranians" refers to those who remained in Persia and Bactria. The term *Iran* shares the same origin as *arya-*, a word with dignified associations used by these peoples to describe themselves.

## Origins and Linguistic Characteristics
The eastern part of Iran is believed to be the region where the Aryans lived as a single people before separating into Iranians and Indians. While the exact stages of the occupation of the Iranian table-land are unknown, it is noted that the people have no tradition of a time when they did not hold these territories. 

Linguistically, the Aryan group is distinguished from other Indo-European languages by four characteristics: the confusion of original *e* and *o* with the original *a* sound; the representation of the short schwa-sound by *i*; the change of original *s* to *h* after certain consonants; and a specific suffix (*-nam*) for the genitive plural of stems ending in a vowel. Iranian languages further differ from Sanskrit through the confusion of voiced aspirates (*gh, dh, bh*) with original *g, d, b*, and the transformation of breathed aspirates (*kh, th, ph*) into spirants.

## Geography and Culture of Bactria
Bactria (known in Persian inscriptions as *Bakhtri*) is an ancient country located between the Oxus (Amu Darya) and the Hindu Kush (Paropamisus) range, with its capital at Bactra (modern Balkh). It is characterized as a mountainous region with a moderate climate, abundant water, and fertile land, though it is limited by the Turanian desert.

Bactria was the home of one of the Iranian tribes. It is widely believed that the prophet Zoroaster preached and gained his first adherents in this region, and that his religion subsequently spread to the western parts of Iran. Consequently, the sacred language of the Avesta, the holy book of Zoroastrianism, was once referred to as "old Bactrian," though scholars have since abandoned this term.

## Political History and Governance
In early times, the Bactrians and their neighbors were ruled by petty local kings, such as Vishtaspa, who protected Zoroaster. While some modern authors have proposed the existence of a vast "old Bactrian empire" that defeated the Turanians, this is regarded as a myth—a reflex of the later Achaemenid and Sassanid empires. The actual historical tradition highlights an eternal feud between the Iranian peasants and the nomads of Turan, based on geographical conditions.

Bactria was subjugated by Cyrus and became a satrapy of the Persian empire. Following the defeat of Darius III, the satrap Bessus attempted to organize national resistance, but the region was conquered by Alexander. It then became a province of the Macedonian empire under Seleucus. The Macedonians established numerous Greek towns, making the Greek language dominant for a period.

Around 255 B.C., Diodotus, the satrap of Bactria, declared independence and founded the Graeco-Bactrian kingdom. His successors, including Euthydemus and Demetrius, expanded their power into eastern Iran and the Indus valley. However, internal dissensions and usurpations by generals like Eucratides undermined Greek rule. Over time, the Greeks introduced a native standard of coinage and the native language began to be used alongside Greek.

## Later Conquests and Transitions
The Graeco-Bactrian kingdoms were eventually overthrown. In the west, the Arsacid empire conquered districts such as Areia (Herat). In the north, Mongolian tribes known as Scythians—specifically the Tochari (Yue-chi)—entered Sogdiana in 159 B.C. and conquered Bactria in 139 B.C. 

By the middle of the 1st century B.C., eastern Iran and western India were part of the "Indo-Scythian" empire under the Kushan dynasty. The Kushans, whose most famous king was Kanishka (a protector of Buddhism), established their principal seat in Bactria. By the 3rd century, the Kushan dynasty decayed, and by the 5th century, the Ephthalites ("white Huns") subjected Bactria around 450. They were followed by the Turks around A.D. 560.

Following the overthrow of the Sassanian empire by the Arabs, Bactria and the entirety of Iran up to the banks of the Jaxartes submitted to the rule of Islam and the caliph.
