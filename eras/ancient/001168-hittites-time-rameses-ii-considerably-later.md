# The Hittites

The Hittites were an Oriental people deeply influenced by Babylonian culture who established themselves on the shores of the Aegean and overran Asia Minor before the reign of the Egyptian king Rameses II. They are characterized as one of the various Semitic powers of Asia Minor, Syria, and Mesopotamia that engaged in prolonged conflicts with the civilization of the Nile.

## Conflicts with Egypt and Rameses II
During the Nineteenth Dynasty of Egypt, Rameses II reigned for sixty-seven years, a period during which Egypt reached high levels of prosperity. The Hittites were among the powers that engaged in warfare with the Egyptians, utilizing vast droves of war chariots. The horse, used specifically for glory and war, had spread into these old civilizations from Central Asia.

Rameses II fought the Hittites in the battle of Kadesh, located near the Syrian town of Homs (ancient Emesa). The significance of this region was due to its command of the great north road from Palestine, Egypt, and Damascus via the Orontes valley. To commemorate his military successes, Rameses II added an extensive court to the Temple of Luxor in Thebes. He embellished the pylons and walls of this court with scenes depicting his victories over the Syrians and the Hittites, and he placed several colossal statues within the area.

## Religious Beliefs
In the time of Rameses II and continuing considerably later, the Hittites recognized a specific Baal of the heavens. This deity was part of a broader regional religious landscape; for instance, the Syrian city of Emesa was devoted to the worship of Baal, the sun god. In Babylonia, Baal had been identified as a definite individual deity associated with the planet Jupiter.

## Expansion and Influence in Asia Minor
The Hittites left memorials in Lydia, including inscriptions in Hittite hieroglyphics. One such inscription was discovered by G. Dennis attached to the figure of "Niobe" on Sipylus, and another accompanies a figure in the pass of Karabel. Some traditions suggest a Hittite conquest of Sardis, as the city was captured by the Cimmerii in 1078 B.C., an event that may be a tradition of earlier Hittite activity.

## Relations with Other Peoples
Following the era of Rameses II, during the time of Rameses III, the Hittites formed alliances with other Aryan or Aryanized races, including people from the Aegean seaports. These Aegean peoples, who had been pushed from Italy and Greece by southward-moving Aryan-speaking races, invaded the African coast and the Egyptian delta, aligning themselves with the Hittites.

In other regions, the Hittites were involved in the instability of Mesopotamia. According to a Babylonian chronicle, Hittites from Cappadocia raided and captured Babylon during the reign of Samsu-ditana, the last king of Dynasty I. This attack occurred while Babylon was in a weakened state, leading to the city subsequently falling to the Kassites.

## Legacy
The ancestral elements of the Hittites, along with those of the Phrygians, Trojans, Lydians, Ionian Greeks, Galatians, Cimmerians, and Italians, continued to flow in the blood of the people inhabiting the tablelands of Asia Minor (modern Anatolia). However, by the time the region became largely Turkish in speech and Moslem in religion under the Seljuk Turks, these ancestral Hittite elements had long been forgotten.
