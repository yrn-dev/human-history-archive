# Basilides

Basilides was a prominent exponent of Gnosticism who lived in Alexandria, likely during the first decades of the 2nd century. While Eusebius’s *Chronicle* dates his first appearance to A.D. 133, other evidence suggests an earlier activity; Agrippa Castor, who lived under the reign of Hadrian (117-138), wrote a polemic against him, potentially placing his activity before 138.

## Writings and Sources
Basilides authored an exegetical work consisting of twenty-four books based on a gospel, though the specific identity of this gospel remains unknown. Additional writings are attributed to his son, Isidorus, including *Exegetika* on the prophet Parchor, *Ethika*, and *Peri prosphuous psuches*. Fragments of these works have been collected and commented upon by Hilgenfeld.

Knowledge of Basilides' system is derived from various early Christian sources, though they often conflict. Irenaeus provides a sketch of his school of thought, a view closely related to the *Syntagma* of Hippolytus (preserved in the works of Philaster, Pseudo-Tertullian, and Epiphanius). These accounts are further supplemented by notices in the *Stromateis* of Clemens Alexandrinus. Conversely, the *Philosophumena* of Hippolytus presents a different account with a pronounced monistic tendency. Modern opinion generally favors the account of Irenaeus and the *Syntagma* over the *Philosophumena*.

## Cosmological System
The system of Basilides is characterized by its relationship to Iranian dualism and its distinction from other Gnostic sects, specifically in its lack of the concept of a fall within the godhead or the figure of the fallen female god Sophia Achamoth. 

The "innatus pater" (supreme God) is described as having emanated 365 heavens and their corresponding spirits, a concept reflecting astronomical views of the 365 daily aspects of the heavens. The supreme God is associated with the name Abraxas (or Abrasax), a name with a numerical value of 365. This numerical value was also associated in antiquity with the Persian god Mithras.

The supreme God is surrounded by a series of Hypostases—ranging from five to seven. According to Irenaeus, these include *Nous*, *Logos*, *Phronesis*, *Sophia*, and *Dunamis*; Clemens suggests *Dikaiosune* and *Eirene* may also be included. Additionally, the world originates from seven powers, viewed as the lowest emanations of the supreme God. These are identified as the seven planetary deities of later Babylonian religion, though in this system, they are viewed as semi-daemonic or daemonic forces.

## Soteriology and the Saviour
Salvation in the system of Basilides is introduced as a response to the extreme confusion in the world. To remedy this, the supreme God sends his *Nous*—also called Christ—into the world. Clemens refers to this Saviour as *diakonos* or *pneuma diakonoumenon*. 

The relationship between this Saviour and the historical Jesus of Nazareth is subject to varying interpretations:
* Basilides recognized an earthly Jesus and denied the principle of his sinlessness.
* Irenaeus suggests the Saviour appeared only as a phantasm.
* The *Excerpta ex Theodoto* claims the *Diakonos* descended upon Jesus during his baptism in the form of a dove. Consequently, followers of Basilides celebrated the day of the baptism (*epiphaneia*) as a high festival.

Redemption is viewed as the restoration of all things and the "great separation of the sexes," wherein that which was combined for evil is separated once more.

## Later Developments and Magic
Among later followers of Basilides, Gnosticism took on a magical character. These followers believed that knowing the secret names of the rulers of the various heavens would allow a soul to pass through those heavens after death to reach the supreme God. They believed Christ possessed a mystic name (*Caulacau* or *QAW LAQAW*) that enabled his descent to earth and subsequent ascent to the Father. In this context, redemption was conceived as the revelation of mystic names, a tradition associated with Abraxas gems. It remains undetermined whether Basilides himself introduced this magical tendency.

## Historical Reception
The teachings of Basilides were condemned by early church authorities. By the last quarter of the 2nd century, writings associated with Basilides were condemned in the lists of New Testament books, such as the Muratorian Fragment.
