# Romans (King of the Romans)

The title of King of the Romans refers to a political office associated with the German empire and the subsequent claim to the Imperial crown. The position evolved through a system of election and specific legal maxims regarding the relationship between German leadership and the territories of Italy and Rome.

## Legal Maxims and Imperial Succession
The Imperial crown was fixed in the name and nation of Germany following the actions of Otho, who subdued the kingdom of Italy and delivered the pope. This era established two primary maxims of public jurisprudence:
1. A prince elected in the German diet immediately acquired the subject kingdoms of Rome and Italy.
2. The prince could not legally assume the titles of Augustus and emperor until he had received the crown from the Roman pontiff.

## The Nature of the Office in the Fourteenth Century
By the fourteenth century, the Roman empire of Germany had lost nearly all the provinces once held by Constantine or Trajan, with the exception of the borders of the Danube and Rhine. During this period, the German emperor functioned as an elective and impotent magistrate for an aristocracy of princes. The emperor possessed no personal villages; his primary prerogative was the right to propose and preside over the national senate when convened.

An example of this diminished power is seen in Charles the Fourth. Although he was unanimously saluted as the future emperor and King of the Romans, his power was centered in his native kingdom of Bohemia, which served as his richest source of revenue and firmest seat of power. When Charles passed the Alps, his army consisted of only three hundred horse. Upon entering Italy, he was admitted only with a peaceful train, and the gates of the city were shut against him. While he was crowned with the iron crown in the cathedral of St. Ambrose and later with the golden crown of the empire in the Vatican, he withdrew from Rome immediately without spending a single night within its walls, pursuant to a secret treaty.

## Elections and Political Struggles
The election of the King of the Romans was often entangled in dynastic strife and the influence of the papacy. In 1273, Rudolph, count of Habsburg, was elected King of the Romans. It is considered very probable that the German crown had been offered to Premysl Ottakar II previously, but he had refused it.

In other instances, the papacy attempted to use the election as a tool for political leverage. Following the excommunication of Lewis of Bavaria, the Roman pontiffs—then in exile and captivity in Avignon—offered the vacant empire to Charles the Fourth.

## Conflict and Rivalry
The struggle for the German crown frequently involved rebellious nobility. In the eleventh century, German nobles opposed Henry IV, using his excommunication as a pretext to set up a rival king, Duke Rudolph of Swabia, in March 1077. This led to a period of dynastic strife where both parties sought the support of the pope to gain the upper hand. After the victory at Flarchheim in January 1080, the pope decided in favor of Rudolph of Swabia. Following Rudolph's death in October of that year, Hermann of Luxemburg was put forward as a new claimant in August 1081, though Henry IV's power eventually ascended.
