# Legends of Thebes

The legends of Thebes, the city of the Kadmeians, are among the most rich and prominent mythical antiquities of the historical Hellenes, second only to the siege of Troy. These narratives encompass the city's foundation, the tragic destinies of its royal houses, and two major sieges by the Argeians.

## Foundation and Early Lineage
Thebes was founded by Kadmus, whose origins vary by account. Herodotus and Euripides describe him as an emigrant from Phoenicia searching for his sister, Europa. Other accounts, such as that of Apollodorus, suggest he came from Egypt or Libya before arriving in Phoenicia. The writer Conôn proposes that Kadmus was sent from the Egyptian city of Thebes on a project of conquest, borrowing the name of his ancestral seat for his new establishment in Boeotia.

Upon the city's founding, five primitive families known as the Sparti were established. Kadmus had four daughters: Inô, Semelê, Autonoê (mother of Actæôn), and Agavê (mother of Pentheus). The legends record that Pentheus met a miserable end after resisting the god Dionysus. Other early figures associated with the city's origins include Labdakus, Antiopê, Amphiôn, and Zêthus.

## The House of Oedipus
The city is centrally linked to the legendary celebrity of Oedipus and his family. The narratives detail the encounter with the Sphinx and the subsequent tragic destiny of Oedipus, who slew his father and married his mother. Following the death of Oedipus, a quarrel erupted between his sons, Eteoklês and Polynikês, over the possession of the sceptre.

This conflict led Polynikês to retire to Argos, where he received aid from Adrastus.

## The First Siege of Thebes
The first siege was conducted by an army led by seven chiefs, including Adrastus and Polynikês. During the conflict, the Thêbans were defeated in the field, an event marked by the heroic devotion of Menœkus. The war culminated in a single combat between the brothers Eteoklês and Polynikês, in which both perished.

The aftermath of the battle saw the destruction and repulse of the Argeian chiefs; all were killed except for Adrastus. Amphiaräus was swallowed up by the earth. Following the victory, Kreôn, the king of Thebes, forbade the burial of Polynikês and the other fallen Argeian chiefs. This led to the devotion and eventual death of Antigonê, while the Athenians intervened to ensure the interment of the fallen.

## The Epigoni and the Second Siege
Ten years after the first conflict, the Epigoni—the "descendants" or sons of the seven heroes—undertook a second expedition to avenge their fathers. This campaign was completely successful; Thebes was forced to surrender and was razed to the ground. Among the Epigoni was Thersander, who avenged his father Polynikês.

The victory of the Epigoni and the two sieges of Thebes by Adrastus were favorite subjects of early epic poetry. Lost poems such as the *Thebaïs* and the *Epigoni* were devoted to these events, while the *Œdipodia* focused on the tragedy of Oedipus.

## Other Heroic Associations
Thebes is recognized as the natal city of both Hêraklês and Dionysus. Legend credits Hêraklês with rescuing Thebes from servitude and the payment of tribute to the city of Orchomenos. This legend is reflected in his surname, Rhinokoloustês, referring to the savage mutilations he inflicted upon tribute-seeking envoys.

Additionally, the city maintained a legendary heroic brotherhood with Ægina. Both were considered daughters of Asôpus. This perceived kinship had practical historical consequences; during the 68th Olympiad, when hard-pressed by Athens, the Thêbans applied to the Æginêtans for aid as next of kin, receiving support through the Æakids and armed force.
