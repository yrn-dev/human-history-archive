# BRONZE AGE

The Bronze Age is the term used by archaeologists to describe a stage of human culture situated between the Stone Age and the Iron Age. It is characterized as a period when implements, utensils, and weapons were generally manufactured from bronze. Rather than having an absolute chronological value, the term denotes a stage of civilization that most races are believed to have experienced.

## Chronology and Overlap
The timing of the Bronze Age varied by region. In Europe, most authorities fix its commencement between 2000 B.C. and 1800 B.C., based on the similarity of weapon and implement types found across the continent. However, the period began earlier in some countries and later in others; for example, the Peruvians and Mexicans remained in their bronze age in recent times.

Some archaeologists have disputed the existence of a distinct Bronze Age, citing the discovery of stone, bronze, and iron implements side by side in prehistoric burial sites. Proponents of the Bronze Age theory argue that these "mixed finds" are the result of overlapping stages of progress. Furthermore, sepulchral mounds were often reopened over generations for fresh interments, and in tombs containing multiple bodies, more valuable bronze objects would have been placed only with the remains of the wealthier tenants. The duration and prevalence of the age in any specific land were determined by the accessibility of the metals required to create the alloy.

## Characteristics and Material Culture
The Bronze Age in Europe is distinguished by weapons and tools that differ in size and design from those of the preceding Stone Age or the succeeding Iron Age. Common implements include:
*   Swords, daggers, and arrow-heads
*   Knives, awls, and gouges
*   Hammers

A leaf-shaped bronze sword, generally lacking a central ridge, has been found throughout Europe from Lapland to the Mediterranean. Some Japanese archaeologists suggest these may be the "mallet-headed swords" (kabutsuchi) used by Keiko's soldiers around A.D. 82.

The patterns of these arms are described as distinctly oriental, and the handles of daggers and swords are often too narrow and short for the large-handed races of Europe, leading to theories that the knowledge of bronze originated in the East. In other regions, such as Japan, bronze relics—including weapons and bells—are viewed as vestiges of the Yamato procession. Some spear-heads found in this context feature a cross-arm similar to halberds used in China from the seventh century B.C.

## Burial Customs and Art
A primary characteristic of the European Bronze Age is the shift in the disposal of the dead; cremation became the standard mode, whereas burial was the rule during the Stone Age. Barrows and sepulchral mounds from this period are smaller and less imposing than those of the Stone Age and served as family or tribal cemeteries. In Denmark, a single mound has been found to contain as many as seventy deposits of burnt bones.

Excavations of these barrows have yielded:
*   Cinerary urns (hand-made pottery, typically 12 to 18 inches high)
*   Amulets, coronets, and solid gold diadems
*   Vases of elegant form made of bronze and gold

Ornamentation was generally limited to concentric circles, bosses, and spirals. Some swords feature a handle shaped like a cross and tracery believed to be an imitation of the Svastika, an ancient Aryan symbol.

## Trade and Economy
During this era, a primitive form of trade emerged. Bronze and bronze weapons were swapped, stolen, or passed over great distances, alongside gold, jade, skins, cloth, and flax-net. Salt was also likely traded, as it was a necessity for grain-consuming people. This early economic activity transitioned through various forms, including barter, tribute, blackmail, and robbery by violence.

## Regional Examples
In Bosnia, archaeological sites such as the cemeteries of Glasinac and Jezerine, and the lake-dwellings at Butmir, have provided evidence of the development of primitive Illyrian civilization. These sites have yielded bronze ornaments and weapons, alongside stone, horn, and later materials like tin, silver, and amber.

In the context of early Japanese culture, bronze was used for various purposes. While some helmets were made of riveted iron, others were bronze. Additionally, archaic Chinese wine-pots of bronze sometimes featured figures of animals and humans on their lids for useful purposes.
