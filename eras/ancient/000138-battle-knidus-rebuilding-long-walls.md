# From the Battle of Knidus to the Rebuilding of the Long Walls of Athens

## The Battle of Knidus and Its Aftermath
The Battle of Knidus took place in early August 394 B.C. The engagement featured the Greek forces under Konon facing the Phoenicians led by the navarch Farnabazus. Despite the Greek fleet being significantly smaller in number, Konon achieved a memorable triumph. This victory served as a reversal of the defeat suffered by the Greeks at Ægospotami eleven years prior.

The consequences of the battle were significant, primarily resulting in the collapse of the maritime empire of Sparta. This shift diminished the fear Sparta inspired in other states, such as Corinth. Furthermore, the victory enabled Athens to renovate its shipping—both commercial and warlike—and rebuild its fortifications.

## The Outbreak of the Corinthian War
While events unfolded in Asia, war was already raging in Greece Proper, a conflict commonly known as the Corinthian War. This war grew out of a general disposition toward hostility against Sparta, which had expanded its power into Northern Greece and maintained a presence in Herakleia. Discontent grew among Sparta's allies following the accession of Agesilaus.

Hostility was further fueled when the Persian satrap Tithraustes sent an envoy into Greece with money to incite war against Sparta. This funding found success in Argos, Corinth, and Thebes. While the Persian money did not create the hostility, it brought out pre-existing hostile tendencies.

## Conflict in Bœotia and the Rise of Thebes
The Bœotian war saw Sparta engage in active operations against Bœotia. Lysander was dispatched to operate from Herakleia to the north, while Pausanias led an army from the Peloponnesus. In response, the Thebans sought aid from Athens. The Athenians voted unanimously to assist Thebes, a decision reflecting a shift in Greek sentiment and the positive effects of the amnesty following the expulsion of the Thirty.

During these operations, Orchomenus revolted from the Bœotian confederacy to join Lysander. However, Lysander was repulsed and killed during an attack on Haliartus. Following this, Pausanias arrived in Bœotia, but he eventually evacuated the region after receiving the bodies of Lysander and others for burial. Pausanias was later condemned in his absence by Sparta and escaped into voluntary exile.

The death of Lysander—who had been viewed as a mischievous influence and had planned to make himself king at Sparta—encouraged Sparta's enemies. An alliance was formed between Athens, Thebes, Corinth, and Argos, with others, including the Eubœans, joining. This period saw Thebes rise to the rank of a primary power under the leader Ismenias.

## Conflict in the Peloponnesus and the Walls of Corinth
Between 393 and 392 B.C., the fertile plain between Sikyon and Corinth was rendered uncultivable. Corinthian proprietors were forced to move their cattle and servants to Peiræum, a territory northeast of the Akrokorinthus. The Corinthians were protected from Sikyonian assailants by the Long Walls of Corinth, a continuous fortification of twelve stadia connecting the city to the harbor of Lechæum.

The prolonged war and the conversion of the city into a besieged post created strong aversion to the conflict among Corinthian proprietors. Additionally, the revival of Athenian shipping and fortifications following the Battle of Knidus renewed anti-Athenian jealousy and apprehension among the Corinthians, who feared their trade might be driven away to the more secure Peiræus.

## The Rebuilding of the Long Walls
The Long Walls of Corinth had been partially broken down by Praxitas, creating a route for Peloponnesian armies to enter Bœotia or Attica. In response, the Athenians—who had already completed the rebuilding of their own Long Walls—marched to Corinth with masons and carpenters. They quickly re-established the western wall to block Lacedæmonian incursions from Sikyon, followed by the completion of the eastern wall.

However, this advantage was overturned during the summer by a joint attack by the Lacedæmonian king Agesilaus and his brother Teleutias. Agesilaus first ravaged the plain of Argos before marching to the newly repaired Long Walls of Corinth. Teleutias, acting as admiral in the Corinthian Gulf, provided naval support. Together, they defeated the four confederated armies and captured both the Long Walls and the port of Lechæum, including its docks and ships. This victory destroyed the naval power of Corinth in the Krissæan Gulf and established Lechæum as a permanent Lacedæmonian post, making any further Athenian attempts to rebuild the Corinthian walls impossible.

## Relations with Ægina
Following the Battle of Knidus and the rebuilding of the Athenian Long Walls, the new Æginetans remained in peace and commerce with Athens. This peace was disrupted by Eteonikus, the Lacedæmonian harmost on the island, who encouraged the Æginetans to use privateers to attack Attica. Athens responded by sending ten triremes to blockade the island and a force of hoplites under Pamphilus to occupy a fort. This squadron was eventually driven off by Teleutias, who was commanding a fleet at Rhodes. Upon the arrival of his successor, Hierax, Teleutias handed over the fleet and returned to Sparta, where he was highly regarded by his seamen.
