# Babylonia

Babylonia was a prominent civilization of antiquity located in Asia, characterized by its widespread influence, advanced engineering in brickmaking, and a complex history of shifting power between itself and neighboring Assyria.

## Early Civilization and Influence
The influence of Babylonian civilization was likely widespread across Asia. Evidence suggests a connection between Babylonia and China, and it is believed that all Indian alphabets can be traced back to a Semitic original borrowed via commerce from the Persian Gulf. In the realm of industry, Babylonia is credited with the discovery of the method for creating strong, durable bricks by burning blocks of dried clay. Well-burnt bricks were produced more than 6,000 years ago and were used extensively during the time of Sargon of Akkad (c. 3800 B.C.). 

It is probable that the knowledge of brickmaking traveled eastwards from Babylonia across Asia. Specifically, the art of making glazed bricks, which was later highly developed in China, is believed to have moved from the west through northern India and Persia to reach China.

## Art and Architecture
Due to a lack of stone and an abundance of clay, the Babylonians relied heavily on brick for construction. Their temples were massive, shapeless structures of crude brick supported by buttresses, featuring drains to carry off rain (one such drain at Ur was made of lead). This reliance on brick led to the early development of the column and pilaster, as well as the use of enamelled tiles and frescoes. Walls were often brilliantly colored and occasionally plated with gold or bronze.

The Babylonians and Assyrians attained high proficiency in manufacturing bricks with coatings of enamel or colored glaze for wall decoration. Other artistic achievements included:
* **Sculpture:** Unlike the Assyrian preference for bas-relief, Babylonia utilized figures in the round, such as the realistic but clumsy statues from Tello.
* **Gem-cutting:** Because stone was scarce, the Babylonians perfected the art of gem-cutting; notable examples include seal-cylinders from the age of Sargon of Akkad.
* **Metallurgy:** While early specimens are rare—with the exception of the silver vase of Entemena—later periods saw great excellence in gold jewelry, such as bracelets and earrings, as well as skilled copper work.

## Political History and the Assyrian Relationship
The relationship between Babylonia and Assyria was one of fluctuating dominance. The city of Assur was originally founded by Semites from Babylonia. In the law-code of the Babylonian monarch Khammurabi (c. 2250 B.C.), both Assur and Nineveh are mentioned as being under his beneficent influence.

Over time, Assyria grew in power. During the reign of the Kassite kings of Babylonia, political instability led to Assyrian intervention. After a period of nominal vassalage, Shalmaneser I (1300 B.C.) claimed supremacy in western Asia. Subsequently, his son Tukulti-In-aristi conquered Babylon and executed its king, Bitilyasu, establishing Assyria as the mistress of the oriental world.

## The Last Babylonian Empire and Fall
The seat of empire eventually returned to Babylonia. Following the death of the Assyrian king Assur-bani-pal, the Assyrian empire began to break up. After the overthrow of Samas-sum-yukin, Nabopolassar became viceroy. War broke out between Nabopolassar and the last king of Assyria. With the assistance of the Scythian king of Ecbatana, Cyaxares, the Babylonians captured and destroyed Nineveh, ending the Assyrian empire.

Nabopolassar was succeeded by his son, Nebuchadrezzar II, who reigned for 43 years and restored Babylon as the mistress of the civilized world. His annals record an invasion of Egypt in 567 B.C.

The final Babylonian king was Nabonidus. During his reign, Cyrus, the "king of Anshan" in Elam, revolted against his suzerain Astyages (king of the Scythians/Manda) and established himself at Ecbatana. In 538 B.C., Cyrus invaded Babylonia. After the Babylonians were defeated at the Battle of Opis in June, Sippara surrendered. On the 16th of Tammuz, the soldiers of Cyrus entered Babylon without fighting. Nabonidus was captured, and Kurdish guards were stationed at the gates of the temple of Bel to ensure services continued without interruption.
