# Antipater Is Accused Before Varus, And Is Convicted Of

The legal proceedings against Antipater, the son of King Herod, took place in the presence of Quintilius Varus, the president of Syria, who was staying at the palace during Antipater's return from Rome. The trial focused on accusations of parricide, treason, and the orchestration of various plots against the king and the royal family.

## The Accusations and Trial
The trial was presided over by Varus and King Herod, with the attendance of the king's relations, including his sister Salome, as well as friends and individuals who had been subjected to torture to provide evidence. The primary accuser was Nicolaus of Damascus, who presented a comprehensive case against Antipater. Nicolaus attributed the general wickedness of the kingdom to Antipater, specifically accusing him of the murder of his own brothers through calumnies. 

Further charges included the attempt to poison King Herod and the corruption of those closest to the king. Nicolaus highlighted Antipater's role in the affair of Pheroras, alleging that Antipater had encouraged Pheroras to murder his own brother. Additional evidence was brought forward regarding Antipater's conduct in his amours among the women of Pheroras, his lascivious behavior while intoxicated, and testimonies from numerous witnesses. Some of these witnesses had previously remained silent out of fear but spoke out once Antipater was exposed.

## Herod's Testimony and Grievances
During the proceedings, King Herod expressed deep lamentation over the misfortunes caused by his children. He recounted the extensive maintenance, education, and wealth he had provided to Antipater, noting that he had given him a yearly income of fifty talents and thirty talents for his journey to Rome. Herod had also named Antipater as his successor in his written testament.

Herod accused Antipater of being a "profligate wild beast" who sought to become king through parricide because he felt the king lived too long. Herod admitted to Varus that he had been foolish in favoring Antipater over his other sons, whom he had previously put to death for plotting against him—actions Herod believed were largely for Antipater's benefit.

## Evidence of Plots and Forgery
The court examined written evidence, including a letter from the mother of Antipater, which warned him not to return because his father had discovered his actions and that Caesar was his only remaining refuge. 

Following the initial trial, further evidence emerged regarding a plot against Salome. A maid-servant of Julia (the wife of Caesar) named Acme had sent a letter to Herod containing bitter reproaches and accusations against the king, purportedly written by Salome. It was later discovered that Antipater had forged this letter and corrupted Acme to deliver it. This was proven by a letter from Acme to Antipater, in which she confirmed she had written the letter to the king as desired and expressed her belief that the king would not spare his sister. This evidence was recovered from a hidden seam in the inner coat of a slave of Antiphilus.

## Conviction and Sentencing
When Varus called upon Antipater to make his defense, Antipater remained largely silent, offering only a general appeal to God, stating, "God is my witness that I am entirely innocent." Finding no substantial defense, Varus ordered a potion to be brought before the court to demonstrate its potency; a condemned malefactor drank the potion and died instantly.

Consequently, Antipater was condemned to die by both his father and Quintilius Varus. However, rather than immediate execution, Herod laid his son in bonds. Varus wrote an account of the assembly to Caesar and departed for Antioch, the palace of the Syrians, the following day. Herod then sent messengers and letters to Caesar to inform him of Antipater's wickedness and misfortunes.
