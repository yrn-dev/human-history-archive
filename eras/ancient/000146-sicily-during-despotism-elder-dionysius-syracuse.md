# SICILY DURING THE DESPOTISM OF THE ELDER DIONYSIUS AT SYRACUSE

The reign of the elder Dionysius as despot at Syracuse was characterized by the centralization of power, the degradation of free Hellenic communities, and the extensive use of foreign mercenaries. His rule had a desolating effect on the Hellenic populations of both Sicily and Italy, transforming the political and demographic landscape of the region.

## Governance and Political Control
Dionysius established a despotism in Syracuse, turning the city into a great fortress with vast military power. His governing policy was to "pack all Sicily" into Syracuse. To maintain this control, he utilized the voting processes of public assemblies to serve his own "sinister purposes." By ensuring that votes were final and not open to rediscussion or correction, he removed the protections typically available to the people, using the appearance of public assembly to promote his own designs.

## Impact on Hellenic Communities
Under the elder Dionysius, the free Hellenic communities of Sicily and Italy were degraded, enslaved, and half-depopulated. He suppressed and absorbed several once-autonomous Grecian communities, including Hipponium, Kaulonia, and Rhegium, into Lokri and Syracuse. 

In Sicily, Dionysius dispossessed the previous residents of cities such as Leontini, Katana, Naxos, and Messênê. In their place, he substituted settlements of Gallic and Iberian mercenaries, who had been instrumental in his conquests. These transformations resulted in communities that were no longer purely Hellenic and were significantly less populous and flourishing. In the inland regions of Italy, Dionysius allied himself with the barbarous Lucanians, who pressed hard against the Italiot Greeks on the coast.

## Relations with Carthage
The elder Dionysius engaged in a great war against the Carthaginians. During this conflict, the Carthaginian army besieged Syracuse but was ultimately ruined by a terrible pestilence and the treason of its commander, Imilkon. In the first treaty subsequently concluded between Dionysius and the Carthaginians, an express article stipulated that the Syracusans should be subject to the despot.

## Influence of Plato and Internal Court Dynamics
Later in the period, Dionysius became passionately desirous of conversing with the philosopher Plato. This interest was spurred by Dion, who quoted Plato's discourses, and by Pythagorean philosophers in Italy who urged Plato to guide the mind of the powerful ruler. Plato accepted the invitation in hopes of purifying the mind of the chief to influence the corrupt people of Sicily for the better.

However, opponents of Dion sought to counter Plato's influence by persuading Dionysius to recall Philistus from exile. Philistus was an experienced courtier and intellectual who had zealously assisted in establishing the despotism and had served as the chief of the citadel's garrison. Philistus had previously been forced to leave Sicily and take refuge in Adria after Dionysius became angry when Leptines gave Philistus one of his daughters in marriage.

## Aftermath and Legacy
The period following the elder Dionysius was one of intolerable conflict and disorder. His sons, Hipparinus and Nysæus, contested for power, while other factions sought to establish a free popular constitution. Plato described a lamentable state of desolation where the Hellenic race and language were at risk of perishing in the island, potentially being replaced by the Oscan or Punic.

The oppressive nature of the elder Dionysius's rule is highlighted by the contrast with later efforts by Timoleon. While Dionysius's treaty with Carthage mandated that Syracusans be subject to him, Timoleon's later peace treaty stipulated that the territory eastward of the river Halykus should be "free Greek," distributed among free cities and exempt from despots.
