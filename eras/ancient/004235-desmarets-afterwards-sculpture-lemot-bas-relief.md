# Desmarets, Lemot, and the Art of Bas-Relief

## Education of Lorenzo Bartolini
Lorenzo Bartolini (1777–1850) was an Italian sculptor born in Vernio, Tuscany. Before establishing himself in the field of sculpture, he acquired a reputation and significant skill as a modeller in alabaster. In 1797, Bartolini traveled to Paris to further his artistic education. During his time there, he studied painting under Desmarets and subsequently studied sculpture under F. F. Lemot.

## The Bas-Relief "Cleobis and Biton"
Bartolini's transition to fame as a sculptor was marked by the creation of the bas-relief titled "Cleobis and Biton." This work earned him the second prize of the Academy in 1803. The success of this specific piece established his reputation and attracted a number of influential patrons.

## Career and Patronage of Bartolini
Following his early success, Bartolini executed several busts, including those of Cherubini and Mehul, as well as various minor pieces for Denon. His most significant patron was Napoleon, for whom Bartolini created a colossal bust. Under Napoleon's direction, Bartolini was sent to Carrara to establish a school of sculpture. He remained at Carrara until after the fall of Napoleon, at which point he moved to Florence, where he lived until his death.

Bartolini's body of work is varied and contains a vast number of busts. His style is exemplified in works such as the "Faith in God," "Hercules and Lichas," and the group of Charity. In Italy, popular opinion has associated his sculptural qualities with those of Canova and Thorwaldsen.

## Historical Examples of Bas-Relief
The archive contains various examples of bas-relief across different cultures and eras:

*   **Ancient Near East:** In Assyria, palaces and temples were lined with coloured and sculptured stone slabs. The art of these bas-reliefs is categorized into three periods: the simple but vigorous style under Assur-nazir-pal III, the realistic and careful style under Sargon, and the refined but less bold style under Assur-bani-pal. Additionally, bas-reliefs at Persepolis depict four-footed beasts with eagle beaks.
*   **Classical and Lycian Art:** Lycian sculpture closely followed Greek developments. High-order bas-reliefs adorn Lycian tombs, and some of the finest surviving examples of classical art are the exquisite, usually coloured, bas-reliefs found on a Lycian sarcophagus in the museum of Constantinople. Other examples include the "Achilles at Scyros" bas-relief in the Louvre.
*   **Religious and Other Sites:** In Nepal, at a site now called Rummin-dei (derived from the ancient name Lumbini), a small shrine contains a bas-relief representing the birth of the Buddha. In this location, the figure of the mother in the relief is reverenced as a tutelary deity, while the Buddha is forgotten. Other archival mentions include bas-reliefs from the collegiate church of St Katherine near the Tower of London and the seats of the choir of Worcester cathedral.
