# The Obsequies of Achilles

The funeral rites, or obsequies, of Achilles are described in the *Odyssey*. These ceremonies are noted for their striking similarities to the details observed in tumuli from both the Iron and Bronze Ages.

## Funeral Rites and Rituals
The process of the obsequies began with the preparation of the body, which was brought to the funeral pile dressed in an embroidered robe. As part of the ritual offerings, jars of honey and unguents were placed beside the body. Additionally, oxen and sheep were slaughtered at the site of the pile.

Following the cremation, the incinerated bones were collected from the ashes. These remains were placed into a golden urn. Achilles was not interred alone; the bones of Patroclus, described as the dearest friend of Achilles, were placed in the same golden urn.

## The Burial Mound
To mark the site of the remains, a great and shapely mound was raised. This barrow was situated on a high headland, a location chosen specifically so that the monument would be visible from afar to future generations of men.

## Comparison with Other Homeric Funerals
The description of Achilles's obsequies shares thematic and procedural similarities with other funeral accounts in Homeric literature, such as the building of the barrow of Hector described in the *Iliad*. In the case of Hector, the process involved nine days of collecting wood via oxen-drawn carts to build the pyre. After the body burned for twenty-four hours, the embers were extinguished with wine libations. Like the rites of Achilles, Hector's calcined bones were picked from the ashes and placed in a metallic urn, which was then deposited in a cist or hollow grave, covered with large stones, and topped with a barrow of great magnitude before a funeral feast was held.

## Context of the Figures
Achilles and Patroclus are central figures in the Homeric epics. In the *Iliad*, Patroclus is depicted as the attached friend and second to Achilles, maintaining a relation of dependence similar to that of Telemachus to Odysseus. The two are also associated with the early tradition of "lays" or *klea andron* (stories of heroes), which they are described as singing to the lyre while in their tent.
