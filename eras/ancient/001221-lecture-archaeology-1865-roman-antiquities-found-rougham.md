# Lecture on Archaeology (1865) and Roman Antiquities found at Rougham

The *Introductory Lecture on Archaeology* (1865) and the work *Roman Antiquities found at Rougham* (1872) are publications authored by Churchill Babington (1821–1889), an English archaeologist and classical scholar.

## Author Biography
Churchill Babington was born on March 11, 1821, in Roecliffe, Leicestershire. His early education was provided by his father until the age of seventeen, after which he studied under the archaeologist and orientalist Charles Wycliffe Goodwin. Babington entered St John's College, Cambridge, in 1839 and graduated with a B.A. in 1843, achieving a senior optime and placing seventh in the first class of the classical tripos. 

He was elected to a fellowship in 1846, the same year he took orders and earned his M.A. He later received a D.D. in 1879. Babington's clerical career included serving as the vicar of Horningsea, near Cambridge, from 1848 to 1861, and as the vicar of Cockfield in Suffolk from 1866 until his death on January 12, 1889.

## Academic Career and Archaeology
From 1865 to 1880, Babington held the Disney professorship of archaeology at Cambridge. In his capacity as a lecturer, he utilized his personal collections of vases and coins to illustrate his teachings. His lectures focused primarily on numismatics and pottery from the Roman and Greek civilizations.

Beyond his lectures and the specific works on archaeology and the antiquities of Rougham, Babington was a prolific scholar. He catalogued Greek and English coins for the Fitzwilliam museum and classical manuscripts for the University Library. He also published work on inscriptions found in Crete by the explorer T. A. B. Spratt in 1855 and 1865.

## Other Scholarly Contributions
Babington was described as a many-sided man with interests spanning several disciplines:

*   **Natural History:** He possessed a taste for ornithology, botany, and conchology. He authored the *Catalogue of Birds of Suffolk* (1884–1886) and the *Flora of Suffolk* (1889), the latter written with W. M. Hind. He also contributed appendices on ornithology and botany to Potter's *History and Antiquities of Charnwood Forest* (1842).
*   **Classical Scholarship:** He gained fame as a Greek scholar through his editions of speeches by Hypereides (*Against Demosthenes* in 1850, *On Behalf of Lycophron and Euxenippus* in 1853, and the *Funeral Oration* in 1858), which were based on papyri discovered at Thebes, Egypt, in 1847 and 1856.
*   **Editing and Theology:** He edited Bishop Pecock's *Repressor of Overmuch Blaming of the Clergy* (1860) and the first two volumes of Higden's *Polychronicon* (1858). In 1845, he won the Hulsean Prize for an essay regarding the influence of Christianity on the abolition of slavery in Europe. In 1849, he wrote *Mr Macaulay's Character of the Clergy ... considered*, a defense of 17th-century clergy that was approved by Mr Gladstone.
*   **Reformation Studies:** In 1855, he published a facsimile edition of the *editio princeps* (1543, Venice) of *Benefizio della Morte di Cristo*, a Reformation-period book attributed to Paleario that had been largely destroyed by the Inquisition.
