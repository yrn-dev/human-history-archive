# Pompey's Acquisition of Jerusalem

The acquisition of Jerusalem by the Roman general Pompey occurred following a period of internal conflict in Judea between two brothers, Aristobulus and Hyrcanus, regarding the right to the kingdom.

## Initial Diplomatic Contacts
Before the military campaign, Aristobulus had sent a significant gift to Pompey while the general was in Damascus. This gift was a golden vine, valued at five hundred talents, which was later deposited in the temple of Jupiter Capitolinus in Rome.

The conflict between the brothers led them to approach Pompey to argue their respective claims to the government. During this period, Aristobulus had retreated to Alexandrium, a magnificent fortress situated on a high mountain at the entrance to Judea. Pompey commanded Aristobulus to come down from the citadel. Although Aristobulus initially wished to try his fortune in battle, he complied after his friends warned him that Roman power was irresistible. Aristobulus made apologies and argued for the justice of his cause, returning to the fortress several times to avoid appearing too debased while hoping the kingdom would be granted to him. Eventually, Pompey ordered Aristobulus to deliver up his fortresses and forced him to write personal instructions to the governors of those strongholds to yield them. Despite this, Aristobulus retired to Jerusalem in indignation to prepare for war.

## The Approach to Jerusalem
Pompey marched against Aristobulus with his Roman legions, Syrian auxiliaries, and an army he had been leading against the Nabateans. Along the way, he received news at Jericho that Mithridates had been slain by his son, Pharmaces. 

Jericho is described as the most fruitful country of Judea, characterized by a vast number of palm trees and the balsam tree. The balsam produces a precious ointment; its juice is gathered by making incisions in the wood with sharp stones, causing the liquid to drop down like tears. After pitching camp at Jericho for one night, Pompey marched toward Jerusalem.

## The Failed Agreement
As Pompey approached, Aristobulus became affrighted and met him in supplication. He promised Pompey money and agreed to deliver both himself and the city into Pompey's disposal in exchange for peace. Pompey forgave him and sent his lieutenant, Gabinius, along with soldiers to receive the promised money and the city.

However, the agreement was not honored. Aristobulus's soldiers and party refused to admit Gabinius into the city and provided none of the promised funds. This betrayal angered Pompey, who subsequently imprisoned Aristobulus and marched his army to the city.

## The Siege and Capture of the City
Jerusalem was strongly fortified on all sides except for the north, where a broad and deep ditch encompassed the city. The temple was located within this perimeter and was surrounded by a very strong stone wall.

Upon arriving, Pompey found the city divided by sedition. One faction believed it best to deliver the city to Pompey, while Aristobulus's party insisted on shutting the gates because their leader was imprisoned. The party supporting Aristobulus seized the temple and destroyed the bridge connecting the temple to the city to prepare for a siege. Conversely, the opposing faction admitted Pompey's army, delivering both the city and the king's palace to him.

Pompey sent his lieutenant Piso with an army to place garrisons in the palace and the city. He also fortified the houses surrounding the temple.

## The Assault on the Temple
Pompey offered terms of accommodation to those remaining within the temple, but they refused to comply. With the assistance of Hyrcanus, Pompey encompassed the area with a wall and pitched his camp on the north side of the temple, which was the most practicable point of attack. Despite the presence of great towers, a deep valley, and a ditch, the Romans labored daily to raise a bank and fill the ditch.

The completion of this bank was aided by the religious practices of the Israelites. Because their law forbade them from meddling with enemies on the seventh day—though they were permitted to defend themselves during an active assault—the Romans were able to progress their construction without opposition on those days.

Once the bank was sufficiently raised, Pompey brought in battering-rams and mechanical engines from Tyre. These were used to batter the temple with thrown stones, eventually allowing Pompey to take the temple by force and enter the Holy of Holies.

## Aftermath
Following the conquest, Gabinius settled the affairs of Jerusalem according to the inclinations of Antipater before moving on to defeat the Nabateans. Later, the government of the Asamoneans was brought to an end when Herod, who had been made king at Rome, took Jerusalem by force with the assistance of Sosius. Herod's attack on the city walls was conducted in a manner similar to the original assault by Pompey.
