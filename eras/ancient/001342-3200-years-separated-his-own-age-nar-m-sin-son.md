# The Chronology of Nabonidus and Nar[=a]m-Sin

## The Statement of Nabonidus
A significant point of contention in the estimation of the age of Babylonian civilization centers on a statement made by Nabonidus (also referred to as Nabu-na'id). According to this record, a period of 3200 years separated the age of Nabonidus from that of Nar[=a]m-Sin, who was the son of Sargon of Agade. Based solely on this statement, some writers have assigned a date of 3800 B.C. to Sargon.

## Impact on Babylonian Chronology
The figure provided by Nabonidus has historically influenced the dating of early Babylonian and Sumerian remains, with some writers assigning dates as high as 5000 or 6000 B.C. to the earliest Babylonian Semites and Sumerians. However, these high dates have been challenged because they created enormous gaps in the chronological scheme—periods of time not represented by any royal record or name. In an attempt to fill these gaps, the scholar Radau invented dynasties and kings, though their existence has since been definitely disproved.

## Academic Critiques and Emendations
The accuracy of the 3200-year figure has not been universally accepted by historians and scholars:
* **Lehmann-Haupt/Lehmann:** Suggested that the text contained an error, proposing an emendation that reduces the number by a thousand years. Lehmann specifically suggests that the engraver may have added one stroke too many, meaning the figure should be read as 2200 years instead of 3200.
* **Winckler:** Regarded the statement of Nabonidus as an uncritical exaggeration.

## Possible Explanations for the Discrepancy
Archive sources suggest several reasons why the scribes of Nabonidus may have arrived at such a high figure. First, the scribes may have been unwilling to diminish the antiquity of the foundation-inscription of Nar[=a]m-Sin, which Nabonidus had unearthed. Second, recent discoveries suggest that the scribes may have acted in good faith but mistakenly reckoned a number of early dynasties as consecutive when they were, in fact, contemporaneous.

## Relation to Other Eras
Despite the inability to fix a definite date for the early kings of Agade, archaeological and epigraphic evidence indicates that the empire of the Semitic kings of Agade was not separated by a very long interval from the kings of Sumer and Akkad, whose rule began with the founding of the Dynasty of Ur.

## Comparative Chronological Systems
The difficulties in Babylonian dating contrast with the systems used in other regions:
* **Assyria:** Chronology is considered certain from 911 B.C. to 666 B.C. due to the *limmi* (archon) lists from the library of Nineveh. These lists note the beginning of reigns and chief events, such as a solar eclipse calculated to have occurred on June 15, 763 B.C.
* **Babylonia:** Unlike the Assyrian *limmi* system, Babylonia dated years by chief events determined by the government. While registers of dates divided into reigns were kept to compile dynastic lists, the discovered compilations are in an imperfect state.
