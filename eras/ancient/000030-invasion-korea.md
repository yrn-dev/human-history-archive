# THE INVASION OF KOREA

The history of the Japanese archipelago is marked by various periods of interaction with the Korean peninsula, ranging from early diplomatic exchanges and migrations to large-scale military aggressions. These interactions include early conquests, periods of severed relations, and the significant invasion led by Hideyoshi.

## Early Relations and Conflicts
Early Japanese history contains several references to military and diplomatic engagement with Korea. The archive mentions a "conquest of Korea" and "invasions of Korea" in early records, as well as the specific "Jingo's conquest." During the early 7th century, relations were volatile; for five years following 603, all relations with Korea appear to have been severed. However, the Yamato Court showed magnanimity by welcoming immigrants from Shiragi in 608, despite being virtually at war with that country.

By 610, Shiragi and Mimana sent envoys to Japan, which served as a renewal of friendly relations, though the habit of annual tribute-bearing envoys was not revived. In 618, a Korean envoy informed the Yamato court that an invasion of the peninsula by the Sui sovereign, Yang—who led three hundred thousand men—had been defeated. This envoy brought gifts to Japan, including a camel, two captive Chinese, and early instruments of war such as catapults, cross-bows, and flutes.

## The Invasion of Hideyoshi
A major military conflict occurred in the late 16th century under the leadership of Hideyoshi. In 1587, Hideyoshi requested that the Korean monarch explain why the old custom of exchanging envoys had ceased. The King of Korea agreed to renew these relations on the condition that Japanese authorities hand over Korean renegades who had been guiding Japanese pirates in raids on the Korean coast. Once this stipulation was met, a Korean embassy was dispatched and eventually received by Hideyoshi in the hall of audience.

The subsequent invasion in 1592 involved organized military divisions, with Konishi Yukinaga commanding the first division. Konishi was later entrapped by Chinese diplomacy and remained with the last troops in Korea, where he opposed Kato and later Ieyasu.

## Military and Strategic Conditions
The conflict between Japan and Korea was characterized by a significant disparity in military technology and organization. Korea lacked an efficient military organization; its army consisted primarily of men too poor to buy their way out of service. Furthermore, Korean troops lacked match-locks and artillery.

Japan, while aggressive, also faced significant challenges. At the time of the invasion, Japan lacked cartographers and secret-service agents to provide essential intelligence. Consequently, Japanese generals were unfamiliar with the "exceedingly difficult topographical features" of the Korean peninsula, making the environment entirely strange to the landing troops.

From the Korean perspective, the struggle was viewed through the lens of their relationship with China. The dynasty ruling the peninsula owed its existence to Chinese aid and had enjoyed two centuries of peace and prosperity under that protection. This relationship was highlighted by a comment made to Japanese envoys, stating that Japan's ambition to conquer China was akin to attempting to "bail out the ocean with a cockle-shell."

## Other Historical Interactions
Beyond the Hideyoshi invasion, the archive notes other instances of regional instability. In the spring of 1019, during the reign of Go-Ichijo, a large host of invaders entered the island of Tsushima. This island, located approximately sixty miles from both the northeast of Kyushu and the south of Korea, had historically served as a military station for ships traveling between the two regions, although intercourse had been interrupted prior to the 1019 invasion.

Additionally, the archive mentions "Koma" (Korea, now Pyong-yang), noting that its power increased before it was attacked by Japan and Kudara. This led to migrations and the establishment of settlements in Japan, such as Koma-gori in Musashi.
