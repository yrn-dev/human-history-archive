# The Concept of the Unified Kingdom in Ancient History

The historical record contains various instances where the concept of a unified kingdom served as a focal point for national identity, political restoration, or territorial expansion. These movements often arose during periods of fragmentation or foreign dominion, reflecting a desire to return to a perceived state of wholeness or supreme authority.

## The Maccabean Period and the Twelve Tribes
During the early decades of the 2nd century B.C., the Maccabean dynasty expanded its dominion northwards into Galilee and Samaria, and eastwards beyond the Jordan. This territorial growth fostered the belief that the kingdom embraced the entire nation once again. This perspective was based on the supposition that the Twelve tribes were present in Palestine, a belief that displaced an older view holding that nine and a half tribes remained in captivity. However, following the downfall of the Maccabean dynasty in the 1st century A.D., the older belief regarding the captivity of the tribes revived.

## Restoration and Sovereignty in Norway
In the history of Norway, the kingdom established by Harald Harfager eventually fell into decay, leaving no member of his race as the supreme king. The population experienced varying degrees of stability; while the reign of King Hakon (the foster-son of Athelstan) was characterized by contentment, the subsequent rule of Gunhild's sons was marked by tyranny and injustice. This led the people to prefer foreign kings, who generally remained abroad and ignored local customs as long as the "scat" (tax) was paid.

A shift occurred when enmity arose between Earl Hakon and the Danish king Harald. In response to an expedition by the Jomsborg vikings, the people arose to throw off the hostilities and encouraged Earl Hakon to defend the country. However, once Hakon became overbearing, the people of Throndhjem killed him. They subsequently raised Olaf Trygvason to the kingly power. Olaf, who was of the udal succession, was viewed as well-fitted to be a chief, and the general desire of the country was to raise again the kingdom that Harald Harfager had originally created. This restoration eventually led to further tensions, as Olaf imposed unreasonable duties and restricted religious freedom.

## Political Fragmentation and the Frankish Kingdom
In the early medieval period, Spain experienced political fragmentation similar to that of Britain, France, or Italy. This instability was exacerbated by a long-standing feud between the descendants of the Romans and Carthaginians, manifested as hostility between Christians and those of the Jewish faith. This environment facilitated the invasion of Europe by a Moslem army of Arabs and Berbers in 711, who defeated the West Goths and took possession of the country within a few years.

By 720, the expansion of this faith reached the Pyrenees and entered France. This expansion was halted by the kingdom of the Franks, which had been consolidating in North France and the Rhineland for approximately two centuries. The Frankish kingdom served as the western bulwark of Europe, while the Byzantine Empire provided a similar eastern bulwark behind the Taurus Mountains.

## Governance and the Nature of Kingship
Historical analysis suggests a broad antagonism between two methods of social organization: the "community of obedience" and the "community of will." Primitive civilizations often functioned as communities of obedience, centered on god-kings or kings serving under gods. In contrast, nomadic societies—including Nordic, Semitic, and Mongolian peoples—tended toward a community of will, where chiefs were followed rather than masters who compelled.

This distinction is evident in the early governance of various peoples. The Judges of Israel, the early Caliphs, and the "kings" of Tyre and Carthage were elected. Similarly, there was a tradition of election in early German and Frankish kingdoms, and the Great Khan of the Mongols was elected until the time of Kublai. Conversely, settled lands often maintained the idea of a non-elective divinity in kings and an inherent right to rule.

## Other Kingdom Dynamics
In the region of Judea, the management of the kingdom often involved complex political maneuvering. Herod, for instance, managed the high priesthood and royal authority through strategic promises to Alexandra and her son to prevent disturbances within the kingdom he had acquired through extraordinary hazards. Later, the Roman Emperor Claudius restored the kingdoms of Agrippa's grandfathers to him and augmented his dominions. Following Agrippa's death, the youth and unskillfulness of Agrippa Junior led Claudius to appoint Cuspius Fadus as the procurator of the entire kingdom and Judea.
