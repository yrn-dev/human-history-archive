# Triads and Postponements in History and Law

The concept of the triad appears across various historical, religious, and philosophical contexts, while the act of postponement is documented in legal proceedings and diplomatic encounters.

## Mesopotamian and Egyptian Triads
In the religious systems of Babylonia and Assyria, the systematization of the pantheon after the era of Khammurabi included the development of divine triads. One such triad consisted of Anu, Bel, and Ea, which symbolized the three divisions of the universe: the heavens, the earth, and the watery element. Within this system, Bel was assigned the province of the earth. This doctrine allowed Bel to maintain a rank independent of political changes, ensuring his continued importance in the religion of Babylonia and Assyria even after the political decline of Nippur. Consequently, Bel's sanctuary remained a site of pilgrimage for Babylonian rulers and Assyrian kings, including those up to the days of Assur-baui-pal.

A second Mesopotamian triad was composed of Shamash, Sin, and Ishtar, representing the three great forces of nature: the sun, the moon, and the life-giving power. Ishtar, while sometimes associated with Marduk or viewed as the consort of the Assyrian chief god Assur (the "lady" or Belit *par excellence*), was also believed by the people and the priests to stand apart as the source of all life. Depending on the aspect emphasized, Ishtar was viewed as the creative force among animals, the goddess of sexual love, the fertile earth, the mother of mankind, or a general personification of nature.

In Egypt, the Theban triad served as the chief deities worshipped at Deir el Hagar (the stone convent), a Roman-origin ruin located in the oasis of Dakhla.

## Philosophical and Theocratic Triads
In the writings of Bonald, a leading figure of the traditionalist or theocratic school, the triad is used as a formula of constant application to explain all relations as a sequence of cause, means, and effect. Bonald applied this triad to various spheres:
*   **The Universe:** The first cause is the mover, movement is the means, and bodies are the result.
*   **The State:** Power is the cause, ministers are the means, and subjects are the effects.
*   **The Family:** The relation is exemplified by the father, mother, and children.
*   **The Religious World:** A "great triad" consisting of God, the Mediator, and Man, where God is to the God-Man as the God-Man is to Man.

## Legal Postponements in the Epstein Case
In the legal matters involving Epstein, several postponements occurred regarding guilty pleas and victim notifications. In October 2007, Lefkowitz sought the agreement of Acosta to postpone Epstein's entry of a guilty plea in state court from October 26, 2007, to November 20, 2007, citing a scheduling conflict. Acosta, after soliciting input from Sloman, agreed to this postponement. Villafafia later noted that this change in the timeline was a failure to comply with the timeline established by the Non-Prosecution Agreement (NPA), despite the requirement for Epstein to use his "best efforts" to comply.

Additionally, there were postponements regarding the notification of victims. Acosta decided to postpone notifying victims about the terms of the NPA until after Epstein entered his guilty pleas. This decision was based partly on strategic concerns from case agents and Villafafta regarding the preservation of victims' credibility. Specifically, there were concerns that notifying victims earlier would create impeachment evidence suggesting that victims had financial motives to make claims against Epstein, which could be used against them at trial. The Office of Professional Responsibility (OPR) concluded that this postponement and the omission of NPA information during 2008 interviews did not constitute professional misconduct, though it noted that the failure to reevaluate this strategy contributed to victims feeling that the government was intentionally concealing information.

## Diplomatic Postponements in Japan
In 1853, the arrival of Commodore Perry and a US Navy squadron in Uraga Bay caused perturbation in Yedo. The Bakufu summoned a council of feudatories to discuss the situation. However, the death of the shogun, who had been ill, was used as a pretext for postponing discussions with the Americans. Because Perry lacked the authority to use force, he did not press the matter and departed on July 17 after transmitting a letter from the US President to the sovereign of Japan.
