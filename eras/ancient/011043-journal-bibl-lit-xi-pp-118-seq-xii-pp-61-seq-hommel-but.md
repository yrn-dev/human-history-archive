# Asher and the Israelite Tribes

## Geographic and Ethnic Origins
The district located to the west of Galilee was known to the Egyptians as Aser(u) as early as the latter half of the 14th century B.C., during the reigns of Rameses II and Seti I. Based on this, two primary theories exist regarding the nature of this region. One possibility is that Asher was an Israelite tribe that migrated north after separating from other brethren in Egypt, a pattern potentially mirrored by other tribes. Alternatively, the area may have been a district that was not originally closely bound to Israel but was later incorporated into the national kingdom and designated as a "son" of Jacob through a genealogical device. It is also possible that some Israelite populations moved to this area from an earlier home in the south, following a precedent set by Dan.

## Historical Associations and Etymology
Certain clans within Asher, specifically Malchiel and Heber, have been linked to the Habiri (a people) and Milk-ili (a hostile chief), both of whom appear in the Amarna Tablets. However, it is considered unlikely that events from approximately 1400 B.C. would have survived only in this specific form. Similarly, the suggestion that the name Asher derives from a figure named Abd-ashirta from the same period is viewed with skepticism. A more likely etymological connection may exist with the goddess Ashir(t)a.

## The Reign of Josiah
In the broader history of the region, the reign of Josiah is identified as a climax. Historical frameworks of this period include narratives concerning the reforms of Josiah and repairs made to the Temple. These reforms are described in terms that suggest an acquaintance with the teachings of Deuteronomy, which served to promulgate the reforms themselves. While these records exist, they show signs of revision; specifically, certain sections point toward the exile, and some portions appear to be insertions.

## Regional Context and External Influence
The area of Judea and the surrounding territories experienced various external influences over time. During the Hellenistic period, the Seleucid and Ptolemaïc kings were viewed as foreigners by their native subjects. While these kings often patronized native cults as a matter of policy—such as Antiochus I rebuilding the temple of Nebo at Borsippa—they occasionally seized temple treasures during financial emergencies. Antiochus III is noted for bestowing favors on the Temple at Jerusalem, though some documents regarding this relationship are considered spurious.
