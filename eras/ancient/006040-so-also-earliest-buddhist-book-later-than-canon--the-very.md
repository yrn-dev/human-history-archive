# Buddhist Literature and the Question of the Soul

The development of Buddhist literature evolved from oral conversations into a complex system of canonical and post-canonical texts. Because the Buddha taught through conversation, his doctrines were originally embodied in set phrases known as *sūtras*. These served as *memoria technica*—catchwords that allowed practitioners to remember fuller expositions. While writing was known, a lack of suitable materials made lengthy books impossible, leading the Buddha to use Pāli, the ordinary conversational idiom of the day, rather than the Sanskrit used by the Brahmins.

## The Canonical Books
Following the death of the Buddha, his disciples collected his sayings into the Four Nikāyas, or "collections," which reached their final form approximately fifty to sixty years later. A supplementary Nikāya was also created to house sayings and verses attributed to the disciples. These texts were handed down by memory for a generation or two, often accompanied by running commentaries.

The structure of these records grew gradually: individual sūtras were first combined into episodes, which were then woven into *suttantas*, and finally grouped into the Nikāyas. Parallel to these prose statements were verses, which were used because they aided memory and expressed the ecstasy and enthusiasm inspired by the reforming doctrines.

Later additions to the canon include seven works on *Abhidhamma*, which provide a more classified and elaborate exposition of the Dhamma. One such manual, the *Dhamma Sangani* (translated as *Buddhist Psychology*), is estimated to date from the 4th century B.C. and details the psychological conceptions underlying Buddhist ethics.

## The Question of the Soul
A central position in Buddhist exposition is the rejection of the "soul." The books state that the Buddha devoted his very first discourse to the first converts to this subject. This systematic rejection of current theories regarding souls is found in the first of the *Dialogues of Gotama*.

This theme continues in later canonical works. The *Kathāvatthu*, the latest book in the canon, places the question of the soul at the head of its discussions on community disagreements, devoting more space to it than to all other points combined.

This emphasis extends into the earliest Buddhist books written after the canon. In the series of conversations between the Buddhist teacher Nāgasena and the Greek king Menander (also known as the *Milinda*), the author first addresses the question of the soul. In this work, Nāgasena convinces the king that the "soul" in the ordinary sense does not exist, returning to this subject repeatedly.

## Post-Canonical and Sectarian Works
Following the canonical period, a voluminous literature emerged. The *Milinda* is noted as one of the earliest historical novels, focusing on the conversion of King Menander of Bactria. Other significant works include:

*   **The Mahāvastu (Sublime Story):** A Sanskrit work from the Mahā-sanghikas school. It details the Buddha's previous births and his victory over ignorance under the Bo Tree to attain *Sambodhi* (the higher Wisdom of Nirvāna).
*   **The Netti Pakarana:** A Pāli work from the 2nd or 3rd century of our era that discusses important questions within the Buddhist community by quoting canonical works.
*   **The Saddharma Pundarīka:** A Sanskrit text from the 2nd or 3rd century that serves as the earliest exposition of the later Mahāyāna doctrine.
*   **The Prajñā Pāramitā:** A later Mahāyāna treatise that eventually replaced original doctrines in India.
*   **The Divyāvadāna:** A collection of legends concerning the Buddha's leading disciples and members of the order over three centuries.

## Folklore and Poetry
The *Jātaka* book consists of 550 folk-tales from India, presented as verses uttered by the Buddha in previous births. In each story, the Buddha is identified as the hero. This collection is regarded as the most authentic and complete collection of folklore extant, with many stories migrating into medieval European poems and homilies.

Other poetical works include the *Sutta Nipāta*, which contains fifty-five poems, mostly short lyrics. Additionally, the *Khuddaka Nikāya* contains the *Khuddaka Pātha*—which includes a profession of faith and a list of thirty-four constituents of the human body—and the *Dhammapada*, a textbook of 423 verses covering twenty-six points of Buddhist ethics and self-training.

## Evolution and Decline
Over time, the thoroughgoing positions of early Buddhism began to fade. Comparison between canonical books and later texts reveals a gradual approximation of Buddhist views toward other Indian religions and philosophies. This decline was accelerated by the conversion of Asoka in the 3rd century B.C.; the influx of nominal converts led to a relaxation of original doctrines. Consequently, the "soul theory" regained influence, popular superstitions and gods were once again favored, and the original ideal of the Arahat—attaining salvation in this world through self-mastery—was forgotten or condemned.
