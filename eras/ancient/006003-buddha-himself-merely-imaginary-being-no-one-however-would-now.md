# Buddha

## Historical Identity and Origins
In European contexts, the term "Buddha" is used to refer to the last historical Buddha, whose family name was Gotama. He was the son of Suddhoda, a chief of the Sakiyas, which was one of the republican clans existing in India at the time. While the early history of religious leaders is often surrounded by miraculous and legendary tales, it is now admitted that there is a basis of truth beneath these accounts sufficient to allow for an intelligent history. This stands in contrast to views held as late as 1854, such as that of Professor Wilson of Oxford, who argued before the Royal Asiatic Society of London that the life of Buddha was a myth and that he was "merely an imaginary being."

## Chronology
The dates of the Buddha's life are determined by combining the date of Asoka's accession to the throne with the interval between that event and the Buddha's death. Asoka's accession is estimated at approximately 270 B.C., based on mentions of Greek kings in his edicts. According to the Ceylon Chronicles, the interval between Asoka's accession and the Buddha's death was 218 years, placing the Buddha's death at 488 B.C. Given that he was eighty years old when he died, his birth is dated to 568 B.C. However, dates accepted in Siam, Burma, and Ceylon are roughly half a century earlier, citing 623 B.C. for his birth and 543 B.C. for his death.

## The Concept of the Buddha
According to Buddhist theory, a "Buddha" appears in the world periodically to preach the true doctrine. Over time, this teaching is corrupted or lost, remaining absent until a new Buddha appears. Indian belief holds that Wisdom returns to earth at long intervals and is revealed through a chosen person; thus, Gautama Buddha is viewed as the latest in a series of many such Buddhas.

Regarding his own title, there is a distinction between the belief of his followers and his own discourse. While his pupils hailed him as the Buddha after he convinced them of his enlightenment, some sources suggest he did not recognize the theory of a series of Buddhas or use the title himself in his discourses, instead using the title "Arahat."

## Teachings and Mission
At the age of 35, the teacher sought to proclaim his gospel. After discovering his old teachers, Udraka and Alara, had died, he traveled to the Deer-forest near Benares to address five former disciples. During this journey, he encountered a wandering sophist named Upaka. In their exchange, the Buddha described his mission as going to Benares to "beat the drum of the Ambrosia" (setting up the light of the doctrine of Nirvana) in the darkness of the world. He defined himself as a "conqueror" because he had conquered "intoxications"—the mental intoxication resulting from craving after future life, sensuality, or ignorance—and because evil dispositions had ceased within him.

The core of the gospel, as enunciated in ancient scriptures, defines Nirvana as supreme happiness. The practical application of this philosophy is the life of a monk who separates himself from the world. Those seeking the higher life of an ascetic or saint must observe five commandments:
*   To kill no living thing.
*   Not to speak what is untrue.
*   Not to drink intoxicating drinks.
*   Not to touch another's wife.
*   Not to lay hands on another's property.

## Later Interpretations and Legacy
The concept of emancipation from the self proved difficult for many to grasp. Consequently, some teachers sent out from Benares shifted the focus of the teaching toward salvation from sufferings and misfortunes. These interpretations incorporated existing superstitions, such as the transmigration of the soul—an idea contrary to the Master's own teaching. Virtue was urged upon the people to avoid falling into the numerous hells of torment described by Brahminical teachers or living again in miserable forms. In this context, Buddha was represented as a savior from unlimited torment.

In later history, the Buddha's image became a subject of political and religious significance. For example, in 1586, Hideyoshi erected a colossal wooden image of Buddha at the Hoko-ji in Kyoto. After an earthquake destroyed it in 1596, Ieyasu advised Hideyori to replace it with a bronze idol. This project, completed in 1614, cost many thousands of ryo, funded by converting gold ingots stored in the Osaka castle. The dedication ceremony was eventually interdicted by Ieyasu because the temple bell's inscription, written by the prelate Seikan, was deemed treasonable to the Tokugawa.
