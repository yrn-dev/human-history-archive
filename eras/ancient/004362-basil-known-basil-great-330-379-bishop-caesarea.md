# Basil the Great

Basil, known as Basil the Great (c. 330–379), was a prominent 4th-century churchman and the bishop of Caesarea. He is recognized as one of the three great fathers of Cappadocia, alongside Gregory of Nyssa and Gregory of Nazianzus.

## Early Life and Education
Basil was born around 330 in Caesarea, Cappadocia. During his childhood, his family moved to Pontus, though he later returned to Cappadocia to live with his mother's relatives and was raised by his grandmother, Macrina. 

Driven by a desire for learning, Basil spent four or five years studying in Athens and Constantinople. While in Athens, he was a fellow-student of Gregory of Nazianzus. Both men were deeply influenced by the writings of Origen, and together they compiled the *Philocalia*, a well-known anthology of Origen's works. It was during his time in Athens that Basil began to think seriously about religion. He resolved to visit the most famous hermit saints in Arabia and Syria to learn how to achieve enthusiastic piety and how to discipline his body through ascetic devices and maceration.

## Family and Religious Life
Basil came from a distinguished family that provided several notable supporters to the Church. His siblings included his eldest brother, the famous Christian jurist Naucratius; his second brother, Gregory of Nyssa; his youngest brother, Peter, who served as bishop of Sebaste; and his eldest sister, Macrina, who was celebrated for her saintly life. The family was characterized by a tendency toward enthusiastic piety and ecstatic emotion.

Following his ascetic travels, Basil led a convent near Arnesi in Pontus. This community included his widowed mother, Emilia, his sister Macrina, and several other women who dedicated themselves to charitable works and a life of prayer.

## Ecclesiastical Career
Basil was not ordained as a presbyter until 365. This ordination likely occurred because his ecclesiastical superiors wanted to utilize his talents against the Arians. At the time, Arians were numerous in the region and enjoyed the favor of the reigning emperor in Constantinople, Valens.

In 370, Basil was chosen to succeed Eusebius as the bishop of Caesarea. Upon taking this office, his "great powers" were called into action. During his tenure as bishop, Basil established an ecclesiastical center on the plain approximately one mile to the north-east of the old town of Caesarea; this new center eventually supplanted the original town.

## Legacy and Historical Records
Biographical information regarding Basil is derived from several sources, including incidental notices in the writings of Jerome, Basil of Caesarea, and Eusebius, as well as an outline of his early years found in his own eulogy on Origen. While Gregory of Nyssa wrote a panegyric attributing various startling miracles to Basil, these accounts are described as untrustworthy and do not reach the level of narratives found in the *Breviarium Romanum* or the *Martyrologies*.
