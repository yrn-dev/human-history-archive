# Bagdad

Bagdad is the capital of the Turkish vilayet of the same name and serves as the headquarters for the VI. Army Corps, which also garrisons the Mosul and Basra vilayets. The city is situated on both sides of the river Tigris within an extensive desert plain, located at latitude 33° 20' N. and longitude 44° 24' E. At this location, the Tigris and the Euphrates reach their closest proximity, separated by a distance of slightly more than 25 miles.

## Geography and Water Systems
The city is connected to the Euphrates via a series of canals, the northernmost of which is the Sakhlawieh (formerly known as the Isa). This canal leaves the Euphrates near the bridge of boats and the ruins of ancient Anbar, eventually entering the Tigris below the town through the Masudi canal, which encircles western Bagdad. While this canal was navigable for craft of some size during Chesney's 1838 survey, it now primarily serves to increase periodic floods that can turn Bagdad into an island city and threaten its protective dikes.

Historically, Bagdad was intersected by numerous aqueducts and canals that delivered water from both the Tigris and Euphrates into houses and streets. Currently, only one aqueduct remains, which carries Tigris water to the shrine of Abd al-Qadir. The population now draws water directly from the Tigris, distributed by men and asses using goat-skins. The city lacks a sewerage system; street surfaces serve this purpose, and refuse often washes into the Tigris at the same point where drinking water is collected. These conditions contribute to a high death rate and enormous mortality during epidemics, often prompting the population to encamp in the desert to the north.

## Ancient History and Origins
The site of Bagdad has a long history predating Islam. An ancient Babylonian city named Baghdadu may date back as far as 2000 B.C., appearing in lists within the library of Assur-bani-pal. The name is also recorded on the Michaux stone, found on the Tigris near the present city site and dating to 1100 B.C. (the time of Tiglath-Pileser I). 

The original city of Baghdadu was located on the site of western or old Bagdad. This is evidenced by a quay on the west bank of the Tigris, observed by Sir Henry Rawlinson in 1849, which was built of bricks laid in bitumen and bears an inscription of Nebuchadrezzar, king of Babylon. References in the Jewish Talmud indicate the city continued to exist at and after the start of the current era. However, Arabian writers claim that when the caliph Mansur founded the Arab city of Bagdad, only an old convent existed on the site.

Other nearby ruins include the palace of Chosroes at Ctesiphon (or Madain), located approximately 15 miles below Bagdad on the east side of the river. In the desert, about 9 miles west of the city, lies Akerkuf, a tower of unburned brick rising over 100 feet. Inscribed bricks suggest a connection to Kurigalzu, king of Babylon around 1300 B.C., though the site remained important for its canals as late as the Abbasid caliphate.

## City Structure and Architecture
The city is divided into eastern and western sections. The original city was built on the western bank, but for centuries it has been a suburb of the larger eastern city. The western area covers 146 acres within its walls, while the eastern section extends over 591 acres. Both sections were formerly enclosed by brick walls with large round towers and a deep fosse. The western city had three gates, and the eastern had four. One eastern gate, the "Gate of the Talisman" (dated A.D. 1220), has been closed since 1638. Between 1868 and 1872, governor Midhat Pasha destroyed much of these walls to create a public park and esplanade, though some fragments and mounds remain.

The principal public buildings are located in the eastern section along the river bank:
* **The Citadel:** Located to the north, featuring a high wall and a clock-tower.
* **The Serai:** The palace of the Turkish governor, a large, confused structure of varying periods.
* **The Custom House:** Built from the materials of the 1233 A.D. college of Mostansir; only a minaret and small wall sections of the original college remain.
* **Consulates:** The British residency and German consulate are also situated on the river-front.

The city contains approximately thirty mosques within its walls. The Marjanieh mosque features 14th-century arabesque work and a pointed arch door with red sculptured bands. The mosque of Khaseki, dated A.D. 1682, is believed to have been a Christian church; it contains sculptures likely from the caliphate and a prayer niche with a Roman-style arch and fluted shafts. The minaret of Suk el-Ghazl dates to the 13th century.

Within the city, particularly in the north-western quarter, there is significant open space. Gardens containing date-palms, lemons, oranges, figs, and pomegranates are abundant, giving the city the appearance of rising from the trees when viewed from a distance.
