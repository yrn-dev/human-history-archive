# Recognition of Antiquity and Greatness in Native Populations

The recognition of a location's antiquity and historical significance by its native inhabitants is a recurring theme in the records of various global civilizations. This recognition often manifests through specific titles, the preservation of mythical genealogies, or the scientific study of ancestral monuments.

## The City of Balkh
In the case of Balkh, the native populations explicitly recognize the antiquity and greatness of the city, referring to it as the "Mother of Cities." Mythological accounts attribute its foundation to Kaiomurs, who is described as the Persian Romulus. Historically, the city was a rival to Babylon, Nineveh, and Ecbatana at a very early date.

For a significant period, Balkh served as the central seat of the Zoroastrian religion, and it is said that the religion's founder died within the city walls. The city's religious diversity was noted by Hsuan Tsang in the 7th century, who recorded approximately 100 Buddhist convents and 3,000 devotees in the city or its vicinity, along with numerous stupas. One particularly notable monument was the New Convent (Nau Behar or Nava Bihara), which housed a costly statue of Buddha.

Later descriptions highlight the city's infrastructure and commerce. The 10th-century traveler Ibn-Haukal described Balkh as a city built of clay with six gates, ramparts, and a mosque. By the 12th century, Idrisi noted its active trade and various educational establishments, with commercial routes extending as far as China and India. Despite being sacked and leveled by Jenghiz Khan in 1220 and again by Timur in the 14th century, Marco Polo still described it as a "great" and "noble city" in the following century.

## Athenian Archaeology and Scientific Study
In Athens, the recognition of the city's status as the birthplace of European literature and art has led to a concentrated effort in archaeological investigation. Recent excavations conducted by the Greek government and both native and foreign scientific societies have provided new insights into the city's topography, monuments, and art.

Native archaeologists currently hold a recognized position in this scientific work. The archive notes a shift in the approach of these scholars; the patriotic sentiment of previous eras, which occasionally warped judgment, has been replaced by a devotion to science for its own sake. This has resulted in the competent supervision of art collections and excavations. Consequently, Athens has evolved into a center of learning and a basis for research across the Greek world, where the scrutiny of physical sites is used to test theories previously based primarily on literary evidence.

## Mythical Antiquities in Greece
In other regions of the Greek world, antiquity is recognized through legendary and poetic traditions. The Kadmeians, the legendary population of Thêbes, are noted for being rich in heroic and divine mythical antiquities. Thêbes is recognized as the natal city of both Hêraklês and Dionysus. Furthermore, the native Bœôtian poetess Korinna focused on native genealogy, deriving the Ogygian gates of Thêbes from Ogygus, the son of Bœôtus.

On the island of Lesbos, native fabulists and poets focused their archaeological accounts less on Æolic settlers and more on the tribes and heroes who possessed the island prior to that settlement, dating back to the deluge of Deukalion. This included Makar, a great native hero of the island. Similarly, poets from Samos and Chios emphasized the ante-Ionic antiquities of their respective islands. In the case of the Grecian establishment at Magnêsia on the Mæander, its great antiquity was recognized during the era of the Roman emperors.
