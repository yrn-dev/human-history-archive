# Tracts, E. 126 (20)

The reference "Tracts, E. 126 (20)" pertains to the legal and political conflicts involving the Earl of Bristol (Digby) and his adversarial relationship with the English monarchy and the Duke of Buckingham during the early 17th century.

## Diplomatic Conflict and Fall from Favor
The Earl of Bristol was created earl of Bristol on September 15. During his tenure, he urged for a marriage treaty, believing it would provide favorable conditions for Frederick. However, the negotiations eventually failed following the arrival of Buckingham and Charles in March 1623. 

Bristol incurred the resentment of these figures, primarily driven by the "implacable jealousy" of Buckingham. This tension was exacerbated by a letter Bristol wrote to James, which informed the king of the unpopularity Buckingham faced among Spanish ministers. Additionally, Bristol attempted to maintain peace with Spain after the departure of Charles and Buckingham.

## Legal Battles and Imprisonment
By January 1624, Bristol left Spain. Upon his arrival at Dover in March, he found that Charles and Buckingham held complete ascendancy over the king. Consequently, Bristol was forbidden from appearing at court and was ordered to confine himself at Sherborne. Buckingham required him to answer a series of interrogatories; Bristol refused to inculpate himself and instead demanded a trial by parliament.

Following the death of James, Charles I removed Bristol from the privy council and ordered him to be absent from the first parliament. In January 1626, Charles angrily refused Bristol's demand to be present at the coronation, accusing him of attempting to pervert his religion while in Spain.

## Parliamentary Conflict and the King's Obstruction
In March 1626, during the second parliament, Bristol applied to the Lords, who supported his rights. Although Charles sent him a writ, it was accompanied by a letter from Lord Keeper Coventry requesting that he not use it. Bristol ignored this request, took his seat, and demanded justice against Buckingham (Thomason Tracts, E. 126 (20)).

The king attempted to obstruct Bristol's attack by having the attorney-general bring Bristol to the bar on May 1 on an accusation of high treason. The Lords decided that both the charges against Buckingham and the charges against Bristol should be investigated simultaneously. These proceedings were halted on June 15 due to the dissolution of parliament. Subsequently, Charles ordered a prosecution in the Star Chamber, and Bristol was sent to the Tower. He remained imprisoned there until March 17, 1628, when the peers of Charles's third parliament insisted on his restoration to his seat in the Lords and his liberation.

## Later Political Career and Final Imprisonment
After his liberation, Bristol's relationship with the crown shifted. During discussions on the Petition of Right, he supported the king's regal power and the use of the king's prerogative during emergencies, though he joined the demand for the king to fully accept the petition after an initial unsatisfactory response.

Bristol remained out of politics until the Scottish rebellion, at which point he warned Charles that attacking with inadequate forces was dangerous. He served as a commissioner to treat with the Scots at Ripon in September 1640 and was a leader in the Great Council at York. He strongly advised the summoning of parliament and, in February 1641, advocated for administrative reforms.

Despite these efforts, Bristol was eventually declared an "evil counsellor" by the House of Commons on December 27. This was based on a plea by Cromwell on December 28, alleging that Bristol had advocated bringing the northern army to overawe parliament in the previous spring, though the archive notes there is no evidence to support this charge. The hatred for Digby from the parliamentary party was likely caused by his indifference to religion, his "Spanish proclivities," and the unpopularity of his son.

On March 28, 1642, Bristol was sent to the Tower again for failing to disclose the Kentish petition to parliament. After being liberated in April, he spoke in the Lords in June in vindication of the king and in favor of an accommodation. Believing that armed rebellion against the king was a "wicked violation of the most solemn oaths," he joined Charles at York, attended Edgehill, and accompanied the king to Oxford. On February 1, 1643, he was named for removal from the court along with Lord Herbert of Raglan.
