# GASPARO ASELLI

## Biography
Gasparo Aselli (also referred to as Asellius or Asellio) was an Italian physician born in Cremona around 1581. During his career, he served as a professor of surgery and anatomy at Pavia and maintained a medical practice in Milan. He died in Milan in 1626.

## Scientific Contributions
Aselli is credited with the discovery of the lacteal vessels. This finding was published in his work titled *De Lactibus*, which was released in Milan in 1627.

# ASSYRIA

## Geography and Origins
Assyria grew on the banks of the Tigris and Euphrates rivers. The land, known in Assyro-Babylonian literature as *mat Assur* ("land of Assur"), derived its name from the ancient city of Assur. This city was located at the southern extremity of Assyria proper. The territory's boundaries were the lower Zab river to the south and the Zagros mountain range (in present-day Kurdistan) to the north. The kingdom developed through a gradual process of colonization within the rich vales of the middle Tigris region, which was watered by the Tigris and tributary streams, most notably the lower Zab.

## Relationship with Babylonia
Historically and geographically, Assyria and Babylonia were closely intertwined, with the power of one often reflecting the weakness of the other. While classical accounts frequently prioritize Assyria, deciphered inscriptions indicate that for most of their history—with the exception of some seven or eight centuries—Assyria was a dependency or province of Babylonia. Babylonia is described as the mother country, and the Assyrians derived their culture, religion, literature, sciences, arts, and characters from their southern neighbors. The two peoples shared similar language and race.

## Social and Political Structure
Assyria differed essentially in character from Babylonia; while Babylonia was a land of agriculturists and merchants, Assyria was described as an "organized camp." The Assyrian dynasties were established by successful generals, and the king functioned as an autocratic general of the army. In early periods, the king was supported by a feudal nobility, but from the reign of Tiglath-pileser III, an elaborate bureaucracy was established. The royal palace was separate from the temples of the gods and was more sumptuous than those temples.

Socially, castes were unknown in Assyria. The military aristocracy served as the counterpart to the Babylonian priesthood. Scribes formed a more important class in Assyria than they did in Babylonia, while the commercial and agricultural classes were relatively few. The population consisted largely of soldiers, and the archive notes that even the sailor belonged to Babylonia. Other societal roles included artisans, poets, musicians, and money-lenders. There was also a large body of slaves.

## Military Organization
Assyria is noted for being the first to organize a standing army, which was raised in part through conscription. The *tartan*, or commander-in-chief, held a rank second only to the king. Military advancements were introduced by kings of the second Assyrian empire:
* **Tiglath-pileser III:** Introduced high boots and saddles for cavalry, which superseded the use of chariots.
* **Sennacherib:** Created a corps of slingers.

During marches, the army utilized battering-rams, baggage-carts, and tents.

## History and Expansion
The foundation of the monarchy is attributed to Zulilu, who lived after Bel-kapkapi (1900 B.C.). Early Assyria saw Samsi-Hadad (or Samsi-Raman) as a vassal-prince at Assur under Khammurabi. Assyria eventually grew in power at the expense of Babylonia. Assur-yuballidh of Assyria corresponded with Amenophis IV of Egypt and intervened in Babylonian politics to place Burna-buryas on the throne.

In 1300 B.C., Shalmaneser I openly claimed supremacy in western Asia and founded Calah. He campaigned against wild tribes of the north-west and the Hittites, settling colonists in Cappadocia. His son, Tukulti-In-aristi, later conquered Babylon and killed King Bitilyasu, establishing Assyria as the mistress of the oriental world.

## Foreign Conflicts and Decline
Assyrian kings frequently campaigned in Syria and Phoenicia. Shalmaneser, king of Assyria, attacked Syria in 842 and later conducted a hostile expedition against Phoenicia. According to the archives of Tyre, Shalmaneser besieged Tyre for five years, blocking their rivers and aqueducts, though the Tyrians survived by digging wells. In the 9th century B.C., Jehu, king of Israel, paid tribute to Assyria. Later, in 732, Tiglath-Pileser III reduced Damascus after being appealed to for protection by Ahaz, king of Judah.

The decline of Assyria was precipitated by exhaustion from long struggles. Assur-bani-pal fought a devastating contest with Elam, leveling the capital Susa, but the war left Assyria maimed and drained of wealth and fighting population. This exhaustion made it difficult to garrison conquered populations or face the Scythians (Manda), who harassed the frontiers. The empire eventually collapsed after being drained of its fighting population during the age of Assur-bani-pal.
