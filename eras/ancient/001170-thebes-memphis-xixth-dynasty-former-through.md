# Thebes and Memphis in the XIXth Dynasty

The cities of Thebes and Memphis served as principal centers of power and religious activity in ancient Egypt. While their relative importance shifted over various eras, both cities remained central to the administration and spiritual life of the kingdom, particularly during the XIXth Dynasty.

## Political Status and Dynastic Succession
According to the records of Manetho, an Egyptian high priest and keeper of sacred archives, the Egyptian kings were not drawn uniformly from a single city. Manetho enumerated twenty-six dynasties prior to the Persian conquest by Kambysês. In this framework, Memphis was the seat of the third, fourth, sixth, seventh, and eighth dynasties. Thebes, referred to as Diospolites, was the seat of the eleventh, twelfth, and thirteenth dynasties, as well as the seventeenth, eighteenth, nineteenth, and twentieth.

The XIXth Dynasty is specifically identified as Diospolite, indicating that Thebes was the center of power during this period. This dynasty included Rameses II, described as a great builder of temples who reigned for seventy-seven years (approximately 1317 to 1250 B.C.). Some accounts suggest Rameses II may have been the Pharaoh of Moses. Another monarch of the XIXth Dynasty, Rameses III, is depicted on his sarcophagus bearing the symbols of three great gods: the sun ball and feathers of Ammon Ra, the horns of the cow goddess Hathor, and the two sceptres of Osiris, the god of Day and Resurrection.

## Religious Life in the XIXth Dynasty
During the XIXth Dynasty, Thebes and Memphis were sites of diverse religious veneration. Both cities hosted the worship of Baal and Astarte. The deity Baal was known among the Hittites during the time of Rameses II and was identified with the planet Jupiter in Babylonia. Through the influence of the Aramaeans—who used the Babylonian spelling "Bel"—Baal eventually became known to the Greeks as Belos, who was identified with Zeus.

## Comparative Influence of Thebes and Memphis
The historical relationship between Thebes and Memphis was marked by a shift in predominance. There was an earlier period in which Thebes was the seat of Egyptian power, making upper Egypt more consequential than middle Egypt. The grandeur of this era is evidenced by the immeasurable range of rock-hewn sculptures, ornamental painting, and the stupendous magnitude of palaces and temples remaining at Thebes, as well as at Elephantinê, Edfu, and Ombi. These ruins suggest that Thebes was once the location where the kings and priests resided and where land taxes from the productive delta were paid.

Eventually, Memphis usurped this honor from Thebes. Situated on the left bank of the Nile, slightly above where the delta begins, Memphis benefited from its proximity to the delta's large number of cities and wide surface of productive territory. By the time the Greeks became cognizant of Egypt, Memphis was considered the first city in the country.

## Later Significance of Memphis
Memphis continued to be a center of religious and political importance long after the XIXth Dynasty. It was home to the priests of Hephaistos and the sacred bull Apis. Later, Psammetichus built magnificent propylæa for the temple of Hephaistos and a splendid new stable for Apis. The city also remained a focal point during the Persian period; when Cambyses arrived at Memphis, the appearance of the god Apis (called Epaphos by the Hellenes) triggered widespread festivities among the Egyptians.
