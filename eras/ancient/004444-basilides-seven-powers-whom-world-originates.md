# Basilides

Basilides was a prominent exponent of Gnosticism who lived in Alexandria, likely during the first few decades of the 2nd century. While Eusebius's *Chronicle* dates his first appearance to A.D. 133, other evidence suggests his activity may have begun earlier; Agrippa Castor, who lived under the reign of Hadrian (117-138), wrote a polemic against him. Basilides authored an exegetical work consisting of twenty-four books based on a gospel, though the specific gospel he used is unknown. Further writings associated with him include *Ethika*, *Exegetika* on the prophet Parchor, and works by his son Isidorus, such as *Peri prosphuous psuches*.

## Cosmological System and the Seven Powers

The system of Basilides involves a complex series of emanations from a supreme God, referred to as the *innatus pater*. From this supreme God, 365 heavens with their respective spirits emanated, a concept that corresponds to astronomical views of the heavens and their 365 daily aspects. The supreme God is sometimes called Abraxas (or Abrasax), a name with a numerical value of 365.

The *Pater innatus* is surrounded by a series of Hypostases. According to Irenaeus, there are five: *Nous*, *Logos*, *Phronesis*, *Sophia*, and *Dunamis*. Clemens suggests that *Dikaiosune* and *Eirene* may be added to this group, bringing the total to seven.

Within this framework, the world originates from seven powers, which are regarded as the lowest emanations of the supreme God. These seven world-creating angels are described as the forces that create and rule the world. These powers are identified as the seven planetary deities of later Babylonian religion. In the Gnostic context, these deities became semi-daemonic or daemonic forces, reflecting a shift where Iranian monotheism superseded Babylonian religion and degraded its gods to daemons. Basilides identified the highest of these seven angels as the creator of the worlds.

## Redemption and the Saviour

As confusion in the world increased, the supreme God sent his *Nous*—also called Christ—into the world to bring salvation. Clemens refers to this Saviour as *diakonos* or *pneuma diakonoumenon*. The relationship between this Saviour and the historical Jesus of Nazareth is subject to varying accounts. Basilides acknowledged an earthly Jesus but denied that he was sinless. Irenaeus reports that the Saviour appeared only as a phantasm, while the *Excerpta ex Theodoto* claims the *Diakonos* descended upon Jesus during his baptism in the form of a dove. Consequently, followers of Basilides observed the day of the baptism of Jesus, the *epiphaneia*, as a high festival.

Salvation in this system is viewed as the restoration and fulfillment of all things through the "great separation of the sexes." Redemption consists of separating that which had been combined for evil.

## Later Developments and Magic

Among the later followers of Basilides, magic became a significant element of the faith. They maintained the names of the rulers of the various heavens as weighty secrets, believing that knowledge of these names would allow a soul to pass through the heavens after death to reach the supreme God. 

This belief extended to Christ, who was thought to possess a mystic name—*Caulacau* (or *QAW LAQAW*)—which enabled him to descend through the heavens to earth and subsequently ascend to the Father. In this tradition, redemption was conceived as the revelation of such mystic names, a practice linked to the use of Abraxas gems.

## Comparative Context

Basilides' Gnosticism is characterized as being closest to Persian dualism in its final form and is closely related to the teachings of Satornil (Saturninus). He is distinguished from most other Gnostic sects, except perhaps Jewish-Christian Gnosticism, by the total absence of the idea of a fall within the godhead or the figure of the fallen female god, Sophia Achamoth. His system is viewed as a further development of Iranian dualism, which later influenced the religious system of Mani.
