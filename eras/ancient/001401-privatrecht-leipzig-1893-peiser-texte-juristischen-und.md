# Legal and Historical Bibliography of the Ancient Near East and Greece

The study of ancient legal systems and historical documentation is supported by a vast array of specialized texts, ranging from the analysis of cuneiform tablets in Mesopotamia to the judicial records of the Athenian republic. These sources provide the foundation for understanding the contracts, laws, and social structures of early civilizations.

## Babylonian and Assyrian Law

The legal life of ancient Babylonia and Assyria is documented through various collections of contracts and deeds. Key scholarly contributions to this field include the works of J. Kohler and F. E. Peiser, specifically *Aus dem babylonischen Rechtsleben* (published from 1890 onwards) and Peiser's *Babylonische Vertrage* (1890) and *Keilinschriftliche Actenstuecke* (1889). Further insights into old Babylonian private law are provided by Br. Meissner in *Beitraege zur altbabylonischen Privatrecht* (1893), while F. E. Peiser's "Texte juristischen und geschaeftlichen Inhalts" appears in volume iv of Schrader's *Keilinschriftliche Bibliothek* (1896).

The transfer of property in Assyria is detailed in the three-volume work *Assyrian Deeds and Documents relating to the Transfer of Property* by C. H. W. Johns (1898), who also authored *Babylonian and Assyrian Laws, Contracts and Letters* (1904). Other foundational histories include H. Radau's *Early Babylonian History* (1900) and the general study of contracts by Oppert and Menant in *Documents juridiques de l'Assyrie et de la Chaldee* (1877).

## The Code of Hammurabi

A central focus of Mesopotamian legal study is the Code of Hammurabi, King of Babylon, dated to approximately 2250 B.C. The *Editio princeps* of the code was produced by V. Scheil in 1902 within the *Textes Elamites-Semitiques*. Other significant analyses of these laws include works by H. Winckler in *Der alte Orient*, D. H. Mueller's *Die Gesetze Hammurabis* (1903), and the collaborative work of J. Kohler and F. E. Peiser titled *Hammurabis Gesetz* (1904). R. F. Harper also published a study on the code in 1904, and S. A. Cook provided a comparative analysis in *The Laws of Moses and the Code of Hammurabi* (1903).

## Attic and Greek Law

The legal framework of ancient Greece, particularly Attic law, is preserved through both primary texts and modern treatises. Primary Greek sources include the *Corpus juris Attici* edited by I. B. Telfy (1868) and Aristotle's *Constitution of Athens*, with notable editions by Kenyon (1891, 1903), Blass (1903), and Sandys (1893). The legal orations of Lysias, Isaeus, and Demosthenes—including the *Private Orations* and *Against Midias*—serve as critical evidence for judicial proceedings. Additionally, the *Inscr. juridiques grecques* (1891-1904) provides a record of Greek legal inscriptions.

Modern scholarly interpretations of Greek law include:
* **General Treatises:** L. Beauchet's four-volume *Droit prive de la rep. athen.* (1897) and G. Glotz's study on family solidarity in Greek criminal law (1904).
* **Procedural Studies:** J. H. Lipsius's works on the Attic process (*Der attische Process*, 1883-1887) and the broader legal proceedings in *Das attische Recht und Rechtsverfahren* (1905).
* **Comparative and Historical Analysis:** B. W. Leist's *Graco-italische Rechtsgeschichte* (1884), L. Mitteis's research on imperial and folk law (1891), and J. H. Lipsius's *Von der Bedeutung des griechischen Rechts* (1893). 

Further contributions to the understanding of Greek legal development include G. Gilbert's writings in *Jahrb. fur kl. Philologie* (1896) and H. J. Hitzig's 1906 work on the significance of ancient Greek law for comparative legal science.
