# Bryales

The Bryales are a group of mosses characterized by a very large number of genera and species. In Britain alone, more than one hundred genera and between five and six hundred species are found. These plants inhabit diverse environments, including rocks, trees, and soil, with a few instances, such as *Fontinalis*, occurring in water.

## General Structure and Life History
The sexual generation of a moss is always a leafy plant. This plant is not developed directly from a spore but is borne on a protonema, which is usually filamentous. The nature of the protonema varies by species: in some of the simplest forms, such as *Ephemerum* (which grows on the damp soil of clayey fields), the protonema is long-lived, and the small plants borne upon it primarily serve to protect the sporogonia and sexual organs. In *Buxbaumia*, which occurs on humus-rich soil and may be partially saprophytic, the filamentous protonema is capable of assimilation; consequently, the leaves of the small plants lack chlorophyll and depend on the protonema. Conversely, in *Ephemeropsis* of Java, which grows on the leaves of living plants, the persistent protonema is considered an adaptation to its specific living conditions. Other genera, including *Georgia*, *Diphyscium*, and *Oedipodium*, possess a highly developed protonema with leaf-like assimilating organs.

## The Sporogonium
The sporogonium is generally constructed on a consistent plan, though there are notable exceptions. In most Bryales, there is a definite columella extending from the base to the apex of the capsule. The archesporium is derived from the outermost layer of cells of the endothecium, and an air space forms between the spore-sac and the capsule wall. In the Polytrichaceae, an additional air space separates the columella from the spore-sac.

The sporogonium consists of a foot, a seta, and a capsule. The seta varies in length and is sometimes practically absent. At the base of the capsule, the seta widens into a region called the apophysis. This region usually bears stomata and serves as the main organ of assimilation. In *Oedipodium*, the apophysis constitutes most of the long pale stalk supporting the capsule, while in the Splachnaceae, it is expanded for assimilation.

A distinct operculum is typically detached via the annulus, which may leave the mouth of the capsule widely open. More commonly, a peristome is present, consisting of one or two series of teeth. The peristome serves to narrow the opening and ensures that spores are shed gradually during dry weather.

## Variations in the Group
*Archidium*, a small moss found occasionally on the soil of wet fields, differs from other mosses in its capsule structure and development. It has a small foot and practically no seta. Unlike other Bryales, it lacks a sterile columella and an archesporium; instead, a variable number of cells in the endothecium become spore-mother-cells, while others nourish the spores. The spores are very large and thin-walled, escaping when the capsule ruptures the archegonial wall irregularly. Because of the absence of a columella, *Archidium* is sometimes placed in a distinct group, though it is currently retained among the Bryales as its peculiarities may have arisen through reduction.

## Example: Funaria hygrometrica
*Funaria hygrometrica* is a common moss found in towns on the soil of paths and at the foot of walls. It grows in crowded tufts of short leafy shoots attached to the soil by branched rhizoids composed of rows of elongated cells. The leaves are simple and, except for the midrib, are only one layer of cells thick. The stem contains a central strand of narrow cells that function as water-conducting tissue.

Sexual organs develop in groups at the apices. The antheridial group usually terminates the main axis and is made conspicuous by brown, hair-like paraphyses. The archegonia are borne on a lateral branch and are enclosed by surrounding leaves. The sporogonium develops from a fertilized ovum via a two-sided apical cell. As it grows, the upper region forms the capsule and the lower portion forms the foot and long seta. The archegonial wall is eventually broken through; the upper part becomes the calyptra, which is carried up on the capsule, while the basal portion remains as a tubular sheath around the lower end of the seta.
