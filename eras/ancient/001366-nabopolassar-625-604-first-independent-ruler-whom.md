# Nabopolassar

Nabopolassar (625–604 B.C.) was the first independent ruler of Babylonia, marking the beginning of the neo-Babylonian period. His reign inaugurated a new era of power and prosperity for Babylonia, though this period of political independence was short-lived, eventually ending with the conquest of Babylon and Babylonia by Cyrus in 539 B.C.

## Rise to Power and Conflict with Assyria
Nabopolassar ascended to power during a time of significant instability within the Assyrian Empire. Following the death of Assur-bani-pal, the empire began to break up. Under the successor Assur-etil-ilani, the Scythians penetrated Assyria, reaching the borders of Egypt and burning Calah. While the walls of Nineveh protected the remaining Assyrian army, the resources of Assyria were depleted, as evidenced by the small size and architectural poverty of a palace erected among the ruins of a neighboring city.

During this period of unrest, Nabopolassar succeeded Kandalanu (the viceroy appointed after the overthrow of Samas-sum-yukin). War subsequently broke out between Nabopolassar and the last king of Assyria, identified as Sin-sar-iskun (also referred to as Saracus).

## The Fall of Nineveh
To combat the Assyrians, Nabopolassar entered into a particular alliance and league with Cyaxares, the Scythian king of Ecbatana. The combined forces of the Babylonians and the Scythian army besieged Nineveh. The city was captured and destroyed, and King Saracus was killed. This victory, along with the destruction of northern Babylonian cities that had sided with Assyria, brought the Assyrian empire to an end.

Following the fall of Nineveh, the two allied armies divided the spoils of the city. Cyaxares proceeded to take mastery over all the cities of the Assyrian kingdom, with the exception of Chaldea and Babylon, which remained under the control of Nabopolassar.

## Infrastructure and Architecture
Nabopolassar contributed to the physical development of Babylon through significant building projects. German excavations have identified the *Qasr* mound as the site of Nabopolassar's old palace. Additionally, he is credited with the construction of the brick quays of the Arakhtu, which is known as "the river of Babylon," located on the south side of the mound.

## Legacy and Succession
Nabopolassar was succeeded by his son, Nebuchadnezzar. Together, the reigns of Nabopolassar and Nebuchadnezzar (625–561 B.C.) represented the maximum of the aggressive disposition and power of the Assyrians of Babylon (also referred to as the Chaldæans). Under this dynasty, Babylonia maintained its status as a dominant power until the Persian conquest.
