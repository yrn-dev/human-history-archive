# Buddhist Monasticism and the Teachings of the Buddha

The historical and textual record of Buddhism provides insight into the life of the Buddha, the structure of his community, and the specific practices adopted by his followers, including the Buddhist Bhikshu.

## The Life and Identity of the Buddha
According to canonical Pali sources, which reached their current form before the 4th century B.C., the life of the Buddha is recorded in several episodes. These include his birth, the adoration of the babe, his youth at home, and his "going forth." The latter involves a period of study and penance, the attainment of Buddhahood and Nirvana, and the conversion of his first five converts. Later episodes detail his visit to Kapilavastu and his final days, as recorded in the *Mahaparinibbana Suttanta*.

Regarding his identity, the Buddha consistently used the title "the Arahat" to refer to himself. The provided records indicate that he did not call himself "the Buddha," nor did his followers address him by that title.

Chronological estimates for his life vary. In Europe, the generally accepted dates are 568 B.C. for his birth and 488 B.C. for his death. These figures are derived by combining the date of Asoka's accession to the throne (approximately 270 B.C.) with the interval of 218 years between that accession and the Buddha's death. However, in Siam, Burma, and Ceylon, the dates are accepted as being roughly half a century earlier, placing his birth in 623 B.C. and his death in 543 B.C.

## Monastic Life and Infrastructure
The Buddhist community utilized specific structures for their retreats. Initially, these were simple huts constructed in groves of palm-trees, intended to serve as retreats for mendicants during the rainy season. Over time, these structures increased in magnificence and splendour, though they eventually declined as Buddhism itself decayed.

## Practices of the Bhikshu
The training of the Buddhist Bhikshu included the practice of ecstatic meditation, a tradition adopted from previous beliefs. This practice was already in use by the Buddha's own teachers and was present when Buddhism arose. 

In the Buddhist context, these states of rapture are referred to as "Conditions of Bliss." They are viewed as useful tools for removing mental obstacles that hinder the attainment of Arahatship. Specifically, these conditions comprise one group of four within the thirty-seven constituent parts of Arahatship. 

While these practices are considered pleasant and useful, they are not the primary goal of the faith. The records emphasize that the end goal—Arahatship—can be reached without them. Consequently, seeking Arahatship through the practice of ecstasy alone is regarded as a "deadly heresy."

## Textual Authorities and Translations
The archive contains numerous translations and manuals that document these teachings and the life of the Buddha:
*   **The Dialogues of the Buddha**: Translated by Rhys Davids (1899), this work includes the *Mahali Suttanta* and records regarding the "going forth" and the most ancient forms of meditative exercises.
*   **Vinaya Texts**: Translated by Rhys Davids and Oldenberg (1881-1885), these texts cover the *Anatta-lakkhana Sutta* and the early years of the Buddha's teaching.
*   **Other Key Works**: The *Dhammapada* (Max Müller), *Sutta Nipata* (Fausböll), and *Questions of King Milinda* (Rhys Davids).
*   **Specialized Manuals**: The *Yogavacaras Manual of Indian Mysticism as practised by Buddhists*, edited by Rhys Davids (1896), provides elaborate forms of meditative practice.
*   **Literary Collections**: Neumann's *Lieder der Mönche und Nonnen* (1899) focuses on the songs of monks and nuns.
