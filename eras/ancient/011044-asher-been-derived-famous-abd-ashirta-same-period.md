# Asher

## Tribal Identity and Origins
Asher is identified as a tribe of Israel, named after the son of Jacob and Zilpah (the maid of Leah). According to the narrator of Genesis xxx. 12 seq. (J), the name is intended to mean "propitious" or "happy," which may be an allusion to the fertility of the territory held by the tribe. Alternatively, the name may have originally served as a divine title, similar to the name Gad.

The tribe's territory was located to the north of Zebulun and Issachar, to the south of Dan, and bordered Naphtali. However, the archive notes that references to this territory are obscure and the boundaries are not definite. Due to its close proximity to the Aramaeans and Phoenicians, the population of Asher was likely exceptionally mixed. This is supported by the description of the occupation of Palestine in Judges i. 31 seq., which indicates a strong Canaanite element within the tribe.

## Historical and Biblical Accounts
In the Blessing of Moses (Deut. xxxiii. 25), Asher is bidden to defend itself, which is evidently a reference to resisting invasion. In other narratives, the tribe is criticized for failing to participate in the fight against Sisera (Judg. v. 17). While Asher is mentioned alongside Naphtali and Zebulun in the account of Gideon's defeat of the Midianites (Judg. vi. 35, vii. 23), this is not considered the older account of the event; it is probable that a late redactor incorporated the name into the text.

## Egyptian Records and Theories of Migration
Egyptian records from the latter half of the 14th century B.C., specifically during the time of Rameses II and Seti I, indicate that the Egyptians knew the district to the west of Galilee as Aser(u). This has led to two primary inferences regarding the tribe's origins:
1. Asher may have been an Israelite tribe that separated from its brethren while in Egypt and migrated north, a pattern possibly followed by other tribes.
2. It may have been a district that was regarded as part of the national kingdom and treated as Israelite through the genealogical device of being named a "son" of Jacob, even if it was never closely bound to Israel.

It is also possible that some of the Israelite population of Asher moved from an earlier home in the south, following the example of Dan.

## Etymological and External Associations
There are several theories regarding the name Asher and its associations with external peoples and deities:
* **Amarna Tablets:** Two clans of Asher, Malchiel and Heber, have been associated with the Habiri (a people) and Milk-ili (a hostile chief) mentioned in the Amarna Tablets. However, it is considered scarcely probable that events from approximately 1400 B.C. would survive only in this form.
* **Abd-ashirta:** A suggestion has been made that the name Asher was derived from a famous figure of the same period named Abd-ashirta. This suggestion is viewed with similar skepticism to the Amarna Tablets theory.
* **Deity Connection:** A connection between the name and the goddess Ashir(t)a is considered not unlikely.

## Other Historical Figures
The archive also records an 'Asher Ben-Yehiel (known as Rosh), a codifier and rabbi who lived between approximately 1250 and 1327. Born in the Rhine district, he fled to Spain to escape 13th-century persecutions of German Jews and became the rabbi of Toledo. A disciple of Meir of Rothenburg, 'Asher was a man of austere piety who opposed the study of philosophy. He is noted for his talent for systematization; his chief work, a *Compendium* of the Talmud compiled between 1307 and 1314, superseded the work of 'Al-phasi and is included in most editions of the Talmud. His son, Jacob, authored the four *Turim*, a popular codification of rabbinical law that served as the basis for the *Shulhan 'Arukh*.
