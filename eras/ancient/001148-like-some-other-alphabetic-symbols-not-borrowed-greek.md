# The Development of the Letter B

The letter B corresponds to the second symbol in the Phoenician alphabet and maintains this position in all European alphabets, with the exception of those derived from medieval Greek, such as Russian. The symbol represents a voiced stop produced by vibrating the vocal chords while closing the lips. This sound differs from *p* due to the presence of vocal chord vibration and from *m* because the lips and the nasal passage are both closed.

## Phoenician and Early Origins
The earliest forms of the symbol in the Phoenician alphabet are either pointed or more rounded. The rounded form is also present in the earliest Aramaic. The earliest known name for the symbol is the Hebrew *beth*, a term to which the Phoenician name was closely akin. The Greek name, *beta*, was borrowed from this source with an added vowel.

## Adaptation into Greek
The symbol was not borrowed by the Greeks in its original form. In very early rock inscriptions from Thera (700-600 B.C.), which were written from right to left, the letter appears in a form resembling the ordinary Greek *lambda*; this is believed to have resulted from writing the Semitic symbol upside down. 

During the 5th and 6th centuries, inscriptions from Syracuse, Selinus, and Melos suggest the influence of Aramaic forms, characterized by an open head of the letter. Other adaptations include the forms found at Corcyra and in Corinth, as well as those appearing on Byzantine coins. In the alphabets of Delos, Naxos, and other Ionic islands during the same period, the symbol takes a form that is difficult to explain. Aside from these regional variations, the symbol generally varied only between rounded and pointed loops.

## Evolution in Latin and Slavonic
In the Latin alphabet, the letter B retained its position and sound. However, in medieval Greek, the pronunciation of the symbol shifted from *b* to *v*. Because of this change, the Slavonic alphabet—which was derived from medieval Greek—required a new symbol to represent the genuine *b* sound, as there was no corresponding sound in Greek at the time of adoption. 

This new Slavonic symbol was created by removing the upper loop of the B, resulting in a character resembling a lowercase *b*. This new symbol took the second position in the alphabet. The original B was retained but served as a numeral with the value of 2 (the Greek *beta*), while the new symbol was given no numerical value.

## Phonetic Relationships
The sounds *b* and *m* are closely related. The primary difference is that in the pronunciation of *m*, the nasal passage remains open, allowing the sound to be prolonged, whereas *b* is an explosive or instantaneous sound. In various languages, *b* is inserted between *m* and a following consonant, such as in the English word "number" (from the Latin *numerus*) or the Greek *mesembria* ("mid-day").

Additionally, when an audible emission of breath accompanies the production of the *b* sound, the aspirate *bh* is formed. This specific sound was frequent during the pro-ethnic period of Indo-European languages and survived into the Indo-Aryan languages.
