# Akarnanians and Epirots

The Akarnanians and Epirots were peoples inhabiting the northwestern regions of ancient Greece, situated between the Pindus mountains and the Ionian Sea. While the Akarnanians served as a transitional link between the Hellenic world and the non-Hellenic tribes of the interior, the Epirots comprised a diverse group of tribes often viewed by the Greeks as distinct from the Hellenic aggregate.

## The Akarnanians
The Akarnanians occupied a territory bounded by the Ionian Sea, the Ambrakian gulf, and the river Achelôus. They were recognized as Greeks and were permitted to compete in the Pan-Hellenic games, yet they maintained close connections with non-Greek groups such as the Agræi and the Amphilochi. In terms of intelligence, sentiments, and manners, they were considered half-Hellenic and half-Epirotic.

Socially and politically, the Akarnanians were divided into numerous petty communities living in unfortified villages. They frequently engaged in mutual plundering and remained constantly armed. During attacks, they would move their families and livestock—primarily cattle—into marshes or difficult mountain terrain for protection. While few were trained as Grecian hoplites, they were brave and skilled in their own style of warfare, particularly in the use of the sling, which was a weapon of formidable efficiency.

The Akarnanians generally viewed powerful seaports with apprehension. Despite their hostility toward the Ambrakiots, they feared Athenian control of Ambrakia more than they feared the city remaining in the hands of its own citizens. Before 220 B.C., the Akarnanians acquired the temple of Apollo Aktius and merged the town of Anaktorium into the Akarnanian league.

## The Epirots
The term "Epirot" was a geographical designation given by the Greeks to the region northward of the gulf of Corinth, rather than an ethnical label. In its most primitive sense, this region included the Akarnanians and Ætolians, some of whom spoke dialects that were difficult to understand and whose habits differed from those of the Hellenes.

The Epirots consisted of various tribes with little to no ethnical kindred among them. Some were ethnically connected to the Macedonians, while others shared connections with tribes in southern Italy. These tribes included the Chaonians, Molossians, Thesprotians, Kassopæans, Amphilochians, Athamānes, Æthīkes, Tymphæi, Orestæ, Paroræi, and Atintānes. Many of the latter were small communities dispersed throughout the mountainous Pindus region.

The Epirotic territory was distributed into villages, lacking considerable cities. The coast of Epirus was generally discouraging to Grecian colonization. Governance varied by tribe; some were ruled by kings, while others were not. For example, the Chaonians and Thesprotians did not acknowledge hereditary kings, with the Chaonians being led by annual chiefs chosen from a regal gens. In contrast, the Molossians and Atintânes were governed by a regent, Sabylinthus, on behalf of the young prince Tharypas, and the Paranæi were ruled by King Orœdus.

## Relations and Conflict
The relationship between the Greeks and Epirots was anciently centered on the oracle of Dodona, though this union was later superseded by Delphi. Due to similarities in dress, hair fashion, and language, it was often difficult to distinguish Epirots from Macedonians.

The Akarnanians formed an alliance with Athens following a conflict over the town of Argos in Amphilochia. Argos had been occupied by the Amphilochi—a non-Hellenic tribe with a lineage intermediate between Akarnanians and Epirots—before being seized by colonists from Ambrakia. The Athenians, led by the admiral Phormio, assisted the Amphilochians and Akarnanians in recapturing the town and reducing the Ambrakiots to slavery. This event established a personal attachment between the Akarnanians and Phormio.

In separate military operations, a diverse force of Epirotic and Macedonian auxiliaries joined the Ambrakiots, Leukadians, and Anaktorians in an invasion of Akarnania. This force included one thousand Chaonians, the Thesprotians, the Molossians, the Atintânes, the Paranæi, and one thousand Orestæ (a tribe more Macedonian than Epirot). During the march toward the interior town of Stratus, the Chaonians—regarded as the most warlike and rash of the Epirotic tribes—advanced without order. Driven by conceit and a desire for sole glory, they attempted to attack Stratus single-handedly before the Greek divisions could arrive.
