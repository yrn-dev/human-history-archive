# The Babylonian-Assyrian Writing System and Culture

The writing system used in ancient Babylonia and Assyria was a complex adaptation of a non-Semitic cuneiform script. This system was originally developed by the Sumerians (or Sumero-Akkadians), the non-Semitic settlers of the Euphrates valley. When the Babylonians settled in the same region, they adopted this script and general culture, adapting the cuneiform system to their own language, which belonged to the Semitic group.

## The Cuneiform Writing Method
The primary difficulty in reading Babylonian and Assyrian proper names stems from the use of an "ideographic" method of writing. In this system, words could be written in two different ways:
1. **Ideographic:** A sign or combination of signs expressed an entire word.
2. **Phonetic:** Words were spelled out in syllables.

Because the script was originally Sumerian, many signs represented Sumerian words. However, in a Babylonian or Assyrian text, these signs were often read as their Semitic equivalents. For example, the sign MU represents the Sumerian word for "name," but in a Semitic text, it must be read as *shumu*. Similarly, the sign SIG-BA represents the Sumerian word for "clothing," but in Semitic texts, it is read as *lubushtu*, though it could also be written phonetically as *lu-bu-ush-tu*.

Over time, the Babylonians utilized Sumerian words as phonetic tools, treating the signs as syllables without regard for their original meaning. This evolved into the Babylonian-Assyrian syllabary as the culture spread north into Assyria. This syllabary was further modified over time, with some Semitic equivalents being abbreviated or distorted to create new phonetic values.

## Challenges in Deciphering Names
Reading ancient names is a complex task for scholars, particularly when distinguishing between genuine Sumerian and pseudo-Sumerian names. In the oldest historical periods, hundreds of genuinely Sumerian names appear, but because signs have multiple phonetic values and knowledge of the Sumerian language remains imperfect, scholars often disagree on the correct readings. For instance, a king of Ur previously read as Ur-Bau is now read as Ur-Engur, and a center previously read as Shir-pur-la is now likely Shir-gul-la.

Further ambiguity arises when it is unclear if a name's bearer was a Sumerian or a Semite. If the bearer was a Semite, the signs might be read as Semitic equivalents, though some Semites bore genuine Sumerian names. There is also the question of whether a name is a phonetic reading or an ideographic compound. An example is a ruler of Kish whose name was written Uru-mu-ush; while some initially read this ideographically as Alu-usharshid ("he founded a city"), scholarly opinion eventually accepted Urumush.

Additionally, Babylonian-Assyrian proper names typically included a divine element, a verbal form, and an object. Determining the exact form of the verb is difficult. For example, the sign KUR (equivalent to *na[s.][=a]ru*, "protect") could be read as an imperative (*u[s.][s.]ur*), an active participle (*n[=a]sir*), or a third person perfect (*i[s.][s.]ur*). The sign MU is particularly ambiguous because it can mean "name" (*shumu*) or "give" (read as *iddin* or *n[=a]din*). In names containing the sequence MU-MU, scholars must determine which MU refers to the verb and which refers to the noun.

## Assyro-Babylonian Culture and Education
Assyrian culture was derived from Babylonia, though differences existed. Education in Babylonia was general and long-established, with libraries found in most temples and towns. In contrast, education in the northern kingdom of Assyria was largely confined to a single class.

Students in Semitic times were required to learn both the extinct Sumerian language and a complicated syllabary. To aid this, scholars compiled grammars, vocabularies, interlinear translations, and commentaries on obscure phrases. Literature was primarily inscribed with a metal stylus on clay tablets (referred to by Pliny as *laterculae coctiles*), though papyrus was also used.

As Nineveh grew into a trade center under the second Assyrian empire, the educated class also learned Aramaic, the language of diplomacy and commerce. Later, under the Seleucids, Greek was introduced, and some tablets have been found featuring Sumerian and Assyrian words transcribed in Greek letters.

## Origins and Political Evolution
The Semites entered the plain of Babylonia at a remote period, and their language was profoundly influenced by Sumerian, just as Sumerian borrowed from Semitic Babylonian. The first Semitic empire was founded in Akkad, and Semitic settlers spread from Susa to Sippara.

Politically, both Assyria and Babylonia were originally led by a *patesi* (high-priest) who served as the viceregent for the god, the nominal ruler of the state. Eventually, many high-priests assumed the title of king. When Babylonia was unified under Semitic princes, the earthly king became an incarnate god, although at Babylon, the king still had to be adopted by Bel-Merodach to secure the right to rule.

The city of Assur was founded by Semites from Babylonia. In the law-code of Khammurabi (c. 2250 B.C.), Assur is mentioned as *A-usar (ki)*, where *-ki* indicates a land territory. The name *Assur* has various etymologies: *A-usar* (possibly meaning "well watered region"), *An-sar* (meaning "host of heaven" in the Creation tablet), and the stem *asaru* (related to the epithet *Asir* or "overseer").
