# Ecclesiastical Architecture: The Basilica

The basilica is a form of ecclesiastical architecture characterized by specific internal arrangements and ritual spaces. While the exterior of these buildings was typically plain, consisting of vast brick walls often unrelieved by ornament—with exceptions for entrance fronts that might feature painted stucco or marble mosaic plates—the interior was designed to produce a fine effect through mosaic decoration, the apse, and the arch of triumph.

## The Bema and Apse
The bema (from the Greek *baema*) is the semicircular recess or exedra of the basilica. In earlier times, this space served as the area where judges sat; in later ecclesiastical use, it became the location for the altar. The bema is generally railed off and ascended by steps, typically featuring a roof in the form of a half-dome. 

The apse, which terminates the nave and dominates the interior space, contains the seating for the clergy. The bishop's throne is positioned in the center of the arc or semicircle, often approached by a steep flight of steps. In some examples, such as the cathedral at Torcello and the basilica of Parenzo, the bishop's throne is flanked by rows of stone benches for the presbyters that follow the curve of the wall, rising one above another in a manner similar to theater seating. The walls of the apse were frequently inlaid with rich marble, mother-of-pearl, or covered with mosaic pictures depicting the Lord, often accompanied by St. Paul, St. Peter, and other saints.

## The Altar and Sanctuary
The altar is typically situated in the center of the chord of the apse on a raised platform reached by flights of steps. To make the altar conspicuous, it was often topped with a *ciborium* or *baldacchino*—a lofty canopy supported by marble pillars from which rich curtains depended. In the original Basilica of St. Peter's, the altar was protected by a double screen.

Beneath the altar, some basilicas featured a *confessio*, which is a subterranean chapel containing relics of holy persons or the body of the patron saint. These were accessed via descending flights of steps from the aisles or nave. The bishop or officiating presbyter would advance from the central seat of the apse to the altar to celebrate the Eucharist, facing the congregation.

## Nave and Aisles
The central space of the basilica is the nave, which is flanked by aisles. The configuration of these aisles varied by building:
* **St. Peter's and St. Paul's:** Both featured a nave and aisles.
* **St. John Lateran:** This modernized basilica was five-aisled and featured a rare double-vaulted aisle running around the apse.
* **S. Clemente:** This church included specific aisles designated for men and women.
* **Torcello:** This cathedral featured narrow aisles measuring only 7 feet in width.
* **Parenzo:** In this basilica, nine pillars divide each aisle from the nave.

In some later architectural developments, such as St. Sophia in the 6th century, the central dome was supported by vaulted aisles in two storeys to the north and south.

## Ritual Fittings and the Choir
The area at the foot of the altar steps, occupying the upper portion of the nave, served as a choir for readers, singers, and inferior clergy. This oblong space was separated from the western nave and the aisles by low marble railings or walls known as *cancelli*. 

Projecting from these walls were *ambones* (pulpits) with marble desks. At S. Clemente, these are specifically identified as the gospel-ambo (octagonal in form) and the epistle-ambo (square in plan), the latter featuring two marble reading-desks for the reading of the gradual and the epistle.

## Secular Origins of the Bema
The term *bema* originated in Greece as a general name for any raised platform. In Athens, it referred to the tribunal used by orators to address citizens. Examples include the stone platform at the Pnyx, which stood 10 to 11 feet high where the Ecclesia met, and platforms in Athenian law courts used by witnesses and counsel. Additionally, the term applied to the platform holding urns for bronze disks (*psiaephoi*) used by judges to record decisions at the end of the 4th century.
