# Caius and the Command for the Temple Statue

The conflict regarding the erection of a statue of Caius Caesar in the temple of God arose from a combination of diplomatic disputes in Alexandria and the personal ambitions of the Roman Emperor. Caius, who asserted his own divinity and claimed to be the brother of Jupiter, demanded honors from his subjects that exceeded those due to mankind.

## The Dispute at Alexandria
The tension began with a tumult in Alexandria between Greek and Jewish inhabitants. Three ambassadors from each party were sent to Caius to resolve the variance. Among the Greek ambassadors was Apion, who accused the Jews of neglecting the honors owed to Caesar. Apion claimed that while other subjects of the Roman Empire built temples and altars to Caius and received him as a god, the Jews alone refused to swear by his name or erect statues in his honor. 

Philo, the leader of the Jewish embassy and a man skilled in philosophy, attempted to defend his people against these accusations. However, Caius prohibited him from speaking and dismissed him in a rage. Philo encouraged his compatriots to remain courageous, suggesting that while Caius showed anger toward them, he had already set God against himself.

## The Command to Petronius
Viewing the refusal of the Jews to honor him as a sign of being despised, Caius appointed Petronius as the president of Syria, succeeding Vitellius. Caius issued a formal order to Petronius to invade Judea with a large body of troops. The mandate was specific: if the Jews would willingly admit the statue of Caius, it was to be erected in the temple of God; if they remained obstinate, Petronius was to conquer them by war and then install the statue.

Petronius obeyed the epistle by assembling two Roman legions and as many auxiliaries as possible. He wintered at Ptolemais before preparing to begin the war in the spring.

## Jewish Resistance and the Plea to Petronius
Upon the arrival of the Roman forces, many ten thousands of the Jews approached Petronius at Ptolemais. They petitioned him not to compel them to violate the law of their forefathers. The petitioners stated that they could not permit things forbidden by their legislator and the determination of their ancestors—which they viewed as instances of virtue—to be done while they were alive. They told Petronius that if he were resolved to erect the statue, he should first kill them.

Petronius responded with anger, explaining that he was not the emperor and did not have the liberty to follow his own inclination. He stated that he was under the necessity of being subservient to the decrees of Caesar, as disobedience would bring inevitable destruction upon him. The Jews replied that just as Petronius would not disobey the epistles of Caius, they would not transgress the commands of their law.

## The Intervention of King Agrippa
King Agrippa, who had gained significant favor with Caius through his magnificence and virtue, intervened in the matter. Agrippa had previously been released from bonds by Caius after the death of Tiberius and had been made king of Philip's tetrarchy. 

During a feast where Agrippa had gone to great expense to please the Emperor, Caius expressed his admiration for Agrippa's affection and offered to grant him any request that would contribute to his happiness. Rather than asking for larger revenues or more territory, Agrippa requested that Caius rescind the order regarding the statue.

Caius, admiring Agrippa's concern for public tranquility and the Divinity, initially granted the request. He wrote to Petronius stating that if the statue had already been dedicated, it should stand, but if not, Petronius should dismiss the army and cease the effort, as Caius no longer had an occasion for the statue's erection.

## Aftermath and Character of Caius
Despite the temporary reprieve granted to Agrippa, Caius remained a volatile ruler. He was described as a slave to base and vicious actions who found pleasure in indulging his anger without restraint. His "madness" extended beyond Judea to all Roman territories. In Rome, he targeted the senate, the nobility, and the equestrian order, often slaying men to seize their riches. 

His perceived divinity led him to perform eccentric acts, such as constructing a bridge of thirty furlongs from Dicearchia to Misenum across the sea, simply because he found rowing in a small ship tedious and believed that, as a god and lord of the sea, the water should obey him. He also plundered Grecian temples and their engravings.
