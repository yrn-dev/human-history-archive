# Ancient Fortifications and Flanking Structures

The study of historical fortifications encompasses a wide range of military architecture, from the defensive walls of ancient city-states to the specialized towers and earthworks of later eras. These structures were designed to protect urban centers, control strategic geography, and provide tactical advantages during sieges and battles.

## Fortifications of Avallon
In the town of Avallon, located in central France within the department of Yonne, the remnants of ancient fortifications are still visible. These remains specifically include seven of the flanking towers. The town is situated on a promontory, with its base washed by the Cousin stream to the south and other small streams to the east and west. Avallon also features a statue of Vauban, a noted military engineer.

## The Fortifications of Syracuse
The city of Syracuse in Greece historically consisted of an inner city on the island of Ortygia and an outer city known as Achradina. The outer city occupied a peninsula north of Ortygia and possessed its own defensive system. It was protected on the north and east by a sea-wall and the sea, where rocky terrain made landing difficult. On the land-side, its wall ran from the sea—eastward of the cleft known as Panagia or Santa Bonagia—in a south-westward direction until it reached the end of the high ground of Achradina. From there, the wall turned eastward along the Novanteris and Capucins stone quarries, where the steepness of the ground reduced the need for extensive fortification.

Under Dionysius, the fortifications of Syracuse were significantly expanded. He added a new line of wall extending from Euryalus to the suburb of Neapolis along the southern cliff of Epipolæ. The fort at Euryalus was also enlarged to a point of grandeur. This resulted in the entire slope of Epipolæ being protected by fortifications from its apex at Euryalus down to its base at Achradina. During this period, Syracuse became the largest fortified city in Greece, surpassing Athens in its then-current state. The city was divided into five separately fortified portions: Ortygia, Achradina, Tychê, Neapolis, and Epipolæ. While each had its own fortification, the first four were contained within the same outer walls. The construction of these extensive works led to the demolition of a chapel dedicated to Dioklês.

## European Fortress Developments and Dismantling
In Bohemia, the town of Königgrätz (originally named Chlumec Dobroslavsky) saw the erection of fortifications under Joseph II, which required the demolition of several convents and churches. This fortress was eventually dismantled in 1884.

In other contexts, fortifications were dismantled as a result of peace treaties. Following a conflict, all fortresses, field works, and fortified works located in German territory within a zone of fifty kilometers east of the Rhine were ordered to be dismantled within three months, with the construction of new fortifications in that zone being forbidden. Interallied commissions of control were tasked with overseeing these demolitions.

## Specialized Fortification Terms and Structures
Military architecture employs specific terminology for various defensive shapes. A "lunette," derived from the French word for moon, refers to a crescent-shaped object. In the context of fortification, a lunette was originally an earthwork of a half-moon shape. Over time, it evolved into a redan with short flanks, resembling a bastion that stood alone without curtains on either side, typically featuring an open gorge.

## Strategic Fortifications in Warfare
Fortifications often served as critical strategic anchors. Antwerp was regarded as the sole stronghold of the Belgian nation and the true left flank of the Allied front in the west. Beyond its permanent fortifications, the city was further strengthened by inundations and rivers.

In other military engagements, fortifications were the site of intense struggle. During a conflict involving Russian and Japanese forces, Russian defenders occupied twenty-seven versts of fortifications. Following the fall of a fortress, captured material included 546 guns, 35,252 rifles, 60 torpedoes, and 30,000 kilograms of powder, among other supplies.
