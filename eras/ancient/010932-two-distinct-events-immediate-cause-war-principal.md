# Causes and Occasions of Historical Conflicts

The history of global conflict is often defined by a combination of remote and immediate causes, ranging from the personal influence of political leaders to the movement of entire populations. These factors determine whether nations seek peace or engage in sustained warfare.

## The Second Punic War
The rupture between the Romans and the Carthaginians, which led to the Second Punic War, was the result of steps that had been preparing for a long period before the conflict openly broke out. This war was fought across several seats, including Africa, Spain, Sicily, and Italy. A central figure in this conflict was Hannibal, who is described as perhaps the greatest warrior of antiquity.

During the course of the war, Carthage experienced a continual vicissitude of security and fear, joy and grief, and prosperity and adversity. At one point, a disastrous accident caused great consternation in Carthage. Because the road between the camps was impassable, Barca was unable to advance hastily to assist his colleague, as the news of the misfortune took a long time to reach him. In response, Carthage made a bold effort by pressing all youth capable of bearing arms into service. To ensure national welfare, thirty senators were deputed to urge generals Hamilcar and Barca to sacrifice their resentments and forget past quarrels. The generals complied, reconciled sincerely, and embraced.

## Athenian Conflict and the Influence of Kleon
In the history of Athens, the decision to pursue war or peace was often tied to the influence of specific individuals. During the blockade of Sphakteria, a considerable party in Athens was disposed to accept a peace offer from the Lacedæmonians; however, the ascendency of Kleon was one of the main causes for the rejection of that offer.

Following the return of captives from Sphakteria, the general temper of Athens became warlike, and the influence of Kleon was no longer required to dismiss Lacedæmonian pacific offers. Although Athens later became more disposed to peace after a year of unfavorable chances—leading to a one-year truce—Kleon remained eager to renew warlike operations in Thrace. His sanguine character and personal influence were main causes for the resumption of the war, which eventually resulted in the battle of Amphipolis and the death of both Kleon and Brasidas.

## The Fall of the Western Roman Empire
The fall of the Western empire of Rome was driven by a principal and immediate cause: the presence of the Goths within Roman territory. While some accounts suggest the Goths abandoned the region after exterminating Valens and his army, other records indicate that after the defeat of Valens, the Goths never abandoned Roman territory. According to Claudian, thirty years after crossing the Istrum, the Goths had fixed their footprints on Thracian soil, having forgotten their own homeland.

## Religious Warfare and the Seljuks
In the East, the death of Haroun-al-Raschid in 809 led the Abbasid empire immediately into confusion and civil war. Two hundred years later, the Turks, led by the Seljuk family, moved southward from Turkestan. They outflanked the Taurus Mountains—a barrier that had previously held back the Moslems—and conquered Asia Minor and the empire of Bagdad.

These Turks were Moslems of a primitive type. Their arrival caused a revival of vigour in Islam and shifted the focus of the Moslem world back toward religious war against Christendom. This followed a period of truce between the two religions after the decline of the Omayyads, during which warfare had been limited to border-bickering rather than sustained conflict. By the eleventh century, this had returned to a bitter, fanatical struggle.
