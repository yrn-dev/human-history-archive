# Tactical Maneuvers and Strategic Road Control

In military history, the control of roads and the use of circuitous routes have frequently determined the outcome of engagements, allowing forces to bypass enemy strengths, achieve surprise, or secure vital lines of communication.

## Circuitous Routes and Surprise Attacks
The use of indirect paths to avoid enemy forces is a recurring tactic to achieve tactical surprise. In one engagement involving a Grecian army, a detachment of two thousand men utilized a circuitous road to reach an elevated position held by the Karduchians under the cover of night. After surprising and dispersing the Karduchians at that location, the detachment used a mist at daybreak to steal silently toward another Karduchian position located in front of the main Grecian army. 

Upon reaching their target, the detachment signaled the main force—led by Cheirisophus—with trumpets and shouts. This coordinated attack allowed the main Grecian force to storm the height, with some soldiers using the regular path and others climbing the slope by pulling each other up with spears. The joining of these two bodies of Greeks on the summit successfully opened the road for further advance.

## Strategic Road Control in the World Wars
During the Great War, the control of roads and valleys often dictated the stability of entire army fronts. On the Upper Isonzo, the town of Caporetto served as a critical strategic point because it sat at the head of an important valley leading back to the mountains where the Italian main line was situated. Furthermore, several easy roads led from Caporetto into the plain beyond. When German forces pressed in force along these roads on October 24, it threatened to place the enemy on the flank and rear of the Italian Army, which consisted of a million men.

In other sectors, the ability to move reserves quickly via road and rail was a decisive advantage. During an attack beginning on September 26, troops penetrated 3 to 7 miles deep, capturing several villages including Montfaucon and Exermont. While the attacking force had mastered first-line defenses and taken 10,000 prisoners, they remained mindful that the enemy possessed good roads and ample railroad facilities to bring up reserves and artillery for a reaction.

Similarly, during the defense of Paris and Amiens, French divisions utilized motor-lorries to speed along roads converging toward Montdidier. In this sector, General Pellé's group occupied Mont Renaud to bar the road to Compiègne, maintaining a defensive pivot on the hills south and south-west of Noyon.

## Deception and Positioning
Beyond the physical control of roads, commanders have used deception to mitigate the disadvantage of smaller numbers. While on his road to Italy, Julius Cæsar faced a general rising of the Gauls. When he moved to release Cicero from a blockade with only seven thousand men, the besiegers attempted to cut him off. Cæsar avoided the enemy continually and occupied an advantageous position. To encourage the enemy to despise his strength, he had his men increase the height of the ramparts and build up the gates as if they were afraid. This maneuver led the Gauls to attack in scattered bodies, at which point Cæsar sallied out and put them to flight.

## Main Road Engagements and Forced Marches
The main road can also serve as the primary axis for decisive counter-attacks. In one instance, Napoleon and Desaix framed a plan of attack against an Austrian column. Boudet's division, having received a recall message while on the Alessandria-Genoa road, performed a forced march to reach the battle-field. The division deployed and moved forward along the main road Alessandria-Tortona. 

As the Austrian commander Zach arrayed a deep column to pursue along the main road, the French combined all arms. Marmont’s artillery acted on the right of the road to check the advanced infantry, while Desaix advanced against the head of the Austrian main column. The engagement culminated when Kellermann’s cavalry of 400 sabres wheeled against the flank of the Austrian column, resulting in the capture of Zach and more than 2,000 men.
