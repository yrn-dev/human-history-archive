# Julius Agricola and the Conquest of North Britain

Julius Agricola was a Roman general described as virtuous and one of the best-known generals of his era. In A.D. 80, he moved to conduct the conquest of the farther north of Britain. His campaigns were directed toward Caledonia, the Roman name for North Britain (a term still used in poetry for Scotland).

## Campaigns in Caledonia
The Romans first invaded Caledonia under Agricola around A.D. 83. To secure his progress, Agricola established a frontier between the Forth and the Clyde, which was intended to be permanent. This frontier was guarded by a line of forts; two of these, located at Bar Hill and Camelon (near Falkirk), have since been identified and excavated.

Agricola advanced further into Caledonia, penetrating into Perthshire. This progression culminated in a decisive victory against the collected forces of the Caledonians at Mons Graupius (occasionally misspelled as Grampius). This battle took place on the slopes of the mountain, likely near the confluence of the Isla and the Tay. Evidence of this period includes the Roman encampment of Inchtuthill, located in the policies of Delvine, 10 miles north of Perth; it is the most northerly ascertained Roman encampment in Scotland and is believed to belong to the age of Agricola. The modern name of the Grampian Hills is derived from the misspelling of this battlefield.

## Romanization and Social Change
Beyond military conquest, Agricola engaged in "Romanizing work" that significantly altered the policy and dress of the local population. Under this influence, British chiefs began living in Roman villas, using Roman baths, and speaking the Latin tongue. Their attire shifted to include the linen tunic and white woollen toga, while their wives adopted the veil, the pall, and the stole.

## Recall and Abandonment
Following his victories, Agricola envisioned completing the Roman success through the reduction of Ireland. He believed this would be an easy task requiring only a few auxiliaries and one legion. He argued that the acquisition of the western isle would make the Britons more reluctant to resist their chains.

However, the Roman home government disagreed. The government was averse to costly and fruitless campaigns in barren hills and required the Legio II Adiutrix for work in other locations. Additionally, there was a tendency among the first Cæsars to fear the military fame of their lieutenants, viewing such merit as an invasion of the Imperial prerogative. Whether due to a fear of a too-brilliant general or a desire to avoid expensive conquests, the government abruptly recalled Agricola and his legion. 

As a result, his nominal conquests in the north—all areas beyond the Cheviot or the Tweed—were abandoned. The exact frontier adopted after his departure remains unknown.

## Subsequent Roman Activity in the North
Following Agricola's departure, the military history of Britain remained a blank for thirty years (A.D. 85-115). Around A.D. 115 or 120, northern Britons revolted and destroyed the Ninth Legion stationed at York. In A.D. 122, Emperor Hadrian arrived in Britain with the Sixth Legion to replace the Ninth. He established a new frontier policy by building a continuous rampart from Wallsend to Bowness (Tyne to Solway), marking the definite limit of the Roman world and ending the era of conquest.

Later, around A.D. 140, the district up to the Firth of Forth was annexed, and the Wall of Antoninus Pius was constructed. Roman forts at Carpow (near Abernethy) and Ardoch (north of Dunblane) were occupied. However, this conquest was stubbornly disputed, and the land north of Cheviot appears to have been lost again between A.D. 180 and 185.
