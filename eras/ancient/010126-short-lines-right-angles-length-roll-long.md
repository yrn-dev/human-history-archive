# Methods of Writing on Rolls

The historical use of rolls made from paper, vellum, or papyrus involved three distinct methods of arranging text. These methods determined how the documents were written, stored, and eventually transitioned into book formats.

## Arrangements of Text on Rolls

The first method of writing on rolls consisted of short lines written at right angles to the length of the roll. Documents produced in this manner were simply rolled up and stored in cylinders of a similar shape. These cylinders were sometimes kept together in a box known as a *scrinium*, with a title tag attached to the end of each roll.

The second method involved writing in long lines, where each line spanned the entire length of the roll. This specific form is most notably found in Buddhist prayer-wheels. To facilitate their use, these rolls are kept in circular boxes equipped with handles through the centers, allowing the rolls to revolve easily.

The third method utilized short lines written parallel to the length of the roll. In this arrangement, each page or column of writing was designed with a space left on each side. This format is still employed for scrolls of the law used in synagogues, which are maintained on two rollers, one positioned at each end.

## Transition from Rolls to Books

The third method of writing—parallel columns with vacant side spaces—led to the development of a more convenient preservation technique. By folding the roll backwards and forwards, using the vacant spaces between columns as the bends, the roll was transformed into a book. 

This specific form is known as *orihon* in Japanese and Chinese traditions. In an *orihon*, all writing is placed on one side of the paper strip, while the other side remains blank. While some of these books are protected by two boards, they are generally fastened along one side, which serves as the back of the book. The earliest method of fastening these books involved "stabbing" the substance of the roll near the edge with holes and running a cord or fiber through them as lacing.

This "stabbed" binding is recognized as the earliest method for keeping book leaves together. An example of this occurs in a Coptic papyrus from approximately the 8th century discovered at Thebes. However, this binding method is rarely used with papyrus because the material is too brittle to properly retain the threads. The *orihon* thus serves as the historical link between the roll and the book.
