# The Bronze Age in Europe

The Bronze Age is the archaeological term for a stage of human culture situated between the Stone Age and the Iron Age. It is defined as the period when implements, utensils, and weapons were generally manufactured from bronze. While it is viewed by some as a distinct stage of civilization through which most races passed, other archaeologists argue against its existence as a separate era, citing the discovery of weapons from different ages side by side in prehistoric burial sites.

## Chronology and Development
Most authorities fix the commencement of the Bronze Age in Europe between 2000 B.C. and 1800 B.C., though it is inferred to have been synchronous across the continent due to the similarity of weapon and implement types. However, the timing varied by region; it began earlier in some countries and later in others. In Mexico and Peru, the bronze age persisted into recent times.

The transition into this period was gradual. For a significant duration, bronze axes and other tools were cast in moulds that mimicked the shapes of the stone implements they replaced. The prevalence and duration of the age in any given land were determined by the accessibility of the metals required for the alloy. Consequently, the Stone, Bronze, and Iron Ages often overlapped. This overlap is evidenced by "finds" in sepulchral mounds and tumuli where stone, bronze, and iron implements are found together.

## Material Composition and Origin
Bronze in classical antiquity primarily consisted of copper alloyed with tin, zinc, lead, or silver. Analysis of remains shows copper content varying from 67% to 95%. In the case of Greek coins, an alloy of copper and tin was used until 400 B.C., after which lead was used with increasing frequency.

There is an argument that the knowledge of bronze originated from the East. This theory is supported by the patterns of arms, which are described as distinctly oriental. Furthermore, the handles of daggers and swords from this period are notably narrow and short, suggesting they were not designed for the large-handed races of Europe. Another supporting argument is that objects found in one part of Europe are identical in pattern and size to those found in other parts.

## Implements and Artistry
The tools and weapons of the Bronze Age were distinct in size and design from those of the preceding and succeeding eras. Implements included:
*   Swords and daggers
*   Arrow-heads
*   Knives
*   Hammers
*   Awls
*   Gouges

The swords often featured handles in the shape of a cross and tracery believed to be an imitation of the Svastika, an ancient Aryan symbol. High workmanship is frequently evident in the weapons. Additionally, barrows have yielded gold coronets, amulets, diadems of solid gold, and elegant vases made of bronze and gold. Ornamentation was generally limited to concentric circles, bosses, and spirals.

Pottery from this period was hand-made. The majority of excavated pottery consists of cinerary urns, typically 12 to 18 inches in height, decorated with a projecting flange or a band around the upper portion.

## Burial Customs and Social Structure
The Bronze Age is characterized by the use of cremation for the disposal of the dead, a shift from the burial rules of the Stone Age. The dead were placed in barrows and sepulchral mounds, which served as family or tribal cemeteries. These mounds were smaller and less imposing than those of the Stone Age. In Denmark, some mounds contained as many as seventy deposits of burnt bones, indicating use over many years.

The distribution of goods within these tombs suggests social stratification; more valuable objects were typically placed only with the remains of the wealthier tenants. The mixing of implements from different ages in these mounds is also attributed to the practice of opening sepulchral mounds over generations to make fresh interments.

## Settlement and Transition
Evidence from Switzerland reveals that during the Neolithic and early Bronze Ages, prehistoric pile dwellings were built over water. These lake dwellings, also found in Ireland and Scotland (such as at Glastonbury), provided sanitary advantages and defensive value. Excavations of these sites have recovered earthenware, bone, stone, and wooden utensils, as well as garments and nets.

The Bronze Age eventually gave way to the Iron Age. In Europe, men began smelting iron—by blowing charcoal fires and wroughting the metal through heating and hammering—perhaps as early as 3000 years ago (and even earlier in Asia Minor). While the introduction of bronze provided a significant advantage in warfare to the tribes who first obtained it, the subsequent revolution in weapons and implements caused by iron did not fundamentally change the general character of daily surroundings.
