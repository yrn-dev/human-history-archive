# Arc-Measurement and Geodetic Determination

Arc-measurement and the determination of geodetic coordinates are fundamental processes in the history of cartography and planetary science, evolving from ancient approximations to rigorous 19th-century telegraphic and triangulation methods.

## Ancient and Medieval Arc-Measurement
Early efforts to determine the circumference of the globe were pioneered by Eratosthenes (276-196 B.C.), a native of Cyrene who led the royal library in Alexandria. Eratosthenes introduced an effective mode of arc-measurement by utilizing the distance between Alexandria and Syene, which he knew to be 5,000 stadia apart on the same meridian. By observing that the sun was 7 deg. 12' south of the zenith at the northern end of this arc while being vertically overhead at the southern end, he inferred the entire circumference of the globe to be 252,000 stadia.

In the medieval period, Bagdad became a center for learning where Arab astronomers studied the works of Ptolemy. In 827, during the reign of caliph Abdullah al Mamun, an arc of the meridian was measured in the plain of Mesopotamia. Later Arabian astronomers further refined these measurements. Zarkala (Azarchel) determined the meridian distance between Bagdad and his observatory in Toledo to be 51° 30´, which represented an error of only 3°, a significant improvement over Ptolemy's error of 18°. Additionally, Abul Hassan (1230) reduced the great axis of the Mediterranean to 44°.

## 19th Century Geodetic Surveys in Asia
By the late 19th century, exact surveys based on triangulation were established across large portions of Asia. In Russia, these surveys extended as far east as Chinese Turkestan, reaching a longitude of approximately 75 deg. E. of Greenwich. In India, geodetic triangulation provided the basis for exact surveys extending to the eastern boundaries of Burma, at approximately 100 deg. E. By the close of the century, the final links of the great geodetic triangulation of the Indian peninsula were forged, although a direct geodetic connection to European systems remained to be completed.

While a direct process of earth measurement had not yet connected Asia and Europe, a topographical connection was established between Indian and Russian surveys. This connection demonstrated that the deductive methods used by both nations to determine the co-ordinate values of fixed points were sufficiently aligned to ensure no difficulty in adjustment for future Asiatic cartography.

## Telegraphic Determination of Longitude
Since 1890, the application of the telegraphic method for determining longitudes differentially with Greenwich has become more rigorous. This method involved measuring four specific arcs:
1. Greenwich to Potsdam
2. Potsdam to Teheran
3. Teheran to Bushire
4. Bushire to Karachi

One element of uncertainty in this process was the retardation of the current; for example, the current took 0^S.20 to travel the 3,000 m. between Potsdam and Teheran. However, it is probable that the final value is correct to within 0^S.05. This refined telegraphic method resulted in a slight correction of about 2" of arc compared to previous determinations made through Suez. The final result of this determination placed the Madras observatory 2' 27" to the west of the position previously adopted based on absolute astronomical determinations.

## Cartographic Projections and Representations
To represent these measurements on flat surfaces, various projections are employed. The globular projection represents a hemisphere where the equator and central meridian are equal straight lines at right angles. In this system, meridians and parallels are arcs of circles, making it an effective tool for conveying the general shape and position of land masses.

Conical projections represent parallels as concentric circles and meridians as equally spaced radii. A useful form of this is the projection with rectified meridians and two standard parallels, where two selected parallels are represented by their true lengths on the sphere and degrees along the meridians are represented by their true spherical lengths. Other constructions, such as those by O'Farrell of the ordnance survey, allow for maps where parallels and meridians intersect at right angles through specific geometric constructions involving the radius of the parallel and the longitude of the required point.
