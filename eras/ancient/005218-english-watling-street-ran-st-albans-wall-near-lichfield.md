# Watling Street

Watling Street is the name given by the English to a significant Roman road that formed part of the communication network in the lowlands of Britain. It was one of several main groups of roads radiating from London, providing the Romans with adequate communications across the region.

## Route and Geography
The road began in London and ran west, passing through St Albans Wall and continuing near Lichfield (Letocetum) toward Wroxeter and Chester. In addition to this primary route, Watling Street featured a branch that provided access to Lincoln and Leicester. Further north, the road crossed the Cheviots at Brownhartlaw, reaching an elevation of 1,664 feet. In this region, it passed close to the camp of *Ad Fines*, which served as a strategic point to keep the Gadeni and Otadeni of the north and the warlike Brigantes of the south in check.

In the south-east, the Roman Watling Street is also identified as the old high road through Kent, passing through the area of Greenwich.

## Historical and Political Significance
Watling Street served as a landmark for political boundaries during the Viking invasions of England. Following the peace of Wedmore in 878 and the subsequent ratification in the peace of Alfred and Guthrum (approximately 880), the boundary of Guthrum's East Anglian kingdom was defined. This boundary ran from the Thames estuary to the mouth of the Lea, up to its source near Leighton Buzzard, north to Bedford, and eastwards up the Ouse to Watling Street near Stony Stratford or Fenny. From that point, the boundary continued a few miles along Watling Street if Northamptonshire was included in Guthrum's kingdom. The territories handed over to the Danes, including East Anglia, Northern Mercia, Northumbria, and most of Essex, became known as the Danelagh.

## Local Impact and Urban Development
The road played a role in the development of various towns. In Dunstable, a municipal borough in Bedfordshire, the main part of the town extends for one mile along the broad, straight stretch of Watling Street. In the center of the town, Watling Street is crossed by the high road from Tring to Luton, which represents the ancient Icknield Way. 

According to tradition, King Henry I cleared the forest where Watling Street and the Icknield Way met to combat the depredations of robbers, subsequently encouraging subjects to settle there through grants of privileges. This led to the establishment of Dunstable as a royal borough. There is also evidence suggesting that a Romano-British village may have existed on the site of Dunstable along the road.
