# Military Marches and Strategic Movements

The history of warfare and strategic movement is characterized by the use of marches to position forces, the employment of stratagems to overcome geographical obstacles, and the logistical challenges of transporting troops and wounded personnel.

## Strategic Marches and Maneuvers
Military leaders have frequently utilized marches to secure territory or outmaneuver opponents. Darius I, for example, invaded Europe to reach the homeland of the Scythian horsemen in South Russia. His army crossed the Bosphorus and marched through Bulgaria to the Danube, where he utilized a bridge of boats to push northward. However, this march proved difficult as the infantry force was harassed by mounted Scythians who cut off supplies and avoided pitched battles, eventually forcing Darius into an inglorious retreat.

In other instances, marches were used to secure specific tactical positions. During the conflict involving the Athenians and the Chalkidians, Kallias marched his main force and Athenians to the isthmus to take a position in front of Aristeus. Similarly, auxiliaries from Olynthus began a march upon a concerted signal, though they were ultimately kept in check by Macedonian horse.

## Stratagems in Mountainous Terrain
The use of terrain and deception has been critical when armies are trapped or blocked. Hannibal, while marching through a valley, found himself harassed by Fabius, who used superior knowledge of the country to place four thousand men at a narrow outlet and establish a main body in the mountains. This maneuver threw Hannibal's army into disorder and resulted in the death of approximately eight hundred men.

To extricate himself from the mountain passes, Hannibal employed a stratagem involving two thousand captured oxen. He ordered torches or faggots of dry wood to be bound to the animals' horns and set them on fire at night. As the oxen were driven toward the enemy's camp, the Romans mistook the flames for an army marching in a great column with torches. The resulting confusion and alarm caused the Romans to abandon the pass, allowing Hannibal's light troops to take the heights and his main army to march through.

## Tactical Movements and Retreats
The nature of a march often depends on the geography of the route. In one instance, the main army of Brasidas passed through a defile along a long, winding road that made the ascent gradual but left the army exposed to the heights. To clear the way, a detachment of three hundred men scaled the steep heights to drive away the enemy, after which the main army pursued the regular road.

Retreats are also a significant aspect of military movement. Aristeus, finding the victorious Athenians between himself and Potidæa, faced the choice of cutting through the enemy or making a retreating march to Olynthus. He chose to force his way through the Athenian flank, wading into the sea to turn the extremity of the Potidæan wall to save his detachment.

## Logistics and the Movement of Wounded
Beyond the movement of active combat troops, military operations involve the organized march of wounded personnel. In the West, convoys of slightly wounded soldiers have been observed marching slowly across fields to reach main roads. These soldiers, often suffering from bullet wounds to the hand or forearm, are moved from the field of battle to a principal dressing station. 

The logistical chain for these individuals involves a sequence of movements: from the dressing station to a field hospital or collecting station, then to a clearing hospital, and finally home. For those too badly wounded to march, hammock-stretchers or ambulance wagons are used to transport them to sheltered halting-places.
