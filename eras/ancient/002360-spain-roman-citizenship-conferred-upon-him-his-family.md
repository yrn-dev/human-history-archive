# Roman Citizenship and Lucius Cornelius Balbus

## The Concept of Roman Citizenship
During the expansion of the Roman Republic and the early empire, the idea of citizenship served as a primary cohesive force. Roman citizenship was viewed as both a privilege and an obligation, providing individuals with confidence in their rights under Roman law and a willingness to make sacrifices for Rome. The prestige of Rome as a law-upholding and just entity extended well beyond its official boundaries.

The Roman Senate, particularly in the years preceding the first Punic War, adopted a strategy of incorporating sturdy antagonists on terms of equality. Citizenship was extended cautiously but steadily; some cities became Roman and were granted a voting share in the government, while others were granted the right to marry or trade in Rome without receiving full citizenship. This inclusivity was made possible by the internal struggle of the plebeians, who broke down the exclusive barriers of the old families and established a working equality.

However, this sense of citizenship was eventually undermined by the growth of slavery and wealth, starting as early as the Punic wars. While the legal status of citizenship continued to spread, the underlying idea of citizenship did not. The empire failed to educate its increasing multitudes of citizens or invite their cooperation in decision-making, leading to the eventual death of the spirit of citizenship.

## Lucius Cornelius Balbus Major
Lucius Cornelius Balbus, referred to as *Major* to distinguish him from his nephew, was born early in the last century B.C. and is generally considered to be of Phoenician origin. Balbus provided services against Sertorius in Spain, and as a result of these services, Pompey conferred Roman citizenship upon him and his family.

As a naturalized foreigner of significant wealth and influence, Balbus became a chief financier in Rome and was involved in the formation of the First Triumvirate. He maintained a close relationship with Caesar, serving as his chief engineer (*praefectus fabrum*) during Caesar's time as propraetor in Spain (61) and in Gaul (58).

## Legal Challenges and Political Ascent
Balbus's status as a naturalized foreigner and his influence made him many enemies. In 56, a native of Gades prosecuted him on the charge of illegally assuming the rights of a Roman citizen. This charge was also directed at the triumvirs. Balbus was acquitted after Crassus, Pompey, and Cicero spoke on his behalf.

During the subsequent civil war, Balbus attempted to have Cicero mediate between Pompey and Caesar to prevent Cicero from siding with Pompey. Later, Balbus served as Caesar's private secretary. Following the murder of Caesar, Balbus attached himself to Octavian. He achieved high political office, serving as praetor in 43 or 42 and as consul in 40. The consulship was an honor that, at that time, was conferred on an alien for the first time. Balbus also maintained a diary documenting the chief events of his own life and the life of Caesar.

## Roman Influence in Spain
The Roman presence in Spain was marked by both conquest and administration. In the northern regions, the Romans subjugated the district south of the Cantabrians around 25 B.C., naming it Augustana. The capital of this region was Asturica Augusta, known today as Astorga in the province of Leon. Astorga served as a provincial capital and a meeting point for four military roads. Although it was later sacked by the Moors and the Goths in the 5th century, the city still retains massive walls of Roman origin.
