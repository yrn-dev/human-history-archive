# The Barbiton and Other Ancient Greek Instruments

The study of ancient Greek musical instruments relies on a combination of classical writers, sculptural representations, and comparative analysis with other cultures. Among these, the barbiton stands as a significant example of a stringed instrument that shared characteristics with the lyre but maintained a distinct identity and cultural perception.

## The Barbiton
The barbiton (also known as the barbitos) was an ancient stringed instrument derived from Persia and known through Roman and Greek classics. According to Greek writers, the barbiton possessed features in common with the lyre, which justified its classification alongside it. However, it was often regarded by the Greeks of Hellas as a "barbarian" instrument, favored only by those with unorthodox artistic tastes. By the time of Aristotle, the instrument had fallen into disuse, though it later reappeared under the Romans.

Specific characteristics of the barbiton are detailed by various authors:
* **Sound and Construction:** Pollux, in the *Onomasticon*, refers to the instrument as the barbiton or barymite (derived from the Greek words for "heavy" and "string"). He describes it as an instrument that produced deep sounds, noting that its strings were twice as long as those of the pectis and sounded an octave lower.
* **String Count:** The Sicilian poet Theocritus describes the barbiton as having many strings—specifically more than seven. This contrasted with the cithara of the best period, where seven strings were considered the perfect number.
* **Usage:** The poet Anacreon wrote that his barbitos produced erotic tones. Additionally, Pindar attributes the introduction of the instrument into Greece to Terpander and notes that one could "magadize," or play in two parts at an interval of an octave, using two such instruments.

While some authors, such as Carl Engel, have represented the barbiton in reproductions of sculpture as boat-shaped and without a neck, other evidence suggests a different form. Certain sculptures show an unidentified stringed instrument that combines the features of the lyre and the rebab, featuring a vaulted back and a gradual narrowing to form a neck. This instrument resembles a large lute with a wide neck and typically possesses seven or nine strings.

## The Lyre and Cithara
In Greek literature, the lyre is referred to by several names, though connecting these names to specific varieties of the instrument is difficult. Plato and other writers explicitly distinguished between the lyre and the cithara. According to tradition, Hermes invented the lyre, while Apollo invented the cithara; consequently, the two deities shared an altar at Olympia.

The lyre and the chelys (meaning "tortoise") are grouped together, as are the cithara and the phorminx. The chelys may have been the smallest lyre, supported by the knees or one arm, with a sound-chest resembling a tortoise. Apollo is said to have carried a golden phorminx.

The primary distinction between the lyre and cithara lay in the construction of the sound-chest:
* **The Lyre:** Featured a vaulted back imitating a tortoise, with a flat sound-board of parchment or wood glued directly over it.
* **The Cithara:** Possessed a shallower sound-chest where the front and back were connected by ribs or sides.

## Other Instruments in Greece
Beyond stringed instruments, the bag-pipe was known in Greece, though it was considered a rustic instrument used exclusively by peasants and shepherds. A single passage by Dion Chrysostom provides evidence that the instrument existed in Greece by A.D. 100. It is believed the Greeks received the bag-pipe from Chaldaea or Egypt (in the form of the *as-it*).

There is also scholarly discussion regarding the term *sumphonia*. While some suggest the Aramaic *sump[=o]ny[=a]* in the book of Daniel is a loan-word from the Greek *sumphonia*, this is contested. The original natural meaning of the Greek *sumphonia* was "a concordant interval" or "concord of sound." Evidence that it referred to a specific instrument is slight and dates to the 2nd century B.C., with only one passage in Polybius bearing on the matter, though the context of that reading remains uncertain.
