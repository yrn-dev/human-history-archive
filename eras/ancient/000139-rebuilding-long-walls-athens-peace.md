# From the Rebuilding of the Long Walls of Athens to the Peace of Antalkidas

## The Restoration of Athenian Fortifications
Following 404 B.C., Athens existed for eleven years without the Long Walls that connected the city to its seaport town, Peiræus, nor did it have walls surrounding Peiræus itself. This state of vulnerability was a sentence imposed by enemies to ensure Athens possessed poor defenses against aggression from the "mistress of the sea," as well as limited trade and few mercantile or armed ships.

The process of rebuilding these fortifications was initiated through the efforts of Konon, who petitioned the satrap Pharnabazus for the use of a fleet. Konon argued that the renovation of the connected fortifications of Athens and Peiræus would be a mortifying and destructive blow to Sparta, depriving the Lacedæmonians of the most significant harvest of the Peloponnesian War. Pharnabazus, feeling indignant toward the Lacedæmonians, supported these plans, leaving the fleet under Konon's command and providing a considerable sum of money to fund the fortifications.

## Strategic Impact and Maritime Ambitions
The financial and military support from Pharnabazus provided the confederates with a preponderance of power over Sparta for that year. Konon's objectives were extensive; beyond completing the Long Walls and the fortifications of Peiræus, he organized a mercenary force for the defense of Corinth—a system later improved by Iphikrates. 

To establish a renewed maritime power for Athens, Konon demonstrated his force among the islands. He further attempted to expand Athenian influence by sending an envoy to Dionysius at Syracuse to detach him from his alliance with Sparta. This effort was supported by Evagoras, the despot of Salamis in Cyprus, who offered his sister in marriage to Dionysius. The proposal was based on mutual sympathies: Evagoras was at variance with the Phœnicians in Cyprus and Phœnicia, while Dionysius was engaged in hostilities with the Carthaginians in Sicily. However, the attempt failed, and Dionysius remained an ally of Sparta.

## The Corinthian War and Regional Conflict
The period was marked by the Corinthian War in Central Greece, characterized by growing hostility toward Sparta. While Sparta's power extended into Northern Greece and Herakleia, discontent grew among its allies. This tension was exacerbated when Sparta became engaged in a war against Persia. The Persian satrap Tithraustes sought to ignite further conflict against Sparta by sending money to Argos, Corinth, and Thebes. According to the records, this money did not create hostility but rather amplified pre-existing hostile tendencies.

Conflict also erupted between Sparta and Thebes, known as the Bœotian War. Sparta conducted active operations in Bœotia, with Pausanias leading an army from the Peloponnesus and Lysander acting from Herakleia. In a significant shift of Greek sentiment, the Thebans applied to Athens for aid. The Athenians, benefiting from the political stability following the amnesty after the expulsion of the Thirty, voted unanimously to assist Thebes.

## Military Engagements and the Fall of Lysander
During the Bœotian conflict, Orchomenus revolted and joined Lysander, who subsequently invaded Bœotia and attacked Haliartus. Lysander was repulsed and killed during this engagement. Following his death, Pausanias arrived in Bœotia, but upon receiving the bodies of Lysander and others for burial, he evacuated the region. This outcome led to anger against Pausanias in Sparta, resulting in his condemnation in absentia and his flight into voluntary exile.

The death of Lysander encouraged Sparta's enemies, leading to an alliance between Athens, Thebes, Corinth, and Argos, which was later joined by the Eubœans. This shift elevated Thebes to the rank of a primary power under the leadership of Ismenias.

## Defensive Operations at Corinth and Ægina
The Athenians demonstrated their engineering capabilities and strategic alertness when Praxitas breached the Long Walls of Corinth, which threatened the road to Bœotia or Attica. Having already completed their own Long Walls, the Athenians marched to Corinth with masons and carpenters. In a few days, they re-established the western wall—the critical barrier against Lacedæmonian incursions from Sikyon—before finishing the eastern wall and retiring.

Simultaneously, tensions rose with the Æginetans. Despite a period of peace and reciprocal commerce following the battle of Knidus and the rebuilding of the Athenian Long Walls, the Lacedæmonian harmost Eteonikus forced the Æginetans into war. With the approval of the ephors, Eteonikus encouraged the use of privateers to depredate Attica. Athens responded by sending ten triremes to blockade Ægina and a body of hoplites under Pamphilus to occupy a permanent fort on the island. This squadron was eventually driven off by Teleutias, who was commanding a fleet at Rhodes.
