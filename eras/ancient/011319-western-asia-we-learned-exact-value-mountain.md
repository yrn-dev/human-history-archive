# Geographical Surveys and Mountain Barriers of Western and Central Asia

The geographical understanding of Asia has been significantly advanced through scientific enterprise, geodetic triangulation, and exploratory surveys. By the close of the 19th century, western Asia—with the exception of Arabia—was considered to be freed from geographical perplexity. This progress has allowed for the precise assessment of the region's topography, particularly the mountain systems that serve as political and physical barriers.

## Mountain Barriers of Western Asia
In western Asia, the exact value of the mountain barrier situated between Herat and Merv has been determined. This system has been mapped in its connection to the Elburz of Persia. Furthermore, researchers have come to appreciate the practical political significance of the irregular mountain system that links the southern abutment of the Hindu Kush near Bamian with the water-divide located north of Herat. 

The exploration of this region has been thorough, resulting in the recording of every significant route and important pass. This geographical clarity has allowed for the assessment of the Pamirs and Herat as bases of aggression and has provided the basis for the demarcation of a boundary, giving Afghanistan a new political entity.

## Central Asian Mountain Systems and Basins
Central Asia is characterized by great mountain barriers that separate the Eastern, Western, and Southern peoples of the continent. Three primary mountain systems radiate eastward from the central mountain masses:
*   **The Himalayas:** Extending south-eastward, south of Tibet.
*   **The Kuen Lun:** Extending eastward, north of Tibet.
*   **The Thien Shan:** Extending north-eastward to join the Altai mountains.

Between the Kuen Lun and the Thien Shan lies the Tarim Basin (roughly Eastern Turkestan), an area featuring rivers that end in intermittent lakes and swamps rather than reaching the sea. While this basin was more fertile in the past, it remains a key geographical feature. The mountain barrier to the west of the Tarim Basin is described as high but not forbidding, offering practicable routes into Western Turkestan. Travelers can move from China to Kashgar via the Tarim valley or the northern foothills of the Kuen Lun; from Kashgar, roads lead over the mountains to Bokhara, Samarkand, and Kokand. This region serves as a natural historical meeting place for Mongolian and Aryan peoples.

## The Sarikol Range and the Pamirs
On the western edge of the Kashgar plains, the Sarikol range defines the political boundary between China and Russia. Known to the ancients as Taurus and in medieval times as Bolor, the range consists of two parallel chains. The western chain serves as the water-divide of the Pamirs, while the eastern chain (the Kandar or Kashgar range) is split by lateral gorges that allow for the drainage of the eastern Pamir slopes.

## Geodetic Surveys and Mapping Accuracy
The accuracy of Asiatic geographical surveys has been raised to levels previously deemed unattainable through various scientific methods:

**Triangulation and Longitude:**
Exact surveys based on triangulation extend as far east as Chinese Turkestan (approximately 75 deg. E. of Greenwich) in Russia, and as far east as the boundaries of Burma (approximately 100 deg. E.) in India. To determine longitudes differentially with Greenwich, the telegraphic method has been utilized through a series of arcs: Greenwich to Potsdam, Potsdam to Teheran, Teheran to Bushire, and Bushire to Karachi.

**Exploration and Topography:**
Russian explorers, including Pevtsov and Roborovski, conducted scientific expeditions between 1889 and 1894 that greatly increased knowledge of the northern borders of Tibet and the topography of western Chinese Turkestan. Other notable 19th-century Russian explorers include Potanin, P.K. Kozlov, Lessar, and Annentkov, the latter of whom bridged the Trans-Caspian deserts with a railway.

**The Central Desert:**
The Gobi desert is bounded by the Khingan range to the east, the northern face of the Tibetan plateau to the south, and the Yablonoi Mountains and Thien-shan to the north and north-west. This region is characterized by high elevations; no part of the desert is believed to be less than 2,000 ft. above sea level, with the plains around Yarkand and Kashgar ranging from 4,000 to 6,000 ft. Some mountain ridges in this central area exceed 12,000 or 10,000 ft.

## General Form of the Continent
When viewed on a globe to avoid the distortions of flat maps (such as Mercator’s projection, which exaggerates Northern Asia and Siberia), Asia resembles a great isosceles spherical triangle. Its north-eastern apex is at East Cape in the Bering Strait. Its two equal sides, each about 6,500 m., extend to the extremity of the Malay peninsula in the east and the southern point of Arabia in the west. The base, measuring about 4,500 m., is deeply indented by the Bay of Bengal and the Arabian Sea.
