# Troubles in Korkyra and Related Athenian Naval Operations

The history of Korkyra is marked by its strategic importance in the Mediterranean, its internal and external conflicts, and its pivotal role in the tensions between Athens and Corinth during the Peloponnesian War.

## The Conflict Between Epidamnus and Korkyra
The troubles involving Korkyra were closely linked to the city of Epidamnus, located on an outlaying peninsula of the Illyrian Taulantii. While Epidamnus had once been prosperous with a large population, it suffered severe reverses due to internal sedition between the people and the oligarchy, as well as attacks from neighboring Illyrians. Following a revolution in which the people overthrew the oligarchy, the exiled oligarchs allied with the Illyrians to harass the city by land and sea.

The Epidamnian democracy, desperate for assistance, sent suppliants to the temple of Hêrê in Korkyra to request mediation with the exiles and military aid against the Illyrians. Despite being democratically governed themselves, the Korkyræans refused to help. This refusal stemmed from the fact that the Epidamnian oligarchy—not the demos—maintained the strongest ties to Korkyra, as their forefathers had emigrated from the island and their kinsmen and family burial-places remained there. After receiving an unqualified sanction from the Delphian oracle, the Epidamnians turned to Corinth for aid, appealing to the Corinthians as their œkists and chiefs.

## Korkyra and the Peloponnesian War
The quarrel between Corinth and Korkyra, and the subsequent intervention of Athens between 433 and 432 B.C., served as a catalyst for the Peloponnesian War. This intervention had two primary effects: it fostered a violent anti-Athenian spirit in Corinth—a city with which the Sicilian Dorians shared strong sympathy and commerce—and it provided Athens with a necessary maritime footing in Korkyra to operate in Italy and Sicily.

While some historians, following Plutarch, suggest that Athens held ambitious projects in Sicily at this time, Thucydidês contradicts this. The Athenian policy at the outbreak of the war was conservative, whereas their enemies were aggressive. For Athens, the value of Korkyra as an ally lay primarily in the ability to obstruct reinforcements in ships, money, and provisions that Sparta and Corinth expected from the Sicilian Dorians, rather than in any pursuit of conquest. The contests between Athens and Corinth, occurring first indirectly at Korkyra and later at Potidæa, triggered significant movements within the Lacedæmonian alliance.

## Later Athenian Expeditions and Naval Actions
In the fifth year of the Peloponnesian War, while troubles occurred in Korkyra, the Athenian general Nikias targeted the rocky island of Minôa. Located at the mouth of the Megarian harbor, Minôa was occupied by a Megarian garrison. Nikias used battering machines from his ships to destroy two towers at Nisæa that defended the narrow channel. By also fortifying the causeway that connected the island to the mainland via a lagoon, Nikias completely insulated Minôa, making it an Athenian possession. This allowed Athens to maintain an effective blockade of the Megarian harbor, a task they had previously only managed from the opposite shore of Salamis.

In a later period, specifically beginning in April or May of 373 B.C., the admiral Timotheus undertook a preliminary cruise to collect funds for an expedition to Korkyra. He ordered allies, including the Bœotians who sent several triremes, to assemble at Kalauria, an island off Trœzen consecrated to Poseidon. However, Timotheus's prolonged absence left the unpaid fleet in a state of disorganization and distress. When news reached Athens that Korkyra was under great pressure, the Athenians—prompted by accusations from the orator Kallistratus and Iphikrates—cancelled Timotheus's appointment. They instead named Chabrias, Kallistratus, and Iphikrates to equip a fleet and proceed to Korkyra without delay.

## Historical Records of Disturbances
There has been scholarly debate regarding the reality of certain tumults in Korkyra mentioned by Diodorus. While Sievers controverted these accounts because they were absent from Xenophon's *Hellenika* and seemingly contradicted by Thucydidês, other interpretations suggest that Thucydidês's phrasing refers specifically to the first ten years of the Peloponnesian War (between the Peace of Nikias and the surprise of Platæa), thereby leaving room for the truth of the disturbances recorded by Diodorus.
