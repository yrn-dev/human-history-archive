# The Concept of Happiness and Liberation

In various historical and philosophical contexts, the concept of happiness has been linked to liberation, whether from physical constraints, mental distress, or the cycle of existence. These interpretations range from the spiritual goals of early Indian thought to the rationalist frameworks of Greek philosophy and the political structures of ancient governance.

## Spiritual Liberation in Indian Thought
In the accounts of Gotama, the term "happy" is described as possessing a double meaning. While it typically refers to joy or delight—such as the delight felt by the people of Kapilavastu upon the birth of the raja's only grandson—it also carries a deeper spiritual significance. In this context, to be "happy" meant to be saved, delivered, and freed from the chains of rebirth. 

This distinction was highlighted when Gotama's cousin sang a stanza declaring the happiness of the father, mother, and wife of a son and husband. Gotama, who no longer valued worldly ties and viewed the birth of his own son as a "new and strong tie" to be broken, recognized that the girl's use of the word "happy" reminded him of his highest hopes for liberation.

## Epicureanism and the Removal of Pain
In the moral philosophy of Epicurus, happiness is defined through a qualified hedonism. Unlike the Cyrenaic doctrine, which viewed pleasure as active, positive moments, Epicurus viewed pleasure as a habit of mind characterized by tranquility. 

For the Epicurean, true pleasure and happiness are defined negatively as the removal and absorption of all that causes pain. This state implies a freedom from trouble of the mind and pain of the body. This "grave and solemn pleasure" is an unobtrusive ease of heart rather than excitement. Epicurus taught that prudence is the chief excellence and the only real guide to this happiness, as the intellect must balance possible pleasures against ensuing evils to secure lasting tranquility. He asserted that living wisely, nobly, and righteously is a necessity for living pleasantly.

## Philosophical and Political Applications in Greece
In the time of Sokratês and Plato, happiness was viewed as a subject for scientific study. They spoke of a "science which has for its object to make men happy," distinguishing this theoretical art of behaving in society from a mere irrational knack. Sokratês proposed that there is a comprehensive end to human living: the security and happiness of every person within a society.

Similarly, in the political sphere, the philosopher Dion urged the young Dionysius to educate himself in virtue. Dion argued that by contemplating virtue and forming his character upon the noblest models, Dionysius could achieve great happiness for himself and bestow it upon his citizens through just and mild paternal rule. Dion contrasted this with the "adamantine chains" of terror and force used by Dionysius's father, suggesting that the goodwill and loyalty engendered by justice provide a more secure foundation for rule.

## Divine Guidance and Social Order
In the accounts of the Israelites (Bani Israel), happiness is associated with divine instruction and political order. Following a period of fear, Moses appeared to the people in a joyful and exalted state, which freed them from their apprehensions. Moses informed the Hebrews that God had suggested a "happy method of living" and an order of political government for their common advantage. These institutions were presented not as the work of Moses, the son of Amram and Jochebed, but as the work of the God who had provided food from heaven, parted the sea, and established the lineage of Abraham, Isaac, and Jacob.

## Other Perspectives on Happiness and Freedom
In the history of the Japanese people, the introduction of the Indian creed offered a vision of happiness in the next life and a means of securing insensibility to suffering in the current life. This creed was seen as a practical teaching and a source of positive hope that contrasted with the "negative pallor of Shinto."
