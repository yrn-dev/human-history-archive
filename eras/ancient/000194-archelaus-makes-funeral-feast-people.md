# Archelaus and the Funeral Feast of Herod

## The Funeral Feast and Initial Succession
Following the death of Herod, his son Archelaus observed a period of mourning for seven days. During this time, Archelaus provided a very expensive funeral feast for the multitude. According to the archive's records, this custom of feasting the multitude is a practice that often leads many of the Israelites into poverty, as those who omit the feast are not regarded as holy persons.

After the mourning period, Archelaus dressed in a white garment and visited the temple. From a throne of gold and an elevated seat, he spoke kindly to the people, thanking them for their submission and the zeal they displayed during his father's funeral. While the people greeted him with various acclamations, Archelaus clarified that he would not yet assume the title or authority of a king. He explained that he had previously refused the diadem at Jericho and would wait until Caesar, who was designated as the lord of the matter by testament, confirmed the succession. He promised to provide abundant requitals to both the soldiers and the people for their goodwill once the Romans granted him a complete title to the kingdom, stating his ambition to be better in all things than his father.

## Civil Unrest and the Passover Massacre
Despite these initial gestures, disturbances arose. A group of innovators began to lament Matthias and other teachers of the law who had been slain by Herod. These individuals had been condemned for pulling down a golden eagle, and because of the fear inspired by Herod, no funeral mourning had been observed for them at the time of their deaths. The people demanded that Archelaus punish those who had been honored by Herod and replace the high priest with one of greater purity and adherence to the law.

Archelaus, who intended to travel to Rome to seek Caesar's determination, was offended by these demands. He initially attempted to quiet the innovators through persuasion, sending his general to explain that the deaths of their friends were lawful and that the current time was improper for such petitions. However, the seditious individuals responded with passion and violence, throwing stones at the general and other messengers sent by Archelaus.

The tension culminated during the feast of unleavened bread, known as the Passover. This festival is a memorial of the Israelites' deliverance from Egypt, characterized by the offering of many sacrifices and the arrival of an innumerable multitude from within and beyond the country to worship God. During this time, seditious persons gathered in the temple, bewailing the Rabbins and sustaining themselves through begging.

Fearing that this "madness" would infect the entire multitude, Archelaus first sent a tribune and a cohort of soldiers to suppress the violent efforts. The multitude reacted by stoning the soldiers and wounding the tribune. Concluding that the situation could not be restrained without bloodshed, Archelaus deployed his entire army. Footmen entered through the city while horsemen moved through the plain to prevent those outside the temple from assisting those within. The horsemen killed approximately three thousand people, while the survivors fled to neighboring mountains. Archelaus then ordered a proclamation for the people to return to their homes, ending the festival.

## Legal Challenges and Banishment
The actions of Archelaus became a central point of contention when his right to the kingdom was debated in Rome. Antipater, the son of Salome and an enemy of Archelaus, argued before Caesar that Archelaus had usurped royal authority before it was granted. Antipater specifically cited the destruction of the multitude at the Jewish festival as evidence that Archelaus had acted as a king rather than a private citizen. He further accused Archelaus of changing army commanders, determining law-suits, and sitting on a royal throne.

In the tenth year of his government, Archelaus's brothers and the principal men of Samaria and Judea accused him before Caesar of barbarous and tyrannical usage. They argued that he had broken Caesar's commands to behave with moderation. Caesar, angered by these reports, summoned Archelaus to Rome via his steward. Upon hearing the accusations and Archelaus's replies, Caesar banished him to Vienna, a city in Gaul, and seized his money.

## Family and Relations
Archelaus was the son of Herod and Malthace. He had a half-brother, Philip the tetrarch, who was born to Cleopatra. Although they had different mothers, they were raised together privately in Rome. In other dealings, Archelaus had previously assisted a son-in-law out of danger through a stratagem; following these reconciliations, they spent time in agreeable entertainments and feastings. Upon a previous departure, Herod had presented Archelaus with a golden throne set with precious stones, seventy talents, several eunuchs, and a concubine named Pannychis.
