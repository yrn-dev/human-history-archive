# Amalek and the Amalekites

## Origins and Geography
Amalek was born to Esau and a concubine named Thamna, making him a non-legitimate son. The descendants of Amalek, the Amalekites, inhabited a region known as Amalekitis. This area was part of the larger country of Idumea, which also included Gobalitis (the original Mount Seir) and Akrabatine. While Idumea served as the general name for the whole region, the specific parts retained the names of their particular inhabitants.

## Conflict with the Israelites
The Amalekites are recorded as having caused a great deal of mischief to the Hebrews during their time in the wilderness and their journey from Egypt toward their own country. Due to this hostility, God commanded the prophet Samuel to instruct King Saul to punish the Amalekites through war. 

The divine mandate given to Saul was to subdue the Amalekites and leave none of them alive, extending the punishment to women and infants. Saul was ordered to pursue them through every age and to destroy everything, including asses and other beasts, rather than reserving any for his own possession. This total destruction was intended to blot out the name of Amalek entirely, in obedience to the commands of Moses.

## Relations with the Kenites
The Kenites were a tribe or clan located in the south of Palestine. Although they were closely associated with the Amalekites, the Kenites did not share the Amalekites' hostility toward Israel. Because of this distinction, Saul spared the Kenites when he was commanded by Yahweh to destroy Amalek. Similarly, David appeared to be on friendly terms with them while living in Judah.

## Oracles and Prophecies
In the biblical text of Numbers xxiv, there are various poems and oracles. The "Fifth" oracle (xxiv. 20) specifically deals with the ruin of Amalek. The date of this oracle is uncertain; some suggest it may be early if it refers to the historical Amalek, while others propose that Amalek may be used symbolicaly. 

Additionally, the "Third" oracle (xxiv. 3-9) celebrates the glory and conquests of the monarchy and mentions Agag. However, it is noted that Agag in this verse can hardly be the Amalekite king mentioned in 1 Samuel xv, as Amalek was too small and obscure. Some versions of the text substitute the name Gog, which would imply a post-exilic date, though both Agag and Gog are considered by some to be textual corruptions.
