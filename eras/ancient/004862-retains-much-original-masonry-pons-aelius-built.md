# Roman Bridges and Masonry

## Bridges of Ancient Rome
The earliest known bridge constructed in Rome over the Tiber was the timber Pons Sublicius, which was defended by Horatius. Subsequent Roman bridge construction transitioned toward stone. The Pons Milvius (currently known as Ponte Molle) was reconstructed in stone in 109 B.C. by M. Aemilius Scaurus; it is believed that portions of the original bridge remain in the current structure, with arches spanning between 51 and 79 ft. 

Other notable Roman bridges include the Pons Fabricius, dating to approximately 62 B.C., which remains practically intact, and the Pons Cestius, likely built in 46 B.C., which retains much of its original masonry. The Pons Aelius was built by Hadrian in A.D. 134 and later repaired by Pope Clement IX and Pope Nicholas II; now known as the bridge of St Angelo, it featured eight arches with a maximum span of 62 ft.

## Imperial Engineering and Infrastructure
Roman engineers extended their bridge-building capabilities across the empire. In A.D. 104, Trajan constructed a bridge over the Danube, which Dio Cassius notes may have been 3000 to 4000 ft. in length. While some piers are said to still exist, a bas-relief on the Trajan column depicts the structure with timber arches and masonry piers, though this representation is considered probably conventional. Trajan also built the bridge of Alcantara in Spain, a stone block structure built without cement. This bridge reached a total length of 670 ft. and stood 210 ft. above the stream, utilizing six arches.

In the 6th century, the bridge of Narses was constructed to carry the Via Salaria over the Anio. This bridge included a fortification, a feature that became common in later bridges for the purposes of enforcement of tolls or defense. The bridge of Narses was destroyed in 1867 during Garibaldi's approach to Rome. Additionally, Roman engineers constructed vast lines of aqueducts from 300 B.C. onwards; where these were carried above ground, they functioned as arched bridge structures of remarkable magnitude, generally composed of concrete and brick.

## Roman Roads and Fortifications in Britain
In northern Britain, three principal roads were established. One route led north from York, passing through forts at Lanchester, Ebchester, Binchester, Piers Bridge, and Catterick Bridge, continuing toward Scotland. Branches from this road through Chester-le-Street reached the mouth of the Tyne at South Shields and the Tyne Bridge (Pons Aelius) at Newcastle. A second road traveled north-west from Catterick Bridge, crossing the Pennine Chain via forts at Brough-under-Stainmoor, Bowes, and Rokeby, before reaching Birrens and Hadrian's wall near Carlisle (Luguvallium). A third, more complex route started from Chester and moved up the western coast. Forts along this western route included Ribchester (Bremetennacum), Manchester (Mamucium or Mancunium), Brougham Castle (Brocavum), Old Penrith (Voreda), and Old Carlisle (possibly Petriana). Western branches also included Waterhead, Hardknott above Eskdale, Watercrook near Kendal, and Maryport (Uxellodunum).

In Wales, a road system connected Chester to a fort at Carnarvon (Segontium) via a fort at Caer-hyn. Another road ran along the south coast from Caerleon-on-Usk to Carmarthen, passing a fort at Cardiff. A third road, roughly parallel to Cardigan Bay, connected these northern and southern routes with forts at Tommen-y-mur and Llanio. The interior was supported by a system of forts at Gellygaer, Merthyr, the Gaer near Brecon, Castle Collen near Llandrindod Wells, and Caer-gai on Bala Lake.

## Masonry Techniques and Theory
In architectural terms, drafted masonry refers to large stones where a sunken surface or "draft" is dressed around the edge, leaving the center as it came from the quarry. This is worked vertically with an eight-tooth-per-inch adze to a width of 2 to 4 in. While the earliest example is the platform built by Cyrus in 530 B.C. at Pasargadae, the finest examples are found in Hebron, the walls of the Haram in Jerusalem, and the tower of David from the time of Herod.

Regarding the physics of masonry, the arch is the most natural form for brick, concrete, or masonry because it is subjected throughout to compression. Stability depends on the line of pressure in relation to the intrados and extrados of the arch ring. To increase safety and reduce uncertainty regarding stress, engineers sometimes introduce hinges at the springings and crown. In recent masonry arched bridges with spans up to 150 ft., this has allowed for greater economy.
