# Buddhist Asceticism and the Cessation of Craving

## Core Philosophy and the Four Noble Truths
Buddhist asceticism is characterized as a philosophical and ethical product centered on the goal of deliverance from life and the inevitable suffering associated with it. The foundation of this system rests upon the Four Noble Truths, which the Buddha taught to his brethren. These truths concern sorrow, the cause of sorrow, the cessation of sorrow, and the path that leads to such cessation.

The Buddha stated that the long wandering on the weary path of transmigration was a result of not understanding and grasping these four truths. According to the teachings, once these truths are grasped, the craving for existence is rooted out. This process destroys that which leads to renewed existence, thereby ending the cycle of birth.

## The Mechanism of Rebirth and Craving
In contrast to popular beliefs and brahmin theology—which posit that a subtle entity called the soul leaves the body at death to enter a new one—the Buddhist adaptation teaches that no memory, consciousness, or soul passes from one body to another. Instead, it is the craving and grasping that persist at the moment of death that cause a new set of *Skandhas* to arise. These *Skandhas* constitute the new body along with its capacities and mental tendencies.

The Buddhist adaptation of the theory of *Karma* attempts to explain the connection between all forms of life, the mysteries of inherited character, and the influence of an immeasurable past on the individual. For those who believe in this system, there is a hope of escaping the miseries of transmigration. An Arahat, having conquered the cravings that produce a new body, performs actions that are no longer *Karma* but *Kiriya*, which do not lead to rebirth.

## The Path to Emancipation
The "Noble Truth as to the passing away of pain" is described as the emancipation from and the giving up of "craving thirst," leaving no passion remaining. The method to achieve this is the Noble Eightfold Path, which consists of:
*   Right Views
*   Right Aspirations
*   Right speech, conduct, and mode of livelihood
*   Right Effort
*   Right Mindfulness
*   Right Rapture

The Buddha believed that an elect disciple possessing this way of Truth could be assured of final salvation, effectively destroying the possibility of rebirth as a ghost, an animal, or in any place of woe, including hell.

## Interpretation and Popular Understanding
While the core teachings are presented as simple, they are considered pregnant with meaning and are expanded upon extensively in the *suttas*. However, the concept of emancipation from the self has historically been difficult for the general multitude to grasp. 

Because this idea was often beyond the reach of some teachers sent out from Benares, the teaching frequently took on the appearance of salvation from sufferings and misfortunes rather than salvation from the self. Consequently, some utilized existing superstitions regarding the transmigration of the soul—despite this being contrary to the Master's own teaching—to urge virtue. They warned the people against falling into innumerable hells of torment or being reborn in miserable or degraded forms, presenting the Buddha as a savior from such unlimited torment.
