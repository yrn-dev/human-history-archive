# THE EPISTLE OF BARNABAS

The Epistle of Barnabas is categorized as one of the apocryphal books of the New Testament. It is part of a broad fringe of primitive Christian literature that approximated the character of the New Testament, specifically grouped among the writings of the so-called Apostolic (or Sub-Apostolic) Fathers. This group includes other works such as the *Didache*, the *Shepherd* of Hermas, the letters of Ignatius and Polycarp, and the Second Epistle of Clement. While the writers of these texts were generally leading persons in the Church who continued apostolic tradition, the Epistle of Barnabas is noted as being more eccentric.

## Canonical Standing and Manuscripts
The epistle once possessed a level of quasi-canonical authority, a status supported by the writings of Eusebius. This authority was most prominent in Alexandria, where Clement commented on the text in his *Hypotyposes* as he did with canonical scriptures, and Origen cited it in a similar spirit.

Evidence of its historical placement is found in several manuscripts. In the 4th-century *Codex Sinaiticus*, the Epistle of Barnabas appears at the end of the New Testament as a sort of appendix, followed by the *Shepherd* of Hermas. In an 11th-century manuscript that also contains the *Didache*, the epistle is followed by two writings that serve as an appendix to the New Testament in the *Codex Alexandrinus*.

## Authorship and Attribution
Clement ascribed the work to "the prophet" or "the apostle" Barnabas, specifically referring to the fellow-apostle of Paul. However, internal evidence indicates that this ascription is impossible, and the text of the epistle itself makes no claim regarding its authorship. 

Scholars have offered different theories regarding the true author. Lightfoot suggested the writer might have been an unknown namesake of the famous Barnabas. An alternative theory posits that the Alexandrian Church attributed the work to the apostle Barnabas because the writing was so highly favored and they were ready to believe it was of apostolic origin. This attribution aligns with a tradition that identifies Barnabas as an evangelist in Alexandria.

## Literary Relations
The Epistle of Barnabas is linked to other ancient texts through citations and shared influences. It cites the *Book of the Secrets* (also known as *Slavonic Enoch*). Additionally, the author of the epistle was likely acquainted with the same literature as the authors of the *Apocrypha of Baruch* and the *Ascension of Isaiah*.

## Related Works and Distinctions
The Epistle of Barnabas must be distinguished from other texts bearing the name of Barnabas. The *Acta Barnabae* is a separate apocryphal work that purports to have been written by Mark. Furthermore, there is a "Gospel of Barnabas." While antiquity mentions an apocryphal Gospel of Barnabas in the *Decretum Gelasii*, the only extant version is a longish gospel found in an Italian manuscript. This specific version is written from a Mahommedan standpoint and is believed by some to be the work of a Christian renegade to Mahommedanism from between the 13th and 16th centuries.
