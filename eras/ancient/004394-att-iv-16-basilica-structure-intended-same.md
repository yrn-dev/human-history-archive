# Basilica

The basilica was a large covered building used in antiquity to accommodate significant numbers of people. While the term was frequently used in Latin literature and inscriptions, it strictly denoted a structure situated near a city's business center, designed for the convenience of litigants, merchants, and those engaged in public service. In a more derived sense, the term was applied to various large structures regardless of location, including riding schools (*basilica equestris exercitatoria*), halls of audience, covered promenades in private palaces, and stores for goods such as clothing (*basilica vestiaria*) or flowers (*basilica floscellaria*).

## Origins and Etymology
The word "basilica" is a Latinized version of the Greek adjective *basilike*, meaning "royal," which would have been paired with a feminine substantive such as *stoa* or *domus*. One theory suggests the term originates from the *basileios stoa* in Athens, where the court of the Areopagus sometimes assembled and the *archon basileus* conducted business. Another example of this name is the grand structure built by Herod the Great along the southern edge of the Temple platform at Jerusalem, which featured a nave, aisles, and clerestory lighting.

Regarding the Roman basilica, it is unclear if they were derived directly from Athenian examples or mediately through other Greek cities. However, M. Porcius Cato, the censor, erected the first Roman basilica—the Basilica Porcia—in 184 B.C. shortly after visiting Athens. The earliest known example outside Rome is the basilica at Pompeii, which may date to the 2nd century B.C.

## Function and Relation to the Forum
Vitruvius describes the basilica as an adjunct to the forum. In early classical times in Italy and Greece, political, commercial, and legal business, as well as pageants and shows, took place in the open forum. As populations grew, additional accommodations became necessary: theatres and amphitheatres handled games and performances, while markets served buyers and sellers. To relieve pressure on the original forum, new *fora* or *agorae* were established, such as the imperial fora in Rome, the most splendid of which was that of Trajan.

The basilica served a similar purpose to these open spaces but functioned as a covered forum. It provided merchants with shelter and quiet for business transactions and served as a location for legal tribunals. Magistrates presiding over these tribunals sometimes used permanent curved or rectangular platforms.

## Architectural Form
While there is variety among existing remains, evidence suggests a "normal form" for the civil basilica. This typically consisted of a rectangular space surrounded by colonnades in two stories, featuring a central space with galleries and an arrangement for clerestory lighting.

Examples of this normal scheme include the basilicas at Pompeii, the Julia, the Ulpia, and the Basilica Aemilia. However, variations existed:
* **Partial Colonnades:** In Silchester, the palace basilica of Domitian, and some North African examples, colonnades ran down the long sides but not across the ends.
* **Open Interiors:** Basilicas at Timgad and Trier (the latter a vast hall 180 ft. long and 100 ft. high) lacked interior divisions.
* **Vaulted Structures:** The Basilica of Maxentius was entirely vaulted, resembling the halls of Roman Thermae, and contained vaulted apses that may have held tribunals.

Notably, the Basilica Julia was used for the centumviral court but possessed no tribunal, and the semicircular projection of the Ulpia was distinct from the basilica itself and not a vaulted apse.

## Transition to Christian Architecture
The term "basilica" was eventually applied to the extensive buildings used for Christian worship during the era of Constantine and his successors. Early Christian buildings, appearing in the 2nd or 3rd century A.D., likely began as oblong interiors terminated by an apse. As the number of faithful increased after Constantine, side aisles were added, giving them an appearance similar to civil basilicas. This evolution is supported by the discovery at S. Saba on the Aventine, where a small early church without aisles was found beneath the floor of a later basilica.

Despite the similarities, civil and Christian basilicas differ in key respects. Early Christian basilicas in the west rarely had galleries over the side aisles and were always dominated by a semi-dome apse terminating the central nave. In contrast, there is no known instance of a vaulted apse in a columned civil basilica of the normal kind, with the possible and doubtful exception of Silchester.
