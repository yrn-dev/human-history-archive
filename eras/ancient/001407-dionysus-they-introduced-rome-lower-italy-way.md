# Dionysus

Dionysus, also known as Bacchus or Iacchus, is a deity associated with wine, generation, and production. His worship and the rites associated with him had a profound influence on the religious and cultural developments of Greece and Rome, particularly in the evolution of the dramatic arts and the establishment of mystic festivals.

## Origins and Greek Worship
The worship of Dionysus was introduced into Greece by the Phoenicians. In this early form, he was revered as a tauriform sun-god, adored by worshippers with loud cries. He was also viewed as a god of production and generation, symbolized by a phallic emblem. Over time, this worship was integrated with the Dorian religion of the sun-god Apollo. Dionysus and Demeter eventually superseded or blended with the Pelasgian and Achaean divinities of the moon and sun.

The rites of Dionysus were specifically conducted in relation to his attributes as the god of wine. He was attended by satyrs, who were deified representations of his original worshippers and wore the skins of goats sacrificed to the god.

## Influence on Greek Drama
Greek drama originated from the festivals of Dionysus, which were held between the beginning of winter and the beginning of spring. These festivals featured rustic worshippers gathering around an altar to sing hymns—known as dithyrambs—detailing the sufferings or victories of the god during his travels across the earth.

The two primary branches of Greek drama emerged from these religious usages:
*   **Tragedy:** Derived from "the goat-song" (*tragos*), referring to the goat sacrificed to Dionysus before the hymn.
*   **Comedy:** Derived from "the village-song," treating the hymn as an occasion for rustic jest.

The structure of these performances evolved from a chorus leader assuming the role of the god or a messenger, to the introduction of a dialogue between the leader (*koryphaios*) and an "answerer" (*hypokrites*), an innovation attributed to the Attic Thespis around 536 B.C. Later, the satyric drama was developed by Pratinas of Phlius and Choerilus of Athens. In the tragic tradition, poets often presented a tetralogy, consisting of three serious dramas (a trilogy) and a fourth satyrical drama featuring the companions of Dionysus.

## The Bacchanalia in Rome
In Rome, the festivals of Dionysus were known as the Bacchanalia. These wild and mystic rites were introduced from lower Italy via Etruria. Initially, the Bacchanalia were held in secret near the Aventine hill in the grove of Simila (also referred to as *Semele* or *Stimula*). These early celebrations occurred three days a year and were attended exclusively by women. Eventually, the rites were extended to men, and the frequency of celebrations increased to five times per month.

The festivals developed an evil reputation due to perceived political conspiracies, crimes, and gross debaucheries. This led the senate to issue the *Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus* in 186 B.C. This decree, recorded on a bronze tablet discovered in Calabria in 1640, prohibited the Bacchanalia throughout Italy, except in specific cases where the senate granted restricted permission. Despite state investigations and severe punishments, the rites persisted for a long time, particularly in southern Italy.

## Traditions and Legends
Various traditions associate Dionysus with specific geographical locations and individuals:
*   **Nysa:** In Bajour, at the foot of a mountain spur from the Kunar range, Alexander found the city of Nysa and a Nysaean colony. Tradition states these were founded by Dionysus. The three-peaked mountain Koh-i-Mor has been identified as the Meros, the site from which the god issued. Some members of the Kafir community of Kamdesh continue to chant hymns to the god who sprang from Gir Nysa.
*   **Spartacus:** During the insurrection of the gladiators, a woman of the same tribe as Spartacus—who was skilled in divination and possessed by the mysterious rites of Dionysus—interpreted a sign. She observed a snake folded over the sleeping Spartacus's face and declared it a sign of a formidable power that would lead him to a happy termination.
