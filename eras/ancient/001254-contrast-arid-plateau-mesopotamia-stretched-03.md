# The Alluvial Plain of Chaldaea and the Mesopotamian Plateau

## Geography of the Region
The geography of western Asia, specifically the district enclosed between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers, forms a single country that was recognized by writers of antiquity as Assyria, though Babylonia is noted as a more accurate designation. This region is divided into two primary geographical sections: a northern area that is more or less mountainous and a southern area characterized as flat and marshy. The transition between these two occurs where the undulating plateau of the north sinks suddenly into the Babylonian alluvium.

In contrast with the arid plateau of Mesopotamia, the rich alluvial plain of Chaldaea stretched across the south. This plain was formed by the deposits of the two great rivers that enclosed it. The soil of Chaldaea was extremely fertile and supported an industrious population.

## Borders and Landmarks
The alluvial plain of Chaldaea was bordered by various geographical features and ethnic groups. To the east rose the mountains of Elam. To the south were the sea-marshes, inhabited by other Aramaic tribes and the Chaldaeans (or Kald). To the west, the civilization of Babylonia extended beyond the banks of the Euphrates into the territory of the Suti, who were Semitic nomads.

Several significant cities occupied this landscape. Ur (more correctly called Muqayyar) served as the country's earliest capital. Babylon, along with its suburb Borsippa (Birs Nimrud) and the two Sipparas (referred to in Scripture as the Sepharvaim and now known as Abu Habba), were situated on both the Chaldaean and Arabian sides of the river.

## Water Systems and Marshes
The Arakhtu, known as the "river of Babylon," flowed past the southern side of the city of Babylon. To the south-west of the city, on the Arabian bank, lay the freshwater inland sea of Nejef. This sea was surrounded by red sandstone cliffs of considerable height, measuring 35 meters in breadth and 40 meters in length at its widest point.

Extending from Borsippa to Kufa, above and below the sea of Nejef, are the famous Chaldaean marshes. These marshes are dependent upon the state of the Hindiya canal; they disappear entirely if the canal is closed. Historical accounts note that Alexander was nearly lost in these marshes.

## The Northern Region and Mesopotamia
In the earliest recorded times, the northern portion of the district was included in Mesopotamia, only becoming definitely marked off as Assyria following the rise of the Assyrian monarchy. The chief cities of this northern region—Calah, Nineveh, and Arbela—were all located on the left bank of the Tigris, with the exception of the original capital, Assur. This preference for the eastern bank was due to the abundant supply of water.

Conversely, the great Mesopotamian plain on the western side, known today as El-Jezireh, relied on streams flowing into the Euphrates. This vast flat is approximately 250 miles long. It is interrupted by a single limestone range that branches off from the Zagros mountains under the names of Hamrin, Sarazur, and Sinjar. While now largely a wilderness, remains of old habitations indicate that this level tract was once thickly peopled.

Beyond the plateau to the north lies a well-watered, undulating belt of country. This area contains low limestone hills that are sometimes covered with dwarf-oak and sometimes arid. These hills often enclose fertile valleys and rich plains between their north-eastern flank and the main mountain line. The region is ultimately bounded by the massive ridges of the Zagros and Niphates ranges, where the Euphrates and Tigris rivers originate, separating Assyria from Kurdistan and Armenia.
