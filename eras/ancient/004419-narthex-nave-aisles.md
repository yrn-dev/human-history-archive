# The Basilican Church

The basilican type of early Christian church is characterized by a specific architectural layout consisting of a central nave and flanking aisles, typically terminating in an apse. This structural form was utilized across various regions, including Rome, Ravenna, Constantinople, and Roman Africa, with some examples also found in Britain.

## General Layout and Structure
The interior of a basilica typically featured a long and wide nave, which could reach widths of up to 80 feet. This central space was separated from the side aisles by colonnades of marble pillars. These pillars supported either horizontal entablatures, as seen in S. Maria Maggiore, S. Lorenzo, and Old St Peter's, or arches, as found in S. Clemente, S. Agnese, and the basilicas of S. Apollinare at Ravenna. 

Above these colonnades, the clerestory wall rose to a significant height, featuring a range of plain round-headed windows. The area between these windows and the colonnade was often decorated with mosaic pictures in panels. While some basilicas had colonnades that extended to the end of the building, others ceased shortly before the end to allow for a transverse aisle or transept, a feature present in Old St Peter's, St Paul's, and St John Lateran.

## The Narthex and Entrance
The basilican plan generally included a porch known as a narthex at one end. In early examples prior to approximately A.D. 420, the narthex was often located at the east end, with the apse situated at the west. In these instances, a laver for the ablutions of intending worshippers was typically located near the porch. 

The exterior of these buildings was generally plain, consisting of vast brick walls. However, the entrance front occasionally received decoration through painted stucco or marble mosaic plates, as seen at S. Lorenzo and Old St Peter's. Some churches, such as the Cathedral of Parenzo in Istria, retained a cloistered atrium and a baptistery—the latter being square on the outside and octagonal within.

## The Apse and Sanctuary
The church always terminated in a semicircular apse. The arch spanning this apse was designated the "arch of triumph" from Carolingian times due to the suspension of a cross from it. The semi-dome, or conch, of the apse was typically adorned with mosaic pictures of the Lord, often accompanied by St Paul, St Peter, and other saints.

The altar stood on a raised platform at the center of the apse's chord, often rendered conspicuous by a ciborium or baldacchino (a lofty canopy supported by marble pillars). Beneath the altar was the confessio, a subterranean chapel containing relics and the body of the patron saint, accessible via steps from the aisles or nave. 

The sanctuary area also included:
* **The Bema:** A raised platform at the foot of the altar steps that served as a choir for readers, singers, and inferior clergy.
* **Cancelli:** Low marble railings or walls that separated the choir from the western nave and aisles.
* **Ambones:** Marble pulpits with desks that projected from the cancelli.
* **The Bishop's Throne:** Located in the center of the apse's semicircle.

## Regional Variations and Galleries
A primary distinction between Roman and Eastern basilicas is the use of galleries. While rare in the West, galleries were a characteristic feature of Eastern churches, such as the 5th-century church of St John Studios in Constantinople. These galleries were intended for women to provide greater privacy.

In Rome, galleries are found in the basilicas of S. Agnese and S. Lorenzo fuori le Mura, though it is questioned whether they were part of the original designs. S. Lorenzo originally consisted of a short nave of six bays and an internal narthex that spanned the full height of the building before it was altered in the 13th century by Honorius III.

## Examples of Basilican Churches
* **St Paul's, Rome:** Dedicated in 324 A.D. and rebuilt between 388 and 423, it featured a narthex, nave, side aisles, bema, and apse.
* **St John Lateran:** A modernized five-aisled basilica featuring a baptistery and a unique double-vaulted aisle running around the apse.
* **Parenzo, Istria (c. A.D. 542):** A small basilica (121 ft. by 32 ft.) that retains its atrium, narthex, and a bishop's throne and presbyter bench in the apse.
* **S. Sabina, Rome (c. A.D. 430):** Noted for preserving a primitive aspect, including an open roof and carved wooden doors.
* **British Example:** A small early Christian church (42 ft. by 27 ft.) consisting of a nave and two aisles, ending in a porch and an apse with two flanking chambers.
