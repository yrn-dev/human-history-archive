# Bicester

Bicester is a market town located in England within the Woodstock parliamentary division of Oxfordshire. It is situated 12 miles north-north-east of Oxford, accessible via a branch of the London & North-Western railway. The town is positioned in a pastoral region near the northern edge of the flat open plain known as Ot Moor. As of 1901, the population of the urban district was 3,023.

## History and Development
According to the Domesday survey, Bicester was held by Robert d'Oily. In 1182, Gilbert Basset founded an Augustinian priory in the town. This institution served as the center for Bicester's industrial life and development from its founding until its dissolution in 1538. Today, scanty remains of the Perpendicular style are visible, incorporated into a farm-house.

The town's economic history is characterized by trade and markets rather than significant manufactures. In 1253, William Longspey was granted a fair to be held at the feast of St Edburg. By the 14th century, a Friday market was established. Richard II later granted a Monday market and a fair held at the feast of St James the Apostle. In 1440, an additional market was granted for the area of the town known as Bury-End, which became known as Market-End from that date forward. During the 16th century, the cattle market in Bicester was especially famous. In the modern era, the town maintains a brewing industry and considerable agricultural trade, and it is regarded as a favorite center for hunting.

## Architecture and Landmarks
The town's church, dedicated to St Eadburg (the virgin of Aylesbury), features a cruciform design with a western tower. The building contains architectural examples from the Norman period and every succeeding style. Notably, the nave contains a single rude angular arch that is considered to be of Saxon origin.

## Roman Influence and Infrastructure
While the termination "-cester" often indicates a Roman origin, this is not the case for Bicester itself. The name is perhaps copied from nearby locations such as Chesterton and Alchester. Chesterton, located 2 miles west of Bicester, contains a small Roman site that was likely a wayside village. This site served as a meeting point for roads coming from the east, north-east, west, and south (from Dorchester).

Bicester is linked to the broader Roman road network of Britain through Alchester, which lies outside the town. An obscure road known to the Saxons as Akeman Street provided alternative access from London, passing through Alchester to reach Bath.

This road was part of a wider system of communications provided by the Romans throughout the lowlands of Britain. This system included four main groups of roads radiating from London and a fifth oblique route. The roads connected London to various key destinations:
*   **South-east:** To Canterbury and the Kentish ports, specifically the frequented port of Richborough (Rutupiae).
*   **West:** To Silchester, with branches extending to Gloucester, Exeter, Bath, Winchester, and South Wales.
*   **North-west (Watling Street):** Running via St Albans Wall and near Lichfield (Letocetum) to Chester and Wroxeter, with branches to Lincoln and Leicester.
*   **East/North:** Serving York, Lincoln, and the eastern counties, including Colchester.
*   **Oblique (The Fosse):** Joining Leicester and Lincoln with Bath, Exeter, and Cirencester.

Additionally, another obscure road connected the Humber with the lower Severn, winding south from near Sheffield and passing through Birmingham and Derby.
