# The Walls and Dimensions of Babylon

The city of Babylon was constructed on both sides of the river in a square configuration. According to the accounts of Herodotus and Ctesias, the city was enclosed by a double row of lofty walls, though Ctesias specifies the existence of a third wall.

## Circumference and Area
Historical sources provide varying estimates regarding the circumference of the outermost wall. Herodotus recorded the measurement as 480 stades (56 m.), which would encompass an area of approximately 200 sq. m. In contrast, Ctesias estimated the circumference at 360 stades (42 m.), a figure that would imply an area of about 100 sq. m. assuming the stade is of its usual length.

Other sources provide figures similar to those of Ctesias:
*   **Quintus Curtius (v. 1. 26):** 368 stades.
*   **Clitarchus (ap. Diod. Sic. ii. 7):** 365 stades.
*   **Strabo (xvi. 1. 5):** 385 stades.

## Wall Structure and Dimensions
The height and width of the walls are also subject to differing reports. Herodotus stated that the walls reached a height of about 335 ft. and a width of 85 ft., while Ctesias estimated the height at approximately 300 ft. While these measurements may seem exaggerated, evidence from the time of Xenophon indicates that the ruined wall of Nineveh remained 150 ft. high.

The walls of Babylon featured 250 towers. The spaces between these towers were sufficiently broad to allow a four-horse chariot to turn. The bricks used for the walls were manufactured from clay excavated from the city's moat.

## Gates and Enclosures
The city's fortifications included 100 gates, all constructed of bronze with bronze posts and lintels. The two inner enclosures were decorated with enamelled tiles depicting hunting scenes.

Additionally, two walls were situated along the quays and banks of the Euphrates. Each of these walls contained 25 gates, a number that corresponded to the number of streets into which the gates led.

## City Connectivity and Transport
The two parts of the city were joined by a movable drawbridge, 30 ft. broad, which was supported on stone piers. Furthermore, ferry-boats operated between the landing-places of the gates to facilitate movement.
