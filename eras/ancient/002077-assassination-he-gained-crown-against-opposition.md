# Succession and Assassination in World History

The transmission of power and the acquisition of crowns have frequently been marked by political opposition, familial conflict, and violent intervention. Historical records across various empires and nations detail the diverse methods by which rulers ascended to power or were removed from it.

## Persian Dynastic Struggles
In the Persian Empire, the transition of the sceptre was often characterized by "bloody tragedies." One such instance involved Artaxerxes and his eldest son, Darius. Although Artaxerxes had declared Darius his successor, a conflict arose when Darius requested a favorite inmate of the king's harem. This request displeased Artaxerxes, leading the king to consider appointing his younger son, Ochus, as successor instead. In response, Darius was persuaded by a courtier named Teribazus to plot the assassination of Artaxerxes. The plot was betrayed, resulting in the execution of both Darius and Teribazus.

Ochus subsequently secured his path to the crown by removing two older brothers: one through a "treacherous deceit" involving poison and the other via assassination. Following the death of the elderly Artaxerxes, Ochus succeeded him without opposition, taking the name Artaxerxes.

In a later period, Bahram V (420-439), the son of Yazdegerd I, gained the crown following the sudden death or assassination of his father. Bahram V ascended the throne despite opposition from the grandees, achieving power with the assistance of al-Mondhir, the Arabic dynast of Hira. While Persian tradition remembers Bahram V (also called Bahram Gor, "the wild ass") for his beauty, valour, and victories over the Turks, Indians, Negroes, and Romans, other accounts suggest he was a weak monarch who catered to the priests and grandees.

## Political Violence in Japan
Japanese history records numerous instances of assassination and violent removal from power. During the periods of military conflict and political maneuvering, figures such as Noriyori were assassinated after commanding forces at Ichino-tani and blocking the Taira from withdrawing into Kyushu. Similarly, Yoriiye, who served as shogun in 1202 and as lord high constable and chief landsteward, was killed by Tokimasa.

Other recorded attempts and acts of assassination include:
*   **Fujiwara Tanetsugu:** Assassinated by Prince Sagara during the reign of Emperor Kwammu.
*   **Hojo Suketomo:** Assassinated by Homma Saburo.
*   **Tanuma Okitsugu:** Targeted in an assassination attempt by Sano Masakoto.
*   **Sanetomo:** An attempt was made to assassinate him.

## European Sovereignty and Conflict
In the Netherlands, the struggle against Spanish rule led to the assassination of the Prince of Orange. After the Prince offered the crown to the Duke of Anjou (the brother of French King Henry III) and proclaimed the forfeiture of Philip II's sovereignty over the Netherlands, Philip II declared the Prince an outlaw. In 1584, Balthazar Gérard assassinated the Prince of Orange at Delft. Despite this, the Prince is remembered as the "Father of the Fatherland" for initiating the United Provinces of Holland.

In France, the succession of Louis III (c. 863-882) and his brother Carloman in April 879 was met with doubt regarding their legitimacy because the emperor Charles the Bald had not recognized their parents' marriage. This led a strong party to propose offering the crown to Louis, the East Frankish ruler and son of Louis the German. This proposal failed, and the brothers were crowned in September 879 before dividing the kingdom.

## Other Forms of Recognition and Opposition
Not all "crowns" mentioned in the archives refer to sovereign rule. In Athens, Demosthenes was crowned with public proclamation at the Dionysiac festival of March 338 B.C. as a result of his role as the chief adviser of the Theban alliance.

In other contexts, opposition to the crown took the form of parliamentary obstruction. In Hungary, the "crown" faced obstruction from Clericals and extreme Independents who sought the Magyar words of command. This political struggle involved the "guillotine" motions of Count Tisza and eventually led to the Opposition invading and wrecking the Chamber on December 13, 1904.
