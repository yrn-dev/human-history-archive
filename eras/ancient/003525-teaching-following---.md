# The Nature and Transmission of Teachings

Across human history, the transmission of knowledge and moral guidance has occurred through diverse methods, ranging from oral traditions and personal mentorship to the development of formal doctrines and written codes. These teachings have often intersected, influenced one another, or diverged sharply in their vision of human conduct.

## Mentorship and Knowledge in the Qur'an
The archive's records of the Qur'an describe specific instances of the pursuit of knowledge and the dangers of forbidden teachings. In one account, Moses encounters a servant of God who had been granted mercy and taught knowledge from God. Moses requests to follow this individual so that he may be taught "right conduct" or "right knowledge" of the truths the servant had been taught. The servant warns Moses that he will be unable to have patience, questioning how one can have patience regarding matters for which their understanding is not complete. Moses asserts that, if Allah wills, he will be patient and will not disobey. The servant agrees on the condition that Moses asks no questions about anything until the servant speaks to him concerning it.

Conversely, the texts describe the teaching of magic. It is noted that "evil ones" or devils falsely attributed the teaching of magic to the kingdom of Solomon. This knowledge, which came down at Babylon to the angels Harut and Marut, was taught only after the angels warned that they were a "trial" or "temptation" and commanded students not to blaspheme or disbelieve in the guidance of Allah. Those who learned these arts used them to sow discord between husbands and wives. The texts emphasize that such knowledge harmed the learners rather than profiting them, and that those who trafficked in magic would have no share in the happiness of the Hereafter.

## The Teachings of Jesus and the Development of Christianity
The teachings of Jesus are characterized in the archive as focusing on the "Kingdom of Heaven," the "Kingdom," and the "new birth of the human soul," emphasizing charity, service, and brotherhood. While these teachings were revolutionary and difficult for respectable citizens—such as priests, traders, or imperial officials—to accept without changing their lives, they are noted as being compatible with the teachings of Gautama Sakya.

However, a distinction is made between the original teachings of Jesus and the later dogmas of the Christian church. While the gospels contain Jesus's personality and teachings, the broad lines of Christian belief were laid down in the epistles by his followers, most notably St. Paul. Paul, who had been a persecutor of the disciples before his conversion, was well-versed in Judaism, Mithraism, and the Alexandrian religion. He incorporated ideas and terms from these sources into Christianity. 

Specifically, Paul introduced the concept of a "sacrificial person" offered to God as atonement for sin, viewing Jesus as a sacrifice for the redemption of mankind, similar to the "Easter lamb" or traditional victims of primordial civilizations. This differed from Jesus's own focus. By the fourth century, the simpler teachings of Jesus were often neglected in favor of complex arguments regarding whether Jesus was identical to or separate from God.

## Comparative Religious Influence and Ritual
Religions flourishing side by side often adopt each other's outward peculiarities. For example, Buddhism in China adopted temples and priests similar to Taoism, despite the original teachings of the two being almost flatly opposed. Similarly, Christianity adopted devotional phrases, theological ideas, and formal elements—such as candles, altars, chanting, images, and shaven priests—from the Mithraic and Alexandrian faiths.

Regarding ritual, the *Teaching of the Apostles* (c. 90-120) provides instructions on baptism. It commands that baptism be performed into the name of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit using "living water." If living water is unavailable, other water may be used, and if cold water is unavailable, warm water is permitted.

## Eastern Philosophies and Educational Methods
In India, Gautama Sakya (the Buddha) and his disciples established an Academy in the Deer Park at Benares. Because writing was likely non-existent in India at the time, all teaching was conducted by word of mouth. To facilitate memory in an undocumented world, the master composed brief aphorisms, lists of "points," and pithy verses, which were then expanded upon by disciples. This led to the numerical structure of the teachings, such as the Four Truths and the Eightfold Path.

In Japan, different systems of teaching produced varying moral results. The teachings of Chutsz produced many learned men, but some were criticized as being narrow-minded and prejudiced. In contrast, the doctrines of Wang were seen as serving their moral purpose more effectively. Followers of Wang were described as "fine specimens of humanity" who were abreast of or ahead of their age. This philosophy is regarded as one of the greatest benefits Japan received from the neighboring continent, aided by the personality of Nakaye Toju, who made the teachings known.
