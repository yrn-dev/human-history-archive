# Roman Assimilation of Basque Deities

## Pre-Christian Basque Religion
Knowledge regarding the religious practices of the Basques prior to the introduction of Christianity is limited. Available records indicate a belief system centered on the worship of the elements, specifically the moon, the sun, and the morning star. Additionally, there was a belief in the immortality of the body, provided it remained unburied and unburnt.

The Basques used the terms *Jaincoa* or *Jaungoikoa* to refer to the Deity. These terms may translate to "master" or "lord," or "Lord of the high." In the Roncal dialect, the word *Goikoa* refers to the moon, which would render *Jaungoikokoa* as "Lord of the moon." The term *Jaun* (lord or master) was also used in a secular context, such as *Etcheko Jauna*, meaning the lord or master of the house, a title applied to every householder.

## Roman Influence and Assimilation
During the period of Roman presence, the Romans assimilated local Basque deities into their own pantheon. This process of identification is evidenced in Latin inscriptions, which mention deities such as *Herauscorrtsehe* and Deo *Baicorrixo* (associated with Baigorry).

This practice of assimilation was a broader trend observed in various empires. As political institutions evolved, religious beliefs often followed; men tended to assimilate the bodily forms and lives of gods to their own. In the era of great Greek-speaking empires under autocratic monarchs, there was a shift away from purely local, tribal, or city deities toward gods with a wider outlook. This led to a process where diverse deities were identified as being similar or as different names for a single god. For example, the Roman Jupiter, the Babylonian Bel-Marduk, the Greek Zeus, and the Egyptian Ammon were all identified as sufficiently similar.

## Related Archaeological and Cultural Findings
In northern Spain, ancient sepulchres and monuments—including the *toros de guisando* (stone boars or bulls)—contain Iberian inscriptions. Some figures found at the *Cerro de los Santos* in Murcia recall the physical type of modern Basques, though they are found alongside figures of varied types. Regarding other customs, Strabo attributed the practice of the *couvade* to the Cantabri, but this custom is unknown among modern Basques.

## Municipal and Legal Legacy
The influence of Rome extended beyond religion into the municipal institutions of the Basques. They retained the old Roman term *respublica* to describe the *civitas* and its accompanying territory. This foundation contributed to a long-standing habit of self-government and the development of the *fueros* (or *fors*), which were codes of written or unwritten customary laws.

These *fueros* allowed the Basque provinces to exist as a "land apart" (*una tierra apartada*) or a self-governing republic (*una verdadera autonomia*) even under absolute monarchies. The kings of Spain, Navarre, and Castile were required to swear to observe these laws upon their accession. This autonomy remained largely unchallenged until 1833.
