# Religion of Babylonia and Assyria

## Relationship Between Babylonian and Assyrian Traditions
The empires of Babylonia and Assyria, situated along the Tigris and Euphrates, shared a deeply intertwined history, race, and language. While classical accounts often prioritize Assyria, deciphered inscriptions indicate that Babylonia served as the mother country for much of the region. Consequently, the Assyrians derived their culture, literature, arts, sciences, and religion from their southern neighbors in Babylonia.

Despite these shared foundations, the Assyrians maintained a distinct individuality that manifested in their religious and artistic expressions. This distinction mirrored the different characters of the two states: Babylonia was a land of agriculturists and merchants governed by a powerful hierarchy of priests, whereas Assyria functioned as an organized military camp led by autocratic generals.

## The National God of Assyria
A central element of Assyrian religious life was the god Assur. The primitive capital of Assyria, Assur, grew around a great temple dedicated to this deity, the foundation of which was attributed to the High-priest Uspia. There is scholarly discussion regarding whether the national god took his name from the city or if the city was named after the god; however, it is considered most probable that the city itself was deified.

In terms of character, Assur functioned as a national god. This role is described as being almost identical to the character of the early Yahweh of the sons of Israel. For many centuries, the land of Assur (Assyria) was governed by high-priests under the suzerainty of Babylonia before these priests eventually established themselves as independent kings.

## Religious Infrastructure and Influence
In Assyria, the religious landscape was characterized by temples and lofty towers, particularly in the northern parts of the capital city. Notably, the Assyrian king's palace was separate from the temples of the gods and was more sumptuous than the temples themselves.

While Assyria eventually became the chief power and suzerain of Babylon during the 9th and 8th centuries B.C., the religious influence of Babylonia remained potent. Even after the destruction of Nineveh and the collapse of the Assyrian military power in 606 B.C., Babylon continued to exert a significant influence on religion and culture for many centuries following the Persian conquest.
