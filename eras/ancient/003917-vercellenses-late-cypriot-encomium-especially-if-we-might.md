# Barnabas and Related Historical Records

## Life and Mission of Barnabas
Barnabas was a figure whose early activities included sailing to Cyprus with Mark to resume mission work. While much of his subsequent history is obscured, certain records provide glimpses of his principles and reputation. According to 1 Cor. ix. 6, Barnabas followed the principle of self-support during his missionary efforts. By approximately A.D. 60, his name was respected and well known at Colossae, as noted in Col. iv. 10.

Tradition, including the writings of Clem. Alex., suggests that Barnabas was among the seventy. Further traditions, such as those found in _Clem. Hom._ i. 8 and ii. 4, place him in Alexandria, a connection supported by the ascription of the Alexandrine _Epistle of Barnabas_ to him.

## Evidence of Travels and Authorship
There is varied evidence suggesting that Barnabas visited Rome, and later tradition mentions a visit to Milan. These claims are supported by sources including _Clem. Recog._ i. 7, _Hom._ i. 7, the late Cypriot _Encomium_, and the early _Actus Petri Vercellenses_. 

Some Western traditions, beginning with Tertullian in _De Pud._ 20, ascribe the epistle to the Hebrews to Barnabas. This ascription may be an inference based on the text's self-description in xiii. 22 as a "word of exhortation," linking it to the "son of exhortation" described in Acts iv. 36.

## Death and Later Legends
The date of Barnabas's death remains uncertain, though it is probable that he was no longer living when the book of Acts was written (c. A.D. 75-80). While legends exist regarding his missionary labors in Cyprus and a martyrdom at Salamis, these accounts are regarded as untrustworthy and late.

## Historical Context of Cyprus
Cyprus experienced significant shifts in possession and population during the sixth century B.C. During this time, the Phil-hellenic Amasis acquired possession of the island, leading to an extension of Grecian commerce with Egypt. This period saw a probable increase in Grecian immigration to Cyprus, including a group of settlers invited to Soli by Philokyprus with the assistance of the Athenian Solon.

The Cypriots of this era exhibited a blend of influences; Herodotus describes their arms and costume as half Oriental. In battle, the Salaminians utilized chariots of war, a practice also seen among the Carthaginians before they adopted the training of elephants.
