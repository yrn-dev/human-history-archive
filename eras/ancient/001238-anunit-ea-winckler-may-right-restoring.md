# Anunit and A[=e] (or Ea)

## Early Babylonian Sanctuaries
The earliest known mention of Babylon is found in a dated tablet from the reign of Sargon of Akkad, dated to 3800 B.C. According to this record, Sargon built sanctuaries in Babylon dedicated to the deities Anunit and A[=e] (also referred to as Ea). 

## The Foundation of Babylon
There is scholarly discussion regarding the origin of the city's name. H. Winckler suggested a restoration of a mutilated passage in the annals of Sargon of Akkad, which would indicate that Babylon owed its name to Sargon himself, who established the city as the capital of his empire. If this interpretation is correct, the city later reverted to the status of a provincial town for several centuries before ascending again to become the capital of the "first dynasty of Babylon" and subsequently the empire of Khammurabi around 2250 B.C. From that period onward, Babylon served as the capital of Babylonia and was regarded as the holy city of western Asia.

## Anunit and the City of Sippara
Beyond the sanctuaries built by Sargon, the goddess Anunit is associated with the city of Sippara. Archaeological explorations in Babylonia, specifically those conducted by Hormuzd Rassam between 1880 and 1881, helped fix the position of the temple of the sun-god of Sippara at Abu-Habba. This site is located south-west of Bagdad, positioned midway between the Tigris and Euphrates rivers on the south side of a canal. The Sippara associated with the goddess Anunit is located on the opposite bank of this canal and is known today as D[=e]r.

## Political and Religious Significance of Babylon
Because of its status as a holy city, possession of Babylon was essential for any claimant seeking legitimate supremacy in Asia. To be accepted as the inheritor of the old Babylonian empire and as an adopted son of Bel-Merodach, a ruler had to undergo the ceremony of "taking the hands" of Bel-Merodach at Babylon. 

This requirement drove the ambitions of various Assyrian kings, including Tiglath-pileser III. However, not all succeeded in gaining the necessary religious legitimacy. Sennacherib failed to secure the support of the Babylonian priesthood and never performed the ceremony. Consequently, Babylonia remained in a state of constant revolt during his reign, which ended only when Sennacherib razed the capital's temples, palaces, and walls to the ground in 689 B.C., throwing the debris into the Arakhtu canal. This destruction was viewed as a shock to the religious conscience of western Asia, and the subsequent murder of Sennacherib was considered an expiation for the act. 

His successor, Esar-haddon, worked to rebuild the city so that he could receive his crown there and maintain it as a residence for part of the year. Later, during the reign of Assur-bani-pal, the city was again besieged and starved into surrender following a revolt led by Samas-sum-yukin. While Assur-bani-pal purified the city and performed a "service of reconciliation," he did not venture to "take the hands" of Bel.
