# Frigidarium

The frigidarium is a cold room specifically designed and fitted with a cold bath. Its primary purpose within a bathing sequence is for use at the end of the bathing ceremony.

## Use in Public Baths
In the context of ancient town baths, such as those found at Silchester, the frigidarium was part of a larger suite of public facilities located east of the forum. The layout of these baths typically began with a peristyle court used by loungers and a latrine. From there, the bather would enter the apodyterium, which served as a dressing room, before proceeding to the frigidarium. Following the use of the cold room, the bather would enter a series of hot rooms. This overall structure and sequence of bathing are noted to resemble many modern Turkish baths. In their initial form, the baths at Silchester measured approximately 80 ft. by 160 ft., though they underwent considerable extension at a later date.

## Roman Bathing Customs
Bathing habits in Rome evolved over time. Early Roman customs included a level of modesty regarding nudity; for example, it was customary for fathers not to bathe with their sons, and fathers-in-law often avoided bathing naked in the presence of their sons-in-law. However, the Romans later adopted the Greek habit of bathing naked, a practice they eventually extended to bathing in the presence of women.

## Other Bathing and Water Facilities
Beyond the structured public baths of the town, other forms of bathing and water-based treatments existed across different regions and eras:

*   **Private Facilities:** In Roman Britain, private houses varied in design, often consisting of rows of rooms with corridors. Some larger houses, such as those in Silchester, included a special annexe dedicated to baths.
*   **Mineral and Mud Baths:** In Marienbad, a town in Bohemia, Austria, mineral springs are used for both drinking and bathing. Some springs, such as the Ferdinandsbrunnen and Kreuzbrunnen, provide cold alkaline-saline waters. Other treatments available in the town include mud-baths (or moor-baths) utilizing peat that is described as the richest in iron in the world.
*   **Military Accommodations:** In modern British barracks for infantry battalions, ablution rooms containing foot-baths and basins are located conveniently near the barrack-rooms. General baths are typically grouped in a central bath-house situated adjacent to the cook-house, featuring the provision of hot water.
