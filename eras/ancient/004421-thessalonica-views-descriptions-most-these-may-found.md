# Thessalonica

Thessalonica (also referred to as Saloniki) is a city located in Macedonia on the Thermaic Gulf. Historically, it served as a significant administrative and strategic center within the Roman and Byzantine spheres of influence.

## Roman Period and Infrastructure
Thessalonica was a key destination on the Via Egnatia, a road that extended eastward from Dyrrachium. During the Roman era, the city served as a location for high-ranking officials and political figures. For example, Cicero traveled to Thessalonica in Macedonia to visit Cn. Plancius, who was serving as quæstor to L. Apuleius, the Prætor of Macedonia. Records indicate Cicero arrived in the city on May 23rd and wrote a letter to Atticus from there on May 29th.

The city also played a role in the Roman civil wars. Pompeius spent a winter at Thessalonica with two hundred senators. During this time, the Senate continued the consuls, prætors, and quæstors of 49 B.C. for the year 48 B.C., designating them as Proquæstors, Proprætors, and Proconsuls. This was necessitated by the party of Pompeius being unable to appoint new magistrates due to the lack of a Lex Curiata ceremony.

In later Roman history, Thessalonica was one of the occasional residences used by Emperor Constantine to inspect the provinces or exercise the legions. It was also listed as one of the principal cities of the empire—alongside others such as Milan, Treves, Alexandria, and Carthage—that received circular epistles during a revolution intended to restore the ancient dignity of the Roman senate. Additionally, some writers note that the emperor Theodosius experienced a period of illness and long repose at Thessalonica.

## Byzantine Architecture and Religious Sites
The city is home to the church of St Demetrius, described as a very noble basilican church. This structure is noted alongside other significant Eastern basilicas, such as those at Mt. Sinai and Athens. Detailed views and descriptions of the church of St Demetrius can be found in the works of the count de Vogue, Couchaud's *Choix d'eglises byzantines*, and Pullan and Texier's *Byzantine Architecture*.

## The Fourth Crusade and the Latin Empire
During the early 13th century, Thessalonica became a focal point of conflict following the fourth crusade and the capture of Constantinople. Baldwin I, the emperor of Romania, sought to secure Thessalonica and break Greek resistance in Thrace during the summer of 1204. This objective led to a collision between Baldwin and Boniface of Montferrat.

Boniface, a rival candidate for the empire, was designated to receive a large territory in Macedonia and hold the title of king of Saloniki. Seeking independence from the empire and wishing to avoid doing homage, Boniface opposed Baldwin's march to the city. The dispute was further aggravated by tensions between Lombards and Flemings. Baldwin's insistence on proceeding to Thessalonica, coupled with Boniface's siege of Hadrianople, brought the parties to the brink of civil war.

The conflict was eventually resolved through the mediation of the count of Blois and Dandolo. Under the resulting agreement, Boniface was appointed commander of the forces marching to conquer Greece and received Thessalonica as a fief from the emperor.
