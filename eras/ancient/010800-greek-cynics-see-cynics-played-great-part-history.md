# The Greek Cynics

The Greek Cynics were a philosophical group that played a significant role in the history of asceticism. Characterized by their individualism and rejection of traditional societal norms, they served as precursors to later Christian hermits and monks. Their influence was such that descriptions of the Cynics in profane literature have sometimes been mistaken for accounts of early monasticism.

## Philosophy and Lifestyle
The Cynics sought to imitate the independence and rugged strength of their master, Socrates, though they often pursued this goal to extremes that resulted in a caricature of him. Unlike the perspectives of earlier thinkers such as Aristotle and Plato, who viewed the ideal life as that of a householder and citizen, the Cynics identified as citizens of the world. They maintained no loyalty or respect for the ancient city-state, and their rise coincided with the decay of those states.

In practice, the Cynics affected the lifestyle of beggars. They renounced riches, honors, and pleasures, owning nothing and carrying only a wallet and a staff. Their commitment to renunciation was extensive; beyond property and marriage, they often renounced good manners, knowledge, and cleanliness.

## Influence and Legacy
The Cynics contributed to the development of asceticism—the practice of self-mortification and bodily abstinence. They, along with the Neo-pythagoreans, utilized technical terms derived from athletic contests and the palaestra (wrestling-place) as metaphors for philosophic asceticism. These terms were later adopted by Christians and Greek Jews.

In the realm of public protest, Greek philosophy condemned gladiatorial shows. In an act of protest against these events, two Cynics gave their lives in the arena before the shows were eventually abolished. In later historical comparisons, some modern writers, such as La Mothe le Vayer and Lipsius, have compared the Cynics to the Capucins.

## Notable Figures and Associated Writers
The archive records several individuals associated with Cynic philosophy:

*   **Bion of Borysthenes:** A Greek moralist and philosopher who flourished in the first half of the 3rd century B.C. Born to a salt-fish dealer and a courtesan, Bion was sold as a slave to a rhetorician who later freed him and made him his heir. After studying at the Academy, the Peripatetics, and the Cyrenaics, Bion attached himself to the Cynics. He traveled through Macedonia and Greece, was admitted to the literary circle of Antigonus Gonatas, and taught at Rhodes. In his *Diatribae*, Bion used caustic wit to satirize human folly, attacking the wealthy, astrologers, geometricians, musicians, and the gods, while eulogizing philosophy and poverty.
*   **Teles:** A Cynic philosopher who lived toward the end of the 3rd century B.C. and made extensive use of Bion's writings.

## Historical Interactions
The Cynics occasionally clashed with religious and political authorities. Records indicate that an Imperial pontiff composed a religious discourse specifically to censure the "bold impiety" of a Cynic.
