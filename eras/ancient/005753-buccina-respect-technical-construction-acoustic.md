# The Buccina

The buccina was a brass wind instrument used extensively by the ancient Roman army. It is characterized as the ancestor of both the trombone and the trumpet. Its technical evolution is marked by a transition from primitive organic prototypes to sophisticated metal construction, and eventually to the straight-tubed instruments of the Middle Ages.

## Technical Construction
The Roman buccina consisted of a brass tube with a narrow cylindrical bore, measuring approximately 11 to 12 feet in length. The instrument featured a cup-shaped mouthpiece, a design that differs little from those used in modern trumpets and trombones. 

The tube was bent round upon itself from the mouthpiece to the bell, forming a broad "C" shape. To provide stability during performance, the curve was strengthened by a bar across the bend, which the performer grasped. The bell of the instrument curved over the player's head or shoulder, a configuration similar to that of the modern helicon. This characteristic curve distinguishes the buccina from the tuba, which was straight, and allows for its identification in reliefs such as those on Trajan's Column.

## Acoustic Properties
The buccina is noted for its ability to produce higher harmonics more easily than the cornu. While the cornu had a wider bore and a deeper pitch with a natural scale consisting of only the first eight harmonics, the buccina's narrower bore provided a different acoustic profile. A facsimile of a buccina found in Pompeii indicates that the instrument is in G and possesses a harmonic series nearly identical to that of the trumpet and the French horn.

## Prototypes and Variations
The military buccina was a more advanced development of the *buccina marina*. The *buccina marina* was a primitive trumpet consisting of a conical shell, often featuring a spiral twist, and was referred to in poetry as the *concha*. This primitive form is frequently depicted in the hands of sailors or Tritons.

Additionally, the archive notes a connection between the buccina and the shofar of the ancient Hebrews. The shofar, a cow's horn used during the siege of Jericho, was translated as *buccina* in the Vulgate and as *buccina ex cornu* in the Chaldee paraphrase.

## Military and Social Use
In the Roman army and camp, the buccina served as a signal instrument alongside the tuba and the cornu. It was used to give orders and to sound the four night watches, which were known as *buccina prima, secunda*, and so on. A specific signal known as the *classicum* was used to summon soldiers. The importance of the *buccinator* (trumpeter) is highlighted by a stratagem recorded by Frontinus, in which a general escaped an enemy encirclement by leaving a buccinator behind to sound the watches throughout the night.

Beyond military use, the buccinator is depicted in mosaics—such as those in a Roman villa at Nennig—accompanied by the hydraulus.

## Evolution and Legacy
Following the fall of the Roman Empire, the technical skill required to bend metal tubes was gradually lost. Consequently, the buccina lost its characteristic "C" curve. During the Middle Ages, it survived in principle and name but evolved into the *busine* (or *busaun* and *posaun* in Germany), which took two primary straight forms:
1. A long straight tube of narrow cylindrical bore consisting of three to five joints, where only the final joint was conical and ended in a pommel-shaped bell.
2. A long straight cylindrical tube with a wider bore and a bell that curved out abruptly.

The derivation of the German word for trombone, *Posaune*, and the word *Sackbut* from *buccina* establishes the linguistic and technical link between the Roman instrument and its successors. This relationship was recognized in Germany during the 15th and 16th centuries, as evidenced by translations of Vegetius which described the buccina as the trumpet or the trombone ("the trumpet which is drawn out and in").
