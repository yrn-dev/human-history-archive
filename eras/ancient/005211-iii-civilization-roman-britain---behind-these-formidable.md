# The Civilization of Roman Britain

The civilization of Roman Britain developed primarily in the lowlands of eastern and southern Britain. Sheltered from barbarians by formidable garrisons and maintaining easy contact with the Roman Empire, this region underwent a process of Romanization. In areas bordering the Thames estuary, including Middlesex, Essex, and Kent, this process may have begun even before the Roman conquest.

## Development of Roman Culture
The spread of Roman culture occurred through two primary mechanisms: government initiative and automatic growth. The Roman government encouraged Romanization by establishing towns populated by Roman citizens, who were generally legionaries that had been discharged from service. These towns were granted constitutions and franchises similar to those of Italian municipalities.

Beyond official policy, the civilization grew through the enthusiasm of the Britons, who accepted Roman civilization in a manner similar to the Gauls. Led by their nobles and potentially encouraged by sympathetic Romans, the Britons began to adopt the Latin language and utilize the material resources of Roman civilized life. Over time, they shifted their identity, viewing themselves as British members of the Roman state rather than unwilling subjects of a foreign empire.

## Municipal Centers and Urbanization
The transition to a Romanized society can be traced through specific dates and locations. Shortly after the Claudian invasion, a *colonia*—a municipality for time-expired soldiers—was established in Colchester (Camulodunum), the former native capital. Although it initially functioned as a fortress and caused hatred among the Britons, it eventually became a source of civilizing influence. Simultaneously, the town of Verulamium (St Albans) was granted the status of a *municipium*, a designation that differed little from that of a *colonia* at the time.

As the movement of Romanization increased, new towns such as Silchester were established. These were laid out in the Roman fashion and equipped with public buildings of the Roman type.

## Conflict and Military Influence
The process of Romanization was not without violent opposition. During the great revolt of Boadicea in 60 AD, the nationalist party massacred thousands of Romanized Britons along with actual Romans.

Britain also served as a strategic location for the Roman Empire to manage foreign threats. Following a conflict with the Marcomanni and Quadi—barbarians who had led a general conspiracy against the Romans under Marcus Antoninus—the emperor commanded the defeated tribes to deliver the "flower of the youth." These young men were sent to Britain, a remote island, to serve as soldiers and remain secure as hostages.

## Decline and Conquest
The Roman province of Britain eventually faced collapse. While some barbarians who entered other Roman regions were half-civilized and maintained towns or adopted Latin, the groups that submerged Roman Britain—the Saxons, Angles, and Jutes—were agriculturalists. These groups had no use for towns and appeared to sweep southern Britain clear of its Romanized population. They replaced the Latin language with their own Teutonic dialects, which eventually evolved into English.
