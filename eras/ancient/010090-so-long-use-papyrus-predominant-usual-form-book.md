# The Evolution of the Book: From Papyrus to Codex

The history of the written book is characterized by a transition from rigid tablets and rolls to the bound volumes used in the modern era. This evolution was driven largely by the materials available for writing, moving from clay and papyrus to parchment and eventually paper.

## Early Writing Materials and Tablets
Before the prevalence of bound books, various civilizations developed methods for recording information. In Babylonia, as early as 2600 B.C., records were written with a stylus on small clay slabs. By 2400 B.C., these clay tablets were used for revenue accounts and legal decisions, stored in jars on shelves and identified by labels attached with straws. Some of these tablets featured impressions from cylinder seals and nail marks for identification. In the 7th century B.C., Sargan (721-705 B.C.) founded a library of literary works on clay tablets at Nineveh, some of which included catalogues. While these tablets functioned as books, they are not direct ancestors of the modern printed book.

Other early materials included palm leaves and the inside of tree bark—the latter providing the root for the word *liber* (book). Additionally, wax-covered tables were used, where characters were impressed with a stylus that was sharp at one end for writing and flat at the other for erasing. At Pompeii, diptychs were discovered consisting of wooden leaves with blackened wax interiors hinged by leather thongs; one such record from A.D. 55 is the earliest known Latin manuscript.

## Papyrus and the Volumen
Papyrus, made from the pith of a reed found chiefly in Egypt, is believed to have been used as a writing material as early as 4000 B.C. The plant consists of triangular stalks reaching six or seven cubits in height. The Greeks derived their word for book, *biblion* (the plural of which became "Bible"), from the name for papyrus, *bublos* or *biblos*.

So long as papyrus remained the predominant material, the usual form of a book was the *volumen*, or roll, wound around one or more sticks. Papyrus rolls were typically bound with papyrus tape, often sealed with Nile mud, and stored in rectangular wooden hollows. There were three primary ways to write on these rolls:
1. Short lines written at right angles to the length of the roll.
2. Long lines extending the entire length of the roll (seen in Buddhist prayer-wheels).
3. Short lines parallel to the length of the roll, with spaces left on each side.

Rolls written in the third manner are still used in the scrolls of the law in Jewish synagogues, kept on two rollers. This specific arrangement also led to the *orihon* (a Chinese and Japanese form), where the roll is folded backwards and forwards in the vacant spaces between columns, creating a link between the roll and the bound book.

## Transition to the Codex and Parchment
The modern form of the book is the *codex*, a Latin term originally referring to a block of wood or the tablets sawn from such a block. The *codex* began gaining fashion in Rome during the time of Martial, increasing in popularity as parchment superseded papyrus. Parchment was developed in the 2nd century B.C. by Eumenes II, king of Pergamus, who improved the preparation of sheep and calf skins. The word "parchment" is derived from *pergamentum*, after the kingdom of Pergamus.

The transition from *volumen* to *codex* influenced the layout of pages. Early codices mirrored the narrow columns of the unrolled *volumen*. For example, the 4th-century Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus featured four and three columns per page, respectively. By the 5th century, the Codex Alexandrinus used two columns, and by the 6th century, the Codex Bezae used only one. This single or double column arrangement remained largely unchanged until the invention of printing.

## Later Developments and Bindings
The external form of books remained conservative over centuries. An 8th-century miniature in the Codex Amiatinus, written in England, depicts a red leather cover that closely resembles 15th-century red leather bindings found in the Biblioteca Laurenziana at Florence. Similarly, some 15th-century Oxford binding stamps are nearly identical to those used in England three centuries prior.

While papyrus was the primary vehicle for writing until the early Christian era, it persisted in specific uses: it was used for pontifical bulls until A.D. 1022 and in Coptic manuscripts as late as A.D. 1250. However, papyrus was rarely used for "stabbed" binding (lacing cord through holes), as the material was too brittle to hold the threads.

The eventual introduction of paper from China, via Central Asia and the Arabs, further transformed the medium. Before paper became abundant, the art of printing was limited because papyrus was strictly limited in supply, lacked a standard sheet size, and required strips to be fastened together.
