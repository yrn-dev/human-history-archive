# Geology of Bolivia

The geological composition of Bolivia is characterized by distinct variations between its eastern and western mountain ranges and the plateau situated between them.

## The Andean Ranges and Plateau
The eastern ranges of the Bolivian Andes are primarily composed of Palaeozoic rocks, which include various granitic and other intrusions. In contrast, the Western Cordillera consists mainly of Jurassic and Cretaceous beds, along with ashes and lavas resulting from great volcanoes. 

The plateau located between these two cordilleras is covered by terrestrial and freshwater deposits. Within this plateau, ridges of Palaeozoic rock rise, as do a series of gypsiferous marls and red sandstones. The age of these specific deposits is somewhat uncertain, though they are probably, at least in part, Cretaceous.

## Palaeozoic and Sedimentary Formations
Palaeozoic beds in the region have yielded fossils dating to the Carboniferous, Devonian, Ordovician, and Cambrian ages. The distribution of these beds varies by region:
* **Southern Bolivia:** Cambrian and Ordovician beds make up the majority of the eastern Andes.
* **Northern Bolivia:** Devonian and Carboniferous beds are extensively developed, particularly within the north-eastern ranges.

In eastern Bolivia, the plains are punctuated by hills known as the Chiquitos, which are composed of ancient sedimentary rocks of an unknown age. The Palaeozoic beds are directly overlaid by gypsiferous marls and red sandstones, which are similar to the *formacion petrolifera* found in Brazil and Argentina. These layers frequently feature a tuff or conglomerate of porphyritic rocks at the base.

## Marine and Freshwater Deposits
Marine fossils discovered by Gustav Steinmann in the middle of the sedimentary series indicate an age no earlier than the Jurassic; Steinmann specifically refers these fossils to the Lower Cretaceous. While it is possible that this series represents more than one geological system, no later marine deposits have been identified in the plains of Bolivia or the eastern Andes.

Freshwater beds from the Tertiary period and later occupy a wide area. A large portion of the depression between the Western and Eastern Cordilleras is covered by recent deposits. These appear to be partly of torrential origin, similar to talus-fans found at the foot of mountain ranges in other dry regions. Additionally, the Pampa Aullaguas (Poopo) and Lake Titicaca were undoubtedly more extensive in the past than they are currently.

## Volcanic Activity and Peaks
Volcanic activity in Bolivia is almost entirely concentrated in the Western Cordillera. The great summits of the eastern range, including Sorata and Illimani, are not volcanic but are instead formed of Palaeozoic rocks with other intrusions and granitic compositions.
