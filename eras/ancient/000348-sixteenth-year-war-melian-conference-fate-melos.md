# Sixteenth Year of the War—The Melian Conference—Fate of Melos

The events surrounding the island of Melos occurred during the sixteenth year of the Peloponnesian War. These proceedings are characterized by the Athenian effort to compel the island's submission and the subsequent violent resolution of the conflict.

## Background and Athenian Objectives
Melos and Thera were inhabited by ancient colonists from Lacedaemon. Unlike other islands in the Aegean, Melos had refused to become a subject of the Athenian empire or a member of the Athenian alliance. The Athenians believed they were entitled to command all islands in the Aegean and viewed the neutrality of Melos and Thera as exceptions that defaced the symmetry of their empire.

Athenian justifications for the expedition included the belief that the Melians benefited from the protection of the Aegean from piracy without contributing to the cost. However, the Melians had remained neutral and had given no ground of offense to Athens.

## Military Expeditions
The Athenian approach to Melos involved multiple military efforts. In one instance, an armament of sixty triremes under Nikias undertook an expedition to the island. After summoning the inhabitants in vain, Nikias ravaged the lands but retired without initiating a siege. Following this, he sailed to Oropus, where his hoplites landed and marched into Boeotia to ravage the Tanagraean territory in coordination with a land force from Athens.

In another account of the sixteenth year, the Athenians dispatched a force consisting of thirty Athenian ships, six Chian vessels, two Lesbian vessels, sixteen hundred heavy infantry, three hundred archers, and twenty mounted archers from Athens, supplemented by fifteen hundred heavy infantry from the allies and islanders. This force was led by generals Cleomedes, son of Lycomedes, and Tisias, son of Tisimachus.

## The Melian Conference
Before causing harm to the land, the Athenian generals sent envoys to negotiate. The Melians declined to bring these envoys before the general population, instead directing them to speak with the magistrates and "the few." 

The Athenians proposed a method of negotiation where no set speeches would be made; instead, the Melians were to interrupt and settle any points of disagreement as they arose. The Melian commissioners agreed to this format but noted that the advanced state of Athenian military preparations suggested that the only outcomes were war (if the Melians proved they had right on their side) or slavery (if they refused to submit). The Athenians responded by urging the Melians to consult for the safety of their state based on the facts before their eyes rather than presentiments of the future.

## The Resolution and Fate of Melos
Following the conference, the Melians maintained their original resolution. They refused to deprive a city of its freedom after seven hundred years of habitation, placing their trust in the gods and the potential help of the Lacedaemonians. They invited the Athenians to allow them to remain friends to Athens and foes to neither party through a mutual treaty.

The Athenians rejected this, stating that the Melians were deceived in their trust of the Lacedaemonians and their hope in the future over the reality before them.

The subsequent treatment of the Melians is described as a piece of gross cruelty and injustice. After the capture of the island, the Athenians carried out wholesale executions and sold the remaining inhabitants as slaves. This included the sale of Melian women; for example, it is recorded that Alkibiadês purchased a Melian woman among the captives and fathered a son by her.

## Historical Assessment
The acquisition of Melos provided no material value to Athens and was not sufficient to cover the expenses of the armament used for its capture. The action is viewed as a gratification of the "pride of power" and the principle of maritime empire. Rather than strengthening the empire, the deed raised significant odium against Athens and is regarded as one of her first and most dishonoring misdeeds.
