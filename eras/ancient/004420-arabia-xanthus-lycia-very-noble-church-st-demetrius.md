# Arabia, Xanthus in Lycia, and the Church of St Demetrius

## Early Christian Basilicas of the East
Among the basilican churches of the East that are noted for their significance are several key sites. These include the church of St Philip at Athens, the monastery of St Catherine on Mt. Sinai (built by Justinian), and the church of Dana, which is located between Bir and Antioch and dates from the same period as St Catherine's. Other notable examples include the church of Bosra in Arabia, the church at Xanthus in Lycia, and the very noble church of St Demetrius at Thessalonica.

## Geography and Territory of Lycia
Lycia is a district in the southwest of Asia Minor, situated on the coast between Pamphylia and Caria. It extends inland to the ridge of Mt Taurus and takes the form of a peninsula projecting southward from the interior mountain masses. The region is characterized as a rugged, mountainous country traversed by offshoots of the Taurus range, which end in lofty promontories on the coast.

The coastline is marked by a succession of bays, most notably the Gulf of Macri (ancient *Glaucus Sinus*) in the extreme west. To the southeast lies the "Sacred Promontory" (*Hiera Acra*), a long narrow tongue of rocky hill accompanied by three small islets known as the Chelidonian islands.

The interior is dominated by several mountain ranges, including Daedala in the west, Cragus on the sea-coast, Solyma in the extreme east, and Massicytus, which reaches 10,000 feet near the center of the region. The only direct communication between Lycia and Pamphylia is a steep, rugged pass called the Climax ("Ladder"), located between the sea and Solyma.

## Hydrography and Urban Centers
Lycia possesses two considerable rivers: the Limyrus, which enters the sea near Limyra, and the Xanthus. The Xanthus descends from the central mass of Mt Taurus and flows through a narrow valley. Upon reaching the city of Xanthus, the river forms a plain of some extent before reaching the sea. These two river mouths provide the only level ground in the region, though the surrounding hills feature rich arborescent vegetation and the upper valleys provide good pasture for sheep.

The region was thickly studded with towns. In the days of the Lycian league's integrity, Artemidorus and Strabo noted twenty-three towns, though Pliny claimed Lycia once had seventy, with twenty-six remaining in his time. Principal towns in the league included:
*   **Xanthus**: The capital, located at the entrance of the Xanthus valley.
*   **Patara**: Also located at the entrance of the Xanthus valley.
*   **Myra**: One of the most important cities, situated at the entrance of the valley of the Andriacus.
*   **Olympus**: Located on the east coast.
*   **Other cities**: Pinara, Tlos, Telmessus, and Phaselis (though Phaselis was more important and remained independent of the league).

## History and Governance of the Lycians
The Lycians, known as *Lukki* in the Tel el-Amarna tablets (1400 B.C.), were mentioned in lists of nations who invaded Egypt during the reign of Mineptah. While they may have initially occupied the Cilician coast, they later entered Lycia from the sea. Herodotus records that they called themselves Termilae and were invaders from Crete, though the original inhabitants were the Solymi and Milyans.

The capital, Xanthus (or Arnna), was completely destroyed by Harpagus, a general of Cyrus, after the fall of the Lydian empire. Despite Persian suzerainty, the Lycians remained practically independent and briefly joined the Delian league. An obelisk at Xanthus contains a boast from the "son of Harpagus" regarding the sacking of cities in alliance with the Athenian goddess.

The Lycians were later incorporated into the empires of Alexander and his successors. Even after Roman conquest, they maintained federal institutions until the time of Augustus. The Lycian league's general assembly managed taxation and the appointment of magistrates and the Lyciarch. Principal towns like Xanthus and Myra held three votes in the assembly, while others held two or one. Lycia was formally annexed to the Roman empire under Claudius and united with Pamphylia, though Theodosius later made it a separate province.

## Culture, Language, and Art
Lycian art was modeled on Greek styles. Sculpture was of a high order, particularly the colored bas-reliefs found on sarcophagi. Rock-cut tombs often mirrored the houses of the living; some, such as the "Harpy-tomb," featured a tall square tower containing the sepulchral chamber.

The Lycian language belongs to a separate family of speech termed "Asianic" and is not connected to the Indo-European family. The Lycian alphabet was derived from the Doric alphabet of Rhodes, with ten additional characters added for sounds not found in Greek. Most surviving inscriptions are sepulchral, though the obelisk at Xanthus serves as a significant historical document. Mentioned deities include Trqqas (or Trqqiz) and Trzzube (Trosobis).
