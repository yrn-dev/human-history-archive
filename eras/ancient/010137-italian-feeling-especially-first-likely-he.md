# Italian Influence on Arts and Culture

The influence of Italian artistic and cultural practices has historically extended across Europe, manifesting in various disciplines including bookbinding, illumination, music, and literature. These influences often emerged through the movement of masters, the translation of texts, and the adoption of specific stylistic frameworks.

## Bookbinding and Illumination
The art of gold tooling on leather, which likely arrived in Venice from the East, reached its finest expression in Italian work during the late 15th century. This technique eventually spread to England, where Thomas Berthelet, the Royal Binder to Henry VIII, practiced it until approximately 1558. Berthelet's work is characterized as being strongly Italian in feeling, particularly in its early stages, leading to the likelihood that he was instructed in the craft by an Italian master.

In the realm of illumination, Italian art influenced by Byzantine elements grew rapidly to maturity, achieving great splendour by the 15th century. While 12th-century frescoes demonstrate early Italian capability, and the *Winchester Psalter* from the middle of that century contains two masterly paintings in the Italian style, early 14th-century specimens were often ordinary. However, Italian illumination expanded with extraordinary energy during the 14th century, specifically in Florence. Notable examples include the legends of the saints (circa 1370) and an address to Robert of Anjou, king of Sicily (1334-1342). Due to political causes and geographical contiguity, Italy and France exerted mutual influence on this art.

## Music and Poetry
The introduction of Italian music significantly colored and schooled English lyric poetry through the adoption of favorite metrical schemes, such as the madrigal. A specific Italian feature, the caressing double ending, became common in English verse, though it was heavier in its English application. 

Italian poems were frequently translated line by line while maintaining their original measure and musical settings. These were then paired with original poems and included in the song-books of Thomas Campion (1601-1619), as well as those of Byrd and Jones (1588) and the lutanist Dowland.

## Literature and Language
Italian influence is also evident in the development of European literature and specific comedic forms. The publication of Marco Polo’s travels had a profound effect on the European imagination, with 15th-century European romance echoing names such as Cambulac (Pekin) and Cathay (North China).

In the field of comedy, the term "burlesque" derives from the Italian *burlesco* (from *burla*, meaning a joke or playful trick). This form of art uses distortion, exaggeration, and ludicrous contrast to excite laughter. The Italian word first appeared in the *Opere Burlesche* of Francesco Berni (1497-1535). Later, English dramatic literature produced burlesques of the Italian opera, such as Gay's *Beggar's Opera*.

Additionally, the Amsterdam school of the late 16th and early 17th centuries showed Italian influence. Pieter Cornelissen Hooft (1581-1647) was a pupil of Guarini in his pastoral drama *Granida* (1605), and his early lyrical and pastoral verse is noted for being full of Italian reminiscences in both matter and style.

## Political and Regional Sentiments
In the context of the Austrian Empire, specific Italian-speaking populations held distinct political desires. The *Italianissimi* of Istria and Trieste were the only people in the empire who truly desired separation from Austria, seeking instead annexation to Italy. In Tirol, the feeling was less intense; except for the city of Trent, the population primarily desired separate local institutions to avoid governance from Innsbruck. Meanwhile, Italian speakers on the coast of Dalmatia sought government support against the pressure of Slav races in the interior, leading them to support German constitutionalists.
