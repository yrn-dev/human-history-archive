# Bactra (Balkh)

Bactra, known in Persian inscriptions as *B[=a]khtri* and today as Balkh, was the capital of the ancient region of Bactria (or *Bactriana*). Located in present-day Afghanistan, the city is situated on the right bank of the Balkh river, approximately 1,200 feet above sea level, 46 miles south of the Oxus (Amu Darya) and 100 miles east of Andkhui.

## Geography and Region
Bactria is defined as the country situated between the Oxus and the Hindu Kush (Paropamisus) range. It is characterized as a mountainous region with a moderate climate, abundant water, and very fertile land. This fertile soil is surrounded and limited by the Turanian desert. Historically, the region was the home of one of the Iranian tribes and is considered the area where the Aryans lived as a single people before separating into Iranians and Indians.

In the great Balkh plain of Afghan Turkestan, a longitudinal ridge has formed that prevents northern affluents from joining the river. This geological upheaval may explain the climatic and physical changes that occurred after the plains of Balkh were green with cultivation and the city served as a great trade center.

## Historical Significance and Religion
Recognized by native populations as the "Mother of Cities," Bactra's foundation is mythically attributed to Kaiomurs, the Persian Romulus. In its early history, it was a rival to the great cities of Babylon, Nineveh, and Ecbatana.

The city and its surrounding country served as the central seat of the Zoroastrian religion. It is said that the prophet Zoroaster preached and gained his first adherents in these regions, and that he died within the walls of the city. Because the religion spread from here to western Iran, the sacred language of the Avesta was once referred to as "old Bactrian," though scholars have since abandoned this term.

In the 7th century, the Chinese traveler Hsuan Tsang recorded that the city and its vicinity contained approximately 100 Buddhist convents with 3,000 devotees, along with numerous religious monuments and stupas. The most notable was the *Nau Behar* (or *Nava Bihara*), the New Convent, which housed a costly statue of Buddha.

## Urban Development and Trade
Historical accounts describe the city's evolution over several centuries:
* **10th Century:** The Arabian traveler Ibn-Haukal described Balkh as a city built of clay with six gates, ramparts, a mosque, and a castle, extending half a parasang.
* **12th Century:** Idrisi noted that the city possessed active trade and various educational establishments. Commercial routes extended from the city as far east as China and India.
* **Later Eras:** The city was a point of transit for travelers, including the Polos, who traveled through the Persian deserts via Balkh to reach Kashgar and eventually Pekin.

## Conquests and Decline
Balkh suffered several catastrophic destructions. In 1220, Jenghiz Khan sacked the city, butchered the inhabitants, and leveled all defensive buildings. A similar devastation occurred in the 14th century under Timur. Despite these events, Marco Polo described it in the following century as a "great" and "noble city."

The city's political control shifted frequently in later centuries:
* It served as the government for Aurangzeb during his youth.
* It was conquered by Nadir Shah in 1736.
* It passed to the Afghans under the Durani monarchy.
* It was captured by Shah Murad of Kunduz in 1820 and later subject to the khan of Bokhara.
* In 1850, Mahommed Akram Khan, Barakzai, captured the city, after which it remained under Afghan rule.

## Archaeological Remains
Currently, much of the city consists of ruins and debris, with about 500 houses of Afghan settlers, a small bazaar, and a colony of Jews. Key remaining structures include:
* **The Fort and Citadel:** Located to the north-east on a barren mound, these are walled and moated, though only a few pillars remain.
* **The Outer Walls:** These measure approximately 6.5 to 7 meters in perimeter; the south-eastern borders feature a rampart of Mongol origin.
* **Religious Sites:** The Masjid Sabz, featuring a green-tiled dome, is said to be the tomb of Abul Narsi Parsar, a Khwaja. The Madrasa survives only by its arched entrance.
* **Buddhist Ruins:** These have proven more durable than the Mahommedan buildings. The Top-i-Rustam is a circular structure 50 feet high and 50 yards in diameter at the base, containing four circular vaults. The Takht-i-Rustam is a wedge-shaped structure built of pise mud (mud mixed with straw).

## Notable Figures
The city is linked to the Barmakids, a noble Persian family. The family founder, Barmak, was a Persian fire-worshipper. Around A.D. 736, Barmak rebuilt and adorned his native city of Balkh following the rebellion of Harith. Additionally, the city is mentioned in the context of the Nestorian church; a priest named Iezdbouzid, who raised a memorial in China in A.D. 781, was the son of a priest who came from Balkh in Tokharistan.
