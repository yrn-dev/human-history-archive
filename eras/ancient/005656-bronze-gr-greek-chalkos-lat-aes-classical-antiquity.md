# The Bronze of Classical Antiquity

In classical antiquity, bronze (referred to in Greek as *chalkos* and in Latin as *aes*) was an alloy composed primarily of copper. To create the alloy, copper was combined with one or more other metals, including tin, zinc, lead, and silver. The proportions of these metals varied based on the time period or the specific purpose for which the alloy was intended. In bronze remains, the copper content is found to range from 67% to 95%.

## Composition and Properties
Bronze is fundamentally an alloy of copper and tin in variable proportions. The addition of tin to copper makes the resulting product harder, less malleable, and more fusible than pure copper, which makes it better suited for casting. Different ratios of these metals produce materials with distinct characteristics:
* **Hard, brittle, and sonorous bronze:** Contains approximately 7 parts copper to 1 part tin and can be tempered to hold a fine edge.
* **Bell-metal:** Varies from 3 to 5 parts copper to 1 part tin.
* **Speculum metal:** Contains 2 to 2½ parts copper to 1 part tin.
* **Statuary bronze:** May contain 80% to 90% copper, with the remainder being tin, or a mixture of tin, zinc, and lead.

## Terminology and Early Use
The terms *chalkos* and *aes* are generally translated as bronze, though their original meanings may have differed. *Chalkos* was likely originally the name for pure copper. This is evidenced by the works of Homer, who describes *chalkos* using terms such as *eruthros* (red), *aithups* (glittering), and *phaennos* (shining)—descriptors that apply specifically to copper. 

However, the process of alloying was known during Homer's time. In the *Iliad* (xviii. 474), Homer describes Hephaestus using a furnace to combine copper, silver, gold, and tin to create the shield of Achilles. Because of this, it is not always clear if the word *chalkos* refers to pure or alloyed copper in early texts. This ambiguity also extends to accounts of the Cyclopes, the Telchines, or the mythical Dactyls of Ida in Crete, who were acquainted with the smelting of *chalkos*. Later Greek writers, however, used *chalkos* to mean bronze in its true sense. For example, Pausanias used the term when describing an ancient statue made of separate pieces fastened with nails.

## Applications in Antiquity
Bronze was utilized for a wide array of functional and artistic objects across various civilizations.

### Coinage
The Greeks used an alloy of copper and tin for their bronze coins until 400 B.C., after which they began using lead with increasing frequency. Silver was rarely found in Greek bronze coins. The Romans also incorporated lead into their bronze coinage, though they gradually reduced the amount. Under the emperors Domitian, Vespasian, Nero, and Caligula, the Romans produced coins of pure copper before eventually returning to a lead mixture.

### Architecture and Hardware
Bronze was used extensively for sheathing and decoration. At Balawat (895-825 B.C.), the tenons of gates were sheathed in bronze. These gates featured bronze bands or strips, 10 inches high, decorated with repousse figures.

### Musical Instruments
Metal wind instruments were used by the Semitic races, Egyptians, and Assyrians before the dawn of civilization in classic Greece. In northern Europe during the Bronze Age (c. 1000 B.C.), prehistoric humans developed the *lur* (or *luurs*), a bronze horn with a wide conical bore bent in a G-shape. One such instrument found in Denmark measures 1.91 meters in length and features a bronze ring joining the U-shaped mouthpiece to the crescent-tube. Additionally, the British Museum holds a pair of bronze Etruscan *cornua* featuring well-preserved cup-shaped mouthpieces.
