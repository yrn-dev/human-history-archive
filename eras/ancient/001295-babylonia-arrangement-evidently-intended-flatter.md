# Babylonia

## Political History and Assyrian Relations
Babylonia's relationship with the Assyrian Empire was characterized by periods of restoration, strategic governance, and violent revolt. One of the first acts of the Assyrian king Esar-haddon was the restoration of Babylon. This process included the return of the image of Bel-Merodach (Bel-Marduk) to its original home and the repopulation of the city using surviving priests and former residents. Following the reconstruction of the temple of Bel-Merodach, Esar-haddon was solemnly declared king there, and Babylon was established as the second capital of the empire. This policy maintained a period of contentment and quiet throughout his reign.

Upon the death of Esar-haddon in October 668 B.C., he was succeeded by Assur-bani-pal. In a strategic arrangement intended to flatter the Babylonians by providing a semblance of independence, Assur-bani-pal appointed his brother, Samas-sum-yukin, as the viceroy of Babylonia. However, this arrangement failed. Samas-sum-yukin adopted a strong Babylonian identity, claiming to be the successor to monarchs whose empire had once reached the Mediterranean. During this time, the Sumerian language was revived as the official tongue. This led to a revolt that severely shook the Assyrian empire. After several years of conflict—during which Egypt regained its independence—Babylon was forced to surrender through starvation, resulting in the execution of the rebel viceroy and his supporters.

## Culture and Education
Assyrian culture was derived from Babylonia, though the two regions differed in their approach to education. While education in the northern kingdom was largely confined to a single class, it was general and of very old standing in Babylonia. Libraries were common in most temples and towns. The rigor of this educational system is highlighted by an old Sumerian proverb stating that those wishing to excel in the school of the scribes must rise with the dawn.

Literacy in Babylonia was extended to both men and women. During Semitic times, this required knowledge of a complicated and extensive syllabary as well as the extinct Sumerian language. Much of the Semitic Babylonian literature was translated from Sumerian originals, and the old agglutinative language of Chaldaea remained the language of law and religion for a long period. To support students, scholars compiled grammars, vocabularies, interlinear translations, commentaries on older texts, and explanations of obscure phrases.

The primary medium for literature was clay tablets, which Pliny referred to as *laterculae coctiles*, inscribed with a metal stylus, though papyrus was also used. Later developments included the addition of Aramaic—the language of diplomacy and commerce—to the curriculum during the second Assyrian empire. Under the Seleucids, Greek was introduced, and some tablets have been found featuring Sumerian and Assyrian (Semitic Babylonian) words transcribed in Greek letters.

## Law and Social Structure
The legal framework of Babylonia was centered around a Code that served as the backbone of its legal system. Recovered fragments from the library of Assur-bani-pal at Nineveh and later Babylonian copies indicate that the Code was studied, divided into chapters (beginning with the words *Ninu ilu [s.]irum*), and recopied for over fifteen hundred years. This Code remained influential through the conquests of the Greeks, Persians, and Parthians, and it eventually influenced later Mahommedan law in Mesopotamia and Syro-Roman law.

The evolution of Babylonian law began with tribal customs that transitioned into city law as Semitic tribes settled in the cities. Early history was marked by struggles for supremacy between these cities. While a dominant metropolis would demand military support and tribute from subject cities, it generally left local customs and cults unaffected, and city rights were respected by conquerors and kings.

## Dress and Material Culture
In old Babylonia, the dress of kings, magnates, and deities was characterized by a garment made of thick, fluted or furrowed material, sometimes featuring variegated and bright designs. A distinctive feature of this attire was that the right shoulder and both arms were originally left uncovered, with one end of the garment hanging loose over the left arm. This style is sometimes compared to the flounced dresses with volants found in the Aegean area, though it is occasionally difficult to distinguish between a true spiral garment and a dress with parallel horizontal stripes.

## Geography and External Records
Modern geographical verification of ancient Babylonia is limited. According to observations by Colonel Rawlinson and Captain Jones, the "Wall of Media" was found to be a misconception; it was actually a line of mounds and an embankment extending seven or eight miles from the Tigris, designed for irrigation and to drain rainwater into a reservoir. Currently, the only points in Babylonia mentioned by Xenophon that can be positively verified are Pylæ—the spot where Babylonia proper begins—and Babylon itself.

Additionally, historical records note that in 597 B.C., Ezekiel was among the captives carried to Babylonia during the reign of Jehoiachin, settling with other exiles in a place called Tel-abib.
