## Mathematical Properties of Single Valued Functions

### Poles and Singularities
A pole is defined as a singular point of a function $f(z)$ that is not a singularity of the function $1/f(z)$. Because $1/f(z)$ is capable of representation about this point ($z_0$) by a series $\sum a_n(z - z_0)^n$, it follows that $a_0$ must be zero. If $a_0$ through $a_{m-1}$ are zero but $a_m \neq 0$, then $1/f(z) = (z - z_0)^m [a_m + a_{m+1}(z - z_0) + \dots]$. Consequently, $(z - z_0)^m f(z) = a_m^{-1} + \sum b_n(z - z_0)^n$. This demonstrates that the expression of $f(z)$ about $z = z_0$ contains a finite number of negative powers of $z - z_0$ and a finite or infinite number of positive powers, meaning a pole is always an isolated singularity.

### Doubly Periodic Functions
In the study of doubly periodic functions, the archive describes the relationship between zeros and poles. For a function $f(z)$, the sum of the orders of its vanishing at points within one parallelogram ($\sum m$) is equal to the sum of the orders of its poles ($\sum n$). This indicates that the number of zeros is equal to the number of poles. 

Furthermore, every conceivable complex value arises as a value for the doubly periodic function $f(z)$ in any one of its parallelograms at least twice. The number of times a value arises is referred to as the "order" of the function.

## Uniform Convergence and Integration
The archive details the conditions for the uniform convergence of an infinite series of functions. Convergence is considered "uniform" in an interval if, after a positive number $\epsilon$ (however small) is specified, a single number $n$ suffices at all points of the interval to ensure that $|f(x) - f_m(x)| < \epsilon$ for all values of $m$ exceeding $n$. If $n$ becomes a function of $a$ that tends toward infinity as $a$ varies, the series does not converge uniformly in the neighbourhood of $a$.
