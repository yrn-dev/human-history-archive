# The Early Development of Marriage Banns and Canonical Requirements

The practice of publishing banns—the public legal notice of an impending marriage—evolved from early ecclesiastical customs intended to ensure the legitimacy of unions and the absence of impediments. While later codified into strict canon law, the origins of this practice are linked to the early Church's desire for public religious ceremonies over private contracts.

## Early Ecclesiastical Customs and Tertullian
In the earliest days of the Church, there was a practice of forewarning the community of intended marriages. The writer Tertullian, in *Ad Uxorem* and *De Pudicitia* (chapter 4), noted that marriages not professed in the Church, known as clandestine marriages, were regarded by Christians as being nearly equivalent to fornication. To avoid this, a custom existed where individuals sought permission to marry from deacons, priests, and the bishop.

Although the Church recognized the validity of private contracts between baptized persons—maintaining that the parties themselves, rather than the priest, served as the "ministers of the sacrament" (*ministri sacramenti*)—it enjoined the addition of a public religious ceremony. This ensured that the union could be sanctified through prayer and the word of God.

## Evolution of the Publication of Banns
The formal publication of banns grew out of the necessity to investigate potential impediments to marriage. By 802, Charlemagne issued a capitulary forbidding the celebration of any marriage until the elders of the people, priests, and bishops had conducted a diligent inquiry into the consanguinity of the couple. 

While this practice was long customary in France—with Bishop Odo of Paris ordering it in 1176—it eventually became a universal requirement. Pope Innocent III made the publication of banns obligatory for the entire Church, and the Lateran Council of 1215 rendered the practice compulsory across all of Christendom.

## Regional Canonical Enactments
The implementation of banns varied by region and era:

*   **England:** The first canonical enactment regarding banns in the English church occurred in A.D. 1200 during the synod of Westminster in London. The 11th canon of this synod decreed that no marriage could be contracted unless banns were published in the church three times, except by the special authority of the bishop.
*   **United Kingdom:** Under statute and canon law, banns remain the normal preliminary to marriage, though a registrar's certificate or a licence may allow a marriage to be solemnized without them.
*   **America:** There is no statutory requirement for banns; the practice is now primarily confined to Roman Catholics, although it was general during the colonial period.
*   **Eastern Church:** The primitive practice survives through the ceremonial blessing of the betrothal by a priest, which is distinct from the marriage ceremony.

## Rituals and Alternative Origins
Historically, the publication of banns occurred at different times in the liturgical calendar, sometimes during mass and sometimes at vespers. In early times, the priest would formally betroth the couple in the name of the Blessed Trinity.

Some theories suggest the custom may have originated from a secular tradition involving knights. According to this view, "true and good knights" participating in tournaments would hang their shields in the nearest church for several weeks before the event. This allowed any existing "impediment" to be identified so the knight could be "warned off."

## Clandestine Marriages and the Council of Trent
The ecclesiastical recognition of clandestine marriages persisted for centuries. This was eventually addressed by a decree of the council of Trent (Sess. xiv. *de matrim.*), which established that for a marriage to be valid, consent must be declared before a priest and in the presence of three witnesses. Under current Roman Catholic law, if the Tridentine decree has been published, a civil marriage is only valid if followed by a religious ceremony in the prescribed form; otherwise, the marriage is treated as voidable due to the *impedimentum clandestinitati*.
