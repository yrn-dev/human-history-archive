# The Japanese People and Their Civilization

The islands of Japan, excluding Sakhalin (of which half is Japanese), are located between the 30th and 45th parallels. The geography of the group is characterized by a line of volcanic mountains, some of which remain active, with the highest point reaching approximately 13,000 feet above the sea. The land is generally fertile, well-watered, and well-cultivated.

## Origins and Early Population
The Japanese proper are of the Mongolian race and physically resemble Amerindians. There are notable resemblances between prehistoric Japanese pottery and similar products from Peru, leading to theories that they may be a back-flow from the early heliolithic culture's trans-Pacific drift, or that they absorbed Malay and Negrito elements from the south. The original inhabitants of the islands are believed to have been the Hairy Ainu, a northern people with remote Nordic affinities.

Written annals, including the Records and the Chronicles, identify six peoples in the origin of the nation:
* Izanagi and his fellow Kami (identified with original immigrants in the story of the islands' "birth").
* Jimmu and his followers, who re-conquered the islands.
* The Yemishi, who are identical to the modern Ainu.
* The Kumaso.
* The Sushen.
* The Tsuchi-gumo (earth-spiders).

## Development of Civilization
Japanese civilization, writing, and literary and artistic traditions are derived from the Chinese. In the second and third centuries of the Christian Era, the Japanese were emerging from barbarism. During this early period, Queen Jingo led an invasion of Korea, an act that played a significant role in establishing their civilization. Later, Chinese letters, ethics, and the sutras and images of northern Buddhism reached Japan via Hiaksai in Korea.

The early Japanese developed a tradition of chivalry and a feudal system. Their historical attacks on China and Korea are described as an Eastern equivalent to the English wars in France. In terms of national character, records of relations with Korea up to A.D. 645 describe the Japanese as a trustful and magnanimous nation, though one with aggressive tendencies.

## Imperial Capitals and Architecture
In ancient times, the Imperial capital was customarily changed with every new sovereign. This practice was driven by the Shinto conception of impurity associated with death and sickness. This custom placed a heavy burden on the people, whose forced labor was used to build new palaces, and it hindered architectural development.

Efforts were made to establish fixed capitals. Emperor Kotoku selected Naniwa, while Emperor Tenchi moved to Omi, Temmu to Asuka, and Empress Jito to Fujiwara. The Court eventually moved to Nara in the second year of Empress Gemmyo's reign, remaining there for seventy-five years. Nara is noted for its scenic beauty and ancient temples containing pictorial and glyptic art from the seventh and eighth centuries. The only surviving building from the Nara palace is the Shoso-in storehouse, which contains articles used by sovereigns of that epoch.

## European Contact and Isolation
Japan first encountered Europe in the sixteenth century. In 1542, Portuguese explorers arrived in a Chinese junk, followed in 1549 by the Jesuit missionary Francis Xavier. Initially, Japan welcomed this intercourse, and many converts to Christianity were made. William Adams of Gillingham, Kent, became a trusted adviser to the Japanese and taught them to build large ships, which were subsequently used for voyages to Peru and India.

However, conflict arose between Portuguese Jesuits, Spanish Dominicans, and English and Dutch Protestants, who each warned the Japanese of the others' political designs. Additionally, the Jesuits persecuted Buddhists with acrimony. The Japanese eventually concluded that Europeans were an "intolerable nuisance" and that Catholic Christianity served as a cloak for the political ambitions of the Spanish monarchy and the Pope. Following a great persecution of Christians, Japan was absolutely closed to Europeans in 1638. For over 200 years, the country remained isolated; it was forbidden for Japanese to go abroad, for Europeans to enter, or for any ship larger than a coasting boat to be built.

## Modernization and Global Influence
Following its period of seclusion, Japan became the pioneer country in the recovery of Asiatic peoples. During the first decade of the Meiji era, the government sought to equip the population with the products of Western civilization. This reform movement was led by statesmen of intellectual superiority and far-seeing vision. Because the Japanese had a traditional habit of looking to officialdom for direction, the government had to provide both the inspiration and the models for assimilating a foreign civilization that had been an object of national repugnance for nearly three centuries.

By combining their ancient civilization and remarkable artistic faculty with an adaptation of Western methods and a capacity for progress in commerce and war, the Japanese people established themselves as a great modern world-force.
