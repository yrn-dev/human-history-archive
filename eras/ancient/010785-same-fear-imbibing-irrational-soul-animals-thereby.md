# The Fear of Imbibing the Irrational Soul of Animals

The belief that consuming animal flesh or blood could lead to the absorption of an animal's irrational soul or psychic qualities has served as a primary motivation for various dietary restrictions and ascetic practices across multiple cultures and religious traditions. This fear was centered on the idea that the irrational soul of an animal resides especially in its blood, and that imbibing it could reinforce the lower instincts and appetites of a human being.

## Dietary Restrictions and Vegetarianism

The desire to avoid the "taint" of the irrational soul inspired the vegetarianism of Apollonius of Tyana and the Therapeutae. The Therapeutae were specifically careful to ensure their sacred meals featured tables free from meats containing blood. Similarly, the fear of absorbing an animal's psychic qualities motivated the early Christian and the rule among the sons of Israel against eating things that had been strangled.

Some beliefs distinguished between different types of animals based on their origin. It was held by Manichaean sects that fish, because they are born in and of the waters without sexual connection with other fish, are free from the pollution that affects animals generated through copulation. Consequently, fish were considered safe to eat, unlike flesh. This belief is cited as the origin of the Catholic rule of fasting.

In the *Cretans* of Euripides, a confession by one initiated in the mysteries of Orpheus (who became a "Bacchos") expresses a similar scruple against flesh-eating, describing the act of exiling the "touch of all meat where life hath been" from the lips. This Orphic fast was traditionally broken only by an annual sacramental banquet, which may have originally consisted of human flesh, but later shifted to raw bovine flesh.

## The Role of Blood and Spirit

The irrational soul was believed to be concentrated in the blood. This led to the general rule against eating strangled meats, except during sacramental meals where the god inherent in the animal was partaken of. 

Similar concepts were applied to the consumption of wine. In ancient Egypt, kings avoided wine at the table and in libations because they believed vines grew from the rotting bodies of rebels who had fought the gods. Drinking wine was viewed as imbibing the blood and souls of these rebels, with the resulting intoxication seen as possession by their spirits. Medieval sons of Israel held a similar view, believing a "cardiac demon" in wine takes possession of those who are drunken. A similar superstition underlies the Mahommedan prohibition of wine-drinking. Because of these beliefs, the avoidance of wine—practiced by Pythagoreans, Nazirites, Rechabites, and Arab dervishes—as well as the avoidance of leaven in bread, is viewed as parallel to the abstention from flesh.

## Transmigration and Mantic Powers

Porphyry documented a further widespread scruple against eating meat based on the doctrine of transmigration. This view held that the souls of humans could transmigrate into animals; therefore, eating animals was seen as a form of cannibalism, as one might consume their own kind. While some meat-eaters argued that killing animals was a pious duty to release these imprisoned human souls, others maintained a strict avoidance. Porphyry argued that a "lover of wisdom" and priest of the universal God would abstain from animals to avoid polluting demons and inferior souls.

Conversely, some sought to utilize these souls for specific gains. Porphyry relates that wizards acquired mantic powers—the power of divination—by swallowing the hearts of birds such as hawks and ravens, as the soul of the bird entered the wizard through the flesh.

## Related Beliefs on the Soul

The concept of the soul's relationship with animals appears in other cultural contexts. The Egyptians believed that the souls of vicious men were imprisoned in the bodies of ill-conditioned or unclean beasts to expiate past transgressions before eventually animating human bodies again.

In other traditions, the relationship with animal spirits was not one of avoidance but of acquisition. In some North and Central American and West African tribes, males acquire a tutelary spirit at puberty. In certain Indian tribes, a youth kills the animal he dreams of during an initiation fast to create "medicine" from its skin, feathers, or claw. In Alaska, a candidate for magical powers kills an otter and cuts out its tongue to secure specific powers. Among the Malays, it is believed that the office of the *pawang* (priest) is hereditary only if the soul of the dead priest passes into the son's body in the form of a tiger.
