# The Coins of the Greek and Scythic Kings of India

The study of the Greek and Scythic kings of India is a specialized field of numismatics and history, drawing upon a variety of archaeological and textual sources. A primary reference for this subject is the work *The Coins of the Greek and Scythic Kings of India*, published in 1886 by P. Gardner as part of the Catalogue of Greek Coins in the British Museum (volume x).

## Historical Context and Kingdoms
The historical record for the Graeco-Bactrian and Indo-Scythian kingdoms is reconstructed through a combination of numismatic evidence and ancient texts. The archive identifies several key scholarly works addressing these periods, including H. H. Wilson's *Ariana Antiqua* (1841), A. von Sallet's *Die Nachfolger Alexanders des Grossen in Baktrien und Indien* (1879), and A. von Gutschmid's *Geschichte Irans und seiner Nachbarlaender von Alexander dem Grossen bis zum Untergang der Arsaciden* (1888).

Further insights into the Hellenistic influence in these regions are provided by W. W. Tarn in "Notes on Hellenism in Bactria and India" (1902) and Vincent A. Smith, who wrote on the Kushan or Indo-Scythian period of Indian history in the *Journal of the R. Asiatic Soc.* (1903) and *Early History of India* (1908).

## Numismatic Analysis and Interpretations
Coins serve as critical evidence for identifying rulers and their titles. For example, the coinage of the prince Kanerkes features inscriptions that have been the subject of scholarly debate. Some texts suggest a native title, *Non* or *None*, which Pauthier associates with the style "*Rao-nana (or nano) Rao*" found in Indo-Scythic coinage. However, H. H. Wilson interprets *Raonano* as a genitive plural of *Rao*. This view is supported by General Cunningham, who notes that the inscription corresponds precisely to the Greek phrase ΒΑΣΙΛΕΥΣ ΒΑΣΙΛΕΩΝ ΚΑΝΗΡΚΟΥ.

Other Indo-Scythic coins bear the word *Korano* attached to the name of the prince. Dr. Bird of Bombay has asserted that this refers to the *Ḳaraunah*, described as a Græco-Indo-Scythic tribe of robbers in the Punjab. This conclusion is contested by Quatremère, who argues there is no mention of the *Ḳaraunahs* prior to the Mongol invasion. While Reiske cites a 10th-century writer, Hamza of Ispahan, regarding troops called *Ḳaráunahs*, it is suggested that the correct reading should be *Kazáwinah*, referring to the people of Kazvin.

## Source Material and Documentation
The history of these regions is reconstructed from a diverse array of sources:
*   **Greek and Byzantine Authors:** A limited number of notices are preserved from Greek authors, specifically Strabo and Justin (including the prologues of Trogus). Later information is derived from Byzantine authors and Armenian and Persian sources, such as the translation of Tabari by Th. Noeldeke.
*   **Chinese Sources:** Research by Deguignes, E. Specht, and Sylvain Levi has utilized Chinese records to examine the history of the Greek kings of Bactriana and the Indo-Scythians.
*   **Other Records:** Arab geographers of the 9th century mentioned *Ṭáṭaríya* coins. While General Cunningham connected these to Scythic or Tátár princes ruling in Kabul at the beginning of the era, other interpretations suggest that "Tartar" as a generic title for Turanian hosts following Chinghiz was unknown in Western Asia before his time.
