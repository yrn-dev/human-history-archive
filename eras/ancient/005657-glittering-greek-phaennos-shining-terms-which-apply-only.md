# Glittering and Shining Terms

In the study of historical linguistics and material culture, terms denoting "glittering" or "shining" are applied across various contexts, ranging from the description of ancient metals and minerals to the characterization of human attributes and royal attire.

## Applications to Metals and Minerals
In classical antiquity, the Greek term *chalkos* (translated as bronze) was used to describe alloys consisting primarily of copper, often mixed with tin, zinc, lead, or silver. While later Greek writers used *chalkos* to refer to bronze in its true sense, the poet Homer employed the term to describe pure copper. In this specific context, Homer used the descriptors *aithups* (glittering) and *phaennos* (shining), as well as *eruthros* (red). These specific terms—glittering and shining—are noted as applying only to copper in Homer's usage.

Beyond metals, the term "shining" is found in the etymology of various materials and descriptors. The word marble is derived from the Greek *marmaros*, meaning "shining stone." This term is restricted by accurate writers to crystalline and compact varieties of carbonate of lime (and occasionally carbonate of magnesia) that can be polished. The "beautiful lustre" and "sparkling facets" of polished statuary marble result from light penetrating the rock and reflecting off the surfaces of deeper-lying crystals.

## Royal and Personal Adornment
Descriptions of "glittering" and "shining" are also used to denote status and military presence. William II is described as having appeared in military uniforms of the "most glittering sort." His personal preferences for attire included long white cloaks and breastplates that were "silver shining."

In the context of Japanese architecture, figurative epithets involving the sun's light were applied to the sovereign's palace. These included *Asahi-no-tada-sasu-miya*, meaning the "miya on which the morning sun shines direct," and *Yuhi-no-hiteru-miya*, meaning the "miya illumined by the evening sun."

## Linguistic and Figurative Usage
The concept of shining appears in the derivation of adjectives related to physical appearance. The Middle English word "balled" (bald) is sometimes connected to a stem *bal*, meaning white or shining, a derivation suggested by the Greek term *phalakros*.

In a figurative and moral sense, "shining qualities" refers to attributes esteemed by the world, such as beauty of genius, delicacy of taste, perfection in the arts, profound policy, skill in government, fortitude, valour, and capacity for abstruse sciences. These are described as the things that "glitter most in the eye of the world" and are capable of dazzling it. According to historical reflections on these qualities, the Almighty has sometimes indulged these shining attributes to his enemies while refusing them to his most faithful servants, despite the servants possessing talents of a superior nature.
