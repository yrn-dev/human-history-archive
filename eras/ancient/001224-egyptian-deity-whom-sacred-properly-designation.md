# Ancient Egyptian Religious Practices and Deities

The religious system of ancient Egypt was characterized by a complex relationship between the divine, the natural world, and symbolic representation. This system integrated the worship of anthropomorphic deities, the veneration of sacred animals, and a deep preoccupation with the immortality of the soul.

## Divine Representation and Rituals
Egyptian deities were often regarded as anthropomorphic, and as such, they were provided with clothing that served as symbols of their attributes. These garments were less subject to changing fashions than human attire. Records from the Papyrus Harris (Rameses III) list a vast number of mantles, tunics, and skirts, both dyed and undyed, intended for various deities. The management of these garments was a formal temple operation; as early as the VIth Dynasty, a priest held the title of "master of the wardrobe," supported by laundry servants and weavers.

During the restoration of temples, specific rituals were performed to anoint the divine limbs of the gods and prepare royal linen. Furthermore, the "master of secret things" oversaw the ceremonial clothing of the gods during festal processions.

## Sacred Animals and Symbolism
A prominent feature of Egyptian religion was the worship of animals. While some philosophers later argued that animals were not worshipped for themselves but as symbols or "mirrors" through which the Supreme Being displayed incomprehensible wisdom, the Egyptian populace maintained a fierce devotion to them.

Certain animals were held in such high regard that killing them—whether intentionally or accidentally—could result in death or severe punishment. Cicero noted that Egyptians would suffer extreme torments rather than commit sacrilege against a cat, an ibis, or a crocodile. Diodorus recorded an instance where a Roman who inadvertently killed a cat could not be rescued by the king's guards or the prestige of the Roman name due to the fury of the populace. In times of extreme famine, it is recorded that Egyptians preferred to eat one another rather than feed upon these imagined deities.

Specific animals were dedicated to particular gods:
* **The Baboon:** The *Papio hamadryas* (Arabian baboon) was dedicated to the god Thoth. Additionally, the *Papio anubis* (anubis baboon) was tamed by Egyptians and trained to pluck sycamore-figs from trees.
* **The Bull Apis:** This was the most venerated of all sacred animals. The selection of a new Apis was a rigorous process requiring specific marks, age, and birth conditions. The discovery of a qualified successor triggered universal celebrations in Memphis. This devotion was highlighted when Kambysês, misinterpreting such celebrations as an insult, killed the newly discovered Apis with a dagger.

## Mysteries and Symbolism
Egyptian religious knowledge was often shrouded in symbols and enigmas to make the truth appear more venerable. The priests held sacred books containing the mysteries of divine worship and principles of government. This culture of secrecy was symbolized in sanctuaries by the figure of Harpocrates, who placed a finger upon his mouth, and by the sphinxes positioned at temple entrances.

Public monuments, including obelisks, pyramids, and pillars, were adorned with hieroglyphics. These symbolical writings often used animal figures to convey hidden meanings; for example, a hare was used to signify a piercing and lively attention due to the animal's delicate hearing. Similarly, a statue of a judge with eyes fixed on the ground and no hands symbolized the specific duties of the judiciary.

## Concepts of the Soul and Nature
The Egyptians held a cardinal doctrine regarding the immortality of the soul, which significantly influenced Greek natural theology. This belief was expressed through mysterious recitations and rites of sepulture concerning the soul's migration from its earthly abode to an eternal one.

Central to these ideas was Osiris, viewed as the universal soul of nature or a vivifying power. Osiris was honored by the rural population through processions that closely resembled the Greek phallic processions, though the Egyptian versions lacked choruses. Other deities mentioned in the archive include Bes, who was depicted as nude, and Ptah, who was compared to a deformed and repulsive monster.
