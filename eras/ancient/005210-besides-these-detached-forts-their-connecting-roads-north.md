# Military Defenses of Northern Britain

The northern region of Britain was characterized by a rigorous military system designed to secure a mountainous and difficult terrain. Unlike the lowlands of the south, east, and midlands—which were suited for agriculture and civil life—the uplands of northern England and Wales were regions of gorges, deep valleys, and elevations often exceeding 600 feet. In these areas, civil life was almost entirely absent; no country houses or towns have been discovered more than 20 miles north of York. Instead, the hills functioned as an extensive military frontier defined by strategic roads and a network of forts.

## Hadrian's Wall and its Construction
The primary defense for the north of Britain was Hadrian's wall. The physical works of the wall consisted of three distinct elements. The most prominent feature is a stone wall, originally perhaps 14 feet high and 6 to 8 feet thick, supported by a deep ditch in front and a system of turrets, "mile castles," forts, and a connecting road behind it. Traces of this stone structure remain visible on the high moors between Gilsland and Chollerford.

A second element is the "Vallum," which is a broad, flat-bottomed ditch with earth cast up on either side into continuous mounds resembling ramparts. The third element is the "turf wall," made of sods laid in regular courses with a ditch in front. This turf wall is the oldest of the structures. Because ancient writers identify two wall-builders—Hadrian and Septimius Severus—it is inferred that Hadrian originally constructed the wall of turf, and Severus later reconstructed it in stone. This reconstruction generally followed the original line to utilize the existing ditch, though the turf wall survives only at specific points, such as Birdoswald, where the stone work deviated.

## The Antonine Rampart
Around 142, the successor to Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, advanced further north through his general Lollius Urbicus. He moved from the Solway and Tyne frontier to a narrower isthmus between the Clyde and Forth, which is 36 miles across. In this location, he established a continuous rampart with a ditch in front and a connecting road running from end to end. 

This rampart was built of regularly laid sods, a method verified by excavations conducted between 1891 and 1893. The system included approximately a dozen fair-sized forts. Three of these have been excavated:
* **Bar Hill:** Covering three acres, it featured turf ramparts, a stone headquarters building, a storehouse, a bath, and wooden barrack-rooms.
* **Castle Cary:** Covering nearly four acres, this fort featured massive, well-dressed masonry in its ramparts, with its north face aligned with the frontier wall.
* **Rough Castle:** Located near Falkirk, this smaller fort is noted for its defensive pits (similar to Caesar's *lilia* at Alesia) and its strong earthen and turf-built ramparts and ravelins.

## Northern Road Systems and Forts
The military infrastructure of the north relied on three principal roads that connected various forts:

1. **The Eastern Route:** Leading due north from York, this road passed through forts at Ebchester, Lanchester, Binchester, Piers Bridge, and Catterick Bridge before reaching Scotland and the wall. Branches from Chester-le-Street connected to the Tyne mouth at South Shields and the Tyne Bridge (Pons Aelius) at Newcastle.
2. **The Central Route:** Turning north-west from Catterick Bridge, this road crossed the Pennine Chain via forts at Brough-under-Stainmoor, Bowes, and Rokeby. It descended into the Eden valley, reached Hadrian's wall near Carlisle (Luguvallium), and continued to Birrens.
3. **The Western Route:** Starting from Chester and moving up the western coast, this complex route featured numerous forts, including Ribchester (Bremetennacum), Manchester (Mancunium or Mamucium), Old Penrith (Voreda), and Brougham Castle (Brocavum). A western branch included Maryport (Uxellodunum), Hardknott above Eskdale, Waterhead on Ambleside, and Watercrook near Kendal.

Additionally, several cross roads provided communication between troops stationed in Lancashire and Cheshire and those in Yorkshire. While these roads show evidence of being built at different times for different objectives, there is no evidence that any section was abandoned when new ones were constructed.
