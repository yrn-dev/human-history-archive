# 15th-Century Red Leather Bindings of the Biblioteca Laurenziana

The red leather bindings predominant in the Biblioteca Laurenziana at Florence represent a specific style of book ornamentation from the 15th century. These bindings are notable for their historical continuity and their relationship to earlier forms of book decoration.

## Historical Continuity and Patterns
The arrangement of the patterns found on the 15th-century red leather bindings in the Biblioteca Laurenziana bears a curious resemblance to much earlier work. Specifically, a miniature in the Codex Amiatinus—a book written in England during the 8th century—depicts a book featuring a red leather cover with a pattern similar to those found in the Laurenziana collection. This suggests a degree of conservatism in the external form of books over several centuries. A similar pattern of conservatism is observed in 15th-century Oxford bindings, where some small stamps used are nearly indistinguishable from those employed in England three centuries prior.

## Evolution of the Codex and Binding
The red leather bindings of the 15th century are examples of the *codex* form of the book. The *codex*—a term originally referring to a block of wood or a tree stump, and later to three-leaved tablets sawn from such a block—gained popularity in Rome during the time of Martial as parchment began to supersede papyrus. 

Unlike the earlier *volumen* (a roll wound around sticks), the *codex* allowed for different page arrangements. While early codices like the 4th-century Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus utilized four and three columns per page respectively, and the 5th-century Codex Alexandrinus used two, the arrangement eventually shifted toward single or double columns. This layout remained largely unchanged from the 6th century until the invention of printing.

The physical structure of these bindings evolved from the medieval "half-binding." To protect the projecting bands at the back of sewn quires and to prevent vellum leaves from curling, strong wooden boards were attached to the sides. Initially, a strip of leather covered only the back and the hinges of these boards. Over time, this leather strip expanded to cover the entirety of the boards, creating the "whole" binding, which provided a flat surface suitable for artistic decoration.

## The Transition to Printing and Gold Tooling
The middle of the 15th century marked a significant shift in book production with the introduction of printing in Europe. This led to a reaction against the large, valuable illuminated manuscripts and their precious covers. Early printed books were often "ugly" and cheap, typically bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf and decorated with large blind panel stamps.

However, this period also saw the birth of gold tooling on leather, an art likely brought to Venice from the East. The finest examples of this craft are found in Italian work from the late 15th century. This technique eventually spread to England, where Thomas Berthelet, the Royal Binder to Henry VIII, practiced a style strongly influenced by Italian masters until approximately 1558.

## Related Leatherwork and Materials
While the Biblioteca Laurenziana's bindings are characterized by their red leather, other historical bindings utilized various materials and techniques:
* **Repousse:** *St Cuthbert's Gospels*, likely from the 7th or 8th century, features red leather with a repousse design, where fine lines were impressed by hand and painted yellow and blue.
* **Blind Stamping:** English binders were considered to excel in "blind" stamping (impressions made without gold leaf), often using intaglio-cut stamps to create cameo-form impressions. An example is the 12th-century Winchester Domesday Book, bound in dark brown morocco.
* **Morocco Leather:** Later bindings frequently used morocco leather in various colors, such as the dark green or deep red morocco used by Demetrio Canevari, or the red niger morocco used by Douglas Cockerell.
