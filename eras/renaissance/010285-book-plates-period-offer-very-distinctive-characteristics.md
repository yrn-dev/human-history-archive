# Book-plates

A book-plate, also known as an *ex-libris*, is a printed label used to indicate the ownership of individual volumes. This practice is nearly as old as the printed book itself, serving as a printed evolution of the hand-painted armorial or symbolical personal devices found in medieval manuscripts.

## Origins and Early Development
The earliest known examples of book-plates are German. According to the authority Friedrich Warnecke, the oldest movable *ex-libris* are hand-painted woodcuts from approximately 1480, depicting a shield of arms supported by an angel; these were pasted into books presented to the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim by Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach. 

Other early national examples include:
* **France:** The oldest discovered is that of Jean Bertaud de la Tour-Blanche, dated 1529.
* **England:** The earliest is the gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon for books presented to the university of Cambridge.
* **Holland:** A plate belonging to Anna van der Aa in 1597.
* **Italy:** An example attributed to 1622.
* **America:** A plain printed label for John Williams in 1679.

In France, the "movable *ex-libris*" did not become common until the 17th century, as book-owners previously preferred the more luxurious habit of stamping covers with personal devices. The term *ex-libris*, used as a substantive, originated in France and later became the recognized term for book-plates across the continent.

## English Book-plates: Styles and Evolution
English book-plates from the late Elizabethan to the late Victorian periods reflect the prevailing tastes in decorative art. Early English plates, such as that of Sir Thomas Tresham (dated 1585), were limited in number until the last quarter of the 17th century. These early examples were remarkably simple and plainly armorial, lacking German elaborateness and usually featuring symmetrical mantling with occasional wreaths or palms.

Following the Restoration, book-plates became established accessories for well-ordered libraries. This period introduced two distinctive characteristics:
1. The use of tincture lines and dots, a method devised in the middle of the century by Petra Sancta in *Tesserae Gentilitiae*.
2. More elaborate mantling surrounding the shield, which resembled the periwigs of the era.

This later style was imported from France, and until the French Revolution, English decoration in book-plates generally followed French taste with a delay of several years.

## Composition and Artistic Genres
While heraldic arrangements were common, many plates were essentially pictorial. Specific English genres include:
* **Library interiors and book-piles:** Exemplified by the *ex-libris* of W. Hewer (1699), secretary to Samuel Pepys.
* **Portrait-plates:** Notable examples include those of John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys.
* **Allegories:** Engraved by artists such as George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth.
* **Landscape-plates:** Produced by wood engravers of the Bewick school.

In these pictorial styles, the armorial element typically played a secondary role.

## Classification and Collecting
The systematic study and collection of book-plates began around 1875, gaining significant momentum with the 1880 publication of *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates* by Lord de Tabley. This work established a general classification of styles:
* **Early Armorial:** Previous to the Restoration (e.g., the Nicholas Bacon plate).
* **Jacobean:** A term including the heavy decorative manner of the early Georgian, Queen Anne, and Restoration days (e.g., the Lansanor plate).
* **Chippendale:** A rococo style (e.g., the French plate of Convers).
* **Wreath and Ribbon:** Associated with the period of the urn.

Collecting has led to the formation of societies in England, France, Germany, and the United States, with publications such as *The Journal of the Ex-libris Society* (London), the *Ex-libris Zeitschrift* (Berlin), and the *Archives de la societe francaise de collectionneurs d'ex-libris* (Paris).

## Notable Artists
Book-plates are valued as personal relics and specimens of decorative fashion. Renowned artists whose work appears in this medium include Albrecht Durer and Holbein, as well as the Little Masters of Germany. Other contributors include Hogarth, Bewick, Bartolozzi, and more modern figures such as Aubrey Beardsley, Walter Crane, Kate Greenaway, and Caldecott.
