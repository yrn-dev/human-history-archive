# Edmund Bonner

Edmund Bonner (1500?–1569) was the Bishop of London during the 16th century. His career was marked by his role as an agent for various English monarchs and his eventual prominence in the persecution of heretics.

## Early Life and Education
There are conflicting accounts regarding Bonner's parentage. One account, printed by Strype, suggests he was the natural son of Elizabeth Frodsham and George Savage, the rector of Davenham, Cheshire; Elizabeth later married a sawyer of Hanley in Worcestershire named Edmund Bonner. However, Sir Edmund Lechmere disputed this, asserting that Bonner was born legitimately, though the evidence for this claim is described as not very satisfactory.

Bonner was educated at Broadgates Hall (now Pembroke College), Oxford. He graduated as a bachelor of civil and canon law in June 1519 and was ordained around the same time. He later received a Doctor of Civil Law (D.C.L.) in 1525.

## Service Under Henry VIII
Bonner began his professional ascent as the chaplain to Cardinal Wolsey, remaining with him at Cawood during the cardinal's arrest in 1529. He subsequently entered the service of the king, likely through the influence of Cromwell. In January 1532, he was dispatched to Rome to obstruct judicial proceedings against Henry VIII in the papal curia. In October 1533, he was tasked with informing Pope Clement VII—then a guest of Francis I at Marseilles—of Henry's appeal from the pope to a general council. For these services, Bonner was granted several livings and appointed archdeacon of Leicester in 1535.

During this period, Bonner was regarded as a coarse and unscrupulous tool of Cromwell. He was not a theologian and typically referred theological inquiries to divines. His primary interest in the Reformation was the repudiation of papal control, and his views aligned with the Six Articles. In the final years of Henry's reign, he was mainly occupied with enforcing the "whip with six strings," though he also served as an ambassador to the emperor between 1524 and 1543.

## Conflict Under Edward VI
The accession of Edward VI brought a shift in Bonner's career. While he could not easily repudiate the royal supremacy he had helped establish, he began to doubt it when used by a Protestant council. Bonner, or possibly Gardiner, developed the theory that royal supremacy was in abeyance during a royal minority. This position was neither constitutionally nor legally tenable, as Bonner and Gardiner had both sought licenses to exercise ecclesiastical jurisdiction from the young king.

Bonner's resistance to the government led to his commitment to the Fleet after he resisted a visitation in August 1547. After his release, he became a leading opponent of the first Book of Common Prayer and the first Act of Uniformity during the parliament of November 1548 to March 1549. Because he neglected to enforce these laws and failed to maintain at St Paul's Cross that the royal authority was as great as if the king were forty years of age, he was deprived of his bishopric by an ecclesiastical court presided over by Cranmer and sent to the Marshalsea. On February 7, 1550, the council confirmed his deprivation in the Star Chamber and condemned him to perpetual imprisonment.

## Restoration and Persecutions Under Mary I
Upon the accession of Mary I, Bonner was released and restored to his see, as his previous deprivation was viewed as invalid. He vigorously restored Roman Catholicism in his diocese and submitted to papal jurisdiction. In 1555, he began the persecutions for which he is most famous. While apologists claim his actions were merely "official," Bonner ensured that the condemnation of heretics to the fire became part of his ordinary official duties.

Bonner's approach to persecution was complex. Some records, including those from Foxe, indicate instances where he failed to persecute, and the council sometimes felt he was not severe enough, forcing victims upon him. In this capacity, he acted as an "ecclesiastical sheriff" in a refractory district, reflecting a state where religious persecutions had become state persecutions. However, he showed no mercy to fallen enemies, notably displaying brutal jeers toward Cranmer during the latter's degradation.

Despite his prominence and utility in performing "disagreeable work," neither Henry VIII nor Mary admitted him to the privy council, suggesting he was viewed as a useful instrument rather than a desirable colleague.
