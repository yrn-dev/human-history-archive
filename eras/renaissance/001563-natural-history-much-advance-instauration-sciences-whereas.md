# Natural History and the Instauration of the Sciences

In the philosophical system developed by Francis Bacon, the "Instauration of the Sciences" represents a comprehensive effort to advance human knowledge. Central to this project is the relationship between the *Organum* (Bacon's method of discovery) and "Natural History."

## The Role of Natural History
Bacon identified a critical necessity for a complete natural history, which he viewed as the tabulation and arrangement of the phenomena of the universe. He believed that the *Organum*, even if fully completed, would not significantly advance the Instauration of the Sciences without the support of Natural History. Conversely, he asserted that Natural History alone, even without the *Organum*, would advance the project "not a little."

Bacon viewed the *Organum* and Natural History as correlative components that form the two equally necessary sides of a true philosophy. Their union is what produces the "new philosophy."

## Methodology and the Collection of Instances
Bacon's method was designed for mechanical simplicity and absolute certainty. However, he recognized that the process of scientific discovery requires a vast store of instances. In a letter to Jean Antoine Baranzano, Bacon addressed the potential deterrence caused by the sheer volume of data required, stating that unless a store of instances is procured, the business of science must be given up, as all other paths are impassable.

He argued that a "true natural history" should consist exclusively of:
* Instances
* Connections
* Observations
* Canons

Bacon was not concerned if the description of these instances were to fill six times as many volumes as Pliny's *History*. He further noted that the multitude of required instances could be diminished through the "prerogatives of instances" and specific modes of experimenting.

## The Framework of Knowledge
Bacon's broader vision of science, as detailed in the *Advancement* (or *De Augmentis*), categorizes all human knowledge based on the mental faculty used:
* **Memory:** The collection and storage of bare facts presented to the senses. The exposition of these facts is "history," which is classified as either civil or natural.
* **Imagination:** The division of sense materials according to the mind's pleasure, resulting in "poesy" or feigned history.
* **Reason:** The processing of materials according to the pattern of nature, resulting in "philosophy."

Within this framework, natural philosophy is considered the "great mother of all the sciences." It is divided into speculative (theoretical) and operative (practical) branches. Speculative natural philosophy is further subdivided into physics, which inquires into material and efficient causes, and metaphysics, which examines final and formal causes.

## Errors in Scientific Process
Bacon identified four primary faults in the process leading from senses and objects to axioms and conclusions:
1. **Sensory Impressions:** The senses are faulty and can deceive.
2. **Notions:** Notions derived from senses are often confused and indefinite rather than distinctly bounded.
3. **Induction:** The practice of inferring principles through simple enumeration is flawed; it must instead employ "solutions" or "exclusions" of nature.
4. **Discovery and Proof:** The method of establishing general principles first and then trying intermediate axioms by them is described by Bacon as the "curse of all science" and the "parent of error."

## Conceptions of Nature and Cause
Bacon's approach to natural principles was physical rather than abstract. His "forms" were viewed as highly general physical properties, potentially the modes of action of simple bodies. However, Bacon's view of nature was purely statical. He did not share modern notions of nature as a process of flow or development, nor did he view cause as dynamical. Instead, he understood cause as *causa immanens* (underlying substance), where effects were manifestations rather than consequents.

To unify these findings, Bacon proposed the *Prima Philosophia* (first philosophy), a fundamental doctrine intended to display the unity of nature by connecting the highest axioms of various sciences into a single body of truth.
