# History of Henry VIII and Associated Records

The reign of King Henry VIII is documented through a vast array of primary sources, state papers, and historical chronicles. These records provide insight into the political, domestic, and legal maneuvers of the English crown during the 16th century.

## Primary Documentation and State Papers
A significant portion of the historical record for this period is contained within the *State Papers of Henry VIII* and the *Letters and Papers of Henry VIII*. The latter was extensively compiled by the English historian John Sherren Brewer (1810–1879). Brewer, a professor of English language and literature and lecturer in modern history, was commissioned by the master of the rolls in 1856 to prepare a calendar of these state papers. His work culminated in the *Letters and Papers of the Reign of King Henry VIII*, the introductions of which were published separately in 1884 under the title *The Reign of Henry VIII* and are regarded as a scholarly and authoritative history of the period.

Other critical archival materials include the *Cal. of State Pap. England and Spain, Venetian and Foreign* (1558-1559), as well as the *Colton MSS.* and the "Baga de secretis" found in the reports of the Deputy Keeper of Public Records. The "Baga de Secretis" is a repository that became the regular place of custody for important state trials, though it is noted that this name is commonly used incorrectly.

## Chronicles and Biographical Accounts
The events of Henry VIII's reign are further detailed in various chronicles and specialized histories. These include:
* **The Spanish Chronicle of Henry VIII**, translated by M.A.S. Hume.
* **E. Hall's Chronicle**, which contains original letters.
* **C. Wriothesley's Chronicle**.
* **The Negotiations of Thomas Wolsey**, written by Sir W. Cavendish in 1641.

Biographical and thematic studies have also shaped the historical understanding of the era. These include works by A.F. Pollard, P. Friedman, and J.H. Round regarding Anne Boleyn, as well as J.A. Froude's analysis of the divorce of Catherine of Aragon. Additionally, the *Extracts from the Life of Queen Anne Boleigne* by G. Wyat and the 1536 text *Il Successo de la Morte de la Regina de Inghilterra* provide further perspectives on the royal court.

## Legal and Courtly Offices
The administration of the English state during and around the time of Henry VIII involved specific high-ranking offices. The Lord Steward was a central official of the king's household, serving as a peer, privy councillor, and the first dignitary of the court. In a 1539 act of Henry VIII "for placing of the lords," the office was described as the "grand master or lord steward of the king's most honourable household." The Lord Steward presided at the Board of Green Cloth and was supported by the treasurer and comptroller of the household.

Distinct from the Lord Steward was the Lord High Steward of England, the first of the great officers of state. By the time of the Tudors, appointments to this office were typically made for special occasions, such as the coronation of a sovereign or the trial of a peer by his peers. For the latter, a steward *pro hac vice* was appointed to preside over the court of the lord high steward.

## Notable Figures and Controversies
The records highlight several key figures and disputes. Anne Boleyn's relationship with Henry VIII is a subject of varying accounts; for instance, the *Chronicle of King Henry VIII* identifies her as his mistress. There are mentions of a dispensation granted to Henry to marry Anne that suspended canons relating to impediments created by "affinity rising *ex illicito coitu*," though historians like Froude have rejected certain stories associated with this. 

Furthermore, the archives note that a well-known letter attributed to Anne Boleyn is now recognized as an Elizabethan forgery. Other figures of the era mentioned in the archive's bibliographies include Cardinal Wolsey and Thomas Cranmer, the latter of whom provided authority regarding the dating of the royal marriage.
