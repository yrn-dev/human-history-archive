# Duelling in European History

Duelling has been a recurring practice in European history, characterized by varying levels of popularity, legal prohibition, and social acceptance across different centuries and nations.

## Early Origins and Prevalence in France
Duelling was not common before the 16th century. Hallam suggests its rise was linked to the late 15th-century fashion of wearing swords as part of domestic dress. In France, the practice became an epidemic during the reign of Henry III, who was described as eager for excitement. By 1602, the church and magistrates pressured the king into an edict that condemned anyone who gave, accepted, or acted as a second in a challenge to death, though this statute remained a "dead letter" due to public opposition.

Under Henry IV, an edict against duels was issued, yet the king privately favored the practice, once remarking to de Crequi that he would have served as a second if he were not king. The scale of the practice was significant; Fontenay-Mareuil noted that 2,000 noblemen died in duels between 1601 and 1609. To curb this, a court of honour was established in 1609, requiring aggrieved parties to address the king or his representatives for permission to fight.

By the first half of the 17th century, duelling in Paris had become a pervasive social mania. Disputes were often triggered by frivolous pretexts, such as the color of a riband or an embroidered letter. According to De Houssaye, friends meeting in Paris would frequently ask who had fought yesterday or who was to fight today. During this era, the role of the second evolved; Montaigne observed that seconds were no longer merely witnesses but were expected to participate in the fight to prove their courage and affection.

## State Suppression in France
Cardinal Richelieu viewed duelling with active detestation, partly due to a private cause: his elder brother had been stabbed to the heart and killed in a duel by an enemy of the cardinal. While four edicts had been published under Louis XIII with little effect, a new edict in 1626 proved decisive because it was the first to be actually enforced. This edict condemned to death anyone who killed an adversary in a duel or was found guilty of sending a challenge a second time. Lesser offenses were punished with partial confiscation of goods and banishment.

## Duelling in England
Duelling appeared in England approximately one hundred years after it arose in France, remaining rare until the reign of James I. The practice saw a decline during the Commonwealth period, a gap reflected in the literature of the time. However, the Restoration brought a return to the loose manners and gallantry of French society, leading to a resurgence of duels, which became common in Restoration drama.

The practice continued through the reign of Queen Anne and the reigns of the Georges. During the reign of George III, Mr. Turner estimated that 172 duels were fought, 91 of which resulted in death.

## Legal Consequences and Notable Cases
The legal status of duelling in England fluctuated, often leading to severe penalties for participants. In 1808, Major Campbell was executed for killing Captain Boyd. In 1813, despite a duel between Lieutenant Blundell and another principal being conducted with "perfect fairness," the surviving principal and the seconds were all convicted of murder and sentenced to death; though they received a royal pardon, they were all cashiered.

In 1652, Henry Arundell, 3rd Baron Arundell of Wardour, was arrested for acting as a second in a duel in which one of the combatants was killed. When tried in 1653, Arundell attempted to plead his peerage, but this privilege was disallowed because the House of Lords had been abolished.

By 1843, public attention returned to the barbarity of the practice following a duel in which Lieutenant Monro shot Colonel Fawcett. This event led to correspondence between the prince consort and the duke of Wellington. The duke had previously expressed a permissive view of the practice, stating in 1824 that the probability of Hussars fighting a duel or two was "a matter of no consequence." In 1844, the association for the suppression of duelling presented a memorial to Queen Victoria, who expressed a desire to find an expedient to discourage the practice.

## Other Historical Instances
Beyond Western Europe, ancient Greece saw instances of ritualized combat. In a dispute between the Lacedæmonians and Argeians over the possession of Thyrea, a preliminary duel of three hundred took place. Over a century later, the Argeians proposed resolving the dispute through a similar duel. While the Lacedæmonians acquiesced to keep relations smooth, they viewed the proposition as absurd, reflecting a shift in the Grecian political mind toward statesmanlike calculation following the Persian war.
