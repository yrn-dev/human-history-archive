# Francis I and the Events of 1538

The year 1538 was a period of significant diplomatic, artistic, and religious activity involving Francis I, the King of France. His interactions during this time spanned alliances with the Holy Roman Empire, the patronage of Venetian art, and the complex management of religious printing and diplomacy.

## Diplomatic Alliances and Conflict
In the early 16th century, the Turks were making substantial progress in Hungary, having killed the king of Hungary in 1526 and seizing Buda-Pesth. By 1529, Suleiman the Magnificent nearly captured Vienna. While the Emperor sought to unite German princes against this threat, Francis I remained implacable for a period, leading to a new French war. However, in 1538, the Emperor Charles won Francis I over to a more friendly attitude after ravaging the south of France. This shift in diplomacy resulted in Francis and Charles forming an alliance against the Turk.

## Artistic Patronage: Paris Bordone
Francis I's court served as a destination for prominent artists. In 1538, the Venetian painter Paris Bordone was invited to France by the king. Bordone, who had entered the *bottega* of Titian in 1509 and spent his early career imitating the manner of Giorgione, painted many portraits at the court of Francis I. Although no trace of these specific court portraits remains in French collections—as the two works currently at the Louvre were acquired later—Bordone is noted for a nobility of style and a golden harmony of colour derived from Titian.

## The Printing of the Great Bible
The year 1538 also saw Francis I's involvement in the production of an authorized English Bible, known as *The Great Bible*. At the request of Henry VIII, Francis I granted a licence to Francis Regnault, a famous printer in Paris, to undertake the work. Coverdale and Grafton were sent to Paris in 1538 to superintend the printing process. 

The project faced significant opposition; the printers were "dayly threatened," and on December 17, 1538, the Inquisitor-general for France interdicted the progress of the work and ordered the seizure of the entire impression. Despite this, Coverdale and Grafton managed to rescue a large number of finished sheets—some of which had been sold to a haberdasher rather than being burnt—and transported the presses, types, and workmen to England. The volume was eventually completed in London in April 1539.

## Religious and Political Tensions
The French court under Francis I was a site of tension between the Reforming party and the established order. In 1538, the poet Marot, who had been in exile in Ferrara and Venice due to his involvement in the affair of "The Placards," was among the Protestants allowed to return to Paris. This return was permitted after Pope Paul III remonstrated with Francis I regarding the severity of the treatment of Protestants, provided they recanted their errors. Marot abjured his heresy at Lyons and was later given a house and grounds in the suburbs by Francis in 1539.

Additionally, diplomatic friction existed between the French crown and English representatives. Bonner, who superseded Gardiner at Paris in 1538, engaged in conduct so violent that it led Francis I to threaten him with a hundred strokes of the halberd.
