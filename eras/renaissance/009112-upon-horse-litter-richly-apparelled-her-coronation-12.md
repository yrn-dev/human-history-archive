# Coronation and Ceremonial Attire

## The Nature of Coronation
Coronation, derived from the Latin *corona* (crown), is a solemnity used to inaugurate sovereigns into their office. In pre-Christian Europe, the process began with the election of a ruler, who was then raised upon a shield. This ruler was borne on the shoulders of the nation's or tribe's chief men and carried around the assembled people. This specific ritual, known as the *gyratio*, was typically performed three times. Following the *gyratio*, a diadem—a richly wrought band of linen or silk, distinct from a crown—was bound around the ruler's forehead as a symbol of regal authority, and a spear was placed in his hand.

As Europe transitioned to Christianity, these older forms were not abandoned but were supplemented by a religious service of benediction. The Franks, deriving their customs from the Teutons, continued the practice of the *gyratio*; figures such as Pippin, Sigebert, and Clovis were elevated to the royal estate in this manner. Over time, the combination of these religious services and ancient customs developed into later coronation ceremonies.

## English Coronation Traditions
Traces of original functions are believed to be present in several English traditions. These include the ceremonial procession of the king from the Tower to Westminster—a practice that continued until the coronation of James II—and the elevation of the king into the marble chair located in Westminster Hall. Additionally, the universal practice of delivering a sceptre to a new ruler and the tradition of showing the king of France to the people are seen as remnants of these early influences.

The attire for English coronations includes specific robes, one of which is a silk dalmatic serving as the undermost layer. This garment appears to have been borrowed from the church rather than from Eastern emperors. Along with other robes, the dalmatic is intended to symbolize the quasi-sacerdotal character of the kingship.

## Ceremonial and Royal Apparel
Throughout history, rich apparel has been used to denote status, whether in life, ceremony, or death. In the wardrobe of Emperor Charles at Yuste, there were sixteen robes made of velvet and silk, lined with eider down, the soft hair of the Barbary goat, or ermine. 

In other cultures, such as the ancient Scythians, burial customs involved rich attire for the deceased. A Siberian barrow revealed a chief wrapped in a mantle studded with precious stones and broidered with gold, reclining on a sheet of pure gold. Beside him, a female skeleton was found richly attired, also extended upon a sheet of pure gold and adorned with bracelets and a chain of pure gold.

In the context of public spectacle, such as bull-fighting, brilliance of costume is prioritized. *Banderilleros* and *espadas* wear small-clothes and short jackets of satin that are richly embroidered with silver and gold, paired with heelless shoes and light silk stockings.

## Litters and Transport
The use of litters has varied across different cultures and contexts. In the East, a *kafe* is a covered palanquin or litter drawn by two horses, often featuring iron lattice-work; these were generally used to convey the harem of an Eastern monarch.

In ancient Rome, the vestal virgins used litters, and it was forbidden for anyone to pass under one on pain of death. However, the litter also served a darker purpose: a vestal who broke her vow of celibacy was gagged with thongs, placed in a covered litter, and carried through the Forum in silence to be buried alive at the Colline Gate.
