# The Queen's Marie

## Literary Analysis and Oral Tradition
In the study of early popular poetry, Gaston Paris suggests that such works are typically improvised and contemporary with the events they describe. However, when this principle is applied to historical Border ballads—such as "Kinmont Willie," "Jamie Telfer," "Jock o' the Side," and "The Bonny Earl o' Murray"—it becomes evident that the poets often possessed limited or incorrect knowledge of the actual events.

A comparison between the historical Border ballads, particularly those from 1595-1600, and Bain's *Border Papers* (1894-1896) reveals that facts in these ballads are frequently distorted or confused. This suggests they were either composed in later generations based on erroneous oral traditions or, as is the case with *The Queen's Marie*, were later defaced by the fantastic interpolations of reciters. It is probable that the existing form of most border ballads dates no earlier than the generation of 1603-1633, following the union of the crowns. Furthermore, reciters may have been influenced by printed broadsheets or "stall copies."

## Mary, Queen of Scots
Mary, Queen of Scots, previously served as the Queen of France before returning to her native land as a young widow in 1561. Upon her return, she faced theoretical objections to the "regiment of women" from Knox, although he initially hoped for the best and favored her marriage to the heir of the house of Hamilton. Mary placed herself under the guidance of her half-brother, Lord James Stuart (later the Earl of Moray).

During this period, a conflict arose regarding religion. While a proclamation continued the "state of religion," mass was celebrated within the queen's household, defended by Lord James against Protestant intrusion. In dialogues recorded by Knox, Mary argued that subjects should obey their princes, as God commands it, questioning how a doctrine could be of God if it taught people to receive a religion different from that allowed by their princes. Knox countered that right religion derives its authority from the Eternal God alone, rather than worldly princes.

## Marital Conflicts and the Murder of Darnley
Mary's marriage to Darnley was driven primarily by political motives, despite a short period of infatuation. The union was troubled due to Darnley's haughty and foolish behavior, his vicious habits, and his boisterous companions. Darnley was estranged from Mary and on bad terms with other powerful nobles and the regent Murray.

Darnley believed Mary's relationship with David Rizzio was an injury to him as a husband and participated in the murder of the Italian. However, after being cajoled by the queen, Darnley betrayed his fellow conspirators to her, assisting her escape from Holyrood to Dunbar. This betrayal led his companions to distrust and desert him, and he subsequently lost the queen's favor.

In December 1566, while Darnley refused to attend the baptism of his son, James, at Stirling, a band of nobles at Craigmillar plotted to free Mary from her husband. While the initial intent may have been a divorce, the plan shifted toward killing Darnley. After a brief reconciliation and a visit from Mary in Glasgow, where he had fallen ill (possibly by poisoning), Darnley was persuaded to return to Edinburgh. They stayed at Kirk o' Field, a house inside the city walls, until the evening of February 9, 1567.

The murder of Darnley remains a subject of debate, though it is considered highly probable that Mary was an accessory to the crime, which was organized by her third husband and lover, Bothwell. Darnley is noted as the direct ancestor of all English sovereigns since 1603, though he is described as an insignificant character whose fame rests solely on his connection to Mary.

## Political Opposition and Downfall
Sir William Kirkcaldy of Grange, a Scottish politician and former secret agent in France, was among those who opposed Mary's marriage to Darnley. While he was accessory to the murder of Rizzio, he did not participate in the murder of Darnley. Kirkcaldy was later part of a group of lords who sought to rescue Mary after her marriage to Bothwell. Following the fight at Carberry Hill, Mary surrendered to Kirkcaldy, and his generalship led to her defeat at Langside.
