# Indies

The Indies, encompassing both the West and East regions, served as primary theaters for European colonial expansion, naval warfare, and religious missions. These territories were characterized by intense competition between European powers, including Spain, Great Britain, France, Holland, Portugal, and Denmark.

## Naval Warfare and Privateering in the West Indies

The West Indies were a frequent site of conflict, particularly between Great Britain and Spain. In 1739, Great Britain declared war on Spain, leading to a series of combined military and naval operations. Admiral Edward Vernon led a force to assault Spanish colonies in the West Indies, successfully capturing the harbor of Porto Bello in Panama on November 21, 1739. However, this success was limited, as Vernon failed to stop Spanish convoys from reaching Europe. Subsequent efforts, such as the attack on Cartagena in Colombia between March 9 and April 24, 1741, ended in failure with a heavy loss of life, attributed to the incompetence of military officers and the insolence of the admiral.

Privateering played a prominent role in these conflicts. Spanish privateers operated with destructive effect against British trade in both European waters and the West Indies. In other conflicts, the Spaniards carried on privateering in the West Indies with great success, while the French were similarly active in all seas. The British retaliated with vigor, though the total number of captures by Spanish and French corsairs was likely higher than those by the British. This was partly because there were more British merchant ships available to be taken and because the British government had not yet strictly enforced the use of convoys.

Later naval operations in the West Indies involved a British squadron under Sir Charles Knowles attacking the coast of Cuba. On October 1, 1748, Knowles's squadron met a Spanish force under Admiral Regio in the Bahama Channel. The British sought to intercept Spanish convoys laden with bullion from American mines. While the action was indecisive, the advantage lay with Knowles until peace was established in Europe.

## Colonial Possessions and Expansion

By 1815, the distribution of territories in the Indies and surrounding regions reflected the varying ambitions of European powers. The British Empire held Jamaica and several minor slave-labor possessions in the West Indies. France maintained one or two West Indian islands and French Guiana, while Denmark held an island or two in the West Indies.

In the East Indies, Holland maintained various islands and possessions. Spain retained Cuba and a few settlements in the Philippine Islands. In the Indian peninsula, the East India Company ruled approximately one-third of the region and was the only entity showing a significant spirit of expansion during this period.

## Religious Missions and Commerce

The Indies also served as a focal point for the spread of Christianity, specifically through the Jesuit order. The road to success for propagandism in these regions was found by associating it with commerce. In Japan, the Portuguese had established a flourishing trade on Tanegashima. Father Xavier, intending to return to the Indies, took advantage of a Portuguese vessel calling at Yamaguchi. Upon reaching Bungo on the eastern littoral of Kyushu, he found that the ruler, Otomo, was disposed to welcome him.

## Notable Naval Figures in the West Indies

Several high-ranking officers were deployed to the West Indies to manage these volatile regions. Admiral Rodney commanded in the West Indies during a period when he complained of a lack of support and disaffection among his subordinates. To address this, the Admiralty promoted Samuel Hood to rear-admiral on September 26, 1780, and sent him to the West Indies to serve as second in command under Rodney. Hood joined Rodney in January 1781 and remained in the West Indies or on the North American coast until the end of the war.
