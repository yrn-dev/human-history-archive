# Richard Barnfield

Richard Barnfield (1574–1627) was an English poet whose work provides early examples of the influence of Renaissance Italy and the studies of poets such as Shakespeare and Spenser. Born in Norbury, Staffordshire, he was the son of Maria Skrymsher and Richard Barnfield (or Barnefield), who married in April 1572. Barnfield was baptized on June 13, 1574. Following the death of his mother in early 1581 during the birth of a daughter, Barnfield and his siblings were cared for by his mother's unmarried sister, Elizabeth Skrymsher.

## Education and Early Influences
Barnfield matriculated at Brasenose College, Oxford, in November 1589 and received his degree in February 1592. Although he performed the exercise required for his master's gown, he left the university abruptly without completing his M.A. It is conjectured that he arrived in London in 1593, where he may have become acquainted with Spenser, Drayton, and Watson. Additionally, the death of Sir Philip Sidney, which occurred while Barnfield was a schoolboy, strongly affected his imagination and served as an inspiration for some of his earliest poetic works.

## The Affectionate Shepherd
In November 1594, at the age of twenty-one, Barnfield published his first work, *The Affectionate Shepherd*. Published anonymously and dedicated to Penelope, Lady Rich, the work is a florid romance written in two books of six-line stanza. Barnfield later admitted that the poem was a paraphrase or expansion of Virgil's second eclogue, focusing on the complaint of Daphnis for the love of Ganymede. 

The poem is noted as an extraordinary specimen of the licence introduced to England from Italy during the Renaissance. While successful, it faced censure from a moral perspective due to its subject matter. The work is characterized by Barnfield's use of colored touches of nature and picturesque imagery, including "speckled flower call'd sops-in-wine," "fine ruff-footed doves," and the desire to catch the "long-bill'd woodcock" by the "bright glimmering of the starry light."

## Cynthia and the Sonnets
In January 1595, Barnfield published a second volume titled *Cynthia, with certain Sonnets*. Unlike his first work, he signed the preface, which was dedicated to William Stanley, the new earl of Derby, in terms suggesting a close personal relationship. 

The volume contains two primary components:
*   **Cynthia**: A panegyric dedicated to Queen Elizabeth. Written in the Spenserian stanza, it is likely the earliest extant example of this form outside of *The Faerie Queene*.
*   **The Sonnets**: A sequence of twenty sonnets that celebrate the charms of a young man with the initials J. U. or J. V. 

These sonnets are of unusual poetic merit and preceded Spenser's *Amoretti*. They were published fourteen years before Shakespeare's sonnets, yet they are closer to Shakespeare's manner than any other Elizabethan sonnets. The work shows the influence of Drayton's *Idea*, which had appeared a few months prior and also contained amatory sonnets addressed to a young man. While the quality of the poetry is high, the subject matter has been described as preposterous; it has been suggested that if editors were to courageously change the gender of the pronouns, several of these glowing sonnets might take their place among the highest quality works of the period.
