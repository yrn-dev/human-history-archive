# England under Henry VIII and Edward VI

The reigns of Henry VIII and Edward VI were periods of significant administrative change, religious upheaval, and shifts in the social and legal landscape of England. These eras were marked by the centralization of royal authority, the regulation of information, and the restructuring of regional governance.

## Printing and Censorship
The Reformation in England under Henry VIII and Edward VI, combined with religious dissensions occurring on the continent, generated a substantial demand for books. However, the Tudor dynasty did not tolerate a free press and implemented various measures to curb it. In 1529, Henry VIII granted the first patent for the office of king's printer to Thomas Berthelet. Under this system, only books that had been licensed could be printed; the possession or purchase of an unlicensed book was considered a punishable offense.

Later, in 1556, the Company of Stationers was incorporated and given extensive powers to repress "obnoxious books." This trend of censorship continued into subsequent reigns through the Star Chamber. Despite these precautions, the demand for polemical books remained so high that many were printed outside of England and smuggled into the country.

## Regional Governance and the Welsh Marches
Historically, the "marches" referred to the disturbed districts on the borders of Wales. Following the Norman Conquest, lands in these areas were granted to powerful nobles, known as lords marcher, who were tasked with defending the neighboring English counties. These lords held special privileges but often prioritized their own wealth and power over their original duties, leading to rampant lawlessness and rebellion during the reigns of Edward II and Henry III.

A shift toward stability occurred after the Wars of the Roses. In 1542, Henry VIII established a council of Wales and the marches, which resulted in a notable decrease in disorder within the region. By the time of Elizabeth's reign, the Welsh marches had lost their practical significance and became of primarily historical importance.

## Urban Development and Bridewell
During the reign of Henry VIII, the district of Bridewell in London—located between the Thames and Fleet Street—saw significant development. In 1525, Henry VIII constructed a "stately and beautiful house" in this area specifically to house the emperor Charles V and his suite. This residence also served as the home for Henry and Catherine of Aragon during the hearing of their divorce suit by the Cardinals at Blackfriars.

Following Henry's reign, in 1553, Edward VI transferred the property to the city to serve as a penitentiary and a house of correction for "loose women" and vagabonds. The lord mayor and corporation formally took possession of the site in 1555. The building remained until it was largely destroyed during the Great Fire of 1666.

## Economic and Legal Actions
The reign of Henry VIII saw various economic fluctuations and legal mandates. In the borough of Buckingham, Henry VIII granted Sir Henry Marney a Saturday market and two annual fairs in 1522. However, the town eventually suffered economic decay; in an act of 32 Henry VIII, Buckingham was listed as one of thirty-six impoverished towns.

In terms of national security and trade, Henry VIII passed an act making it a felony to deliver, exchange, or sell any horse within Scotland or to any Scottishman. This act was later revived in the first year of Queen Elizabeth's reign before being repealed by James I.

## Notable Figures and Political Transitions
The transition from the reign of Henry VII to Henry VIII was marked by the fall of several ministers. Edmund Dudley, a privy councillor and former speaker of the House of Commons (1504) under Henry VII, was thrown into prison by order of Henry VIII upon the latter's accession in April 1509. Dudley was charged with constructive treason—nominally for ordering friends to assemble in arms during Henry VII's final illness—and was executed in August 1510. While imprisoned, Dudley wrote a treatise supporting absolute monarchy titled *The Tree of Commonwealth*, though it did not reach the king and was not published until 1859.

Under Edward VI, the political landscape continued to evolve, with figures such as Andrew Dudley (son of Edmund Dudley) holding various important posts.
