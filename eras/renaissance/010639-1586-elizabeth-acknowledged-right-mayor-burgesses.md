# The Borough of Arundel

## Early History and Development
Arundel, referred to in early records as Harundell, is first mentioned as early as 877. At that time, King Alfred included the location in his will, leaving it to his nephew Aethelm. During the era of Edward the Confessor, the town's infrastructure appeared to consist of a fortification or earthwork—likely constructed by King Alfred as a defense against the Danes—and a mill. 

By the time of the Norman Conquest, the town had grown in importance and was recorded in the Domesday Book as a port and thriving borough. Following the Conquest, the town was granted to Roger de Montgomery, who utilized the site of the ancient earthwork to build the castle.

## Governance and Legal Status
The governance of Arundel evolved through various grants and acknowledgments of its corporate status. While no early charters have survived, Queen Elizabeth acknowledged in 1586 the right of the burgesses and the mayor to be a body corporate. This acknowledgment included the right to hold four annual fairs, two weekly markets, and a court for pleas under forty shillings. The mayor and burgesses claimed that these specific rights had been exercised from time immemorial.

Later, in 1688, James II confirmed a charter that had been granted two years prior. This act incorporated the borough under a governing structure consisting of a mayor, 12 burgesses, and 4 aldermen.

## Trade and Economy
Arundel was formerly a thriving seaport. The borough established a tradition of commerce early on, with markets held on Saturdays and Thursdays. In 1285, Richard Fitzalan, the earl of Arundel, secured a grant for two annual fairs, which took place on December 17 and May 14.

The town's physical development faced a significant setback in 1338 when fire destroyed half of the town, though it was rebuilt shortly thereafter. In later years, the town's connectivity was expanded when it was linked to London by canal in 1813.

## Parliamentary Representation
The borough's political representation in parliament began in 1302, during which time it returned two members. This arrangement continued for over five centuries until 1832, when the Reform Act reduced the number of members to one. Eventually, in 1868, the borough was completely disfranchised.
