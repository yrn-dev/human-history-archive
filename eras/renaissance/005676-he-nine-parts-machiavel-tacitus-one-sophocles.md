# The Literary and Intellectual Profile of Brooke

The intellectual character of the writer Brooke is defined by a tension between rigid intellect and poetic expression. According to the critic Charles Lamb, Brooke's work is "nine parts Machiavel and Tacitus, for one of Sophocles and Seneca." Lamb asserts that whether one examines Brooke's most passionate love-poems or his plays, the results are "frozen and made rigid with intellect." This intellectual intensity manifests in an obscurity of expression throughout his poetry, which Lamb attributes to the subtlety and intensity of Brooke's thought rather than a lack of verbal lucidity.

## Published Works and Dramas
During his lifetime, Brooke published only four poems and the *Tragedy of Mustapha*. His other works were released posthumously, including a volume of works in 1633, a biography of Sidney in 1652, and a volume of *Remains* in 1670. One of his early poems was an elegy on Sidney, which appeared in *The Phoenix Nest* in 1593.

Brooke authored two tragedies based on the Senecan model: *Alaham* and *Mustapha*. He stated himself that these tragedies were not intended for the stage; consequently, Charles Lamb suggested they be categorized as political treatises.

## Analysis of the Tragedies
The tragedy *Alaham* is set in Ormuz. Its prologue features the ghost of a former king of Ormuz, who reveals a curse destined for a doomed family, establishing a gloom that persists throughout the development of the piece.

The theme of *Mustapha* was borrowed from Madeleine de Scudéry's *Ibrahim ou l'illustre Bassa* and focuses on the ambition of the sultana Rossa. In both plays, the choruses function as philosophical dissertations and often maintain only a slight connection to the rest of the drama. For example, in *Mustapha*, the third chorus is a dialogue between Eternity and Time. The fifth chorus presents an invective against the evils of superstition, followed by a chorus of priests that fails to remove the impression of scepticism established in the first part.

## Biographical Accounts
The archive contains conflicting reports regarding Brooke's personal character. A rhyming elegy on Brooke, published in Huth's *Inedited Poetical Miscellanies*, accuses him of extreme penuriousness. Conversely, there is abundant testimony regarding his generous treatment of writers who were his contemporaries.
