# Massachusetts

Massachusetts has a history marked by the establishment of diverse religious denominations, the development of legal and educational institutions, and significant contributions to the American War of Independence.

## Religious Development and Dissent
The religious landscape of early Massachusetts was characterized by periods of intolerance and the eventual struggle for religious liberty. In 1663, John Myles, a Welsh Baptist and former one of Cromwell's Tryers, sought refuge in Massachusetts from the government of Charles II. Myles and his congregation settled in Rehoboth; although they were permitted to remain after being identified as Baptists, they were required to place their meeting-place at a considerable distance from the standing order. Myles was instrumental in the growth of the Baptist Church in Boston and other areas.

Other early Baptist influences included Thomas Gould of Charlestown, who shared antipaedobaptist views as early as 1654. By May 1665, several English Baptists in the Boston area, along with Gould, formed a church covenant. This group faced severe persecution, including fines, imprisonment, and the denial of the use of their own meeting-house. Despite a 1679 mandate from Charles II rebuking Massachusetts authorities for denying liberties that they themselves had once sought in exile, and the later Act of Toleration (1689) in England, the Boston Baptists struggled for recognition and the right to exist.

In later years, Universalism gained a presence in the state. Hosea Ballou (1771–1852), a former Baptist minister's son, became a pastor in Dana in 1794 and later in Salem (1815–1817) before serving as pastor of the Second Universalist Church in Boston from 1817 until his death. Ballou was a primary expositor of Universalism, opposing trinitarian views, legalism, and Calvinism. His grand-nephew, also named Hosea Ballou (1796–1861), preached in Roxbury and Medford. The younger Ballou urged the necessity of a Universalist denominational college, which led to the establishment of Tufts College in Medford, where he served as the first president from 1853 until shortly before his death in Somerville.

## Legal and Political History
The legal framework of Massachusetts was shaped by figures such as John Lowell (1743–1802). A graduate of Harvard and a representative for Newburyport and Boston in the Massachusetts Assembly, Lowell was a member of the 1779–1780 Constitutional Convention. He secured the inclusion of the clause "all men are born free and equal," which the state supreme court interpreted in 1783 as the abolition of slavery within the state. Lowell later served as a judge for the U.S. District Court of Massachusetts and the U.S. Circuit Court for the First Circuit.

His son, John Lowell (1769–1840), was a prominent political writer known as "The Boston Rebel" and "The Roxbury Farmer." He wrote several pamphlets opposing the Democratic party and French influence, defending impressment and the policies of the Washington administration over those of Jefferson. Later in life, he became known as "the Columella of New England" due to his agricultural interests and served as president of the Massachusetts Agricultural Society.

## Military Contributions and the War of Independence
Massachusetts played a central role in the American War of Independence. Henry Knox, born in Boston, was a prominent member of the colonial militia who attempted to prevent the Boston massacre in 1770. During the war, Knox planned the defenses of the army camps before Boston and transported essential artillery from border forts and Lake George. He served as brigadier-general and chief of artillery in the Continental army, becoming one of General Washington's most trusted advisers. Following the war, Knox served as the first secretary of war under the Federal government from 1785 to 1794.

The town of Marblehead, incorporated in 1649, served as an important commercial port. Following the Boston Port Bill of 1774, it became the port of entry in place of Boston. During the war, Marblehead provided over 1,000 men to the Continental army. The town was the base for "state cruisers," including the "Lee," commanded by John Manley. In November 1775, the "Lee" captured the "Nancy," securing military stores valued at £20,541 for the army at Cambridge. Additionally, General John Glover's "amphibious regiment" from Marblehead provided critical transport for Washington's troops across the East River and the Delaware.

## Education, Arts, and Journalism
Boston has been a center for higher education and the arts. Abbott Lawrence Lowell (1856– ), a graduate of Harvard College and Law School, served as the president of Harvard University starting in 1909, succeeding Charles William Eliot. He also served as the financial head of the Lowell Institute of Boston.

In the arts, Thomas Ball (1819– ), born in Charlestown, became a renowned sculptor. His works in Massachusetts include the equestrian statue of Washington and the statue of Charles Sumner in the Boston public gardens, as well as a statue of Josiah Quincy in City Hall Square and a monument in Methuen.

Journalism in the region was influenced by Maturin Murray Ballou (1820–1895), who edited *Gleason's Pictorial* and *Ballou's Monthly*. In 1872, he became the editor-in-chief and one of the founders of the *Boston Daily Globe*.
