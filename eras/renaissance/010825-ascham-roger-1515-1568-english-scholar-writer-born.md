# ASCHAM, ROGER (c. 1515-1568)

Roger Ascham was an English writer and scholar born around the year 1515 in Kirby Wiske, a village located near Northallerton in the North Riding of Yorkshire. He was the third son of John Ascham, who served as steward to Lord Scrope of Bolton, and a mother named Margaret, who is described as having been well connected. Much of the available information regarding Ascham's early life is derived from Edward Grant, the headmaster of Westminster, who edited Ascham's letters and delivered a panegyrical oration on his life in 1576.

## Education and Early Career
Ascham did not receive a formal school education; instead, he was educated in the home of Sir Humphry Wingfield, a barrister and the 1533 speaker of the House of Commons. Under the guidance of a tutor named R. Bond, Ascham and other children in the household engaged in archery, a sport Sir Humphry encouraged by bringing bows and shafts from London. This experience influenced Ascham's earliest English work, the *Toxophilus*, and his belief in the importance of archery within educational institutions.

Around 1530, at approximately fifteen years of age, Ascham entered St John's College, Cambridge. During his time there, he was influenced by Sir Thomas Smith and John Cheke. He was particularly guided by Robert Pember, who possessed great learning and facility in the Greek tongue. Following Pember's advice, Ascham taught Greek to boys to accelerate his own mastery of the language. In his Latin studies, he focused specifically on Caesar and Cicero. He earned his B.A. on February 18, 1534/5. Despite being elected to a fellowship through the support of the college master, Dr Nicholas Metcalfe, Ascham faced rebuke and punishment after speaking against the Pope.

## Academic and Diplomatic Service
Ascham's academic career at Cambridge continued with his appointment as a letter-writer for his college and the university starting in 1541 or earlier. In 1548, he wrote a notable letter to the protector Somerset regarding the endowment of Sedbergh school. Upon the retirement of Sir John Cheke in 1546, Ascham was elected the university's public orator.

Ascham later served as a Greek reader for an ambassador during an embassy that visited Venice, Innsbruck, and Louvain. During this period, he read Greek with the ambassador four or five days a week. His letters from this embassy were published in English in 1553 as a "Report" on Germany. Following the accession of Queen Mary, Ascham was appointed Latin secretary to the Queen through Bishop Gardiner, receiving a pension of £20 per year. Although he was a Protestant, he maintained his position, possibly because he had never been in holy orders.

## Later Life and Royal Tutelage
On June 1, 1554, Ascham married Margaret Howe, the niece of Sir R. Wallop; the couple had two sons. Ascham frequently complained of poverty, though he held various lucrative leases of crown and ecclesiastical property. 

In 1555, Ascham resumed his role as a tutor to Princess Elizabeth, teaching her the orations of Aeschines and Demosthenes' *De Corona* in Greek. After Elizabeth's accession, Ascham was granted the prebend and canonry of Wetwang in York minster on October 5, 1559, despite being a layman. Ascham also held high regard for the learning of other contemporaries, ranking Mildred Cooke (wife of William Cecil) and Lady Jane Grey as the two most learned ladies in the kingdom.

## The Scholemaster and Literary Contribution
Ascham is most famous for his work *The Scholemaster*, which he began in 1563. The impetus for the book occurred on December 10, 1563, during a "great plague" in London while the court was at Windsor. During a dinner with other ministers and the secretary of state, Sir William Cecil, a discussion arose regarding reports that scholars at Eaton had fled their school to avoid beatings. Ascham acted as the champion for the "anti-floggers" in a debate over the use of corporal punishment in education. This position was further reinforced when Sir Richard Sackville shared that the brutality of a "fond schoolmaster" had caused him to hate learning.

In the context of the development of early modern English prose, Ascham's writing is characterized as "unstudied speech." His *Schoolmaster* is regarded as the most typical English book of Renaissance culture since *Utopia*.
