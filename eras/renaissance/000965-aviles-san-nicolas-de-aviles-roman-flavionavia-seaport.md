# AVILES

Aviles, also known as San Nicolas de Aviles, is a seaport located in northern Spain within the province of Oviedo. Historically referred to as *Flavionavia* during the Roman period, the town is situated on the Bay of Aviles, which is a winding inlet of the Bay of Biscay. It is located 24 miles west of Gijon by rail. As of 1900, the population of Aviles was 12,763.

## Geography and Urban Character
The town is described as old-fashioned and picturesque, featuring several Gothic churches and ancient palaces. The Bay of Aviles is characterized by a narrow landward extremity that is crossed by a fine bridge. Along the littoral of the region, which spans a distance of 130 miles, Aviles and Gijon serve as the only important harbours.

## Economy and Industry
The bay serves as a port of call for numerous coasting vessels and acts as the headquarters for a fishing fleet. The local economy is supported by several industrial activities:
*   **Mining and Export:** Coal from the mines of Oviedo is exported via the port. In 1904, shipments from Aviles reached more than 290,000 tons, exceeding the shipments from Gijon for the first time.
*   **Manufacturing:** The town produces coarse linen, woollen stuffs, and glass.
*   **Natural Resources:** There are valuable stone quarries located in the neighbourhood of the town.

## Pedro Menendez de Aviles
Aviles is the birthplace of Pedro Menendez de Aviles (1519–1574), a Spanish seaman and the founder of St Augustine, Florida. Born on February 15, 1519, into a gentry family, he was one of nineteen siblings. At age fourteen, he went to sea, spending the next sixteen years as a corsair.

### Naval and Imperial Service
In 1549, Emperor Charles V commissioned Aviles to clear the Canaries and the north coast of Spain of French pirates. By 1554, he was appointed captain-general of the *flota* (the convoy carrying trade between America and Spain), an appointment made by the emperor despite the opposition of the *Casa de Contratacion* (the governing board of American trade). During this period, he also accompanied the prince of Spain, the future Philip II, to England for his marriage to Queen Mary.

Aviles was noted for his diligence and success in returning treasure to the emperor. However, his refusal to accept bribes for permitting regulation breaches made him unpopular with merchants, and his high-handed nature further strained his relationship with the *Casa de Contratacion*. Between 1557 and 1559, he served in Flanders, transporting recruits and money from Spain during the war with France. He later commanded the fleet that returned Philip II to Spain from the Low Countries. Following another successful voyage to America in 1560 and 1561, he was arrested by the *Casa de Contratacion* in 1563 and imprisoned for twenty months on unknown charges.

### Florida Colony and Conflict
Following his release, Philip II entered into an *asiento* (contract) with Aviles on March 20, 1565. Philip II advanced 15,000 ducats to Aviles, naming him the proprietor of any colony he could establish in Florida, provided the money was repaid. This move was intended to prevent French Huguenots from planting a colony in a region the Spaniards claimed and which sat on the trade route from Mexico.

Aviles sailed on July 28, 1565, with 1,500 men, ten sloops, and one 600-ton vessel. He entered and named the Bay of St Augustine on August 28 and began constructing a fort. On September 20, 1565, he captured the French post of Fort Caroline. In October, he exterminated a group of Frenchmen led by the Huguenot Jean Ribault. Aviles hung the bodies of nearly all the prisoners from trees with the inscription, "Not as Frenchmen but as Lutherans."

In 1568, a French sea-captain, Dominique de Gourgues, retaliated by capturing Fort San Mateo (the renamed Fort Caroline) and hanging the garrison with the inscription, "Not as Spaniards but as murderers."

Aviles remained in Florida until 1567, returned to Spain, and later made one final voyage to Florida. He died on September 17, 1574. He was married young to Maria de Solis and had three daughters. His personal letters describe him as a high-minded and pious officer who viewed the Frenchmen he killed as heretics and pirates.
