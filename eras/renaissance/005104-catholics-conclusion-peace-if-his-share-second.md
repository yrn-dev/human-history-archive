## François de Beauvais, Seigneur de Briquemault
François de Beauvais, Seigneur de Briquemault (c. 1502–1572), was a prominent leader of the Huguenots during the first religious wars. Born around 1502 to Adrien de Briquemault and Alexane de Sainte Ville, he began his military career in the Piedmontese wars under the count of Brissac. Upon returning to France in 1554, he joined Admiral Coligny.

In 1562, Briquemault was tasked with the defense of Rouen, though he eventually resigned this position in favor of Montgomery. Following this, he traveled to England, where he concluded the treaty of Hampton Court on September 20. After returning to France, he took Dieppe from the Catholics before peace was concluded. 

While his role in the second religious war was considered less important, Briquemault was highly active in the third. His military contributions included fighting at Montcontour, Roche-Abeille, and Jarnac, as well as assisting in the siege of Poitiers. He nearly faced capture by Catholics at Bourg-Dieu and was involved in efforts to re-victual Vézelay and surprise Bourges. In 1570, he was charged by Coligny to stop the army of the princes in the Rhone valley, crossing Burgundy to join the admiral at St. Étienne in May. He assisted in the victory of Arnay-le-Duc on June 21 and later negotiated a marriage between Elizabeth of England and the prince of Navarre.

Briquemault's life ended during the night of St Bartholomew, when he took refuge in the house of the English ambassador but was arrested. He was delivered to the parlement alongside his friend Arnaud da Cavagnes. Despite seeking to escape death through "unworthy means" when facing his judges, he was condemned on October 27, 1572, to the confiscation of his property and the last penalty. He and Cavagnes were executed on October 29.

## Catholic Policy in Bavaria
In the 19th century, King Louis of Bavaria, who ascended the throne on March 10, 1864, maintained a complex relationship with the Catholic Church. While Louis remained personally true to the Catholic Church, he sought a greater degree of independence from the Vatican. 

His ecclesiastical policy included maintaining friendly relations with Ignaz von Döllinger, a leader of the liberal Catholics who opposed the definition of papal infallibility. However, Louis did not extend his protection to the Old Catholics, who represented the anti-Roman movement.

Louis's reign was also marked by political struggle. In the war of 1866, he and his minister von der Pfordten sided with Austria. At the conclusion of peace on August 22, Bavaria was forced to agree to the foundation of the North German Confederation under Prussian leadership and surrender small portions of its territory. This outcome wounded the king's Bavarian patriotism, leading him to write a letter of reconciliation to King William of Prussia on August 10.

## Legal Actions Against Catholics in England
In England, Sir Thomas Lucy (1532–1600), a Warwickshire squire, demonstrated significant zeal against Catholics during his tenure as a justice of the peace. This opposition was manifested in his active participation in the arrest of Edward Arden in 1583. Lucy, who was knighted in 1565 and served as high sheriff of the county in 1586, was traditionally associated with the youth of William Shakespeare.
