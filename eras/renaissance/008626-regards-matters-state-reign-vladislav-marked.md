# The Reign of Vladislav in Bohemia

The reign of Vladislav (also referred to as Wladislaus or Ladislaus) was characterized by a significant shift in the distribution of power within Bohemia, marked by a decline in royal authority and a corresponding rise in the influence of the nobility.

## Accession and Early Conflict
Vladislav, the son of Casimir, King of Poland, was offered the succession to the Bohemian throne by George of Podebrad, who sought aid from Poland in his struggle against King Matthias. Following the death of George, a period of protracted negotiations occurred because many Bohemian nobles supported the claim of Matthias of Hungary. On May 27, 1471, Prince Vladislav was elected at Kutna Hora. This election represented a victory for the national party and reflected the strong anti-clerical sentiment in Bohemia; while Matthias was an unconditional adherent of Rome, the Polish envoys representing Vladislav promised that the king would maintain the Compacts.

The beginning of Vladislav's reign was defined by a struggle with Matthias of Hungary over the Bohemian throne. This desultory warfare lasted until 1478, when the treaty of Olmutz secured Bohemia for Vladislav. Under the terms of this treaty, Matthias retained the "lands of the Bohemian crown"—specifically Lusatia, Silesia, and Moravia—during his lifetime, with the agreement that they would be restored to Bohemia upon his death.

## State Matters and Social Changes
In matters of state, Vladislav's reign saw a decrease in the royal prerogative. The power of the nobility reached an unprecedented height, which occurred at the expense of the royal power as well as the rights of peasants and townsmen. 

A decree issued in 1487 had a profound impact on the future of Bohemia by practically establishing serfdom in a region where it had previously been almost unknown. This measure stripped the rulers of the country of the ability to rely on the independent and sturdy peasantry that had formed the primary basis of the armies of the Prokops and Zizka.

Furthermore, the rights of Bohemian townsmen were curtailed through various enactments. These changes were codified in a decree known as the "regulations of King Vladislav." This document enumerated the rights of knights and nobles but entirely ignored those of the towns. It operated on the tacit assumption that townsmen possessed no inherent rights, only privileges that could be granted by the sovereign with the consent of the knights and nobles.

## Religious Policy and Civil Discord
Although Vladislav remained faithful to his promise to maintain the Compacts and did not stop Bohemians from receiving communion in both kinds, his general policy was reactionary. In religious controversies and state matters, he appointed government officials in Prague from the section of the Utraquist party that was closest to Rome.

This reactionary stance led to the severe persecution of the Bohemian Brethren, who were known as the extreme Hussites. This resulted in serious riots in Prague, where advanced Hussites stormed three town halls. In response to the menaced reaction, nobles of the same faith formed a league. Peace was temporarily restored following a meeting of all the estates at Kutna Hora in 1485.

## Later Life and Succession
In 1490, Vladislav was elected King of Hungary. In 1502, he married Anna de Candale, who had connections to the royal family of France. They had two children: Louis and Anna (who later married Archduke Ferdinand of Austria). Vladislav died in Hungary in 1516.

He was succeeded by his son, Louis, who had been crowned King of Bohemia at age three. Per Vladislav's instructions, Emperor Maximilian I and Sigismund, King of Poland, served as guardians for the young king. While the Bohemian estates recognized this arrangement, they refused to allow the guardians to interfere in Bohemian affairs. Consequently, the country was ruled almost without control by the great Bohemian nobles, specifically the supreme burgrave, Zdenek Leo, lord of Rozmital.
