# Afranio's Phagotus

The phagotus of Afranio was a musical instrument designed by Afranio, a canon of Ferrara. The instrument's existence and design are documented in a work by Afranio's nephew, Theseus Ambrosius Albonesius, titled *Introductio in Chaldaicam Linguam ... et descriptio ac Simulacrum Phagoti Afranii*, published in Pavia in 1539. This text provides a detailed description of the instrument on pages 33-38 and includes illustrations showing both front and back views on page 179.

## Construction and Design
The phagotus of Afranio was constructed of two similar twin columns, each featuring a base and a capital. These columns contained keys and finger-holes. The instrument's layout included additional columns for different purposes: a shorter column was positioned in front between the two main columns for ornament, while another even shorter column was located at the back. The capital of this rear column could be lifted to allow the insertion of a bag-pipe or a sort of bellows, which served as the means by which the instrument was sounded.

The first physical version of the instrument was constructed by Ravilius of Ferrara, who followed Afranio's specific design.

## Classification and Comparison
Although the name "phagotus" is similar to "fagotto" or "bassoon," the detailed descriptions and illustrations of Afranio's device indicate that it was not related to the bassoon. The author of the 1539 work, Theseus Albonesius, expressed significant puzzlement regarding the etymology of the word.

In terms of physical resemblance, the phagotus most closely resembles a musical curiosity known as the *flute-a-bec a colonne*, which G. Chouquet assigned to the 16th century. The *flute-a-bec a colonne* consisted of a single stopped column with a base and capital, utilizing perforated brass boxes in the upper and lower parts of the column to conceal the whistle and vent. Afranio's phagotus can be viewed as a double version of this type of instrument, played by bellows.

Mersenne further categorized Afranio's invention not as a bassoon, but as a different kind of fagotto. He classed it with the Neapolitan *sourdeline*, which is a complicated variety of musette. According to Mersenne, the instrument consisted of two *bassons* interconnected by tubes and blown by bellows, noting that, similar to the *sourdeline*, these only speak when the keys (springs) are open.

## Historical Context and Etymology
The phagotus of Afranio is described as having had a "short fabulous existence" and is characterized as a "mere freak" of an age defined by ingenious mechanical devices applied to musical instruments. Many such devices did not survive beyond the lifetime of their inventors.

The use of the term *Phagoto d'Afranio* and its associated pretended Greek etymology suggest that another form of *fagotto* already existed in Italy and was known to Afranio. It is concluded that the name *fagotto* was likely a satirical name bestowed in Italy before 1555. While the French equivalent *fagot* was not used for the *basson*, the satirical wit of courtiers may have applied the term *fagotto* to the doucaine. This theory is supported by a 1426 document from the archives published by Edm. van der Straeten, which records that Louis Willay, an instrument maker from Bruges, sold a triple set of wood-wind instruments—including four doucaines, four bombardes, and four flutes—as a gift to Nicolas III, the marquis of Ferrara.
