# February 1631 and the Bastille

The historical records for February 1631 and the Bastille involve disparate events and figures, ranging from the final sermons of English clergy to the function of the Bastille as a state prison and a symbol of French power.

## Events of February 1631
In February 1631, John Donne, the Dean of St Paul's, returned to London following a period of severe illness. His health had broken down completely in 1630, leading him to spend much of that winter at Abury Hatch in Epping Forest with his widowed daughter, Constance Alleyn. It is believed his condition was a malarial form of recurrent quinsy affecting a neurotic system. Despite this, he was able to preach at Whitehall on February 12, 1631. This final sermon was later published after his death under the title *Death's Duel*.

In France, the records of the *Chapelle-Musique* of the kings indicate that by 1631, the court employed more than one cornet player; while Marcel Cayty had held the post since 1604, Jean Daneau is also mentioned as a player in 1631.

## The Bastille as a State Prison
The Bastille served as a place of detention for various individuals across different eras of French history. In the mid-17th century, Godin de Sainte-Croix, a cavalry officer, was imprisoned there for one year via a *lettre de cachet* secured by M. d'Aubray. It is popularly suggested that while held in the Bastille, Sainte-Croix acquired knowledge of poisons from a fellow prisoner, an Italian poisoner named Exili. This knowledge was later used by Sainte-Croix and his mistress, the Marquise de Brinvilliers, to poison the Marquise's father in February 1666 and her two brothers in 1670.

In a later period, the French general Charles Francois Dumouriez was also imprisoned in the Bastille. During his six-month incarceration, he occupied himself with literary pursuits before being moved to Caen, where he remained until Louis XVI ascended to the throne.

## The Fall of the Bastille
The Bastille was a formidable fortress characterized by immense defensive capabilities. Its walls were ten feet thick at the top of the towers and reached thirty to forty feet at the base, making it capable of resisting cannon fire. The fortress was well-supplied with munitions and provisions, and its batteries were positioned to fire down upon Paris, specifically the Faubourg St. Antoine and the Marais.

Despite being considered imprenable without a regular siege involving several days of artillerie, the Bastille was attacked by the people of Paris. This attack is described not as a reasonable act, but as an act of faith, driven by a collective impulse and the sounding of the tocsin.
