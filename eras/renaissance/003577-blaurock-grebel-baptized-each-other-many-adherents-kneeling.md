# The Origins of Anabaptism in Zurich

The Anabaptist movement began in Zurich through the actions of a small group of individuals, including Konrad Grebel (d. 1526), Felix Manz (d. 1527), Simon Strumpf, and Wilheld Reubli (1480-1554). This group separated from Zwingli with the intention of establishing a separate church.

## Core Doctrines and Beliefs
The early Anabaptists advocated for a community based on the model of primitive Christianity, rejecting all traditions and accretions that appeared later than the records of the New Testament. Their goal was to return to a simple church founded on brotherly love. Central to their faith was the belief in religious feeling, conscience, or "inner light" as the sole true ground of religion.

The group held several distinct positions:
* **Non-violence:** They repudiated the use of force and believed that true Christians are like sheep among wolves, who must not defend themselves.
* **Social and Legal Independence:** They advocated for a scriptural communism of goods and asserted that Christians should remain independent of worldly tribunals, exercising patience and love toward one another.
* **Baptism:** Their most radical doctrine was the rejection of infant baptism (paedobaptism), which they viewed as unscriptural. They maintained that baptism should be reserved for believers only.

## The Institution of Anabaptism
The movement gained rapid momentum, attracting adherents such as Hans Broedli, the pastor of Zollikon. However, the refusal to baptize infants and the attempt to form a separate church led to condemnation by Zwingli and the subsequent banishment of several members.

This banishment served as the catalyst for the definitive institution of "Anabaptism." Joerg Blaurock, Konrad Grebel, and others took the step of baptizing one another, after which they partook in the Lord's Supper together. The physical act of baptism for Blaurock, Grebel, and many of their adherents took place in an ordinary room, where they knelt together. The mode of baptism used by the Anabaptists was not immersion, but rather pouring or sprinkling. For example, Balthasar Hubmaier (ca. 1480-1528), a scholarly saint who joined the movement, baptized 300 converts in 1525 using a single bucket.

## The Lord's Supper and Community
The Anabaptists practiced the simple breaking of bread. Their doctrine regarding the Lord's Supper was that brothers and sisters in Christ should partake in remembrance of the death of Christ. Through this act, they sought to renew the bond of brotherly love, which served as the basis for their neighborly life.

## Persecution and Later Developments
Persecution hindered the movement's ability to develop into a homogeneous, self-contained community, meaning they never had the opportunity to adopt a common confession. While the early leaders—such as Blaurock, Grebel, Hubmaier, and the pious Hans Denck (ca. 1495-1527)—preached non-resistance, later developments took a different turn.

Melchior Hofmann (ca. 1498-1543 or 1544) carried Anabaptist teachings to Holland. In Haarlem, a preacher named Jan Matthisson (or Matthyszoon), who prophesied the establishment of the kingdom of heaven and the end of the world, gained followers and sent representatives to Muenster. In Muenster, an attempt was made to realize Matthisson's ideals; by 1533, all who did not embrace Anabaptism were driven out, and Bernt Knipperdolling (ca. 1495-1536) became burgomaster. 

Following the death of Matthisson in early 1534, John of Leiden (Johann Bockelson, 1510-1536) took leadership. Under his rule, the town became a site of cruelty and "grossest licence" until it was taken by a besieging bishop in 1535. Because of these events, the Anabaptists are often remembered for the crimes of John of Leiden and the revelry at Muenster, rather than the teachings and martyrdom of their original leaders.
