# A Divine Poem

*A Divine Poem* is a literary work published in 1601 by the English poet Nicholas Breton. The publication is noted for including the poems "The Ravisht Soul" and "The Blessed Weeper."

## Author and Context
Nicholas Breton (1545?–1626) was an English poet from an old family based at Layer-Breton, Essex. The son of William Breton and Elizabeth Bacon, he was likely born in the parish of St Giles without Cripplegate at a mansion house in Red Cross Street. While there is no official record of his university attendance, the diary of the Rev. Richard Madox indicates that Breton was "once of Oriel College" and was present in Antwerp in 1583.

Breton was a popular writer among his contemporaries, though he was largely forgotten by the following generation. His body of work is diverse, comprising satires, pastoral poems, miscellaneous prose tracts, and religious poetry. His religious works, including *A Divine Poem*, are described as expressions of a devout and earnest mind, though they are sometimes characterized as wearisome due to an excess of sweetness and fluency. Although his references to Mary Magdalene and his praise of the Virgin led some to suggest he was a Catholic, his prose writings provide abundant proof that he was an ardent Protestant.

## Literary Career and Patronage
For a significant portion of his career, Breton found a patron in Mary, countess of Pembroke, writing many works in her honor until approximately 1601, the same year *A Divine Poem* was published, at which time she appears to have withdrawn her favor. 

Breton's output was prolific. His poetical works include early titles such as *The Workes of a Young Wit* (1577) and *A Floorish upon Fancie* (1577), as well as later works like *The Passionate Shepheard* (1604) and *The Honour of Valour* (1605). In addition to *A Divine Poem*, Breton published other religious-themed works in 1601, including *The Soules Heavenly Exercise* and *An Excellent Poem, upon the Longing of a Blessed Heart*. He continued to write religious poetry into 1605 with *The Soules Immortall Crowne*.

His prose contributions include twenty-two tracts, such as *Wit's Trenchmour* (1597) and *A Poste with a Packet of Mad Letters* (1603). The latter work contains letters signed "N.B." that are considered autobiographical, detailing a life marked by hardships, including imprisonment, sickness, and the experience of being wounded in wars.

## Editorial History
The works of Nicholas Breton were later preserved and reprinted through the efforts of Alexander Balloch Grosart (1827–1899), a Scottish divine and literary editor. Dr. Grosart, driven by a strong interest in Puritan theology, sought to reprint rare Elizabethan literature. As part of his "Chertsey Worthies Library" series, Grosart produced editions of the works of Nicholas Breton, making them accessible to a newer audience.
