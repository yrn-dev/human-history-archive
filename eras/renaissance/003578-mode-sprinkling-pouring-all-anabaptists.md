# The Anabaptists

The Anabaptists were a body of Christians who emerged as a radical anti-ecclesiastical movement during the spiritual upheavals of the 15th and 16th centuries. Characterized by a revolt against authority, the movement is distinguished primarily by its rejection of infant baptism (paedobaptism) as unscriptural, asserting instead that baptism should be administered to believers only.

## Origins and Early Development
The Anabaptist movement proper began in Zuerich when Konrad Grebel (d. 1526), Felix Manz (d. 1527), Simon Strumpf, and Wilheld Reubli (1480-1554) separated from Zwingli to establish a separate church. This new community sought to model itself after primitive Christianity, rejecting traditions and accretions that appeared after New Testament records. The movement grew rapidly, attracting scholarly figures such as Hans Denck (ca. 1495-1527) and Balthasar Hubmaier (ca. 1480-1528).

The definitive institution of "Anabaptism" occurred when Joerg Blaurock, Konrad Grebel, and others, having been banished following the condemnation of Zwingli, baptized one another and partook of the Lord's Supper together.

## Doctrines and Practices
The central article of faith linking the Anabaptists to the Zwickau prophets was a belief in religious feeling, conscience, or "inner light" as the sole true ground of religion. They maintained that true Christians are like sheep among wolves and must not defend themselves. Early Anabaptists repudiated the use of force, advocated for a scriptural communism of goods, and believed that Christians should exercise love and patience toward one another, remaining independent of worldly tribunals.

Regarding the ordinance of baptism, the early Anabaptists did not practice immersion. Instead, the mode of administration was pouring or sprinkling. For example, Blaurock and Grebel baptized adherents while kneeling in ordinary rooms, and Hubmaier baptized 300 converts from a single bucket.

Other generally held doctrines included:
*   **The Lord's Supper:** The practice of breaking bread in remembrance of the death of Christ to renew the bond of brotherly love.
*   **Justification:** A rejection of the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith, which they viewed as one-sided.

## The Muenster Rebellion
The movement's history is marked by a contrast between the piety of its early leaders and the violence at Muenster. After Melchior Hofmann (ca. 1498-1543/1544) brought Anabaptist teachings to Holland, Jan Matthisson (Matthyszoon) began prophesying the establishment of the kingdom of heaven and a speedy end to the world. 

Matthisson's followers, including Boekebinder and de Kniper, established a presence in Muenster, where all who did not embrace Anabaptism were driven out in 1533. Bernt Knipperdolling (ca. 1495-1536) became burgomaster. Following Matthisson's death in 1534, Johann Bockelson (John of Leiden) took leadership. John of Leiden established a theocracy with twelve elders, a structure suggested by the tribes of Israel, before declaring himself king of the "new Zion." His rule was characterized by severity, pomp, and gross license. The city was retaken by Prince-Bishop Franz von Waldeck on June 25, 1535. John of Leiden was executed on January 22, 1536, and his body was hung in a cage from the tower of the Lamberti church.

## Anabaptism in England
Anabaptist views entered England largely as a foreign importation. Records from London indicate that in 1535, ten Dutchmen identified as Anabaptists were put to death. In 1536, King Henry VIII issued a proclamation labeling Anabaptist opinions as "detestable heresies." Further persecutions occurred in 1538, when four Dutch Anabaptists bore faggots at Paul's Cross and others were burnt in Smithfield. By 1550, Joan Boucher of Kent was burnt for her views regarding the Virgin Mary.

In the late 16th century, Anabaptist communities existed in London, Kent, and East Anglia, with Robert Cooke as a notable representative. Later, in 1609, John Smith (Smyth), under Mennonite influence in Amsterdam, baptized himself and others—including Thomas Helwys—likely using the Anabaptist or Mennonite method of pouring. This group formed the first English Baptist Church, which advocated for absolute liberty of conscience and the belief that the magistrate should not compel any form of religion.

## Relationship to Modern Baptists
While the Anabaptists raised the question of baptism in England, the rise of modern Baptist churches was independent of them. Modern Baptists are distinguished by their insistence on immersion, a mode not used by the early Anabaptists. Some groups, such as the Mennonites, continue to practice pouring or sprinkling. In 17th-century England, General Baptists (influenced by Mennonite Anabaptists) were Arminian, while Particular Baptists (springing from Independents) were Calvinist. Though the Particular Baptists were sometimes falsely called Anabaptists, they were a distinct group.
