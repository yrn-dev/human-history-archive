# Congregation of the Saints and Related Historical Records

The archive's records regarding the "Congregation of the Saints" and related ecclesiastical and historical figures from the late 16th century include details on Puritan separatism, French legal scholarship, and the evolution of church rituals and texts.

## Henry Barrowe and the Separatist Movement
Henry Barrowe (born approximately 1550 in Shipdam, Norfolk) was an English Puritan and Separatist. Related by marriage to the lord keeper Bacon and likely to Bishop Aylmer of London, Barrowe matriculated at Clare Hall, Cambridge, in November 1566 and graduated B.A. in 1569-1570. Following a period of a "frivolous if not licentious life" at court and several years at Gray's Inn starting in 1576, he was deeply affected by a sermon around 1580 or 1581. This led him to a period of study and meditation that resulted in his adoption of the strictest form of Puritanism.

Barrowe became closely associated with the Separatist leader John Greenwood and the "brethren of the Separation" in London. On November 19, 1586, while visiting the imprisoned Greenwood in the Clink, Barrowe was detained and brought before Archbishop Whitgift. He refused to give bail or take the *ex officio* oath, leading to his commitment to the Gatehouse. In May 1587, he and Greenwood were indicted for recusancy under an act originally intended for Papists. Barrowe remained a prisoner for nearly six years, spending time in the Fleet Prison. During an examination before the privy council on March 18, 1588, he vigorously defended the principle of separatism and denounced the prescribed rituals of the Church.

## The Marprelate Controversy (1588–1589)
During 1588 and 1589, a "war of pamphlets" known as the Marprelate Controversy took place between defenders of the Established Church and a Puritan writer using the pseudonym "Martin Marprelate." These tracts utilized a plain, homely style and pungent wit to launch violent personal attacks against Anglican dignitaries, specifically targeting the Episcopacy.

The pamphlets were produced at a secret press established by the Welsh puritan John Penry and printer Robert Waldegrave. Key publications included:
*   ***The Epistle*** (November 1588): A response to Dr. John Bridges' *A Defence of the Government established in the Church of Englande*.
*   ***The Epitome***: A second tract containing more serious arguments.
*   ***Hay any Worke for Cooper*** (March 1589): A reply to Bishop Thomas Cooper's *An Admonition to the People of England*.

To counter these, the ecclesiastical authorities commissioned writers such as Robert Greene, Thomas Nashe, and John Lyly to respond in a similar railing style. This resulted in works such as *Pappe with an Hatchet* (September 1589), attributed to Lyly, and *An Almond for a Parrat*.

## Jerome Bignon and 16th-Century Scholarship
Jerome Bignon (1589–1656) was a precocious French lawyer born in Paris. By age ten, he had acquired a vast amount of knowledge under his father's tuition. In 1600, he published *Chorographie, ou description de la Terre Sainte*, which brought him to the attention of Henry IV. Bignon served as a companion to the duc de Vendome and as tutor to the dauphin (the future Louis XIII).

His subsequent works included *Discours de la ville de Rome* (1604) and *Traite sommaire de l'election du pape* (1605). In 1610, he wrote a treatise on the precedency of the kings of France, and in 1613, he edited the *Formulae* of the jurist Marculfe. Bignon held several high offices, including advocate-general to the grand council (1620) and advocate-general to the parlement of Paris (1626). In 1642, Richelieu appointed him to lead the royal library, a position he held until his death in 1656.

## Ecclesiastical Rituals and the Lives of Saints
Historical records detail various traditions regarding the congregation and the veneration of saints. In the early Church, public penance required sinners to appear before the congregation in ashes and sackcloth. This evolved into the custom of *dies cinerum* (Ash Wednesday), which was likely established by the 8th century and mentioned by the Anglo-Saxon homilist Aelfric in his *Lives of the Saints* (996 or 997).

In later centuries, a movement of revision occurred in France during the 17th and 18th centuries. Figures such as Louis Sébastien le Nain de Tillemont and Jean de Launoy ("le dénicheur des saints") exposed the falsity of numerous lives of the saints. This movement, supported by the Port Royal school, emphasized communion with God over the invocation of saints. This led to the creation of reformed French Breviaries, such as the Paris Breviary of 1680, which required antiphons and responses to be based strictly on the words of Scripture.
