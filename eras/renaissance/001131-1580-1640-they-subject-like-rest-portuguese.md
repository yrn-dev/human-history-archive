# Portugal and its Territories (1580–1640)

Between 1580 and 1640, the Portuguese state and its overseas possessions experienced a period of significant political transition and external conflict. This era was defined by the union between Portugal and Spain, which began in 1580-81, rendering Portugal subject to the crown of Spain.

## Impact of the Spanish Union
The union with Spain had immediate consequences for international trade and diplomacy. Prior to 1580-81, the Dutch had served as the primary carriers of eastern produce from Lisbon to northern Europe. However, the Spanish king shut the Dutch out of the Portuguese trade. This exclusion drove the Dutch to seek their own routes to the East to recover their losses. After unsuccessful attempts to find a northern route through Europe and Asia, the Dutch decided to use the route via the Cape of Good Hope to reach the Spice Islands of the Malay Archipelago. This led to the first Dutch expedition under Cornelius Houtman, which sailed on April 2, 1595.

## Macao and Relations with China and Japan
In the Far East, the Portuguese maintained a presence in Macao. By 1580, Gregory XIII had constituted a bishopric there, and a senate was organized in 1583. Despite these developments, the Portuguese remained largely under Chinese control; the Chinese maintained their territorial rights and authority through mandarins, who insisted that even European criminals be handed over to them. This state of affairs continued until 1849.

During the early 17th century, the Portuguese in Macao attempted to maintain commercial relations with Japan. These efforts continued until 1640, when a diplomatic mission ended in tragedy. Four respected citizens of Macao were sent to Nagasaki as ambassadors with petitions and presents to renew trade. Instead, they were imprisoned. Because they refused to abjure the Christian faith to save their lives, the four ambassadors and fifty-seven companions were decapitated. Only thirteen survivors were permitted to return to Macao to report the events. They carried a document stating that any Christian bold enough to come to Japan—including the God of the Christians or King Philip himself—would pay for it with his head.

## Conflict in the Indian Seas and Persia
The Portuguese monopoly of trade in the Indian seas, particularly the possession of Hormuz, led to increasing friction with the English. The English company's efforts to open traffic with Persia embittered the Portuguese. Shah Abbas, who resented the Portuguese occupation of Hormuz, sought English naval assistance.

Between 1620 and 1621, English and Portuguese ships engaged in several actions in the Indian seas. In December 1621, the English council at Surat decided to engage in what was effectively a maritime war with the Portuguese flag. The English joined with Imam Kuli Khan, the governor of Fars, in an expedition against Portuguese holdings. After attacking a fort at Kishm, English vessels aided in the siege of Hormuz, which began on February 18, 1622. Following a ten-week defense, the Portuguese surrendered on May 1, 1622. Despite this conflict, England remained at peace with the crown of Spain, to which Portugal was then subject.

## Struggles in the Atlantic and Americas
In the Atlantic, the Portuguese faced aggression from the Dutch West India Company. Because the company's early trade was insufficient to cover the costs of armaments raised against Spain and Portugal, the Dutch turned to the plunder of Portuguese and Spanish carracks and galleons. 

In South America, the Dutch targeted Portuguese possessions in Brazil. After a failed attempt to occupy Bahia in 1623—which was quickly retaken by a combined Portuguese and Spanish armament—the Dutch established a firm footing in Pernambuco. The Dutch West India Company's policy of assailing Portuguese territories was largely guided by the advice of refugees in Amsterdam and their fellow religionists who were numerous in Brazil.

## Administrative Developments
Within the Portuguese territories, administrative changes occurred during this period. In Macao, Jeronimo de Silveira became the first royal governor in 1628.
