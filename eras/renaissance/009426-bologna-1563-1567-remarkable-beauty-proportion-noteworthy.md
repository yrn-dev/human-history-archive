# Bologna (1563-1567)

The period between 1563 and 1567 in Bologna is marked by significant contributions to the arts, the conclusion of major ecclesiastical reforms, and the presence of influential figures in theology and engraving.

## Artistic Achievements and Giovanni da Bologna
A primary highlight of this era is the work of the French sculptor Giovanni da Bologna (also known as Jean Bologne or Boullongne). Born in Douai in 1524 and trained in Antwerp, Giovanni settled in Florence in 1553. While he is celebrated for works such as the bronze figure of Mercury and the marble group *Rape of the Sabines* (executed for Francesco de' Medici), he also produced a notable fountain in Bologna between 1563 and 1567. This specific work is recognized for its beauty of proportion. 

Giovanni's broader body of work is characterized by elegance and great spirit. In addition to his contributions in Bologna and Florence—where he cast a bronze equestrian statue of Cosimo de' Medici—he executed various excellent bronze works in Genoa and designed the richly decorated west door of the Pisa cathedral.

## Ecclesiastical and Political Context
Bologna served as one of the intermittent meeting sites for the Council of Trent, which assembled from 1545 to 1563. The council's primary objective was to put the church in order through the reform of the Catholic Church and the clarification of doctrine and discipline. This effort was considered as important as the energy of the Jesuits in preventing further states from leaving the Roman communion.

The city also produced significant religious leadership during this broader era. Ugo Buoncompagno, who would later become Pope Gregory XIII (reigning from 1572 to 1585), was born and educated in Bologna. He taught in the city before being called to Rome in 1539. Gregory XIII later played a prominent role in the Council of Trent between 1562 and 1563.

## Other Notable Figures and Activities
During the mid-1560s, Bologna was a destination for artists and intellectuals. The Dutch engraver Cornelis Cort, known in Italy as Cornelio Fiammingo, visited Bologna after 1567. Having previously worked in Venice for Titian, Cort produced engravings of the great masters of the time while traveling through Bologna and Rome. His work helped circulate the art of masters such as Raphael, Titian, and Baroccio.

Additionally, the Italian architect Giacomo Barocchio (also called da Vignola) conducted early work in Bologna. Though he later became the papal architect under Pope Julius III and succeeded Michelangelo as the architect of St Peter's in 1564, his early career included activity in Bologna, Piacenza, Perugia, and Assisi.
