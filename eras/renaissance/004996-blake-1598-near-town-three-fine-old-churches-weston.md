# Bridgwater

Bridgwater is a municipal borough, port, and market town located in Somerset, England. It is situated on the river Parret, approximately 10 miles from the river's mouth and 151¾ miles west by south of London via the Great Western railway. The town is positioned in a level, well-wooded landscape, bordered by the Quantock hills to the west and the Mendip range to the east. The town itself extends along both sides of the river, which is spanned by a handsome iron bridge.

## Local Landmarks and Architecture
The chief place of worship in the town is St Mary Magdalene's church. This structure features a slender, lofty spire and a north porch and windows that date back to the 14th century, although the building has undergone significant alteration through restoration. It is also noted for possessing a fine painted reredos.

Historical residences in the town include a house located in Blake Street, which was the birthplace of Admiral Blake in 1598; this building has been largely restored. In the vicinity of the town, there are three fine old churches located at Middlezoy, Chedzoy, and Weston Zoyland, which are noted for containing carved woodwork and some good brasses.

## History and Geography
The surrounding area contains several sites of historical and social significance. Within 3 miles of the town lies the battlefield of Sedgemoor, the location where the Monmouth rebellion was finally crushed in 1685. Additionally, Charlinch, the home of the Agapemonites, is located not far from the town.

The town covers an area of 926 acres. As of 1901, the population of Bridgwater was 15,209. The municipal government consists of a mayor, 18 councillors, and 6 aldermen.

## Commerce and Industry
Bridgwater maintains a considerable coasting trade. The town imports timber, tallow, hemp, wine, coal, and grain, while exporting earthenware, farm produce, cement, plaster of Paris, and Bath brick. Bath brick is the staple article of commerce and is manufactured exclusively in Bridgwater, utilizing the clay and sand deposited by every tide.

The town also engages in iron-founding. The river Parret is navigable for vessels up to 700 tons, though it is subject to a bore during spring-tides; in rough weather, this bore can reach a height of 9 feet.
