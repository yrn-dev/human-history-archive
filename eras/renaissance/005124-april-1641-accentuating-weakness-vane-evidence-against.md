# The Speech of George Digby, April 1641

## Context of the Impeachment of Strafford
In the early 1640s, George Digby, the 2nd Earl of Bristol, served as a member of Parliament for Dorsetshire in both the Short and Long parliaments. During this period, Digby was an active participant in the opposition to Charles, working in conjunction with Hampden and Pym. On November 9, Digby moved for a committee to examine the "deplorable state" of the kingdom. Subsequently, on November 11, he was appointed to the committee tasked with the impeachment of Strafford, a process for which he initially demonstrated significant zeal.

## The Speech of April 21, 1641
Despite his initial involvement in the impeachment, Digby eventually opposed the attainder of the prisoner. On April 21, 1641, Digby delivered an eloquent speech in which he focused on two primary legal and evidentiary concerns. First, he accentuated the weakness of the evidence provided by Vane against the prisoner. Second, he argued against the injustice of *ex post facto* legislation.

## Consequences and Parliamentary Reaction
Digby's opposition to the attainder and his critique of Vane's evidence led to great hostility toward him from the parliamentary party. He was accused of stealing Vane's notes from Pym's table, notes which served as the main dependency for the prosecution. The House of Commons further signaled its disapproval on July 15, when it ordered that Digby's speech be burnt by the hangman.

## Background of George Digby
George Digby (1612–1677) was the eldest son of the 1st Earl of Bristol. He first appeared before the House of Commons at age twelve to plead for his father, who was then held in the Tower. Digby was educated at Magdalen College, Oxford, where he was a pupil of Peter Heylin and earned his M.A. in 1636. He was described by Clarendon as a remarkably handsome and accomplished person. 

Digby's hostility toward the court may have been influenced by the disfavour shown to his father and his own severe treatment in June 1634, when he was committed to the Fleet until July for striking a gentleman of the court named Crofts in Spring Gardens. In addition to his parliamentary activities, Digby wrote *Letters between Lord George Digby and Sir Kenelm Digby, Knt. concerning Religion* between 1638 and 1639, in which he attacked Roman Catholicism. He also advocated for the reformation of episcopacy and opposed its abolition in a speech delivered to the Commons on February 8.
