# 17th and 18th Century Bookbinding and Jackets

The 17th and 18th centuries were periods of significant evolution in the aesthetics and technical production of books, particularly regarding their external "jackets" and bindings. In some instances, the value of a book to a collector was derived more from these external elements than from the text itself.

## French Binding and Mastery
France maintained a strong tradition of fine binding during the 17th and 18th centuries. The attraction of fine bindings was particularly potent in France, where high prices for French first editions and Elzevirs were often attributed more to their 17th and 18th century jackets than to the books they contained.

Several master binders defined the styles of these eras:
*   **Nicholas Eve:** Known for binding the *Livre des Statuts et Ordonnances de l'ordre du Benvist Sainct Esprit* for Henry III.
*   **Clovis Eve:** Credited with inventing the "fanfare" style, which featured leafy stem spirals filling a delicate tracery over the book's boards.
*   **Le Gascon:** Invented "dotted work" by scoring his small arabesque and curve gilding tools so that impressions appeared as dotted lines rather than right lines. This style was continuously followed and imitated by others, most notably the Dutch binders Magnus and Poncyn in Amsterdam during the 17th century.
*   **Florimond Badier:** Worked in a style similar to Le Gascon and, unlike Le Gascon, sometimes signed his work.
*   **Padeloup le Jeune:** A member of a family of excellent binders active in the 17th and 18th centuries. His work is often identified by small name tickets and "dentelle," a lace-like gold tooling border, and is frequently inlaid.

## English Binding Developments
In England, the appreciation for old bindings grew significantly following an 1891 exhibition at the Burlington Fine Arts Club. This led to increased demand for embroidered bindings, English blind stamped bindings, and works attributable to Samuel Mearne, who worked during the time of Charles II. Mearne was also known for painting pictures on the fore-edges of books that remained hidden until the volume was opened.

By the end of the 18th century, English binding had reached a decorative low point until the arrival of Roger Payne in London. A native of Windsor, Payne introduced several innovations:
*   The invention of "straight grain" morocco graining (graining in one direction only).
*   The use of ornamental "doublures" (inside linings) and richly gold-tooled corner-pieces.
*   The creation of his own exquisitely made iron binding tools.

Other notable 18th-century English binders included John Edwards of Halifax, who patented a method in 1785 to make vellum transparent for use over delicate paintings, and John Whitaker, who used strong acids and engraved metal plate prints to ornament calf bindings. Both Edwards and Whitaker utilized classical borders and ornaments, leading their work to be known as "Etruscan."

## Technical Transitions in the 18th Century
The 18th century saw a general improvement in presswork, type, and paper across England and France, with Germany and Italy following toward the end of the century. While the average technique of 18th-century binders was better than that of the 17th century, the best binders of the 18th century were considered inferior to their 17th-century predecessors until the work of Roger Payne.

There was a shift in materials for trade bindings; the use of calf and sheepskin became much less common, and books were primarily cased in paper boards.

## The Culture of Book Collecting
Book collecting as a modern pursuit grew as civilization advanced. While early owners like Jean Grolier (1479-1565) bought books to encourage printers or share Renaissance scholarship rather than out of antiquarian instinct, later figures in France and England began to treat books as luxury objects. Figures such as the historian Jacques Auguste de Thou in France, and in England, Henry prince of Wales, Lords Lumley and Arundel, and Archbishop Cranmer, collected the best learned literature of their day. They placed these works in handsome leather jackets, often enriching them with heraldic badges and coats of arms.
