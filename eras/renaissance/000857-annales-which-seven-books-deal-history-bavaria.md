# Annales Boiorum

The *Annales Boiorum*, also known as the *Annals of Bavaria*, is a historical work written by Johann Turmair, who used the name Aventinus. The author took this pseudonym from Aventinum, the Latin name for Abensberg, the town where he was born on July 4, 1477.

## Authorship and Composition
Aventinus was educated at several institutions, including Paris, Cracow, Vienna, and Ingolstadt. In 1509, he became the tutor for Ernest and Louis, the younger sons of the late duke of Bavaria-Munich, Albert the Wise, a position he held until 1517. During this period, he authored manuals and a Latin grammar for his students.

Encouraged by William IV, duke of Bavaria, Aventinus began writing the *Annales Boiorum* around 1517. He finished the work in 1521 and subsequently produced a German version of the text titled *Bayersche Chronik*. In his research, Aventinus helped found the *Sodalitas litteraria Angilostadensis*, an organization that brought several old manuscripts to light. Due to his meticulous approach to his work, he is noted for anticipating the modern scientific method of writing history and has been referred to as the "Bavarian Herodotus."

## Content and Scope
The *Annales* consist of seven books. The work details the history of Bavaria in conjunction with general history, covering the period from the earliest times up to the year 1460. Within the text, the author expresses a strong sympathy for the Empire during its struggles against the Papacy.

## Publication History
The *Annales* were first published in 1554. However, this initial edition omitted many important passages because they were critical of Roman Catholics. A more complete edition was later published in 1580 at Basel by Nicholas Cisner. A complete edition of Aventinus's works was published in Munich between 1881 and 1886, and a more recent six-volume edition has since appeared.

## Life of the Author
Beyond his historical writing, Aventinus's life was marked by religious and political tension. While he did not definitively adopt the reformed faith, he sympathized with the teachings of the reformers and held a strong dislike for monks. These views led to his imprisonment in 1528, though he was soon released by his friends. He spent the remainder of his life in an unsettled state until his death at Regensburg on January 9, 1534.
