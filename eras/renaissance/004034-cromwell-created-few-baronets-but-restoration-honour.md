# The Baronetage

The baronetage is a title of honour established as a new dignity positioned between barons and knights. While the specific reasons for the selection of the title are not known for certain, it was instituted by James I in May 1611 primarily to raise funds for the crown.

## Origins and Early Regulations
The initial purpose of the baronetage was to provide financial support for troops in Ulster. Each grantee was required to pay £1,095, a sum intended to cover the pay of thirty men at 8d. a day for a period of three years. To maintain the selectivity of the order, James I covenanted that creations would be limited to two hundred. Furthermore, candidates were required to possess a landed estate worth £1,000 a year and have paternal grandfathers who had borne arms.

To encourage applications, the honour conferred the prefixes "Sir" and "Lady" (or "Dame") and granted precedence above knights, although they remained below the younger sons of barons. In 1616, the king added the privilege that the heirs apparent of baronets would be knighted upon reaching adulthood. Despite these inducements, the initial qualifications were eventually abandoned. In 1619, a separate baronetage of Ireland was instituted, with the king pledging to limit those creations to one hundred.

## The Baronetage of Nova Scotia
In 1624, the baronetage of Nova Scotia was devised to promote the "plantation" of that province. James I intended to create one hundred baronets, each required to support six colonists for two years or pay 2,000 marks instead. Additionally, they were to pay 1,000 marks to Sir William Alexander, who had been granted the province by charter in 1621. In exchange, Alexander received a "free barony" of 16,000 acres and became a baronet of the Kingdom of Scotland.

Charles I carried out the scheme, creating the first Scottish baronet on May 28, 1625. He covenanted that the number of baronets of Scotland or Nova Scotia would not exceed 150 and that no one could receive the honour without paying 3,000 marks (£166, 13s. 4d.) toward the colony's plantation. On November 17, 1629, Charles I granted Nova Scotia baronets the right to wear a badge featuring an azure saltire with a crowned inescutcheon of the arms of Scotland and the motto "Fax mentis honestae gloria," suspended by an orange tawny ribbon.

Because the required number of applicants could not be met, Charles I opened the honour to Irish and English gentlemen in 1633, though he only created slightly more than 120 in total. By 1638, the grant of lands in Nova Scotia ceased to accompany the creation. Following the union with England in 1707, Scottish creations ended, and both Scots and English received baronetcies of Great Britain.

## Evolution and the Restoration
The restriction on the number of baronets was rejected by the son of James I, who used the creation of baronets as a device for raising money. By 1641, blank patents were being sold; Nicholas wrote that baronetcies could be obtained for £350 or £400, and a patent was offered to Mr. Wrottesley for £300. However, some ardent royalists received the honour for nothing at the start of the great struggle.

During the interregnum, Cromwell created a few baronets. Following the Restoration, the honour was bestowed so lavishly that it was described in a June 1660 letter to Sir Richard Leveson as "too common." During this period, the honour could be procured for £300 or £400, although Sir William Wiseman is reported to have paid £500. An example of a Restoration-era creation is Downing, who was created a baronet on July 1, 1663.

## Heraldry and Later History
Baronets are entitled to display the red hand of Ulster on a canton or inescutcheon in their coat of arms, except for those of Nova Scotia, who display the saltire of that province.

The history of the baronetage remained largely uneventful until 1783, when a royal warrant was issued on December 6 to combat the "wrongful assumption of baronetcies." The warrant directed that no one be recognized as a baronet in official documents without proof of the dignity, and that future creations register their pedigree and arms at the Heralds' College. The first regulation was later rescinded due to opposition from the baronets.

In 1827, George IV revoked the right of the eldest sons of baronets to claim knighthood for all future creations. Subsequent attempts to regain this right or to allow all baronets to wear a badge (similar to those of Nova Scotia) were refused in the 1830s. Following the union with Ireland in 1800, new baronets were created as baronets of the United Kingdom rather than of Great Britain or Ireland.
