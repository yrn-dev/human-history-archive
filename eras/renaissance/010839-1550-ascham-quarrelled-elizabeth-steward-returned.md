# Roger Ascham

Roger Ascham (c. 1515–1568) was an English writer and scholar born in the village of Kirby Wiske in the North Riding of Yorkshire. He was the third son of Margaret and John Ascham, the latter of whom served as steward to Lord Scrope of Bolton.

## Education and Early Career
Ascham did not attend a formal school for his early education. Instead, he was educated in the home of Sir Humphry Wingfield, a barrister and 1533 speaker of the House of Commons. Under the guidance of a tutor named R. Bond, Ascham participated in archery, a sport Sir Humphry encouraged by bringing bows and shafts from London during term times. This early experience influenced Ascham's later views on the importance of archery in educational establishments, as well as his first English work, *Toxophilus*.

Around 1530, at approximately fifteen years of age, Ascham entered St John's College, Cambridge. During his time there, he was influenced by Sir Thomas Smith and John Cheke. He also studied under Robert Pember, who was noted for his facility with the Greek tongue. To accelerate his learning, Ascham followed the practice of teaching Greek to boys while he was still a boy himself. His Latin studies focused specifically on Caesar and Cicero. Ascham earned his B.A. on February 18, 1534/5. Despite speaking against the Pope—which resulted in punishment and a rebuke—he was elected to a fellowship by the master of the college, Dr Nicholas Metcalfe. By 1541, Ascham was serving as a letter-writer for his college and the university, and in 1546, he was elected public orator of the university following the retirement of Sir John Cheke.

## Tutorship of Princess Elizabeth
At the start of Edward VI's reign, Ascham publicly professed Protestant opinions during a disputation on the doctrine of the Mass. Although the vice-chancellor stopped the disputation when it moved to the university's public schools, Ascham wrote a letter of complaint to Sir William Cecil, which later benefited him.

Following the death of Princess Elizabeth's tutor, Grindal, in January 1548, Ascham was selected as her new tutor. This appointment occurred through the influence of Cecil and the princess's own wish, prevailing over a candidate supported by Queen Katherine and Admiral Seymour. For two years, Ascham taught the sixteen-year-old Elizabeth primarily at Cheshunt. 

In correspondence with the Strassburg schoolmaster Sturm, Ascham praised Elizabeth's industry, wisdom, stature, and beauty. He noted her proficiency in English, French, and Italian, as well as her ability to speak Latin readily and Greek moderately. He specifically commended her beautiful handwriting in both Greek and Latin. Their curriculum included the Greek Testament, select orations of Isocrates, the tragedies of Sophocles, Melanchthon's Commonplaces, and St Cyprian. Elizabeth derived the majority of her Latin knowledge from the study of Titus Livius and almost all of Cicero.

## Diplomatic Service and Later Life
In 1550, Ascham returned to Cambridge after quarrelling with Elizabeth's steward. Subsequently, Cheke helped him secure a position as secretary to Sir Richard Morrison (Moryson), who had been appointed ambassador to Charles V. While traveling to join Morrison, Ascham visited Lady Jane Grey at Bradgate, finding her reading Plato's *Phaedo*. 

During the embassy, Ascham traveled to Venice, Innsbruck, and Louvain, though he found the university at Louvain to be inferior to Cambridge. He spent four or five days a week reading Greek with the ambassador. His letters from this period were published in 1553 as a "Report" on Germany. Following the accession of Mary, the embassy was recalled. Ascham was appointed Latin secretary to Queen Mary via Bishop Gardiner, receiving a pension of £20 per year. During this time, he quietly suppressed his Protestantism, though he mentioned to Sturm that some tried to block Gardiner's benevolence because of his religion.

Ascham married Margaret Howe, the niece of Sir R. Wallop, on June 1, 1554, and had two sons. He frequently complained of poverty, though he held various lucrative crown and ecclesiastical leases. In 1555, he resumed tutoring Princess Elizabeth, focusing on the Greek orations of Demosthenes' *De Corona* and Aeschines.

## Final Works and Recognition
After Elizabeth ascended the throne, Ascham was granted the prebend and canonry of Wetwang in York minster on October 5, 1559, despite being a layman. In 1563, during a "great plague" in London, the court moved to Windsor. While dining with other ministers and Sir William Cecil on December 10, a discussion arose regarding the brutality of schoolmasters after reports that scholars at Eaton had fled their school to avoid beatings. Ascham championed the anti-flogging position during this debate. This event prompted him to write *The Scholemaster*, which is regarded as a typical English book of Renaissance culture.
