# Francis Bacon and the Triple Subsidy

## Parliamentary Conflict and the Triple Subsidy
During a period of state necessity, the House of Commons initially assented to a double subsidy and formed a committee to draft the necessary articles. However, a message arrived from the House of Lords requesting a conference. The Commons committee was subsequently informed that the crisis required a triple subsidy to be collected in a shorter timeframe than was customary, and that the Lords would not agree to anything less.

Francis Bacon opposed the proposed conference, arguing that the Lords' desire to discuss supply infringed upon the privileges of the House of Commons. He maintained that while a communication from the Lords could be received, the actual deliberation on the matter must be conducted by the Commons alone. Bacon's motion to reject the conference was eventually carried. Following this, the Lords lowered their demands and requested only to make a communication, which the House accepted.

## Opposition to the Collection Period
The House then considered the proposal for a triple subsidy. While the ordinary custom for such a collection would have been six years, the proposal was shaped to collect it in four years. Although Bacon approved of the increased subsidy itself, he opposed the short period in which the funds were to be raised. 

Bacon suggested that the people would find it difficult or impossible to meet such heavy demands, which he believed would lead to trouble and discontent. As an alternative method of procedure, he suggested raising the money through imposition or levy. His motion failed to receive support, and the four-year subsidy was passed unanimously.

## Consequences and Aftermath
Bacon's opposition to the timeframe proved to be a mistake, as the country was able to meet the increased taxation. Despite his conduct being prompted by a pure desire to serve the queen, it caused deep and nearly ineradicable offense. He was subsequently accused of seeking popularity.

In a separate second parliament, there was less opportunity for Bacon to exercise his powers. During this session, the Commons passed severe laws against recusants and granted a triple subsidy, driven by warmer feelings toward the king following the Gunpowder Plot. During this same period, Bacon married Alice Barnham, the daughter of an alderman, whom he had described in a letter to his cousin Cecil as a handsome maiden to his liking.

## Literary and Philosophical Works
Bacon is noted for a variety of literary works, including theological writings, the *Apophthegms*, and historical pieces such as the *History of Henry VII.*, which provides an animated narrative of that reign. His most widely read and admired moral works are the *Essays*. First published with ten essays, the collection grew to thirty-eight in 1612 and fifty-eight by 1625. These works are characterized by a quaint, original style rich in metaphors, analogies, and pithy sayings.

Additionally, Bacon wrote *De Sapientia Veterum*, in which he explored the idea that Greek mythological fables were allegorical and concealed deep philosophical truths. In his *Essays*, specifically "Of Ambition," he expressed approval of princes having favorites, viewing it as the best remedy against "ambitious great ones" because it ensures no other individual can become too powerful.
