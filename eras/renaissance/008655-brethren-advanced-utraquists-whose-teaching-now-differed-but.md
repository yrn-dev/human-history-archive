# The Bohemian Brethren and Utraquists

The religious landscape of Bohemia from the 15th through the early 17th centuries was characterized by a complex division between Roman Catholics and various reformist groups, most notably the Utraquists and the Bohemian Brethren. These groups often fluctuated between internal conflict and strategic alliances against the Habsburg monarchy and the Roman church.

## The Utraquist Church and the Compacts
The Utraquists, also known as Calixtines or moderate Hussites, represented a moderate wing of the church reform movement. Their primary distinguishing claim was the right to receive communion in both kinds. In their faith, the most moderate Utraquists differed very little from the Roman church. 

The legal status of the Utraquists was governed by the Compacts, enactments that granted them freedom of worship. While these laws provided protection for moderates, they were later utilized by Ferdinand I as an instrument to oppress more radical groups, including the advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian Brethren. By the mid-16th century, the Utraquist church was ruled by an ecclesiastical body known as the consistory; however, the church had lacked an archbishop since the death of John of Rokycan.

## The Bohemian Brethren
The Bohemian Brethren were an extreme Hussite group that faced severe persecution during the reign of King Vladislav. By the time of the German Reformation, the Brethren had increased in influence, partly due to the accession of several powerful nobles. They maintained strong sympathies for the Protestants of Germany.

During the reign of Maximilian, the king published new decrees against the Bohemian Brethren. Despite this, the Brethren remained a potent political force. In 1546 and 1547, members of the Bohemian Brotherhood were prominent among the cities and nobles who refused to provide troops for Ferdinand's military campaigns or who participated in unconstitutional assemblies at Prague to recover ancient Bohemian liberties.

## Evolution of the Reformist Parties
Over time, the distinctions between the various reformist factions shifted. Some advanced Utraquists began to differ very little from German Lutherans. By the late 16th century, the term "Protestants" was used customarily to refer jointly to the Lutherans, the Utraquists, and the Bohemian Brethren.

In 1575, after Maximilian refused to recognize the "Confession of Augsburg" (a summary of Luther's teaching), a majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drafted the *Confessio Bohemica*. While largely identical to the Augsburg confession, the *Confessio Bohemica* differed regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper, aligning instead with the views of Calvin. This shift was attributed to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren. Although Maximilian verbally approved the *Confessio*, he refused to make it public or include it among the kingdom's charters.

## Political Struggles and the Habsburgs
The relationship between the Bohemian estates and the Habsburg kings was often defined by religious demands tied to financial subsidies. In 1567, when Maximilian requested funds for warfare against the Turks, the estates responded with religious demands, specifically requesting that the Compacts cease to be part of the country's laws to prevent their use as tools of oppression.

In 1575, Maximilian eventually consented to exempt the advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the jurisdiction of the consistory, allowing them to choose fifteen defenders to represent them. Later, under King Rudolph, the estates sought further concessions. In 1609, they proposed twenty-five Articles demanding that the *Confessio Bohemica* be recognized as a fundamental law of the kingdom, that complete religious liberty be granted to all classes, and that Protestants and Roman Catholics have equal rights to hold offices of state. They also sought to limit the ability of the Jesuits to acquire land.
