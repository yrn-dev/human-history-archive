# BAILLIE, ROBERT (d. 1684)

Robert Baillie, known as Baillie of Jerviswood, was a Scottish conspirator and the son of George Baillie of St. John's Kirk, Lanarkshire. He is remembered for his opposition to the Stuart administration in Scotland and his subsequent execution for treason.

## Early Conflict and Imprisonment
Baillie first drew the resentment of the Scottish government in June 1676. This occurred when he rescued his brother-in-law, a Presbyterian minister named Kirkton, who had been illegally seized and confined in a house by an informer named Carstairs. As a result of these actions, Baillie was imprisoned for four months and fined £500. He was eventually liberated after paying half of that fine to Carstairs.

## Political Activities and the Rye House Plot
By 1683, Baillie became despondent regarding the state of his country and attempted to emigrate to South Carolina, though this plan failed. In the same year, Baillie and several friends traveled to London to communicate with Russell, Monmouth, and their party in an effort to obtain redress. 

Following the discovery of the Rye House Plot, Baillie was arrested. During questioning by the king, Baillie denied any knowledge of the conspiracy itself. However, he admitted with "striking truthfulness" that he had been consulted regarding a potential insurrection in Scotland. Following this interrogation, he was loaded with irons and sent back to Scotland as a prisoner.

## Trial and Execution
Despite a total lack of evidence supporting a connection to the plot, Baillie was kept in close confinement and fined £6000. On December 23, 1684, while in a languishing state, he was brought before the high court on charges of treason. He was pronounced guilty the following day and was hanged on the afternoon of December 24 at the market cross in Edinburgh. The execution was carried out with "all the usual barbarities." His treatment is cited as one of the worst crimes committed by the Stuart administration in Scotland.

## Personal Life and Character
Baillie was married to a sister of Lord Warriston (Sir Archibald Johnston). He had a son, George, who fled to Holland but later returned with William III and was restored to his estates. George Baillie later married Lady Grizel (1665-1746), the daughter of the earl of Marchmont.

Bishop Burnet, who was Baillie's cousin, described him as a man of great virtue and piety who held Presbyterian principles. Burnet further noted that Baillie was learned in languages, mathematics, and the law.
