# Boileau

Nicolas Boileau was a prominent figure in French literature, recognized as the first individual to reduce French versification to rule and to teach the importance of workmanship for its own sake. He is described as a benevolent, honest, outspoken man of warm and kindly feelings, known for his generous actions and frankness of speech when at court.

## Literary Works and Contributions
Boileau's career was marked by several key publications. In 1666, following the appearance of two unauthorized editions, he published *Satires du Sieur D....*, which included the *Discours au roi* and seven satires. Starting in 1669, he published epistles that were more polished in style, maturer in thought, and graver in tone than his satires. These *Epitres* earned him the favor of King Louis XIV.

In 1674, Boileau published *Oeuvres diverses du sieur D....*, which contained earlier works and two of his masterpieces: *Le Lutrin* and *L'Art poetique*. *Le Lutrin* is a mock heroic poem; four of its cantos appeared in 1674. *L'Art poetique*, written in imitation of Horace's *Ars Poetica*, established a code for future French verse. In this work, Boileau analyzed various kinds of verse composition and their specific principles. The work is divided into four books: the first and last focus on general precepts and the rule of *bon sens*; the second addresses the ode, satire, epigram, elegy, and pastoral; and the third covers epic and tragic poetry.

Later works include the ode *Sur la prise de Namur*, the satire *Sur l'homme*, the epistles *Sur l'amour de Dieu* and *A mes vers*, and the satire *Sur les femmes*.

## Critical Influence and Reception
Boileau's influence extended to English literature through contemporaries such as Alexander Pope and through a translation revised by Dryden. Pope's *Essay on Criticism* is a magnificent imitation of Boileau's maxims, and *The Rape of the Lock* used *Le Lutrin* as a model.

As a critic, Boileau was instrumental in destroying the exaggerated reputations of mediocrities of his era, though his judgment was occasionally at fault and he was sometimes unduly harsh in individual cases. While his rules are considered valuable, they have been noted for a tendency to make poetic efforts too mechanical. Despite this, most of his criticisms were substantially adopted by those who followed him. Recent writers have rehabilitated his critical work, though perhaps to an exaggerated degree.

## Life at Court and Later Years
Boileau's relationship with Louis XIV was significant. After the king asked which of his verses were his best, Boileau diplomatically recited some unprinted lines in honor of the monarch, which resulted in a pension of 2,000 livres. In 1677, he was appointed historiographer to the king, after which his literary production decreased.

His satires earned him many enemies. His 10th satire, *Sur les femmes*, prompted Charles Perrault to write *Apologie des femmes*. Additionally, the bishop Bossuet declared the 10th satire subversive of morality and all satire incompatible with the spirit of Christianity. Boileau's epistle *Sur l'amour de Dieu* served as a vindication of the dignity of his art.

Boileau was admitted to the Academy on April 15, 1684, through the wish of the king. In 1687, he moved to a country-house in Auteuil, which Racine referred to as his *hotellerie d'Auteuil* due to the number of guests. He returned to Paris in 1705, living with his confessor in the cloisters of Notre Dame. Around 1705, he wrote the 12th satire, *Sur l'equivoque*, attacking the Jesuits.

## Editions and Death
Numerous editions of Boileau's works were published during his lifetime. Toward the end of his life, he focused on arranging a complete and definitive edition of his works. However, the Jesuit fathers successfully petitioned Louis XIV to withdraw the publication privilege and demanded the suppression of the 12th satire. These difficulties are said to have hastened his death on March 13, 1711.

The last edition published during his life was *Oeuvres diverses* (1701), known as the poet's "favourite" edition. This was later reprinted in 1894 by Alphonse Pauly with notes and variants in two volumes. A critical text of his works was established by Berriat Saint-Prix in *Oeuvres de Boileau* (1830-1837), a four-volume set based on approximately 350 editions. This text was later reprinted by Garnier *freres* in 1860, edited with notes by Paul Cheron and including an essay by Sainte-Beuve and the *Boloeana* of 1740.
