# Bohemia in the 16th Century and Beyond

## Literature and Historiography
During the 16th century, Bohemia experienced a period of comparative tranquillity, which allowed for the extensive development of native literature. This era saw contributions from the community of the Bohemian Brethren as well as three notable historians.

The most prominent of these historians was Wenceslas Hajek of Libocan. Although the date of his birth is not certain, records identify him as a priest in 1524, and he died in 1553. His major work, *Kronika ceska*, was dedicated to King Ferdinand I (the emperor) and produced under the auspices of government officials. Due to these connections, the work exhibits a strong Romanist and dynastic tendency. This alignment allowed the book to circulate even during times when many other Bohemian books were prohibited or destroyed. While Hajek's work was frequently quoted and translated into several languages—sometimes by authors who had never heard of him—it is regarded as grossly partial and inaccurate.

Other historians of the period include Bartos (also known as Bartholomew), surnamed "pisar" (the writer), and Sixt of Ottersdorf. Bartos, who served as a secretary for the city of Prague for many years and died in 1539, wrote the *Chronicle of Prague*. This work is considered to have great historical value, particularly in its description of the troubles affecting Bohemia and Prague during the reign of King Louis, who was an absentee and weak sovereign. Sixt of Ottersdorf (1500–1583) wrote the *Memorials of the Troubled Years 1546 and 1547*. This text focuses on the unsuccessful rising of the Bohemians against Ferdinand I of Austria; the value of the account is enhanced by the fact that Sixt participated in the movement.

## Political Transitions and Conflict
The political landscape of Bohemia was marked by shifts in sovereignty and internal strife. In the 16th century, the kingdoms of Bohemia and Hungary, along with the districts of Upper and Lower Lusatia, passed to the German king, Ferdinand I. Upper Lusatia, which had been connected to Bohemia at various times since the 12th century, was eventually transferred from Bohemia to Saxony in 1635 via the treaty of Prague, though the emperor retained some supremacy to protect Roman Catholic rights.

Bohemia later became a primary theatre for the Thirty Years' War, which began in its capital. In 1631, Saxons occupied a large portion of the country and attempted to re-establish Protestantism. The region was frequently pillaged by Swedish troops, culminating in 1648 when the Swedish general Konigsmark took part of Prague. Despite these events, imperial representatives remained inflexible, and efforts by Swedish envoys to secure toleration for Bohemian Protestants failed.

## The Habsburg Succession and Administration
In the early 18th century, the house of Habsburg faced the potential extinction of its male line. On October 16, 1720, the estates of Bohemia met in Prague to sanction female succession to the throne and recognize the Pragmatic Sanction, which declared the Habsburg realm indivisible. This was intended to favor Archduchess Maria Theresa.

Following the death of Emperor Charles VI, Maria Theresa faced opposition from Charles, the elector of Bavaria, who claimed the Bohemian throne. In December 1741, Charles invaded with an army of French, Saxon, and Bavarian troops, occupying Prague and receiving oaths of allegiance from many Bohemian knights and nobles. Maria Theresa eventually recovered Bohemia and other Habsburg lands. Under her reign and that of her son, Joseph II, the autonomy of Bohemia was further limited through administrative changes. This included a 1749 decree abolishing separate law-courts in Bohemia and the appointment of an Austro-Bohemian chancellor to oversee the administration of Bohemia and the German domains of the house of Habsburg.

## Cultural and Social Notes
In the medieval period, hunting- and post-horns were popular emblems on coats of arms in Bohemia, as they were in Germany. Additionally, the region's history is tied to its early inhabitants, beginning with the Celtic Boii tribe and their capital, Boiohemum. They were subdued by the Marcomanni around 12 B.C., and later the land was conquered by Slavic tribes, most notably the Cechs, who became masters of the country in the 5th century.

Notable locations within the region include Kuttenberg (Kutná Hora), a town featuring the Gothic church of St Barbara (begun 1368), the 14th-century church of St Jacob, and the Late Gothic Trinity church. The town's Steinerne Haus serves as the town hall and houses one of the richest archives in Bohemia.
