# The Religious Dissensions of the Continent and the Reformation

The religious dissensions that occurred across the European continent and the subsequent Reformation were characterized by intense theological conflict, political maneuvering, and widespread social upheaval. These movements led to the fragmentation of Europe into warring factions and a significant shift in the relationship between church and state.

## Continental Dissensions and the Reformers
Between the twelfth and sixteenth centuries, a group of individuals assuming the character of reformers assaulted the "fabric of superstition" erected by the bishops of the Imperial city, who had extended their dominion over both the clergy and laity of the Latin church. One such figure emerged at the university of Wittenberg, where a teacher became a Reformer after feeling compelled to oppose what he perceived as a great religious scandal.

The conflict was marked by extreme severity. While history records various martyrdoms, it is noted that Christians inflicted greater severities upon one another during these intestine dissensions than they had experienced from infidels. The church of Rome defended its empire through violence, employing proscriptions, war, massacres, and the institution of the holy office. Catholic princes, linking their own interests with those of the clergy, used "fire and the sword" to enforce spiritual censures. In the Netherlands alone, Grotius attests that more than one hundred thousand subjects of Charles V suffered by the executioner.

## Theological and Ritual Conflicts
A central point of contention during the Reformation was the doctrine of transubstantiation. The rejection of this doctrine led reformed Churches to suppress the festival of Corpus Christi. Luther, despite believing in the Real Presence, abolished the festival, viewing it as the most harmful of medieval festivals and abhorring its association with the traffic in indulgences. 

Conversely, the procession of the Host on Corpus Christi became a public demonstration of Catholic orthodoxy against Protestantism and religious Liberalism. In countries with sharp religious divisions, these processions were often forbidden, though some exceptions existed, such as the annual procession held by the Italian community in London.

## Political Dimensions and the Role of Rulers
The religious troubles were often utilized as tools for princely diplomacy. While the common people desired truth and social righteousness, many rulers did not act in good faith. One Emperor attempted futile reconciliations through councils, diets, and the trial of various confessions, including the Interim of Augsburg, the settlement at the Diet of Ratisbon, and the Religious Peace of Nuremberg, viewing the unrest as genuine theological difference.

In Northern Europe, Sweden, Denmark, and Norway moved to the Protestant side. In England, Henry VIII—who had previously been titled "Defender of the Faith" by the Pope for writing against heresy—joined the Protestant princes in 1530. His motivations included a desire to divorce his first wife in favor of Anne Boleyn and a wish to loot the vast wealth of the English church.

## The Reformation in England
The English experience was somewhat detached from the broader European struggle between Catholicism and Protestantism, manifesting largely as a political struggle between the King and the Parliament. While both the Crown and the people were formally Protestant, there was an internal divide: the King served as the nominal head of a sacerdotal and sacramental established Church of England (the reformation of the princes), while many people followed a non-sacerdotal, Bible-respecting type of Protestantism (the reformation of the peoples).

The Reformation also impacted religious orders. The Bridgittine convent of Syon, founded by Henry V in 1415, was one of the few houses restored during the reign of Mary. However, upon the accession of Elizabeth, the community migrated to the Low Countries, later moving to Rouen and finally to Lisbon in 1594.

## Impact on Printing and Censorship
The religious dissensions and the Reformation in England under Edward VI and Henry VIII created a massive demand for books. However, the Tudor and Stuart dynasties did not tolerate a free press. Henry VIII granted the first patent for the king's printer to Thomas Berthelet in 1529, requiring licenses for all printed books; possession of an unlicensed book was a punishable offense. 

In 1556, the Company of Stationers was incorporated to repress "obnoxious books," and the Star Chamber later exercised effective censorship. Despite this, the demand for polemical books was so high that many were printed abroad and smuggled into England. Censorship eased under Queen Elizabeth, except for works from Roman Catholics or those touching her royal prerogatives. Bookselling flourished during the reign of James and the Protectorate, the latter of which saw the 1658 publication of *A Catalogue of the most Vendible Books in England*. Restrictions returned under Archbishop Laud and again during the Restoration with the reinstatement of the Licenser of the Press, which lasted until 1694.
