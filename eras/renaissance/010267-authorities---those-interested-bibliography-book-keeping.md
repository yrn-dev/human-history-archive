# Bibliography and Bibliology

Bibliography and bibliology are fields concerned with the study, enumeration, and description of books. While the terms were once used interchangeably or with different meanings, modern practice has established specific definitions and methodologies for the examination of printed works.

## Definitions and Evolution of Terms
The term *bibliographia* originated in post-classical Greek to describe the writing of books. This meaning persisted into the 18th century; for example, Fenning's *English Dictionary* (1761) defined a bibliographer as someone who copies or writes books. A shift in meaning from "a writing of books" to "a writing about books" occurred in France during the 18th century, evidenced by the 1763 publication of de Bure's *Bibliographie instructive*. In England, this updated meaning was popularized in the early 19th century by the Rev. Thomas Frognall Dibdin. During this period, Southey used the term *bibliology*, though it is now rarely employed.

Current custom generally restricts the scope of bibliography to printed books, distinguishing them from manuscripts. The field encompasses various interests of the book-loving antiquary, including book-collecting, book-binding, book-illustration, and the history of printing. Specifically, bibliography is the art of describing, collating, and examining books, as well as arranging them in lists for informational purposes. The study of the literature of this subject is referred to as the bibliography of bibliography.

## Enumeration and Arrangement
In the 18th and early 19th centuries, particularly among French writers, there was a tendency to view bibliography as a tool for appraising the value of books and placing them within a logical classification of all literature and knowledge. Modern bibliographers have adopted a more modest approach, recognizing that the classification of human knowledge is the domain of scientists and philosophers. Instead, bibliography focuses on suggesting general principles of arrangement.

Books may be arranged based on several criteria depending on the objective:
* **Printing History:** Arranged by the printing-houses or places of production.
* **Regional Interest:** Grouped by the author's place of residence or birth.
* **Specialized Needs:** Arranged by dialect or language.
* **General Access:** The most common methods include alphabetical order (by author and title), chronological order by publication date, or a "logical" alphabetical order by subject.

For those seeking detailed information on individual books, a strictly alphabetical arrangement by author and title is considered the most useful for ease of access.

## The Bibliography of Bibliography
The effort to create a universal bibliography has dwindled as the volume of printed material has increased. The closest approximations to such a work are the printed catalogues of the Bibliotheque Nationale at Paris and the British Museum. For rare books not found in these catalogues, records exist in the *Bibliographer's Manual* by Lowndes, Brunet's *Manuel du libraire*, and the collections of Mr. W.C. Hazlitt.

National bibliographies emerged as an alternative to universal lists. Examples include Robert Watt's *Bibliotheca Britannica* (1824) and Querard's *La France litteraire* (1827-1839), though the latter focuses primarily on "gens de lettres, historiens, et savants" from the 18th and early 19th centuries. Germany's book output over two centuries is recorded in the works of Kayser (*Buecherlexikon, 1750*) and Heinsius (*Allgemeines Buecherlexikon, 1700-1815*).

Regarding early printing, students have created nearly exhaustive lists of 15th-century books still extant. For the period between 1501 and 1536, a tentative enumeration exists in the continuation of Panzer's *Annales Typographici* (1803). In England, the *Catalogues of English Books printed up to the end of the year 1640* provide significant material for the period before contemporary trade lists became common.

## Book Collecting and Value
Collectors are often drawn to books associated with famous events or persons, such as those owned by poets, soldiers, statesmen, or royalty (e.g., Mary queen of Scots). The "pedigree" of a book—the series of book-loving owners who previously held it—adds to its value. This interest is documented in works such as *English Book-Collectors* by W.Y. Fletcher (1902) and *Great Book-Collectors* by Charles and Mary Elton (1893).

First editions are highly prized, partly for their superiority in study and partly for the associations they create. While the high prices paid during the era of George III for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics are now difficult to justify—as they are often of no value as texts—the prestige of first editions of French and English literary masterpieces has grown. For example, a first folio Shakespeare (1623) was sold in 1906.
