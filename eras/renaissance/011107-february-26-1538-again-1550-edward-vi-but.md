# Events and Figures of the Mid-16th Century

## Religious Practices and the Anglican Church
Within the reformed Churches, the Anglican Church is distinguished by its observance of a specific day marked by the Commination service. This service is characterized by the solemn reading of "the general sentences of God's cursing against sinners," which are compiled from other places of Scripture and the twenty-seventh chapter of Deuteronomy. While the lections for this day are identical to those of the Roman Church—specifically Matthew vi. 16, &c., and Joel ii. 12, &c.—the American Prayer Book omits the office of Commination, retaining only three concluding prayers. These prayers are derived from anthems and prayers used during the distribution and blessing of ashes according to the Sarum Missal.

The ceremonial use of ashes was not banned during the Reformation in England. Instead, it was mandated by proclamations issued by Henry VIII on February 26, 1538, and again in 1550 during the reign of Edward VI. Despite these mandates, the practice had fallen into complete disuse by the start of the 17th century.

## Ecclesiastical and Political Conflict under Edward VI
The accession of Edward VI brought significant changes and conflicts to the English ecclesiastical landscape. Bishop Bonner, who had been an active agent in establishing royal supremacy, eventually began to doubt that supremacy when it was utilized by a Protestant council. Bonner, along with Gardiner, proposed a theory that royal supremacy was in abeyance during a royal minority. This position was neither constitutionally nor legally tenable, as both men had sought licenses from the young king to exercise ecclesiastical jurisdiction.

Bonner's resistance to the government led to his commitment to the Fleet after he resisted a visitation in August 1547. Although he was released and opposed the Book of Common Prayer and the first Act of Uniformity during the parliament session of November 1548 to March 1549, he failed to enforce these laws once they were passed. On September 1, 1549, the council required him to maintain at St Paul's Cross that royal authority remained absolute regardless of the king's age. Following his failure to comply and a seven-day trial, Bonner was deprived of his bishopric by an ecclesiastical court presided over by Cranmer and sent to the Marshalsea. On February 7, 1550, the council sitting in the Star Chamber confirmed his deprivation and condemned him to perpetual imprisonment.

## Legal and Diplomatic Figures
Several prominent figures navigated the political shifts of this era. Sir Nicholas Bacon (1509-1579), who later became lord keeper of the great seal under Queen Elizabeth, began his public life in 1537 as solicitor of the court of augmentations. He acquired various estates following the dissolution of the monasteries and served as a member of parliament for Dartmouth in 1545. In 1546, he was appointed attorney of the court of wards and liveries, and by 1550, he became a bencher.

In diplomacy, Charles de Marillac (c. 1510-1560), a French prelate, served as the ambassador to England from 1538 to 1543. Marillac held various influential roles, including missions to the imperial court (1547-1551) and the courts of German princes (1553-1554). He was appointed to the bishopric of Vannes in 1550 and the archbishopric of Vienne in 1557. In 1560, he opposed policies of violence at the Assembly of Notables at Fontainebleau, which led the Guises to compel his departure from court shortly before his death on December 2, 1560.

## Literary and Academic Developments
The year 1538 saw significant activity in printing and drama. The "Great Bible," a large folio volume, began printing in Paris in 1538 under the direction of Francis Regnault, with Coverdale and Grafton sent by Henry VIII to oversee the work. Despite an interdiction by the Inquisitor-general for France on December 17, 1538, which led to the seizure of the impression, many sheets were rescued and brought to England. The work was finished in London in April 1539.

In drama, the writer Bale produced several works in 1538, including *A Tragedye; or enterlude manyfesting the chief promyses of God unto Man* and *A brefe Comedy or Enterlude of Johan Baptystes preachynge in the Wyldernesse, &c.* He also printed a morality play in 1538 titled *The Thre Laws of Nature, Moses and Christ, corrupted by the Sodomytes, Pharisees and Papystes most wicked*, which featured characters such as Idolatry (dressed as an old witch) and Ambition (dressed as a bishop).

In the realm of theoretical writing, Albrecht Durer's works saw various editions. His *Geometry and Perspective* (*Underweysung der Messung mit dem Zirckel und Richtscheyt, in Linien, Ebnen und ganzen Corporen*) had editions in 1525, 1533, and 1538. Similarly, his work on fortification (*Etliche Underricht zu Befestigung der Stett, Schloss und Flecken*) saw editions in 1527, 1538, and 1603.
