# John Ashburnham

John Ashburnham (c. 1603–1671) was an English Royalist and a trusted advisor to King Charles I. The son of Sir John Ashburnham of Ashburnham in Sussex, he entered the service of the king early in his career.

## Early Career and Political Service
Ashburnham's early diplomatic and political activities included a mission to Paris in 1627, sent by his relative, the duke of Buckingham, to make overtures for peace. In 1628, he prepared to join an expedition to Rochelle, though this was interrupted by the assassination of the duke of Buckingham. That same year, Ashburnham was elected as a member of parliament for Hastings and was appointed groom of the bedchamber. He continued to represent Hastings in the Long Parliament of 1640, where he served the king by reporting on the proceedings. During this period, he recovered the Ashburnham estates that had been alienated by his father and acquired a considerable fortune.

## Role in the English Civil War
As one of the king's chief advisers, Ashburnham held the full confidence of Charles I. He accompanied the king to York during the outbreak of the war with Scotland. During the Civil War, Ashburnham served as the treasurer of the royal army. In this role, he caused friction with Hyde, the chancellor of the exchequer, by infringing upon Hyde's province, which led to remonstrances and jealousy. In 1644, he served as a commissioner at Uxbridge.

Following the king's flight from Oxford in April 1646 to the Scots, Ashburnham accompanied him. He subsequently escaped abroad and joined the queen in Paris. He later resided at Rouen and was dispatched to the Hague to seek aid from the prince of Orange.

## Events at Hampton Court and the Isle of Wight
After the army seized Charles I, Ashburnham joined the king at Hampton Court in 1647. While there, he participated in several conferences with other army officers and Cromwell. When Charles escaped from Hampton Court on November 11, Ashburnham provided advice that opposed the suggestions of Sir John Berkeley, who had urged the king to go abroad. Following Ashburnham's counsel, the king took refuge in the Isle of Wight, where Ashburnham placed him in the care of the governor, Robert Hammond.

Upon realizing the situation, the king exclaimed, "Oh, Jack, thou hast undone me!" In response, Ashburnham fell into a "great passion of weeping" and offered to kill Hammond. This sequence of events led to Ashburnham being charged with disloyalty and treachery. While Clarendon censured his conduct, he absolved Ashburnham of any crime other than excessive self-confidence and folly. Ashburnham was eventually acquitted by both Charles I and Charles II.

## Later Life and Legacy
Ashburnham had eight children. His eldest son, William, had a son named John (1656–1710) who was created Baron Ashburnham in 1689. A second son, also named John (1687–1737), became the 3rd Baron Ashburnham upon his brother's death in 1710 and was later created earl of Ashburnham and Viscount St Asaph in 1730. The 5th earl (born 1840) was a direct descendant of this line. Bertram, the 4th earl (1797–1878), was the collector of the Ashburnham library, which was dispersed in 1883 and 1884.

Several writings relate to Ashburnham's life and actions. In 1648, *A Letter from Mr Ashburnham to a Friend* was published to defend his conduct regarding the king. In 1647, he wrote *A Letter to W. Lenthall* to repudiate the claim that the king had violated his parole. A longer *Narrative* was published in 1830 by George, the 3rd earl of Ashburnham, though this championship of his ancestor is described as unconvincing and entirely uncritical.
