# Publications and Legacy of Johann Sebastian Bach

## Lifetime Publications
During his lifetime, the publication of Johann Sebastian Bach's works was very limited. The vast majority of the pieces published while he was alive were instrumental collections, most of which Bach engraved himself. His church cantatas were almost entirely unpublished during his life, with the sole exception of *Gott ist mein Koenig*. Although this specific work was written when Bach was only nineteen years old, it is regarded as a very great work.

## Posthumous Rediscovery and the Bach-Gesellschaft
Following his death, Bach's art was largely neglected, viewed by his younger contemporaries as crabbed and old-fashioned. His work survived only in limited aspects through desultory and unintelligent academic study until it was rediscovered by Mendelssohn. This revival was connected to certain tendencies within the "Romantic" movement in music, and the circle of Bach's worshippers grew rapidly through the efforts of Mendelssohn, Schumann, and others, as well as through publications and performances by Crotch and Wesley in England.

In 1850, one century after Bach's death, a society known as the *Bach-Gesellschaft* was established to ensure the correct publication of all his remaining works. The labors of this society spanned more than fifty years, during which approximately four-fifths of Bach's choral works were published for the first time. Wilhelm Rust, who served as editor for fourteen years, is noted as the "Porson of Bach-scholarship" for his unrivaled insight into Bach's style and his ability to restore priceless harmonies from incomplete texts.

## Influence on Later Composers
Despite periods where his musical language seemed forgotten, Bach exerted a profound influence on subsequent great composers. Mozart developed a new depth of harmony and polyphonic richness after studying manuscript copies of Bach's *Wohltemperirtes Klavier* and motets. Beethoven also studied Bach's accessible works profoundly, frequently quoting them in his sketch-books. A comparison between Beethoven's 31st variation of the *Thirty-three Variations on a Waltz by Diabelli* and Bach's 25th variation of the *Goldberg Variations* reveals a deep kinship in their expression of feeling.

Bach's influence extended to the design of later musical forms. The *Passion according to St Matthew* influenced Mendelssohn's approach to oratorios, helping to rescue the genre from the operatic influences that had affected Beethoven's *Mount of Olives*. Furthermore, Wagner's schemes of *Leitmotif* and his bold harmonization were foreshadowed by Bach's close polyphonic textures. Brahms also developed his work by utilizing the artistic devices of both Bach and Beethoven. While Gluck and Berlioz are perhaps the only great composers who escaped Bach's direct influence, Gluck did reproduce the first twelve bars of the *Gigue* from Bach's B flat Clavier-Partita in his aria "Je t'implore et je tremble" in *Iphigenie en Tauride*.

## Summary of Musical Works
Bach's output is characterized by significant stylistic changes across different periods of his life. His time at Coethen was primarily remarkable for instrumental music, while his final period (1733–1750) saw choral works become more numerous and terse, and instrumental music move toward larger designs, as seen in the second book of the *Wohltemperirtes Klavier* (1744) compared to the first (1722).

His church music includes 190 church cantatas, some of which are known only from fragmentary sets of parts. Of these 190:
* 40 are for solo voices.
* Approximately 60 are founded more or less on chorales.
* The remainder are practically short oratorios, often titled as such by Bach.

Additionally, his choral output includes three wedding cantatas, the Easter oratorio, and the Christmas oratorio, which consists of six similar cantatas forming a connected design for performance over six separate days.
