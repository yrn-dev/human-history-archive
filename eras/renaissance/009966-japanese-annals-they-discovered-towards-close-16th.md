# Discovery and History of the Ogasawara Islands

The Ogasawara Islands, also known as the Bonin Islands, have a history of ownership and occupation characterized by periods of isolation and shifting international claims. According to Japanese annals, the islands were discovered toward the close of the 16th century. Following this discovery, they were added to the fief of a Daimyo named Ogasawa Sadayori, which provided the islands with the name Ogasawara-jima.

## Nomenclature and Early Occupation
The islands were also referred to as *Bunin-jima*, a name that was later corrupted by foreigners into "Bonin." This name is derived from the terms *bu* (without) and *nin* (inhabitants), reflecting the islands' lack of a permanent population. Although the islands were claimed, effective occupation did not occur during the early period. Communications with the islands ceased entirely in 1635, a result of the Japanese government's veto against the construction of sea-going vessels.

## Restoration of Communication and Foreign Settlement
Fitful communication with the islands was restored in 1728 by a representative of the Ogasawara family. However, this contact was interrupted again until 1861, the same year an unsuccessful attempt was made to establish a Japanese colony at Port Lloyd.

During the intervening period, the islands were visited by foreign explorers. Captain Beechey visited the islands aboard the "Blossom," where he published descriptions of the islands' features and assigned names to some of them. In 1830, a small party consisting of a Dane, two American citizens, and two British subjects sailed from the Sandwich Islands to Port Lloyd, accompanied by some Hawaiian natives. This group settled on Peel Island (Chichi-jima) and hoisted the British flag.

## Population and Governance in the 19th Century
By 1853, when Commodore Perry arrived, Peel Island had thirty-one inhabitants. This population included four Americans, four English, one Portuguese, and a remainder consisting of natives from the Ladrones and the Sandwich Islands. By 1875, a visit by Mr. Russell Robertson revealed that the colony had grown to sixty-nine people, though only five were "pure whites."

Robertson's observations indicated that the settlers lived comfortably on cultivated land clearings but lacked laws, religion, education, and any formal system of government. The settlers used English as their language and considered themselves to be a British colony.

## Modern Administration
In 1861, the British government renounced all claims to the islands, recognizing Japan's right of possession. Following this, the islands became duly administered, and regular steam communication was established. The population eventually grew to approximately 4,500 people. Geographically, the islands are noted for having no mountains of any considerable height.
