# Sir William Cecil

Sir William Cecil, later Baron Burghley (1521–1508), was a prominent English statesman and secretary of state. Born on September 13, 1521, at Bourne, Lincolnshire, Cecil was the son of Richard, a yeoman of the wardrobe, and the grandson of David, who had been a yeoman of the guard to Henry VII, sheriff of Northamptonshire in 1532, and a justice of the peace for Rutland. While Cecil's own pedigrees attempted to link him to the Sitsyllts of Altyrennes in Herefordshire and ancestors from the reigns of King Harold and Rufus, the earliest authentic ancestor is considered to be his grandfather.

## Early Education and Career
Cecil was educated first at Grantham and Stamford before entering St John's College, Cambridge, in May 1535 at the age of fourteen. During his six years at Cambridge, he acquired an unusual knowledge of Greek through contact with educationists such as John Cheke and Roger Ascham. Although he did not take a degree, he was removed to Gray's Inn by his father in 1541. 

His early professional life involved various legal and administrative roles. He obtained the reversion to the office of *custos rotulorum brevium* and, according to his own notes, sat in parliament in 1543, though parliamentary returns only list him as being elected for the family borough of Stamford in 1547. In 1547, he accompanied Protector Somerset on the Pinkie campaign, serving as one of the two "judges of the Marshalsea" in the courts-martial. During this time, he wrote an independent account of the campaign and shared his notes with William Patten for Patten's own narrative.

## Rise and Fall under the Protectors
By 1548, Cecil was the protector's master of requests, serving as a registrar for a court set up in Somerset House to hear the complaints of poor men. He also acted as the protector's private secretary. Following the fall of the protector in October 1549, Cecil faced danger; he was ordered detained on October 10 and was imprisoned in the Tower in November. He was bound over in recognizances for a thousand marks on January 25, 1550.

Cecil recovered his standing by ingratiating himself with Warwick and was sworn in as one of the king's two secretaries on September 15, 1550. He was knighted on October 11, 1551, and later became the chancellor of the order of the Garter in April. Despite his success, Cecil described his service under Northumberland in his diary as having been made free from a miserable court.

## Political Maneuvering and Later Service
Cecil's involvement in Edward VI's illegal "devise" of the crown was a point of later contention. While he claimed years later to have signed the document only as a witness, in his apology to Queen Mary, he instead emphasized his efforts to shift responsibility onto others, including his brother-in-law Sir John Cheke. Cecil had disliked Northumberland's scheme but lacked the courage to oppose the duke directly. Once the duke departed to meet Mary, Cecil became an active intriguer against him, providing a full account of these efforts to Queen Mary to secure his own immunity.

Cecil's survival during Mary's reign was further aided by the fact that he had played no part in the humiliation of Mary or the divorce of Catherine during the reign of Henry VIII. He also showed a willingness to conform to the religious requirements of the time.

## Personal Life and Associations
Cecil married Mary Cheke in 1541, a rash act that occurred four months after his father had moved him to Gray's Inn to prevent such a union. Their only child, Thomas (the future earl of Exeter), was born in May 1542. Following Mary's death in February 1543, Cecil married Mildred, daughter of Sir Anthony Cooke, on December 21, 1546. Mildred was regarded by Roger Ascham as one of the two most learned ladies in the kingdom.

Cecil maintained significant influence over academic and court appointments. He was instrumental in the appointment of Roger Ascham as tutor to Princess Elizabeth, selecting him against a candidate supported by Queen Katherine and Admiral Seymour.

## Notable Interactions
In December 1563, during a "great plague" in London, the court relocated to Windsor. On December 10, Cecil dined with other ministers, including Roger Ascham. During this dinner, Cecil shared "strange news" regarding scholars from Eaton who had run away from school to avoid beatings. Cecil expressed the opinion that schoolmasters should use more discretion in correction than was common. This conversation sparked a debate between those who supported flogging and those who opposed it, with Ascham acting as the champion for the anti-floggers.

Cecil also maintained professional respect for military figures, describing William, 13th Lord Grey de Wilton, as a "noble, valiant, painful and careful gentleman."
