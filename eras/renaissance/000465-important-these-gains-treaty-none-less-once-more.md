# Diplomatic Treaties and Territorial Shifts in World History

The history of international relations is frequently defined by treaties that redistribute territory, establish spheres of influence, or resolve conflicts between competing powers. These agreements often reflect a tension between immediate gains and long-term strategic interests.

## The House of Habsburg and European Entanglements
In the early 18th century, the house of Habsburg faced a recurring conflict between the interests of its hereditary dominions and its broader European commitments. Despite making gains, treaties of this era often illustrated the sacrifice of the former for the latter. For instance, while Austria could have extended its conquests down the Danube had war continued, Emperor Charles was preoccupied with the danger Spain posed to Italy. Under the guidance of Alberoni, Spain had occupied Sicily and Sardinia.

To address this, Charles joined the Triple Alliance on August 2, 1718, which later became the Quadruple Alliance. The resulting peace saw Charles obtain Sicily in exchange for Sardinia. By 1731, however, Charles found himself isolated. Lacking a son, he sought to secure the throne for his daughter, Maria Theresa, via the Pragmatic Sanction of April 19, 1713, which declared the monarchy indivisible. 

To gain support for this succession, Charles negotiated with various powers. Great Britain and Holland agreed to respect the Sanction in exchange for the cession of Guastalla, Piacenza, and Parma to Don Carlos. Hostility from Bourbon powers led to the War of Polish Succession in 1733. This conflict resulted in France acquiring Lorraine, while Don Carlos acquired Sicily, Naples, and the Tuscan ports. The Habsburg monarchy's power in northern Italy was strengthened through the acquisition of Piacenza, Parma, and Guastalla. Additionally, Sardinia and Spain adhered to the Pragmatic Sanction. Francis, the dispossessed duke of Lorraine, was compensated with Tuscany; he married Maria Theresa on February 12, 1736, and formally ceded Lorraine to France on May 11 of that year.

## British Diplomacy in the Ottoman Empire
In the 19th century, British diplomatic efforts in Constantinople focused on commercial interests and regional power balances. In 1837, Bulwer was appointed secretary of embassy at Constantinople. Under Lord Ponsonby, Bulwer negotiated a commercial treaty designed to remove conditions hindering British trade with Turkey and to weaken Mehemet Ali, the pasha of Egypt, by dismantling his system of monopolies. 

Bulwer's success in this negotiation was aided by Sultan Mahmed II's hatred for Mehemet Ali. Palmerston praised the treaty as a *capo d'opera* on September 13, 1838. Following this, Bulwer served in St Petersburg before being sent to Paris in June 1839. During 1839 and 1840, while Lord Granville was absent, Bulwer served as *ad interim* charge d'affaires at the French court during a period when the feud between the Porte and Mehemet Ali threatened to bring France and England into armed collision.

## French and Genoese Conflicts over Corsica
In the 16th century, King Henry II of France attempted to conquer the island of Corsica. Utilizing information from Corsican mercenaries and a treaty of alliance signed in Constantinople on February 1, 1553, with Sultan Suleiman the Magnificent, France secured the help of the Turkish fleet. The combined forces captured the citadel of Bastia and later Bonifacio, though the latter was followed by a massacre of the garrison. 

The intervention of Emperor Charles V, who sent imperial and Genoese troops, turned the tide of the war. For three years, Corsicans, Genoese, Spaniards, Germans, and French forces engaged in mutual slaughter. While a 1556 truce left most of the island in French hands, the Treaty of Cateau-Cambresis in 1559 restored Corsica to the bank of San Giorgio, which then handed it over to the Genoese republic.

## Ancient Greek Treaties and Truces
In ancient Greece, treaties often included specific stipulations regarding religious freedom. The Peace of Nikias and a one-year Truce both expressly stipulated that all parties had liberty to attend common festivals and temples, specifically mentioning Delphi and Olympia. 

In other instances, treaties served as the basis for legal disputes. In the fourteenth year of a thirty-year truce entered into after the affair of Euboea, the Lacedaemonians held an assembly to determine if Athens had broken the treaty. By acclamation, a decided majority concluded that Athens was guilty of injustice, leading the Lacedaemonians to seek a common resolution for war among their allies.

## The Expansion of the Prophet Muhammad
In the 7th century, a treaty and truce extended the rule of the Prophet Muhammad to Mecca. The core of this agreement involved the faithful changing the direction of their prayers from Jerusalem to Mecca, establishing Mecca as the pilgrimage center of the new faith. This arrangement was acceptable to the men of Mecca as long as the pilgrimage continued. By 629, Muhammad entered the town as its master, smashing the image of the god Manif in the Kaaba. His power eventually extended across all of Arabia by the time of his death in 632 at the age of sixty-two.
