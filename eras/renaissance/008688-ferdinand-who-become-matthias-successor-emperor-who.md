# Ferdinand II

Ferdinand II (reigned 1619–1637) was the successor to Emperor Matthias and a member of the House of Habsburg. A strong-willed ruler and a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome, Ferdinand was determined to win back Germany to the Catholic faith and establish the rule of the Jesuits in Bohemia.

## Accession and the Bohemian Conflict
Ferdinand's path to the imperial throne was marked by significant religious and political tension. As the Duke of Styria, he had already crushed Protestantism within that duchy, earning a reputation as a cruel persecutor of Protestants. Because Emperor Matthias was childless, he sought to secure the succession of the Bohemian throne for his cousin, Ferdinand. Despite Ferdinand's reputation, Bohemian state officials used "not very scrupulous means" to persuade the estates to accept him as heir. Ferdinand was subsequently crowned in Prague on June 17, 1617.

Following this coronation, the policy of Matthias became more pronouncedly ultramontane. The king's councillors, all adherents of the Roman Catholic Church, openly hoped to recover the church's ancient hold over Bohemia. This shift alarmed Bohemian Protestants, led by Count Thurn. Believing a conflict with Ferdinand was inevitable, the Protestants preferred to confront the weak Matthias rather than his more fanatical successor. Tensions escalated in December 1617 when the abbot of Brevnov and the archbishop of Prague suppressed Protestant services in churches on their domains, violating the Letter of Majesty signed by King Rudolph. This led to a series of events in May 1618, including a secret meeting by Thurn and other advanced party members to put Matthias's councillors to death.

## The Bohemian Revolt and the Battle of White Hill
Upon the death of Matthias in March 1619, the Bohemian crown became vacant. The Bohemian estates formally deposed Ferdinand on July 31, 1619, and elected Frederick, the elector palatine, as their king. Frederick and his queen, Elizabeth of England, were crowned at St Vitus's cathedral in Prague. 

Warfare continued throughout 1619, with Thurn occupying Moravia and advancing on Vienna before being forced to retreat. In 1620, Ferdinand, now Matthias's successor as emperor, sought to reconquer Bohemia. He formed an alliance with the powerful Duke Maximilian of Bavaria and received military aid from Poland, Spain, and several Italian states; even the Lutheran elector of Saxony supported his cause.

A large imperialist army, commanded by Bouquoi, Tilly, and the Duke of Bavaria, entered Bohemia in September 1620. After a series of Bohemian defeats, the imperial forces reached the outskirts of Prague on November 7. On the morning of November 8, they attacked the Bohemian army at a fortified position on the plateau of "Bila Hora" (White Hill). The Bohemians were defeated within a few hours. By that evening, the imperialists occupied the port of Prague on the left bank of the Vltava. King Frederick fled Prague the following morning.

## Administration and Alliances
Ferdinand's reign was characterized by the active pursuit of the counter-reformation. In 1620, as a price for his assistance to the emperor, the elector of Saxony, John George I, was allowed to keep the two Lusatias (Upper and Lower), which had been conquered from Bohemia. These territories were definitely transferred to Saxony via the Treaty of Prague in 1635, though Ferdinand retained a certain supremacy as king of Bohemia to protect the rights of Roman Catholics.

Ferdinand's relationship with his advisors was sometimes strained. Melchior Klesl, an Austrian statesman and bishop of Vienna who had been a director of policy for Emperor Matthias, had advised that the question of imperial succession be shelved until an arrangement with Protestant princes was reached. Ferdinand viewed this counsel as hostility toward his own candidature. Consequently, in June 1618, Klesl was seized by order of the archdukes and imprisoned at Ambras in Tirol. Ferdinand eventually allowed Klesl to return to his episcopal duties in Vienna in 1627.

## Legacy and the Thirty Years' War
Ferdinand's determination to establish Jesuit rule in Bohemia and his efforts to extirpate heresy led to the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War. His reign represented a period where the claims of the House of Austria and the progress of the Reformation were the two dominant, conflicting ideas in the region.
