# Aventinus

## Early Life and Education
Aventinus, born Johann Turmair on July 4, 1477, took his professional name from Aventinum, which is the Latin designation for his birthplace, the town of Abensberg. His academic background was extensive, having pursued studies in Paris, Cracow, Vienna, and Ingolstadt. He returned to Ingolstadt in 1507.

## Career as a Tutor and Scholar
In 1509, Aventinus was appointed as the tutor for Ernest and Louis, the two younger sons of the late duke of Bavaria-Munich, Albert the Wise. He held this position until 1517. During his tenure as a tutor, he authored other manuals and a Latin grammar for his students. In 1515, he accompanied Ernest on a journey to Italy.

Aventinus was also involved in the recovery of historical documents, assisting in the foundation of the *Sodalitas litteraria Angilostadensis*, an organization under which several old manuscripts were brought to light.

## The Annales Boiorum
Encouraged by Duke William IV of Bavaria, Aventinus began writing the *Annales Boiorum* (or *Annals of Bavaria*) around 1517. He finished the work in 1521 and subsequently completed a German version titled *Bayersche Chronik*. 

The *Annales* consist of seven books that examine the history of Bavaria in conjunction with general history, spanning from the earliest times up to the year 1460. In this work, Aventinus demonstrated a strong sympathy for the Empire during its struggles against the Papacy. He is noted for taking immense pains with his research, to a degree that anticipated the modern scientific method of historical writing. Because of his contributions, he has been referred to as the "Bavarian Herodotus."

The publication history of the *Annales* was marked by censorship; the first edition, published in 1554, omitted many important passages because they reflected poorly on Roman Catholics. A more complete edition was later published in 1580 at Basel by Nicholas Cisner.

## Religious Views and Imprisonment
While Aventinus did not definitely adopt the reformed faith, he sympathized with the teaching and the reformers. He also expressed a strong dislike for monks. These views led to his imprisonment in 1528, though he was soon released through the efforts of his friends.

## Later Life and Legacy
The final years of Aventinus's life were somewhat unsettled. He died in Regensburg on January 9, 1534. In addition to the *Annales*, he wrote other books of minor importance. A complete edition of his works was published in Munich between 1881 and 1886, and a more recent six-volume edition has since appeared.
