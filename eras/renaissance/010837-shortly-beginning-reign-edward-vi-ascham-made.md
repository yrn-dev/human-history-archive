# Roger Ascham

Roger Ascham (c. 1515–1568) was an English writer and scholar born in Kirby Wiske, a village in the North Riding of Yorkshire. He was the third son of John Ascham, who served as steward to Lord Scrope of Bolton. Much of the information regarding his early life was collected and edited by Edward Grant, the headmaster of Westminster, who delivered a panegyrical oration on Ascham's life in 1576.

## Education and Early Academic Career
Ascham did not attend a formal school for his early education. Instead, he was educated in the house of Sir Humphry Wingfield, a barrister and 1533 speaker of the House of Commons. Under the guidance of a tutor named R. Bond, Ascham and other children in the household engaged in archery, a sport Sir Humphry encouraged by bringing bows and shafts from London during term times. This experience later influenced Ascham's first English work, *Toxophilus*, and his views on the importance of archery in educational settings, including the statutes of Elizabethan schools such as Harrow and St Albans.

Around 1530, at approximately fifteen years of age, Ascham entered St John's College, Cambridge. He was influenced by Sir Thomas Smith and John Cheke, the latter of whom was admitted as a fellow during Ascham's first year. Ascham also studied under Robert Pember, whom he regarded as a friend and a man of great learning in the Greek tongue. Following Pember's advice, Ascham taught Greek to boys to accelerate his own mastery of the language. In Latin, he focused his studies on Caesar and Cicero.

Ascham became a Bachelor of Arts on February 18, 1534/5. Despite the master of the college, Dr. Nicholas Metcalfe, being a "papist," he praised students who excelled in the "new learning." Metcalfe procured Ascham's election to a fellowship, though Ascham notes that he had previously spoken against the Pope, leading to a rebuke and punishment that warned other fellows not to vote for him.

## Public Life and Tutelage of Elizabeth
Shortly after the reign of Edward VI began, Ascham publicly professed Protestant opinions during a disputation on the doctrine of the Mass. This debate started at his college and moved to the university's public schools for greater publicity before being stopped by the vice-chancellor. Following this, Ascham wrote a letter of complaint to Sir William Cecil, a connection that later proved beneficial.

In January 1548, following the death of the princess Elizabeth's tutor, Grindal, Ascham was selected as her new tutor. This appointment occurred through the influence of Cecil and the princess's own wish, overcoming a competing candidate supported by Queen Katherine and Admiral Seymour. Ascham taught the sixteen-year-old Elizabeth for two years, primarily at Cheshunt. He praised her industry, wisdom, stature, and beauty, noting her fluency in English, French, and Italian, as well as her proficiency in Latin and moderate skill in Greek. Under his tutelage, Elizabeth read most of Cicero and a large portion of Titus Livius, from whom she derived her Latin knowledge. Her studies also included the Greek Testament, the tragedies of Sophocles, select orations of Isocrates, Melanchthon's Commonplaces, and St Cyprian.

In 1550, Ascham returned to Cambridge after a quarrel with Elizabeth's steward. Through Cheke, he secured a position as secretary to Sir Richard Morrison (Moryson), the ambassador to Charles V. While traveling to join Morrison, Ascham made a famous morning visit to Lady Jane Grey at Bradgate, finding her reading Plato's *Phaedo* while others were hunting.

## Diplomatic and Later Career
During his time with the embassy, which visited Venice, Innsbruck, and Louvain, Ascham read Greek with the ambassador several days a week. His letters from this period were published in 1553 as a "Report" on Germany. Upon the accession of Queen Mary, Ascham was appointed Latin secretary to the Queen through Bishop Gardiner, receiving a pension of £20 per year. During this period, he quietly suppressed his Protestantism, though he mentioned to Sturm that some attempted to block Gardiner's benevolence toward him because of his religion.

Ascham married Margaret Howe on June 1, 1554, and had two sons. Despite obtaining various lucrative crown and ecclesiastical leases, he frequently complained of poverty. In 1555, he resumed his studies with Princess Elizabeth, focusing on the orations of Aeschines and Demosthenes' *De Corona*. Following Elizabeth's accession, he was granted the prebend and canonry of Wetwang in York minster on October 5, 1559, despite being a layman.

## The Scholemaster
In 1563, Ascham began writing *The Scholemaster*, which is considered a typical English book of Renaissance culture. The impetus for the work occurred on December 10, 1563, during a "great plague" in London while the court was at Windsor. During a dinner with Sir William Cecil and other ministers, Cecil mentioned that scholars at Eaton had run away from school to avoid beatings, expressing a wish for more discretion in correction. Ascham championed the "anti-floggers" in the ensuing debate. This was further reinforced when Sir Richard Sackville, the treasurer, told Ascham that a "fond schoolmaster" had made him hate learning through brutality.
