# The Persecution of the Anabaptists

The Anabaptist movement emerged as a radical anti-ecclesiastical movement during the spiritual upheavals of the 15th and 16th centuries. Characterized by a revolt against authority, the movement was marked by intense persecution from both Romanist and Protestant sources.

## Origins and Core Beliefs
The Anabaptist movement proper began in Zuerich, where individuals including Konrad Grebel, Felix Manz, Simon Strumpf, and Wilheld Reubli separated from Zwingli to establish a separate church. This new community sought to model itself after primitive Christianity, rejecting traditions and accretions that appeared after New Testament records. 

The most radical of their doctrines was the rejection of infant baptism (paedobaptism) as unscriptural. They maintained that baptism should be reserved for believers. The mode of baptism practiced by leaders such as Joerg Blaurock, Grebel, and Balthasar Hubmaier was sprinkling or pouring rather than immersion; Hubmaier, for instance, baptized 300 converts from a single bucket in 1525.

Other central tenets included:
* **The Inner Light:** A belief in conscience, religious feeling, or "inner light" as the sole true ground of religion.
* **Non-resistance:** The belief that true Christians are like sheep among wolves and must not defend themselves.
* **Social and Legal Views:** The advocacy of a scriptural communism of goods and the belief that Christians should exercise love and patience toward one another, remaining independent of worldly tribunals.
* **The Lord's Supper:** The practice of breaking bread in remembrance of the death of Christ to renew the bond of brotherly love.
* **Theology:** A rejection of the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith, which they viewed as one-sided.

## Persecution in Europe
The movement faced severe opposition from the Protestant Council of Zuerich, which instituted cruel persecutions despite its own precarious liberty and fear of Romanists. Zwingli strongly opposed the Anabaptists, describing their views as "fanatical, stolid, audacious, impious." In his 1527 work *Elenchus contra Catabaptistas*, Zwingli detailed seven articles of the Anabaptists, though he was criticized for unfairly combining the fourth and fifth articles to suggest they concerned the avoidance of "abominable pastors."

The intensity of this persecution prevented the development of a homogeneous community and the adoption of a common public confession. While early leaders like Hubmaier, Grebel, and Blaurock preached non-resistance, the vacuum left by their martyrdom contributed to more violent iterations of the movement. In Holland, Melchior Hofmann spread Anabaptist teachings, leading to the rise of Jan Matthisson, a "preacher of vengeance." Matthisson prophesied the end of the world and the establishment of the kingdom of heaven. This culminated in the events at Muenster in 1533, where all who did not embrace Anabaptism were expelled. Under the leadership of Bernt Knipperdolling and later John of Leiden, Muenster became a site of "grossest licence and cruelty" until the city was taken by a besieging bishop in 1535.

## Anabaptism in England
Knowledge of the introduction of Anabaptist views into England is uncertain, though they are described as largely a foreign importation. Records from London indicate that in 1535, ten Dutchmen identified as Anabaptists were put to death in various places, while ten others were saved after repenting. In 1536, King Henry VIII issued a proclamation directing the clergy to teach the people that Anabaptist opinions were "detestable heresies" to be "utterly condemned." Further executions occurred in 1538, including the burning of a man and woman at Smithfield and the public bearing of faggots by three men and one woman at Paul's Cross.

By the reign of Edward VI, Anabaptists continued to exist in separate communities. In 1549, John Hooper noted that Anabaptists flocked to his public lectures and caused him "much trouble." By 1555, Lord Riche characterized Anabaptists as heretics who boasted of the Spirit of God and desired to have "a church by himself."

Despite the general atmosphere of bitterness, records show instances of individual kindness. John Philpot, imprisoned in Newgate after Mary's accession in 1553, wrote a long letter to a fellow prisoner who held condemned Anabaptist opinions. Although Philpot maintained the obligation of infant baptism, he addressed his correspondent as "dear br[other]."

## Legacy and Transition
By the end of the 16th century, Anabaptist communities existed in London, Kent, and East Anglia. Some, such as Joan Boucher of Kent (burnt in 1550), became notorious for heretical views regarding the Virgin Mary. 

While these groups raised the question of baptism in England, the rise of the modern Baptist movement is considered independent of them. The modern movement began with John Smyth (or Smith), who emigrated to Amsterdam and, under Mennonite influence in 1609, baptized himself and others. In 1611, the first English Baptist Church published a declaration of faith asserting that baptism belongs only to those confessing faith and sins, and that the magistrate should not compel men in matters of religion or conscience.
