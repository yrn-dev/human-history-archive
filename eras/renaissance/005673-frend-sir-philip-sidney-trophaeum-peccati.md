# Sir Philip Sidney

Sir Philip Sidney was an English figure of the Elizabethan era, born at Penshurst and descended from William de Sidney, who served as chamberlain to Henry II. He is recognized as a significant figure in English literature, alongside later authors such as Sir Walter Scott, in the depiction of the "gentleman" within novels and drama.

## Relationship with Fulke Greville
Sidney maintained a lifelong friendship with Fulke Greville, the 1st Baron Brooke. The two were sent to Shrewsbury school on the same day in 1564. Their professional paths remained closely linked; in 1577, Greville resigned a post connected with the court of the Marches to accompany Sidney to court. 

The two men shared literary and military ambitions. They were members of the "Areopagus," a literary clique led by Gabriel Harvey that advocated for the introduction of classical metres into English verse. In 1585, Sidney and Greville arranged to sail with Sir Francis Drake on an expedition against the Spanish West Indies, though Queen Elizabeth peremptorily forbade Drake from taking them. While Greville was also refused permission to join the army of Leicester in the Netherlands, Sidney took part in the campaign.

## Literary Contributions and Influence
Sidney was a prominent opponent of the Guevaristic style, specifically the style of Lyly. His work, the *Arcadia*, is credited with supplanting *Euphues*, contributing to the decline of the Euphuistic taste by approximately 1590.

In his writing, Sidney addressed the nature of poetry and art. In *Apologie for Poetrie*, he noted that the "olde song of Percy and Douglas" moved his heart "more than with a trumpet." However, in *On Translating Homer*, he suggested that while an old English balladist might stir his heart, only Homer and a few artists of the "grand style" possess the power to refine and transmute the "raw natural man."

Sidney also provided observations on the theatrical practices of the Elizabethan stage. He noted the reliance on the spectator's imagination, describing how a single scene featuring an imagined garden with imagined flowers was used to represent a battlefield at one time and a shipwreck at another.

## Death and Legacy
Sir Philip Sidney was killed on October 17, 1586, during his military campaign. Following his death, his books were left as a legacy to be shared by Fulke Greville and Sir Edward Dyer. Greville later wrote the *Life of the Renowned Sir Philip Sidney*, which served as an enduring monument to Sidney's memory. Additionally, the work *The Poem of Poems, or Syon's Muse* (1595) was dedicated to Sidney's daughter, Elizabeth.
