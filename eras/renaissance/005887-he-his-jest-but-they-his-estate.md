# He had his jest, but they had his estate

The phrase "He had his jest, but they had his estate" appears in a description of Buckingham, characterizing a life marked by instability and waste. According to the archive's records, Buckingham was viewed by some as an "epitome" of mankind—a man of various interests who could be a statesman, chymist, fiddler, and buffoon within a single moon. However, this characterization is contested; his life is more accurately defined by insignificance, aimlessness, and a neglect of his own talents. While he was capable of approving the best, he frequently followed the inferior. In his final moments, Buckingham acknowledged his fundamental flaw, describing himself as a "prodigal" regarding the possession of time. Ultimately, he was "beggar'd by fools," leaving him with only his jests while others took his estate.

## Jest and Deception in Classical History

In the records of ancient Greece and Rome, the act of jesting is often linked to deception or the trivialization of serious matters. Solon, a figure fond of gaining knowledge and leisure, once questioned Thespis on the morality of the drama, asking if he was not ashamed to tell lies before a crowd. When Thespis argued that there was no harm in doing such things in jest, Solon warned that approving of such jests would lead people to jest with serious business.

Similarly, Solon confronted Peisistratus, who had wounded himself and entered the market-place in a cart to deceive citizens into believing he had been treated poorly by enemies for defending the constitution. Solon accused him of dishonourably imitating Homer's Ulysses, noting that while Ulysses mutilated himself to deceive an enemy, Peisistratus did so to deceive his own fellow citizens.

In the Roman era, the Emperor Nero used the concept of a jest to mask financial opportunism. After the Senate declared Galba a public enemy, Nero claimed to his friends that this provided a "good pretext" for raising money. He joked that he could confiscate Galba's estate and sell the spoils of the Gauls by public auction. In response, Galba ordered Nero's property in Spain to be auctioned, finding that people were more ready to purchase Nero's assets.
