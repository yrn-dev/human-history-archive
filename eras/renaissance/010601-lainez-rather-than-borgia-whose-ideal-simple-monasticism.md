# Francis Borgia and the Borgia Legacy

The name Borgia is associated with divergent ideals in history, ranging from the pursuit of absolute political power to the embrace of austere monasticism. These contrasting paths are exemplified by the lives and legacies of Cesare Borgia and Francis Borgia.

## The Political Ideal of Cesare Borgia
Cesare Borgia (1476–1507), the son of Pope Alexander VI (Rodrigo Borgia), represented the archetype of the Renaissance adventurer. He was a youth of spirit who rose to become the duke of a wide area of Central Italy through the assistance of his father. His career was characterized by audacity, cunning, and a willingness to employ cruelty and fraud. He is noted for having murdered his elder brother as well as his sister Lucrezia's husband, and he betrayed numerous other individuals.

Despite his lack of significant military ability, Cesare possessed considerable administrative power and dexterity. His methods of conquest were treacherous and ferocious; however, once he established control, he governed his subjects with justice and firmness. This approach was often preferred by the populace over the anarchy of local despots and factions.

These traits made Cesare Borgia a central figure in the political theories of Niccolò Machiavelli. After serving as an envoy in Cesare's camp in October 1502, Machiavelli developed a political ideal based on the duke's character. In his work *The Prince*, Machiavelli idealized Cesare as a representative figure of the successful prince, admiring his self-reliance, his use of native troops, his avoidance of half-measures, and his diplomatic prudence. Machiavelli believed Cesare's methods of cementing a new state from scattered elements were worthy of scrupulous imitation. However, this success was built on an unsound basis; upon the death of Pope Alexander VI, Cesare's magnificence collapsed, and he ended his life ruined and contemptible in Rome.

## The Monastic Ideal of Francis Borgia
In contrast to the political ambitions of the earlier Borgias, Francis Borgia (1510–1572) sought a life of piety and religious devotion. Born in Gandia, Valencia, on October 10, 1510, he showed a strong inclination toward monastic life from a young age while being educated in Saragossa.

Despite his personal inclinations, Francis spent many years in the service of the state. In 1528, his father sent him to the court of Charles V, where he became the master of the horse to the empress and was created marquis of Lombay following his marriage to Eleanor de Castro. He participated in military expeditions to Provence in 1536 and Africa in 1535. A turning point occurred in 1539 when he was tasked with convoying the body of the empress to her burial-place in Granada; this experience confirmed his resolve to embrace a monastic life if he survived his wife.

Even when thrust into high office against his will—serving as the commander of the order of St James and the viceroy of Catalonia—Francis maintained a life of great austerity while efficiently discharging his duties. In 1543, after succeeding his father as the duke of Gandia, he resigned his viceroyalty.

## Integration into the Society of Jesus
Francis Borgia became a powerful supporter of the recently founded order of Jesuits, maintaining correspondence with Ignatius Loyola. He established a Jesuit college in Gandia and, following the death of his wife Eleanor in 1546, resolved to join the society himself. Due to his responsibilities toward his young children, a papal dispensation allowed him to retain his worldly possessions and dignities for four years after taking his vows.

By 1551, Francis had formally resigned his estate and rank. He contributed significantly to the order's growth, providing the funds necessary to build the Collegium Romanum during a visit to Rome in 1550. He is remembered as a Roman Catholic saint whose written works include a treatise titled *Rhetorica Concionandi* and a series of *Exercises* modeled after the *Exercitia Spiritualia* of Loyola.
