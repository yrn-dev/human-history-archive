# Khair-ed-Din

Khair-ed-Din (also known as Khizr) was a 16th-century Turkish admiral and sea rover. He is most notably associated with the nickname "Barbarossa" (meaning "Redbeard"), a name applied by Christians to him and his family due to their red or tawny beards.

## Family Origins and Early Life
The family was founded by Yakub, a Roumeliot likely of Albanian blood, who settled in Mitylene following its conquest by the Turks. Yakub worked as a skipper and coasting trader. He had four sons born after 1482: Elias, Isaak, Arouj, and Khizr. While Khizr initially became a potter and Isaak a trader, Elias and Arouj entered the life of sea roving. 

During an engagement with a galley belonging to the Knights of Saint John based at Rhodes, Elias was killed and Arouj was captured. After being ransomed by a Turkish pasha, Arouj returned to the sea, serving the Mamelukes of Egypt for a period. During the conflict between the Mamelukes and Sultan Selim I, Arouj moved to Tunis. There, he worked as a mercenary amidst the conflicts of the Berber princes of northern Africa and conducted piratical raids against Christian trade.

## Rise to Power in North Africa
Khizr eventually joined his brother Arouj in Tunis, at which point he took or was given the name Khair-ed-Din. He was later joined by their brother Isaak. Arouj and Khair-ed-Din collaborated with Moors who had been exiled from Granada following the 1492 conquest by the Catholic sovereigns of Spain, conducting raids on the Spanish coast. The brothers also sought to advance their fortunes by supplanting or fighting native African princes, establishing their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes. In 1512, they attempted to capture Bougie from the Spaniards but were unsuccessful.

## Establishment of Turkish Power
Following the death of Arouj in battle against the Spaniards in 1518, Khair-ed-Din appealed to the Turkish sultan, Selim, who provided him with troops. In 1529, Khair-ed-Din successfully drove the Spaniards from a fort located on a rocky island in front of Algiers. This victory established him as the founder of Turkish power in the region.

From 1518 until 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli. After 1587, the region was governed by Turkish pashas sent from Constantinople for three-year terms. This arrangement lasted until 1659, when a military revolt in Algiers rendered the pashas nonentities. Subsequently, these cities became anarchical military republics that lived by plunder and chose their own rulers, despite nominally remaining part of the Turkish empire.

## Distinction from Later Figures
The archive contains records of another individual named Khaireddin (meaning "Joy of Religion") who died in 1890. This later Khaireddin was a Turkish statesman of Circassian race. Sold as a child to Hamuda Pasha, the bey of Tunis, he received a French education and rose to become the minister of finance. He later served as the grand vizier under Sultan Abdul Hamid, starting in 1879, and was known for his liberal ideas and opposition to the French protectorate over Tunisia.
