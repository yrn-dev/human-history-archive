# Rudolph and the Religious Conflicts of Bohemia

## Political and Religious Tensions in Bohemia
In the early 17th century, the estates of Bohemia sought to address religious grievances with their king, Rudolph. Following a temporary settlement, the estates brought their demands back to the monarch. Rudolph had previously declined to discuss religious matters while his brother's troops occupied portions of Bohemia. A significant diet convened on January 20, 1609, which was heavily influenced by personal enmities. Rudolph harbored a lasting resentment toward his brother's treachery and engaged in secret negotiations with Christian of Anhalt, the leader of the German Protestants, during the period when Rudolph again appeared as the champion of Catholicism.

The court of Spain was aware of these negotiations, and the Bohemians recognized that the king could not rely on Spanish aid. Consequently, the estates were not intimidated when Rudolph adopted a truculent attitude.

## The Twenty-Five Articles
In March of the year preceding the 1609 diet, the estates had drafted a document known as the "twenty-five Articles" to formulate their religious demands. Rudolph viewed this document as illegal and demanded its delivery for destruction. However, the document remained in the possession of Budova, who refused to surrender it.

The Articles contained several specific requests:
* The recognition of the *Confessio Bohemica* as one of the kingdom's fundamental laws.
* The granting of complete religious liberty to all classes.
* Equal rights for Roman Catholics and Protestants—a term then used to collectively describe the Bohemian Brethren, Lutherans, and Utraquists—to hold all offices of state.
* Limitations on the ability of the Jesuits to acquire land.
* Redress for various grievances resulting from Rudolph's misrule.

## Negotiations and Outcome
To resolve the impasse, the estates appointed twelve representatives to negotiate with the king and his councillors, a group that included Count Henry Matthias Thurn. After protracted discussions, Rudolph reached a final decision on March 31, stating that he could grant no concessions regarding matters of religion.
