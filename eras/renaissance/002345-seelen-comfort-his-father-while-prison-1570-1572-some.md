# Balint Balassa

Balint Balassa (1551–1594), the Baron of Kekko and Gyarmat, was a Magyar lyric poet. Born at Kekko, he received his education from his mother, Anna Sulyok, who was a highly gifted Protestant zealot, and from the reformer Peter Bornemissza.

## Early Works and Family
Balassa's first literary contribution was a translation of Michael Bock's work titled *Wuertzgertlein fuer die krancken Seelen*. This translation was produced to provide comfort to his father, who had been imprisoned from 1570 to 1572 due to a political offence. Following his father's release from prison, Balassa accompanied him to court and attended the coronation diet of Pressburg in 1572.

## Military Career and Romantic Life
After his time at court, Balassa joined the army and spent a period of time at the fortress of Eger. During this time, he fell deeply in love with Anna Losonczi, the daughter of the hero of Temesvar. While his verses indicate that this love was mutual, Balassa's mercurial nature led him to a new mistress. This betrayal deeply wounded Anna Losonczi, who subsequently married Krisztof Ungnad. Balassa only realized the depth of his love for Anna after her marriage; despite his attempts to win her back with verses and gifts, she remained faithful to her marriage vows. Balassa eventually enshrined the memory of Anna in immortal verse.

In 1574, Balassa was sent to assist Gaspar Bekesy in a campaign against Stephen Bathory. However, his troops were scattered during the journey, and Balassa was severely wounded and taken prisoner. He remained in captivity for two years, though the archive notes that this period of imprisonment was not very rigorous.

## Later Life and Misfortunes
Balassa reappears in the historical record in 1584 as the winner and wooer of Christina Dobo, the daughter of the commandant of Eger. This marriage became the source of his subsequent misfortunes, as the greedy relatives of his wife nearly ruined him through legal processes. 

In 1586, Balassa converted to Catholicism to escape the persecutions of his wife's relatives. In response, these relatives declared that both Balassa and his son had become Turks. His desertion of his wife led to his expulsion from Hungary. From 1589 until 1594, he lived a vagabond life in Poland, characterized by numerous amours with women ranging from princesses to cithara players.

## Death and Literary Legacy
The Turkish war of 1594 brought Balassa back to Hungary. He died of wounds sustained during the siege of Esztergom in that same year. His poetic works are categorized into four divisions, one of which is religious hymns.
