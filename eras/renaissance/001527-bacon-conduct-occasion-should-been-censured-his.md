# Francis Bacon: Political Career and Judicial Conduct

Francis Bacon was a prominent figure in English political and legal history, serving in various capacities including as attorney-general and as a member of Parliament. His career was marked by a consistent effort to support the royal prerogative, though his conduct on several occasions led to significant controversy and censure.

## Relations with the Crown and Parliament
Bacon maintained a close relationship with King James, often offering prudent and sagacious advice. His theory of government and political sympathies were strongly aligned with the royal party, a tendency that some suggest blinded him to the true character of certain actions that required a straining of political ethics. This one-sided approach failed to appreciate the evolving relationship between the king, the people, and Parliament.

During the parliament that met in April 1614, Bacon represented Cambridge University. This session was stormy, and the House expressed objection to Bacon serving as a member while simultaneously holding the office of attorney-general. Although an exception was made for him, the House resolved that no future attorney-general would be eligible for a seat in parliament.

## Legal Conflicts and the Royal Prerogative
As attorney-general, Bacon was involved in high-profile legal disputes involving the king's rights. One such instance concerned the "commendams," a case centered on whether the king's prerogative included the right to grant livings *in commendam* (to someone who was not the incumbent). When King James learned that arguments in the case were impugning the general prerogative right, he directed Bacon to instruct the judges to delay their judgment until they had consulted with the king. Bacon communicated this to his rival, Coke, and subsequently to the other judges, though Bacon later hinted that this action went slightly beyond his specific instructions.

Bacon's relationship with Coke was characterized by professional rivalry. Coke frequently sought to limit the prerogative through law and precedent, which made him disliked by King James. In 1613, Bacon proposed to the king that Coke be transferred from the court of common pleas to the king's bench. Bacon's primary motive was likely to move Coke to a court where there would be less danger of him colliding with the king on questions of prerogative.

## Judicial Conduct and Allegations of Corruption
Bacon's tenure as a judge ended in a "painful episode" involving severe condemnation for corruption. Bacon himself confessed that his actions amounted to corruption and were worthy of condemnation, though he distinguished between different types of bribes. He explicitly denied the "deliberate sale of justice," asserting that he never had a bribe or reward in mind when pronouncing a sentence or order.

Analysis of the charges reveals that many were of little weight, including a charge of negligence regarding his servants. Many other instances involved gratuities received after a decision had been reached. However, Bacon confessed to receiving bribes from suitors *pendente lite* (while the case was pending) in four specific cases and others by implication. Despite this, there is evidence that his decisions remained just; in several cases, he ruled against the party providing the bribe, and in the case of Lady Wharton, he accepted a present knowing his opinion would be adverse to her cause. Bacon later expressed that his fall would help purify the courts of such practices. He maintained that he was the "justest judge" in England for fifty years, while acknowledging that the censure he received in Parliament was the "justest censure" in two hundred years.

## Early Career and Financial Disputes
Earlier in his career, Bacon was a confidential adviser to the earl of Essex starting around 1591 and took a seat for Middlesex in parliament in February 1593. 

Bacon also faced censure during an emergency regarding state necessities and subsidies. While he spoke against a proposed conference with the House of Lords to protect the privileges of the Commons, he later opposed the short time frame proposed for collecting a triple subsidy. He argued that the people would find such heavy demands difficult to meet and suggested raising money via imposition or levy instead. His motion failed, and the subsidy passed unanimously. Though prompted by a desire to serve the queen, Bacon's conduct led to accusations that he was seeking popularity and caused deep offense.

## Contributions to Science and Philosophy
Beyond politics, Bacon is regarded as a pioneer of the experimental method of natural philosophy. He is hailed by French thinkers as the "father of experimental philosophy" and is credited with providing the impetus for the foundation of the Royal Society through his work *New Atlantis*. His inductive method, discussed in the *Novum Organum*, influenced later thinkers such as Locke and served as a forerunner to the empirical method in ethics and utilitarianism. In the realm of physical science, he censured the deductive methods of Greek philosophers like Plato and Aristotle for admitting false axioms without dispute.
