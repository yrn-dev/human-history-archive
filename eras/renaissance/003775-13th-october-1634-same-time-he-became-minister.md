## Religious and Civil Life of Praise-God Barbon

On October 13, 1634, Praise-God Barbon (c. 1596–1679), an English leather-seller, became a liveryman of the Leathersellers Company. Around this same time, Barbon began serving as a minister to a congregation that met at his own home, "The Lock and Key," located in Fleet Street. His preaching attracted large audiences, though his exact religious views remain unclear. While enemies labeled him an Anabaptist and Brownist, Barbon wrote two books supporting paedobaptism, and his congregation had separated from a larger Baptist group over this specific controversy.

Barbon later became associated with the Fifth Monarchy men. His religious meetings were often the site of riots, and on December 20, 1641, a mob stormed his house, from which he narrowly escaped. A man of substantial property, Barbon was summoned by Cromwell on June 6, 1653, to serve as a member for London in the assembly of nominees, which was derisively called "Barebone's Parliament," though he is noted to have taken little part in the debates.

In 1660, Barbon attempted to prevent the Restoration. He published a book by Needham containing unfavorable anecdotes regarding the morals of Charles and presented a petition to Parliament on February 9 proposing that officials abjure the Stuarts. Consequently, he was forced to sign an engagement to the council on March 31 to maintain peace. He was imprisoned in the Tower on November 26, 1661, alongside James Harrington and John Wildman, and was released on bail on July 27, 1662. Barbon died and was buried on January 5, 1680.
