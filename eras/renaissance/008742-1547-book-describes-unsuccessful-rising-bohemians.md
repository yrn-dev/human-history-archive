# The Bohemian Rising of 1546–1547

The unsuccessful rising of the Bohemians against Ferdinand I of Austria occurred during a period of significant religious and political tension in the mid-16th century. The conflict was rooted in disputes over the hereditary rights of the Habsburg dynasty and the religious alignment of the Bohemian people.

## Political and Religious Context
The tension began with the accession of Ferdinand of Habsburg to the Bohemian throne on October 23, 1526. While Ferdinand showed moderation in religious matters early in his reign, he pursued the establishment of a hereditary right to the throne with obstinacy. A pivotal moment occurred in 1541 when a great fire in Prague destroyed state documents. Ferdinand used this event to obtain the consent of the estates to replace a charter stating he had been elected king with one claiming he was king by the hereditary rights of his wife, Anna. This action caused great dissatisfaction and served as a primary cause for the subsequent troubles.

Religiously, the views of Bohemian reformers had shifted since the beginning of the Reformation in Germany. The Bohemian Brethren, who saw an increase in influence through the support of powerful nobles, sympathized strongly with German Protestants. Additionally, some advanced Utraquists held views similar to German Lutherans.

## The Failed Levies of 1546
Ferdinand attempted to raise a Bohemian army to support his brother, but faced significant difficulty due to the religious sympathies of his subjects. He used a "tortuous policy," persuading the estates to vote for a general levy under the pretext that the Turks menaced Bohemia, as no armed force could be raised without the consent of the estates.

Ferdinand designated the town of Kaaden on the Saxon frontier as the meeting point for the troops. However, many nobles and cities—specifically those associated with the Bohemian Brethren—refused to send men. Among the soldiers who did arrive, many were Protestants who sympathized with their co-religionists in Germany. Consequently, the army refused to cross the Saxon frontier, and Ferdinand was forced to disband the forces toward the end of 1546.

## The Rising of 1547
Early in 1547, Ferdinand again called for an army to aid his brother. While a few retrograde Utraquists and Romanists obeyed, the majority of Bohemians viewed this as an opportunity to recover ancient liberties that Ferdinand had encroached upon by curtailing old privileges and claiming hereditary rights.

In March 1547, the estates met in Prague without a royal summons, an act described as unconstitutional. This assembly was predominant in the influence of the knights and nobles of the Bohemian Brotherhood and representatives from the town of Prague, giving the gathering a revolutionary character. This sentiment was further intensified when news arrived in Prague of the elector of Saxony's victory at Rochlitz.

## Consequences and Historical Records
The insurrection resulted in failure for the Bohemians. One significant consequence was the impact on the city of Kuttenberg; during the insurrection against Ferdinand I, the city lost all its privileges.

The events of this period are documented in the work of Sixt of Ottersdorf (1500–1583), titled *Memorials of the Troubled Years 1546 and 1547*. Because Sixt took a considerable part in the movement, his account is considered to have enhanced value.
