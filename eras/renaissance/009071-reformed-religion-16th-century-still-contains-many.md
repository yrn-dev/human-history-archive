# Reformed Religion in the 16th Century

The Reformed Religion emerged in the beginning of the 16th century as a movement of church reform. This period was characterized by a struggle between reformers and conservatives within the previously undivided Church, which eventually transformed into a conflict between Romanists and Protestants.

## Foundations and the Role of the Bible
Early champions of the reform movement utilized the Bible as their most trustworthy weapon. Reformers found that the picture of Apostolical Christianity presented in the New Testament stood in glaring contrast to the papal system of the later middle ages. This shift in authority was further supported by the "New Learning" of the Renaissance, which revealed that some authorities used by the Schoolmen—such as writings falsely attributed to Dionysius the Areopagite—were not authentic.

Consequently, the Reformed Churches transferred the authority previously ascribed to the Church to the Bible. The watchword for anti-Romanist parties, particularly during the most acute stages of the struggle, became "The Bible, the Bible alone, is the religion of Protestants."

## Martin Luther and Lutheranism
Lutherans are Christians who adopted the principles of Martin Luther in his opposition to the Roman Church; the term also encompasses followers of Calvin and other sectaries of the Reformation era. While the "Reformed" church is a distinct designation, the distinctive name for Lutherans is the *Evangelical*.

Luther's approach to the New Testament involved critical judgment based on his leading convictions. In his German translation, he placed four books—Jude, James, Hebrews, and the Apocalypse—into an appendix with explanatory prefaces. Although these discriminations were largely ignored in practice by the 17th century, they show an affiliation with modern German critical study.

The dogmatic symbols of Lutheranism typically include nine creeds forming the *Book of Concord* (*Liber Concordiae*):
*   **Early Christian Creeds:** The Nicene Creed (in its Western form with the *filioque*), the Apostles' Creed, and the Athanasian Creed.
*   **16th Century Documents:** The Augsburg Confession, the Apology for the Augsburg Confession, the Schmalkald Articles, the Form of Concord, and Luther's two Catechisms.

While the three early creeds and the Augsburg Confession are recognized by all Lutherans, and the shorter Catechism is almost universally accepted, the Form of Concord is expressly rejected by many Lutheran churches. The Augsburg Confession exists in two forms, the *variata* and *invariata*, which differ slightly regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Supper; the *variata* edition was signed by Calvin.

## Geographic Spread and Impact
The Reformed Religion and Protestantism found adherents in various European regions during the 16th and 17th centuries:

*   **France:** The town of Bolbec in northern France was enthusiastic for the Reformed Religion in the 16th century and continued to have many Protestants. In Caen, the city embraced the reform during the Wars of Religion, though its prosperity was later shattered in 1685 by the revocation of the edict of Nantes.
*   **Central Europe:** In Eperjes, the Reformation found many early adherents. The town remained significant during the religious wars of the 17th century, eventually becoming the site of the "butchery of Eperjes" in 1687, when an Austrian general named Caraffa instituted a tribunal that confiscated property and condemned numerous citizens to death for being accused of Protestantism.

## Opposition to the Reform
The movement faced significant intellectual and poetic opposition. In the Netherlands, Anna Bijns (c. 1494–1575), a schoolmistress and poet in Antwerp, was a professed opponent of reformed thought and Lutheranism. A "lay nun" who instructed youth in the Catholic religion, Bijns used her verse as a weapon against the character and faith of Luther. While her 1528 volume scarcely mentioned Lutherans, her 1538 volume was occupied on every page with invectives against them.
