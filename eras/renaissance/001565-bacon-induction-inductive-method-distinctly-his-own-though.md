# Bacon's Inductive Method

Francis Bacon developed a theory of induction that he presented as a radical change in philosophy and science. While the general spirit of his philosophy was not entirely new, his specific inductive method is regarded as distinctly his own. Bacon viewed nature as a "huge congeries of phenomena" and a "vast labyrinth," proposing his new method of induction as the *filum labyrinthi*—the thread required to track a path toward knowledge and power.

## Foundations and Preliminaries
Bacon asserted that all knowledge of nature must begin with observation and experience. He maintained that man, as the "servant and interpreter of nature," can only understand what he has observed in thought or fact. For Bacon, the essential foundation of a purer natural philosophy is a "natural and experimental history," which consists of a competent collection of facts.

To achieve this, Bacon argued that the senses and memory must be assisted through "ministration" to ensure facts are stored and arranged without confusing the mind. He emphasized that during the preliminary collection of data, the mind must remain absolutely receptive and free from preconceived ideas, keeping the eye "steadily fixed upon the facts of nature" to avoid substituting a "dream of our own imagination" for the actual world.

## The Process of Induction
Bacon distinguished his method from the traditional argument of moving from particulars to a general proposition (the *epagoge* of Aristotle). Instead, he identified the essence of induction as "elimination"—the process of exclusion and rejection. This approach was a necessary consequence of his doctrine of forms.

The inductive investigation of a nature's form involves several specific steps and the creation of three types of tables:
*   **Table of Essence and Presence:** A list of instances in which the nature under investigation is present.
*   **Table of Absence in Proximity:** A list of instances where the nature is absent, focusing on cases most akin to those of presence.
*   **Table of Degrees (or Comparison):** A list of instances where the nature is present in varying degrees, either within the same subject or across different subjects.

Following the formation of these tables, the investigator applies the process of exclusion to eliminate the non-essential. Bacon believed that if the tables were complete and notions clear, this process would be a mechanical "counting out" that would infallibly detect the cause or form. Recognizing that these ideal conditions are rarely fulfilled, Bacon devised *adminicula* (helps), which include the Rectification of Induction, Supports of Induction, Prerogative Instances, and Limits of Investigation, among others.

## Relationship to Other Logical Methods
Bacon was not ignorant of deductive methods or the use of hypotheses. He admitted the syllogism into physical reasoning, though he argued it was "incompetent for the principal things." He suggested that the syllogism is appropriate for mathematics, but in physics, it should only be employed after notions and axioms have been well-defined through induction.

He also utilized the *Vindemiatio* and tested axioms to ensure they were not "larger or wider" than the particulars from which they were derived. He warned against the "curse of all science": the method of establishing general principles first and then trying to prove intermediate axioms by them.

## Influence and Criticism
Bacon is regarded by many as the father of English psychological speculation and a forerunner of the empirical method in ethics. His influence is seen in the fundamental principle of Locke's *Essay*—that ideas result from reflection and sensation—and in the development of utilitarianism, which applies the Baconian experimental method to human conduct. However, some evidence suggests his influence was not immediate; for example, his secretary Hobbes made no critical reference to Baconian induction in his works.

The method has faced significant criticism. Some, such as Macaulay, questioned its efficiency. More formidable objections suggest that science has not actually followed the Baconian method and that no single discovery can be definitively attributed to his rules. Critics argue that Bacon's method was based on a false conception of the causal relation, treating it as a matter of co-existence and assuming each phenomenon has only one cause. Logicians have contended that the true scientific procedure is the use of hypothesis followed by verification, rather than the gradually ascending scale of axioms proposed by Bacon.
