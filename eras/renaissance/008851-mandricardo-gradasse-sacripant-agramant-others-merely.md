# Mandricardo, Gradasse, Sacripant, and Agramant

## Origins in Orlando Inamorato
Mandricardo, Gradasse, Sacripant, and Agramant are characters appearing in the *Orlando Inamorato*, a great poem composed by Boiardo. This work is recognized as one of the most significant pieces of Italian literature, serving as the first example of a romantic epic that was worthy of being used as a model. It is noted that the *Orlando Furioso* by Ariosto was undoubtedly produced as a result of Boiardo's work.

The *Orlando Inamorato* was written for the amusement of the court of Duke Hercules, although Boiardo did not write it within the court's precincts. Instead, he composed the poem while retiring to his estates, such as Scandiano.

## Characterization and Naming
In the poem, Mandricardo, Gradasse, Sacripant, and Agramant are depicted as Saracen warriors. According to the accounts of Tiraboschi, Mazzuchelli, Vallisnieri, and Castelvetro, Boiardo derived these names from some of his own peasants. He chose these specific names because their "uncouthness" made them appear appropriate for characters portraying Saracen warriors.

## Literary Influences and Structure
Boiardo's approach to the poem involved several influences and structural choices:
*   **The Iliad:** Writers such as Mazzuchell and Gravina stated that Boiardo used Homer's *Iliad* as a model. In this framework, Angelica occupies the role of Helen, and the city of Paris is besieged in a manner similar to the city of Troy, making the poem a "reflex image" of the *Iliad*.
*   **The Fabulous Chronicle:** Despite the Homeric parallels, the actual subject-matter of the poem is derived from the *Fabulous Chronicle* of the pseudo-Turpin. However, there is little resemblance between the detailed plot of the *Fabulous Chronicle* and Boiardo's poem, with the exception of principal warriors such as Oliver, Roland, and Charlemagne.

## Publication History
Boiardo did not live to finish the *Orlando Inamorato*. The work was printed in Scandiano the year following his death, managed by his son, Count Camillo. While the book itself is without a date, a Latin letter from Antonia Caraffa di Reggio that is prefixed to the poem is dated the kalends of June 1495. A second edition was published in Venice before the year 1500, and the work was subsequently reprinted in Venice twice more.
