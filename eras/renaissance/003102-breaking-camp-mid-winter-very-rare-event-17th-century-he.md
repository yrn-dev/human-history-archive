# Breaking Camp in Mid-Winter

In the military history of the 17th century, the act of breaking camp during the middle of winter was considered a very rare event. Most military operations of the era were constrained by the season, but certain audacious commanders undertook such maneuvers to achieve strategic surprise.

## The Campaign of Johan Baner
A notable instance of this rare occurrence took place during the career of Johan Baner (1596–1641), a Swedish soldier and field-marshal who served with distinction in the Thirty Years' War. Baner had previously held high rank during the campaigns of Gustavus Adolphus in north Germany, serving at the first battle of Breitenfeld, the taking of Munich and Augsburg, and at Donauwoerth. He also commanded the west against the imperial general Aldringer and later led 16,000 men into Bohemia to march on Prague.

During the winter of 1640–1641, while stationed in the west, Baner executed an audacious *coup-de-main* on the Danube. In a move that defied the standard military practices of the 17th century, he broke camp in mid-winter. This maneuver allowed him to unite his forces with the French army under the comte de Guebriant. Together, they surprised Regensburg while the diet was sitting. The capture of the location was ultimately prevented only by the break-up of the ice, which forced Baner to retreat to Halberstadt.

## Comparative Winter Hardships
The difficulty of mid-winter movement is highlighted by records of other historical journeys through high and cold regions. In certain high countries, mid-winter travel is characterized by deep snow and severe cold. Such conditions create an "intolerable accumulation" of hardships, particularly when a party must transport sick and disabled individuals, arms, baggage, and plunder. 

In some regions, the severity of winter is such that communication between villages can be completely cut off for four months of the year due to the depth of the snow. These environmental factors explain why breaking camp and marching during the winter months was generally avoided by military forces and travelers alike.

## Environmental Constraints on Movement
Winter weather significantly impacts both land and sea movement. In the 17th century and beyond, navigation in various northern regions was frequently interrupted by ice. For example:
*   **Sweden and Norway:** In the Skagerrak and along the west and south coasts of Sweden, ice typically interferes with navigation only during severe winters.
*   **The Gulf of Bothnia:** The northern part freezes every winter, while the southern part sees coastal ice every winter. Navigation is generally interrupted by drifting ice from mid-November to early May.
*   **The Gulf of Finland:** The sea is closed to navigation by ice for approximately 150 days a year.

These conditions underscore the logistical challenges that made Baner's mid-winter movement an exceptional military feat for his time.
