# Independents and the Rise of Modern Baptists

The Independents were a religious group in England whose communities included individuals holding Baptist views. This environment contributed to the emergence of the modern Baptist movement, which eventually broke away from Brownism.

## The Transition from Independents to Baptists
The modern Baptist movement in England is linked to John Smith (also known as Smyth, d. 1612). Smyth served as an ordained clergyman and preacher in the city of Lincoln starting in 1600, but he became a separatist by 1605 or 1606. Due to persecution, Smyth emigrated to Amsterdam along with the Gainsborough Independents. 

In Amsterdam, Smyth joined the "Ancient" church alongside Morton and Thomas Helwys (ca. 1560–ca. 1616). In 1609, after coming under the influence of Mennonite teaching, Smyth separated from the Independents. He performed a self-baptism, earning him the title "Se-baptist," while Helwys and others were likely baptized via pouring, following Mennonite or Anabaptist customs.

## The First English Baptist Church
The group formed by Smyth and Helwys established the first English Baptist Church. In 1611, they published a "declaration of faith of English people remaining at Amsterdam in Holland." This document established several core tenets:
* **Baptismal Requirements:** The church stated that baptism should be administered only to members upon the confession of their sins and faith, as wrought by the preaching of the gospel. They asserted that any church constituted differently was not according to Christ's testament.
* **Rejection of Infant Baptism:** The confession defined baptism or washing with water as an outward manifestation of walking in newness of life and dying unto sin; consequently, it was declared that baptism "in no wise appertaineth to infants."
* **Liberty of Conscience:** The group held that no church should claim prerogative over another and that the magistrate should not compel any form of religion or meddle with matters of conscience. This is recognized as the first known expression of absolute liberty of conscience in a confession of faith.

## Development of Baptist Groups in England
Following these origins, two distinct sets of Baptists existed in England with little intercourse between them:
* **General Baptists:** This group was Arminian, influenced by Mennonite Anabaptists.
* **Particular Baptists:** This group was Calvinist and sprang from the Independents.

While both groups utterly rejected the baptism of infants, they were initially unpledged to the practice of immersion and rarely used it. Their doctrinal development focused on three disputes: the proper mode of baptism, the proper subjects, and the proper administrator. By 1644, the Particular Baptist churches of London, which had grown to seven, published a Confession of Faith. These churches were commonly, though falsely, called Anabaptist.

## Distinction from Anabaptists
The archive distinguishes between the Anabaptists and modern Baptists. Anabaptist communities existed in England toward the end of the 16th century, particularly in London, Kent, and East Anglia. A notable representative was Robert Cooke. These communities were often more notorious for heretical views regarding the Virgin Mary than for their anti-paedobaptist positions; for instance, Joan Boucher of Kent was burnt in 1550 for such views.

While the Anabaptists raised the question of baptism and prepared the way for modern Baptists, they were considered an alien element and a foreign importation. The rise of the Baptist churches is described as being wholly independent of the Anabaptists.

## General Baptist Doctrines and Practices
The defining characteristic of all Baptists is the view that baptism should be administered to believers only. While modern Baptists typically use immersion as their distinctive mode of administration, other groups, such as the Mennonites, continue to practice sprinkling or pouring. 

In the Americas, the "Separate" or "New Light" churches—formed during the religious declension preceding the Great Awakening (1740-1743)—also moved toward Baptist views. Many "Separates" concluded that administering baptism to unconscious infants was inconsistent with their requirement for personal regeneration as a term of membership. Some entire "Separate" churches eventually transformed into Baptist churches, viewing infant baptism as a cornerstone of state-churchism and lacking Scriptural warrant.
