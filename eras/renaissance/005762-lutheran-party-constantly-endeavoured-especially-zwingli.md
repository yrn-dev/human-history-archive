# Lutherans

The Lutherans are Christians who adopted the principles of Martin Luther in his opposition to the Roman Church, as well as in opposition to the followers of Calvin and other sectaries of the Reformation era. Their distinctive name is the "Evangelical," which distinguishes them from the "Reformed" church.

## Doctrinal Symbols and Creeds
The dogmatic symbols of the Lutheran church are generally said to include nine separate creeds that together comprise the *Book of Concord* (*Liber Concordiae*). These include three creeds from the Early Christian church: the Apostles' Creed, the Nicene Creed (in its Western form containing the *filioque*), and the Athanasian Creed. The remaining six originate from the 16th century: the *Augsburg Confession*, the *Apology for the Augsburg Confession*, the *Schmalkald Articles*, Luther's two *Catechisms*, and the *Form of Concord*.

While the three early creeds and the *Augsburg Confession* are recognized by all Lutherans, and Luther's Short Catechism is almost universally accepted, the *Form of Concord* has been expressly rejected by many Lutheran churches. The *Augsburg Confession* exists in two forms—the *variata* and the *invariata*—which differ slightly in their statement of the doctrine of the sacrament of the Supper. The *variata* edition was signed by Calvin, who stated he did so in the meaning of the author, Melanchthon.

## Internal Divisions and the Book of Concord
Following the initial Reformation, the Lutheran church faced internal fragmentation. The university of Jena, led by Matthias Flacius, served as the headquarters for stricter Lutherans, while Leipzig and Wittenberg were centers for the Philippists, who followed Melanchthon. 

Efforts to resolve these differences included the *Torgau Book* (1576) and eventually the *Book of Concord* (1577), which was drafted primarily by Nicolas Selnecker of Leipzig, Martin Chemnitz of Brunswick, and Jacob Andreae of Tübingen. The recognition of the *Book of Concord* was largely due to the efforts of Augustus, elector of Saxony. It was accepted by the Lutheran churches of Hungary (1597) and Sweden (1593), but rejected by those in Pomerania, Anhalt, Hesse, Denmark, and several imperial cities. In Germany, churches that refused the *Book of Concord* mostly became Reformed or Calvinistic, often adopting the *Heidelberg Catechism*.

## Worship and Polity
Martin Luther's approach to church organization was characterized by a natural conservatism. He avoided suggesting a uniform ecclesiastical polity or a common order of worship, though he insisted on the use of the vernacular and the singing of German hymns. 

The Lutheran service is popularly distinguished from the Reformed service by its retention of most medieval vestments and ceremonies, such as the use of lights on the altar. However, Luther noted that these could be dispensed with if necessary. The primary distinctions between the Lutheran service and the medieval church were the supreme place given to preaching, the use of the vernacular, and the fact that the people partook of the cup during the sacrament of the Lord's Supper.

The Lutheran polity is based on two core concepts: the spiritual priesthood of all believers and the belief that the state is a divine ordinance. In the state churches of Norway, Sweden, and Denmark, the episcopate has been retained, and the king is recognized as the *summus episcopus* (supreme authority) in ecclesiastical matters, though in Sweden and Norway this power is somewhat limited by parliament.

## Relations with Other Reformers
A significant point of contention between Lutherans and other reformers, specifically Zwingli, concerned the doctrine of the Lord's Supper. At a conference in Marburg, Luther and Zwingli failed to reach an agreement on whether the true body and blood of Christ are corporeally present in the bread and wine. Despite this, the *Articles of Marburg* were signed by ten members—including Luther, Zwingli, Bucer, and Melanchthon—recognizing common ground on the Trinity, baptism, justification, and the rejection of the sacrifice of the Mass.

Martin Bucer, a German Protestant reformer, frequently attempted to maintain unity between the Lutheran party and the Swiss and south German reformers, especially after Zwingli's death. Bucer's opinions on the Lord's Supper were decidedly Zwinglian, but his efforts to formulate a unifying statement of belief led to charges of obscurity and ambiguity.

## Historical and Political Context
The Lutheran movement faced significant opposition from the Holy Roman Empire. At the Diet of Spires in 1526, the Edict of Worms (1521) was decreed to be observed, banning Luther and his adherents. In 1529, a resolution was passed to prevent the further expansion of the Reformation, which was challenged by the "protest" of the Evangelical states, giving rise to the name "Protestants."

In 1530, Emperor Charles presided over a diet where three separate confessions were presented: one from Zwingli, the *Confessio Tetrapolitana* from four cities, and the *Augsburg Confession*. The emperor found reconciliation hopeless and enforced the edict of Worms against Luther and his partisans. In response, Protestant princes formed the Schmalkald League to protect themselves.
