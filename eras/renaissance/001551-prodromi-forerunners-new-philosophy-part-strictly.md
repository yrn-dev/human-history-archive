# V. Prodromi

The *Prodromi*, translated as "forerunners of the new philosophy," constitutes a specific division of a larger work. Strictly speaking, this part is considered extraneous to the general design of the overall project.

## Purpose and Nature
According to the *Distributio Operis*, the *Prodromi* was intended to house certain speculations by Bacon. Unlike the primary goals of the work, these speculations were not formed through the application of the new method, but were instead produced by the unassisted use of Bacon's own understanding. Because they were developed without the new method, these writings are characterized as uncertain or temporary anticipations of the new philosophy.

## Relationship to the New Philosophy
The *Prodromi* serves as a precursor to the "new philosophy," which is described as the result of the new method and the work of future ages. To prepare for the introduction of this new method, Bacon first examined the obstacles to progress—a discussion that occupied nearly the entirety of the first book of the *Organum*. This examination of obstacles, specifically the doctrine of Idols (*Idola*), is regarded as a most important part of his philosophy, despite being strictly extraneous to the scheme.

The new method was specifically contemplated for application to natural philosophy, which Bacon viewed as the "great mother of all the sciences" and the type of all true scientific knowledge. In the general scheme provided in *De Augmentis* (or the *Advancement*), human knowledge is categorized into three areas:
1. **Memory**: The collection and storage of bare facts presented to sense, resulting in history (either civil or natural).
2. **Imagination**: The division of sense materials according to the mind's pleasure rather than nature, resulting in poesy or feigned history.
3. **Reason**: The working of materials according to the pattern of nature, resulting in philosophy.

Within this framework, philosophy is concerned with man, God, or nature, with the study of nature being the most important. Natural philosophy is further divided into operative (practical) and speculative (theoretical) branches. Speculative natural philosophy is subdivided into metaphysics, which studies formal and final causes, and physics, which inquires into material and efficient causes.
