# JOHN III SOBIESKI

John III Sobieski was the King of Poland and a prominent military leader whose career was marked by significant engagements against the Ottoman Empire and varied political allegiances prior to his ascension to the throne.

## Early Career and Military Service
Before being elected as king, Sobieski's military service was characterized by shifts in loyalty. He served in the pay of Louis XIV of France and also fought on behalf of the Swedes against his own native country of Poland. Despite these early associations, he later became a key defender of Polish interests.

## Campaigns Against the Turks
Sobieski achieved notable military success in conflicts with the Ottoman Empire. In 1673, he led Polish forces to a victory over the Turks at Khotin. He also defeated the Turks at Lemberg during a war fought in defense of the Cossacks, who had sought Turkish protection. While these victories were significant, the subsequent treaty of Zurawno, signed on October 16, 1676, saw the Turks retain Podolia, Kaminiec, and a large portion of the Ukraine.

## The Relief of Vienna
The most historically significant action of John III Sobieski occurred in 1683. During this period, the Turks—spurred on by Louis XIV and invited by Hungarian malcontents—burst into Hungary, overran the territory, and appeared before the walls of Vienna. 

On September 12, 1683, the Polish cavalry under King John III Sobieski gained a victory over the Turks. This action not only saved the Austrian capital but served as the first in a series of successes that eventually drove Ottoman power from Hungary. This victory helped determine the Austrian attitude toward the Slav peoples of the Balkans and Turkey for the following century.

## Political Context of Poland
Sobieski ruled a Polish state described as a weak aristocratic republic. The country was characterized by recurrent royal elections, which frequently invited exterior interference and the influence of "foreign money." The Polish noble class, while patriotic and possessing an aristocratic sense of freedom, lived on estates with little intellectual intercourse and were incapable of effective co-operation. 

Furthermore, while Poland remained a comparatively strong power when warfare relied on levies of men and horses, it struggled to keep pace with the development of professional standing forces and modern military art. This internal instability often led disgruntled Polish patriots to seek alliances with foreign enemies.
