# Turks in South-Eastern Europe and the Habsburg Monarchy

The progress of the Turks in south-eastern Europe and the shifting territorial holdings of the House of Habsburg were pivotal factors in the political evolution of Austria and the broader European landscape from the close of the Middle Ages through the 18th century.

## Turkish Expansion in Europe
The Turks, along with the Arabs in Spain, were successful in establishing a footing in Europe because they first conquered the regions of Africa and Asia adjoining the continent, ensuring that invaders remained in contact with Asiatic settlements. The Turks specifically obtained a footing at Constantinople and profoundly affected eastern Europe. According to archive records, the result of these conquests was not the implantation of Asiatic culture, but rather the total arrest of development in the affected countries, which remained in a condition similar to that of the moribund Byzantine empire.

By the end of the 15th century, rulers of Turkish or kindred Mongol race and nomadic tradition dominated several regions, including the Balkan Peninsula, Hungary, Russia, Egypt, North Africa, Persia, India, and China. The Ottoman Turks also extended their reach to the Mediterranean, engaging in naval warfare against the Venetians.

## Impact on the Habsburg Monarchy
The geopolitical pressure exerted by the Turks played a significant role in the internal unification of Austria. During the close of the Middle Ages, the progress of the Turks in south-eastern Europe, combined with the loss of most Habsburg possessions on the Rhine, helped draw separate states together for defensive purposes. Furthermore, the loss of Rhine territories shifted Habsburg attention toward expansion possibilities in eastern Europe.

The conflict between the Habsburgs and the Turks intensified in the 16th century. On August 28, 1526, at the battle of Mohacs, Suleiman the Magnificent defeated and killed Louis, the king of Bohemia and Hungary. This victory allowed the Turks to conquer and retain the greater part of Hungary until the peace of Karlowitz in 1699. Consequently, Archduke Ferdinand spent much of his life combating the Turks and attempting to recover Hungary, facing an active rival in John Zapolya, who was supported by Suleiman.

The Turkish threat reached the heart of the Habsburg lands with the siege of Vienna in 1529 (some sources cite 1530). In 1529, the Turks were defeated more by the weather than by the defenders. Despite these conflicts, the Austrian line remained deeply entangled in Hungarian affairs and, as Ferdinand and his two successors did, paid an annual tribute to the Turk for the small portion of Hungary—approximately 12,228 sq. m.—which Ferdinand held.

## Divergence of the Habsburg Lines
The Turkish danger contributed to a cultural and political divide between the Austrian and Spanish branches of the Habsburg family. After the death of Charles V, the empire was split: his brother Ferdinand took Austria and the empire, while his son Philip II took Spain, the Netherlands, and South Italy. 

The Austrian line remained obstinately Catholic and held its patrimony primarily on the eastern frontiers. Because they were preoccupied with the Turkish threat and Hungarian affairs, they lost their grip on north Germans. The north Germans, conversely, were indifferent or ignorant of the Turkish danger and were more aligned with Baltic and westward affinities and Protestantism.

## Later Conflicts and Territorial Changes
The tide of Turkish expansion was challenged in 1571 at the battle of Lepanto, which broke the perceived invincibility of the Turkish fleet. In the 18th century, the Habsburgs sought further successes in eastern Europe. In 1716, in alliance with Venice, Charles VI declared war on the Turks. This led to Eugene's victory at Peterwardein and the conquest of the banat of Temesvar, followed by the capture of Belgrade in 1717. Under the treaty of Passarowitz, signed on July 21, 1718, the Habsburg monarchy annexed Belgrade, the banat (which rounded off Hungary), and the northern districts of Servia.

By the time of Charles V, the Habsburgs had inherited a vast amount of territory through marriage, including most of the American continent and between a third and a half of the Europe that the Turks had left. Later, in the era of Metternich, Austria's foreign policy in the "Eastern Question" was dictated by its interest in maintaining the integrity of Turkey, providing moral support to the sultan's authority over the Greeks to ensure the stability of the Habsburg monarchy.
