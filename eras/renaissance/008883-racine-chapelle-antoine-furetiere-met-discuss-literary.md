# Literary Reunions at the Moulon Blanc and the Pomme du Pin

## The Literary Circle
During the early period of Boileau's career, a group of prominent writers and intellectuals gathered for reunions at the *Moulon Blanc* and the *Pomme du Pin*. These meetings served as a forum where Boileau, Moliere, Racine, Chapelle, and Antoine Furetiere met to discuss various literary questions. Within this circle, Boileau maintained a constant friendship with both Racine and Moliere, supporting their interests on many occasions.

## Antoine Furetiere
Antoine Furetiere (1619–1688) was a miscellaneous writer and scholar born in Paris on December 28, 1619. His early professional life involved the study and practice of law as an advocate. He eventually took orders and became the abbe of Chalivoy in the diocese of Bourges in 1662. 

Furetiere gained recognition through his satires, including *Voyage de Mercure* (1653) and *Nouvelle Allegorique, ou histoire des derniers troubles arrives au royaume d'eloquence* (1658), which led to his admission into the French Academy in 1662. However, his relationship with the Academy deteriorated over a dispute regarding the publication of a dictionary of the French tongue. The Academy alleged that Furetiere had purloined from their stores and claimed exclusive privilege over such a work. Following bitter recriminations, Furetiere was expelled in 1685. He responded to this "act of injustice" with the satire *Couches de l'academie*, published in Amsterdam in 1687. His own *Dictionnaire universel* was published posthumously in 1690.

Beyond his scholarly work, Furetiere authored *Le Roman bourgeois* (1666), a work that ridiculed the fashionable romances of La Calprenede and Mlle de Scudery by presenting portraits and episodes drawn from the everyday life of his era.

## Boileau and the Evolution of French Verse
Boileau's early contributions to the literary landscape were marked by a revolt against older poets. In 1660, he produced his first satire, imitating Juvenal's third satire, which depicted a poet's farewell to Paris. This was followed by eight more satires, eventually totaling twelve. Through these works, Boileau attacked and parodied high-ranking writers of the time, including Philippe Quinault, Georges de Scudery, the abbe Charles Cotin, and Jean Chapelain.

Boileau is noted for demonstrating the poetical capabilities of the French language. While writers such as Pascal and Descartes had established prose as a powerful and flexible instrument, Boileau—following Malherbe—introduced a method and rule to French versification characterized by perfect regularity of verse structure and vigour of expression. In 1664, he wrote the prose *Dialogue des heros de roman*, a satire that effectively abolished the elaborate romances produced by Mlle de Scudery and La Calprenede. Although read in manuscript, this work was not published until 1713, reportedly out of regard for Mlle de Scudery.

## Relations with Contemporaries
The interactions between these writers were often complex. Boileau expressed his admiration for Moliere in the second satire (1664) and in stanzas written in 1663. Regarding Racine, Boileau once described him as an "extremely clever fellow" to whom he had taught the art of "elaborate rhyming" (*rimer difficilement*). 

Racine's own standing grew over time, eventually challenging the towering reputation of the older poet Corneille. Racine also demonstrated his respect for Corneille by delivering a noble and just tribute to him during the Academical reception of Thomas Corneille. Later in life, Racine referred to Boileau's country-house at Auteuil as his *hotellerie d'Auteuil* due to the large number of guests it attracted.
