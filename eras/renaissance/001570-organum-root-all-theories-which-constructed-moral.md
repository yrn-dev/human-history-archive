# The Influence of Bacon's Organum on Moral Theory

The *Novum Organum* and the broader philosophical framework established by Francis Bacon served as a foundational influence on subsequent intellectual development, particularly within the spheres of natural science, psychology, and ethics. Bacon's approach emphasized a departure from the tyranny of authority, the academic goals of unpractical dialectic, and the vagaries of unfettered imagination.

## The Inductive Method and Ethics
Bacon is regarded as a forerunner of the empirical method in the sphere of ethics. The inductive method discussed in the *Novum Organum*, combined with the spirit of the *De Augmentis*, is considered to be at the root of all theories that have constructed a moral code through the inductive examination of the results of actions and human consciousness. 

A primary example of this influence is utilitarianism, which is viewed as the natural result of applying the Baconian experimental method to the phenomenon of human conduct. While Bacon's influence is seen as a logical fulfillment in the trend of typically English thought regarding moral, mental, and political philosophy, some evidence suggests this influence was not always immediate. For instance, Hobbes, who served as Bacon's secretary, made no critical reference to Bacon in his works nor mentioned Baconian induction.

## The Structure of the Organum and the Instauratio
The *Novum Organum* represents a portion of the *Instauratio*, a great work intended to reorganize the sciences and restore man's command over nature, which had been lost following the fall. The *Instauratio* consisted of six divisions. The first, *Partitiones Scientiarum* (represented by *De Augmentis Scientiarum*), provided a survey and inventory of the possessions of the human mind based on an analysis of the objects and faculties of human knowledge. 

The second division, *Interpretatio Naturae*, focused on the new method used to train and direct the mind toward the renovation of science; this was represented, albeit imperfectly, by the *Novum Organum* (specifically book ii).

## Obstacles to Progress and the Doctrine of Idols
Before unfolding his new method, Bacon devoted nearly the entire first book of the *Organum* to examining the obstacles to progress. This discussion is highly regarded as a critical part of his philosophy. Consequently, Bacon's name is closely associated with the doctrine of Idols (*Idola*), as well as with the classification of the sciences and the theory of induction.

## The Relationship Between the Organum and Natural History
Bacon maintained that his method possessed qualities of mechanical simplicity and absolute certainty. However, he recognized that the method could only produce fruit and be applicable if the phenomena of the universe were completely arranged and tabulated. This necessity led to his demand for a complete natural history.

Bacon viewed the *Organum* and the *Natural History* as correlative and equally necessary sides of a true philosophy; their union produced the "new philosophy." In a letter to Jean Antoine Baranzano, Bacon asserted that a true natural history should consist of nothing except canons, observations, connections, and instances. He argued that without a store of instances, the business of science must be given up, regardless of how many volumes—even six times as many as Pliny's *History*—it might require.

## Legacy in Logic and Psychology
In the domain of logic, the *Novum Organum* is described as a stimulating introduction to logical study, though it is noted that its contents had been more instructively analyzed by Aristotle or in modern works. Its epigrammatic and terse phrases serve as a warning against immature and crude generalization. 

Furthermore, Bacon is seen by many as the father of the most characteristic elements of English psychological speculation. This is evidenced by the first aphorism of the *Novum Organum*, which briefly states the principle that all ideas are products of reflection and sensation—a fundamental principle later found in Locke's *Essay*. Bacon described his own role in this intellectual awakening as having "rang the bell which called the wits together."
