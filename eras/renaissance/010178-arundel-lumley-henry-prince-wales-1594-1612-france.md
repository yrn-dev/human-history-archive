# Arundel, Lumley, and Henry, Prince of Wales

## Book Collecting and Learned Literature
During the period following the invention of printing, which reduced the cost of books by approximately 80%, a specific class of book owners emerged. While early book ownership was often driven by a desire to encourage printers or provide friends with the latest Renaissance scholarship—as seen with Jean Grolier (1479-1565)—there developed a practice of assembling comprehensive libraries of learned literature.

In England, this practice was exemplified by Lords Arundel and Lumley, as well as Henry, prince of Wales (1594-1612). These individuals brought together the finest books of their era across all departments of learned literature. Their approach to these collections involved placing the volumes into handsome leather jackets and enriching them with heraldic badges and their own coats of arms. In France, a similar pursuit was undertaken by the famous historian Jacques Auguste de Thou (1553-1617).

## Context of Modern Book Collecting
The activities of Arundel, Lumley, and the prince of Wales occurred during the transition toward book-collecting as it is understood in the modern sense. Historically, the love of books was focused on the delights of literature, as described in the *Philobiblon* (1345) attributed to Richard de Bury. Later, Sebastian Brant criticized the "book-fool," arguing that possessing books was a poor substitute for actual learning.

Modern book-collecting required a sufficient abundance of books to be available before it could begin. For the collectors of the 16th, 17th, and 18th centuries, the formation of a fine library was often viewed as an "added grace" rather than a separate pursuit. Many of these large-scale buyers operated with public objectives, and their collections were frequently destined for public resting-places.

## Henry, Prince of Wales
Henry, prince of Wales, lived from 1594 to 1612. Beyond his contributions to the assembly of learned literature and the curation of handsome leather-bound volumes, his era marked the final stages of certain medieval traditions in England. While the age of chivalry and its associated pageantry had been discredited on the Continent following the fatal accident of Henry II of France in 1559, tilts and tourneys continued to be patronized in England. These martial exercises were supported by Queen Elizabeth and were occasionally held until after the death of Henry, prince of Wales.
