# Works on Navigation and Mathematics

The archive contains records of various mathematical and navigational works, including those focused on the determination of the pole, the use of logarithms, and the study of magnetism.

## Navigational and Mathematical Tables
A significant body of work exists regarding the creation of tables to assist in navigation and mathematical calculation. One author produced *A Table to find the Height of the Pole, the Magnetical Declination being given*, published in London in 1602. This author also contributed "Tables for the Improvement of Navigation," which appeared in the second edition of Edward Wright's treatise, *Certain Errors in Navigation detected and corrected*, in 1610. Further works by this individual include *A Description of an Instrumental Table to find the part proportional, devised by Mr Edward Wright* (published in 1616 and 1618) and *Arithmetica Logarithmica* (1624).

Other contributions to navigational science include tables designed for the simplification of Sumner's method, which is used to determine the position of a ship at sea.

## Logarithmic and Geometric Studies
The archive lists several specialized mathematical texts. These include *Logarithmorum Chilias prima* (1617), *Lucubrationes et Annotationes in opera posthuma J. Neperi* (1619), and *Euclidis Elementorum VI. libri priores* (1620). Additionally, *Trigonometria Britannica* was published in Goudae in 1663. Other recorded works include *Mathematica ab Antiquis minus cognita* and two letters addressed to Archbishop Usher. Some works remained unpublished, such as *Remarks on the Treatise of Longomontanus respecting the Quadrature of the Circle* and *Commentaries on the Geometry of Peter Ramus*.

## Studies on Magnetism and Compass Error
Research into magnetism has led to practical applications in maritime navigation. Peter Barlow (1776–1862), an English writer on applied and pure mathematics and a fellow of the Royal Society, authored an *Essay on Magnetic Attractions* in 1820. His investigations into magnetism resulted in the discovery of a method for compensating or rectifying compass errors in ships.

Similarly, work conducted around 1873 regarding the mariner's compass led to a process of complete reconstruction. This allowed for the compensation of both temporary and permanent magnetism within a ship. This reconstruction also reduced the weight of the 10-inch card to one-seventeenth of the weight of the previously used standard card, though it increased the time of swing.
