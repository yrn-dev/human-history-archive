# Epitaph

An epitaph is strictly defined as an inscription placed upon a tomb. The term is derived from the Greek *epitaphios* (from *epi*, meaning upon, and *taphos*, meaning a tomb), often implying *logos* (word). While the term primarily refers to inscriptions on sepulchral monuments, its usage has extended to include any writing composed for that purpose, regardless of whether it is actually inscribed on a tomb.

## Nature and Characteristics
Epitaphs vary significantly in their form and purpose. Some serve as literary memorials and are never placed on monuments; in other cases, the writer's intention to have the text placed on a grave may be frustrated or may never have existed. Despite this, a text is still considered an epitaph if it is suitable for the purpose.

A primary external constraint for mural inscriptions is a rigid limitation on length, which prevents an epitaph from reaching the proportions of an elegy. In ancient Athens, epitaphs were applied indiscriminately and typically included the name, descent, demise, and an account of the person's life. In contrast, Sparta reserved such inscriptions only for those distinguished in war. Some Greek epitaphs are purely literary reflections on death and contain no personal names or specific biographical details.

## Historical Development in Britain
The earliest British epitaphs date to the Roman period and follow Roman forms in Latin, often commemorating soldiers of the legions of occupation. Following the Roman period, Latin remained the preferred medium for public inscriptions due to its perceived permanence. English epitaphs from the 11th and 12th centuries were almost exclusively in Latin and generally brief, often beginning with the words "Hic jacet" followed by the name and rank of the deceased. Notable exceptions from this era include the encomiastic leonine verse epitaphs for Gundrada, daughter of the Conqueror (d. 1085), and William de Warren, earl of Surrey (d. 1089).

In the 13th century, French began to be used for inscriptions to celebrated historical figures, a trend that continued between 1200 and 1400. By the 14th century, the word entered the English language as *epitaphy* or *epitaphe*. From 1300 until the Reformation, the majority of preserved English and Latin inscriptions were found on monumental brasses.

During the reign of Elizabeth, English epitaphs began to acquire a distinct literary value, ranking alongside those composed in Latin. By the end of the 16th century, supplying epitaphs in English verse had become a trade.

## Notable Examples and Variations
Epitaphs have taken various creative forms across different cultures and eras:
* **Dialogue and Verse:** An epitaph for St Bernard, likely written by a monk of Clairvaux, is composed as a dialogue in Latin verse. William Browne wrote a notable epitaph for the dowager countess of Pembroke (d. 1621) using daring hyperbole.
* **Self-Penned Inscriptions:** Sir George Udny Yule, C.B., K.C.S.I., penned his own epitaph, which was subsequently inscribed on his tomb in the old cemetery at Tunbridge Wells.
* **Cultural Variations:** In Japan, a monument erected in 1692 for the soldier Kusunoki Masashige bore the simple epitaph, "The Tomb of Kusunoki, a loyal subject." French epitaphs are characterized by grace, wit, and epigrammatic point, such as those by Etienne Pasquier.
* **Specific Terminology:** An epitaph from 1584 in the Beauchamp chapel at Warwick for "the noble Impe Robert of Dudley," son of Robert Erle of Leycester, utilizes the word "Impe" (imp), which until the 17th century was used to refer to a member of a family, especially one of high rank.
