# Catherine de' Medici

Catherine de' Medici was a prominent figure of the 16th century, serving as the queen of France and the wife of King Henry II. Her influence extended across the cultural, intellectual, and political spheres of the French court.

## Family and Relations
Catherine de' Medici was the mother of Marguerite de Valois (1553–1615), who was born on May 14, 1553. Marguerite was known for her beauty, learning, and the cultivation of art and letters, though she was also noted for the looseness of her conduct. Marguerite's life reflected a "Valois mixture" of pious exercises and licentiousness.

Catherine's presence in the French court also intersected with other figures of the era. For instance, Catherine Henriette de Balzac d'Entragues, the marquise de Verneuil and mistress of Henry IV, experienced bitter court scenes when Henry IV married Marie de' Medici, a marriage that followed a promise Henry had made to the marquise. Later, Marguerite de Valois maintained a relationship with Marie de' Medici, visiting her on equal terms.

## Patronage and Cultural Influence
Catherine de' Medici was a patron of the arts and a supporter of intellectuals. She developed a friendship with the Italian humanist Pietro Carnesecchi (1508–1567), who won her favor through his conduct at a conference with Francis I at Marseilles.

Her influence extended to the preservation and publication of literary works. In 1577, Jacopo Corbinelli, one of the Italians Catherine brought from Florence, published the original Latin version of the treatise *De vulgari eloquentia* in Paris. This work, written by Dante, aimed to establish the Italian language as a literary tongue and distinguish noble "courtly" speech from local dialects.

## Material Possessions and Heraldry
Catherine's status is reflected in the decorative arts and historical artifacts of her time. Highly decorative bookbindings were created for her and King Henry II; these were richly colored and gilded, featuring the initials of both the king and queen, as well as emblems of bows and crescents. Similarly, the architecture of the period occasionally incorporated her identity; for example, a church doorway featuring a combination of flamboyant Gothic and Renaissance styles bore a shield with a monogram combining the "H" of Henri II, the double "D" of Diane de Poitiers, and the double "C" of Catherine de Médicis.

In the realm of rare manuscripts, Catherine de' Medici once owned the *Codex Ephraemi Syri Rescriptus*. This 5th-century uncial palimpsest, which originally contained the entire Bible, was likely brought from the east to Italy during the 16th century.
