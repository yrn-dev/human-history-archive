# Henry and the Papal Curia

The interactions between various English monarchs named Henry and the papal curia—the administrative apparatus of the Holy See—span several centuries, ranging from diplomatic obstructions and appeals to conflicts over excommunication and royal authority.

## Henry VIII and the Curia
In the early 16th century, King Henry utilized agents to manage his contentious relationship with the papacy. In January 1532, Edmund Bonner was dispatched to Rome for the specific purpose of obstructing judicial proceedings that were being held against Henry within the papal curia. 

By October 1533, the diplomatic tension escalated. Bonner was tasked with the "unmannerly" duty of delivering a message to Pope Clement VII. At the time, the pope was a guest of Francis I in Marseilles. The message was an official appeal from Henry, directing his appeal from the pope to a general council. While some accounts, such as those by Burnet, suggest that Clement VII threatened to burn Bonner alive in response, the archive notes there is no good authority to support this story.

## Henry IV and the Gregorian Conflict
In the 11th century, the relationship between the papacy and the monarchy was marked by severe conflict during the reign of Henry IV. The papal curia, under Pope Gregory VII, utilized excommunication as a tool of political and ecclesiastical pressure. 

The excommunication of Henry IV served as a pretext for rebellious German nobles to oppose the king. These nobles went as far as to establish a rival king, Duke Rudolph of Swabia, in March 1077. Pope Gregory initially attempted to maintain a neutral stance, hoping to act as an arbitrator in the dynastic struggle. However, after Rudolph's victory at Flarchheim in January 1080, Gregory abandoned this policy. On March 7, 1080, the pope again pronounced the deposition and excommunication of Henry IV, while simultaneously releasing all subjects from any oaths they had sworn to the king, past or future.

Henry IV refused to acknowledge this ban, arguing that it was illegal. This conflict culminated in a council at Brixen on June 25, 1080, where the council pronounced Gregory himself deposed.

## Henry III and Papal Finance
During the 13th century, the interaction between Henry III and the papacy involved financial arrangements that created friction with the English clergy. In 1251, Bishop Grosseteste protested a papal mandate that required the English clergy to pay one-tenth of their revenues to Henry III to fund a crusade. 

Grosseteste criticized the financial expedients of the curia, specifically the system of provisions. He noted that alien nominees of Rome were drawing a sum of 70,000 marks annually from England. Grosseteste presented a written memorial to Pope Innocent IV and his cardinals, attributing the evils of the Church to the "malignant influence of the Curia."

Additionally, during the papacy of Gregory IX (1227–1241), the pope provided support to Henry III in his struggles against the English barons.

## Other Historical Contexts
The archive also notes other instances of "Henry" in relation to papal authority. In 1118, Henry V proclaimed Gregory VIII (Mauritius Burdinus) as pope in Rome after Gelasius II refused to grant the emperor's claim to investiture. Gregory VIII served as an antipope until 1121, when he was driven from Rome by Calixtus II and eventually exiled.
