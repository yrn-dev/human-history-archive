# The Emperors Ferdinand

The history of the House of Habsburg includes several prominent rulers named Ferdinand, most notably Ferdinand I and Ferdinand II, as well as the later Emperor Ferdinand who presided during the mid-19th century. Their reigns were characterized by the consolidation of Habsburg power in Central Europe, conflicts with the Ottoman Empire, and the management of religious and national tensions in Bohemia and Austria.

## Ferdinand I
Ferdinand I ascended to the Bohemian throne on October 23, 1526, after being chosen by twenty-four electors from the three estates. His claim was based on being the husband of Anna, the daughter of King Vladislav. This event marked the permanent accession of the Habsburg dynasty to the Bohemian throne. Ferdinand I also acceded to the Hungarian throne and later became king of the Romans and emperor following the death of Charles V.

Throughout much of his life, Ferdinand I focused on combating the Turks and attempting to secure Hungary. Following the battle of Mohacs on August 28, 1526, where Suleiman the Magnificent defeated King Louis, the Turks retained a large portion of Hungary until 1699. Ferdinand I eventually agreed to pay an annual tribute to Suleiman for the small portion of Hungary he held, totaling approximately 12,228 square miles.

In Bohemia, Ferdinand I sought to establish the hereditary right of his dynasty. In 1541, after a fire destroyed state documents in Prague, he convinced the estates to replace a charter stating he was king by election with one stating he was recognized through the hereditary rights of his wife, Anna. This change caused significant dissatisfaction and contributed to subsequent unrest. Sixt of Ottersdorf's *Memorials of the Troubled Years 1546 and 1547* describes an unsuccessful Bohemian rising against Ferdinand I. Following the failure of this revolt, Ferdinand I executed four principal leaders—two knights and two citizens of Prague—on August 22. This assembly became known as the "Krvavy'sneem" or "bloody diet."

Ferdinand I is also noted for his religious moderation early in his reign, despite his devotion to Rome. He established the Jesuits in Bohemia to influence the nobles and knights. In 1562, he secured the coronation of his eldest son, Maximilian, as king of Bohemia. Ferdinand I died in 1564.

## Ferdinand II
Ferdinand II's rise to power was marked by internal Habsburg tensions and the influence of the Austrian statesman Melchior Klesl. Klesl, a convert to Roman Catholicism and bishop of Vienna, served as the director of policy for Emperor Matthias. Klesl sought to maintain the empire within the house of Habsburg and preserve the dominance of the Roman Catholic Church, but he advocated for shelving the question of imperial succession until an arrangement with Protestant princes was reached.

This cautious approach displeased Ferdinand II, who believed Klesl was hostile to his candidature. Although Klesl's influence remained strong with Emperor Matthias, he was seized by order of the archdukes in June 1618 and imprisoned at Ambras in Tirol. In 1627, Ferdinand II allowed Klesl to return to his episcopal duties in Vienna.

## Emperor Ferdinand and the 1848 Revolutions
In the mid-19th century, the lands ruled by the house of Habsburg faced reviving national aspirations in Bohemia. By 1845, the Bohemian estates opposed the government of Vienna, asserting their right to vote on taxes based on the constitution of 1627. They sought more extensive representation for towns in the diet, stricter control over finances, and the introduction of the Bohemian language in higher schools.

The revolutionary outbreak of 1848, sparked by the February revolution in Paris, led to great excitement in Prague. On March 11, a public meeting petitioned the government of Vienna for liberal reforms, the convocation of a general diet elected on extensive suffrage, and equal rights for the Bohemian language alongside German in government offices.

While a first deputation received an equivocal answer, Emperor Ferdinand responded to a second deputation on April 8. He declared that equality of rights would be secured for both nationalities in Bohemia. He further stated that a new meeting of the Bohemian estates, including representatives from principal towns, would be convoked shortly, and that the question of reuniting Moravia and Silesia with Bohemia would be decided by a general meeting of representatives from all parts of Austria.
