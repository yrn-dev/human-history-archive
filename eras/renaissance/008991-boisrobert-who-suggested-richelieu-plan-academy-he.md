# François Le Metel de Boisrobert

François Le Metel de Boisrobert (1592–1662) was a French poet and priest who became a significant figure in the literary circle of Cardinal Richelieu. Born in Caen, he received training in law and practiced at the bar in Rouen for a period of time.

## Court Life and Career
Boisrobert moved to Paris around 1622 and established himself at court by the following year, contributing to the ballet of the *Bacchanales* performed at the Louvre in February. His career was marked by international travel and diplomatic engagement; he accompanied an embassy to England in 1625 and visited Rome in 1630, where his wit earned him the favor of Urban VIII.

In 1623, Boisrobert was introduced to Cardinal Richelieu. Through his talent as a raconteur and his humor, he became indispensable to the cardinal. This relationship provided him with significant financial security through various benefices. Despite his wealth and status, Boisrobert's personal life was characterized by a passion for play and a friendship with Ninon de l'Enclos. His enemies frequently cited the "undisguised looseness of his life" as a weapon against him, leading to several periods of disgrace. Although he took orders and was appointed a canon of Rouen, he was compelled in his later years to devote more attention to his priestly duties.

## Literary Contributions and the Academy
Boisrobert played a pivotal role in the institutionalization of French letters. He is credited with suggesting the plan for the Academy to Richelieu, and he subsequently became one of its most active and earliest members. 

In addition to his role with the Academy, Boisrobert was one of the "five poets" tasked with the occupation of transforming Richelieu's dramatic ideas into theatrical form. This group also included G. Colletet, C. de l'Etoile, J. de Rotrou, and Pierre Corneille. 

As a writer, Boisrobert authored several volumes of verse and a number of comedies. One of his plays, *La Belle Plaideuse*, is said to have influenced Moliere's *L'Avare*. Additionally, the licentious *Contes*, which were published under the name of his brother D'Ouville, are often attributed to him. Known for his generosity toward other men of letters, he used his wealth to support his peers.

## Later Years and Death
Following the death of Cardinal Richelieu, Boisrobert attached himself to Cardinal Mazarin. He served Mazarin faithfully throughout the period of the Fronde. Boisrobert died on March 30, 1662.
