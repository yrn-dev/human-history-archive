# The Anabaptist Movement

## Origins in Zurich
The Anabaptist movement proper began in Zurich, where a group including Felix Manz (d. 1527), Konrad Grebel (d. 1526), Simon Strumpf, and Wilheld Reubli (1480-1554) separated from Zwingli to propose the creation of a separate church. This group advocated for a scriptural communism of goods and the repudiation of the use of force. They asserted that Christians should remain independent of worldly tribunals, exercising patience and love toward one another.

The movement's most radical doctrine was the rejection of infant baptism, which they viewed as unscriptural. This stance, and the subsequent formation of a separate church, led to their condemnation by Zwingli and the banishment of several members. This banishment prompted Konrad Grebel, Joerg Blaurock, and others to formally institute "Anabaptism" by baptizing one another and partaking together in the Lord's Supper.

## Doctrines and Practices
The Anabaptists sought to model their community on primitive Christianity, rejecting all traditions and accretions that appeared later than New Testament records. They aimed to return to a simple church founded on brotherly love. A central article of faith linking them to the Zwickau prophets was the belief in religious feeling, conscience, or "inner light" as the sole true ground of religion. Additionally, they believed that true Christians are like sheep among wolves and must not defend themselves.

The movement grew rapidly, attracting scholarly saints such as Hans Denck (ca. 1495-1527) and Balthasar Hubmaier (ca. 1480-1528). In 1525, Hubmaier baptized 300 converts. The mode of baptism used by the Anabaptists was pouring or sprinkling rather than immersion; Hubmaier used a single bucket for his converts, while Blaurock and Grebel baptized each other and their adherents while kneeling in an ordinary room.

## The Münster Outbreak
The Anabaptist movement was also associated with a period of insurrection and religious communism. In Wittenberg in 1521, the sect appeared under three "prophets," later breaking into insurrection in 1525. Between 1532 and 1535, insurgents held the town of Münster in Westphalia. During this time, a form of insanity and reports of cannibalism occurred under the leadership of John of Leyden (born Johann Buckholdt, c. 1508-1535).

John of Leyden, a former tailor who had been influenced by Thomas Münzer, joined the movement under Johann Matthysz. Upon Matthysz's death in 1534, Leyden became the prophet and established a theocracy in Münster. He organized a constitution with twelve elders, suggested by the tribes of Israel, but eventually superseded them to proclaim himself king of the "new Zion." His rule was characterized by severity, pomp, and the practice of polygamy. The city was retaken on June 25, 1535, by Prince-Bishop Franz von Waldeck. Leyden and his companions were cruelly executed on January 22, 1536, and their bodies were hung in cages from the tower of the Lamberti church.

## Relation to Other Movements
The Anabaptists are often linked to the Zwickau prophets (Thomas Muenzer, Nikolaus Storch, and Marcus Stuebner), who emphasized the "inner word" and divine revelation received directly from the Spirit of God rather than the Bible or the church. While Muenzer repudiated infant baptism in theory, he did not insist on re-baptism or relinquish the practice, meaning the Zwickau movement was not typical of Anabaptism.

In England, Anabaptist views were introduced via foreign importations, primarily Dutchmen. Records from London indicate ten Dutch Anabaptists were put to death in 1535. In 1536, King Henry VIII issued a proclamation labeling Anabaptist opinions as "detestable heresies." By the reign of Edward VI, Anabaptists were noted for segregating themselves from others. Some communities existed in London, Kent, and East Anglia toward the end of the 16th century, where figures like Robert Cooke and Joan Boucher (burnt in 1550) were known.

## Influence on the Baptist Church
While the English Anabaptists raised the question of baptism, the rise of the modern Baptist churches is considered independent of them. The modern movement in England broke away from Brownism through John Smith (Smyth, d. 1612). After emigrating to Amsterdam and coming under Mennonite teaching in 1609, Smyth baptized himself and others, likely by pouring. 

Smyth, along with Morton and Thomas Helwys (ca. 1560-ca. 1616), formed the first English Baptist Church. Their 1611 declaration of faith stated that baptism should only occur upon the confession of faith and sins and "in no wise appertaineth to infants." They also advocated for absolute liberty of conscience, asserting that the magistrate should not compel any form of religion. Later, two distinct groups emerged in England: the Arminian General Baptists, influenced by Mennonite Anabaptists, and the Calvinist Particular Baptists, who sprang from the Independents.
