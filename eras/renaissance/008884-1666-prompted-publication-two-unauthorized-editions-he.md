# Boileau

## Early Publications and Royal Patronage
In 1666, prompted by the publication of two unauthorized editions, Boileau published *Satires du Sieur D....*, a work that included the *Discours au roi* and seven satires. Following this, from 1669 onwards, he produced epistles. These works were characterized by a style that was more polished and exquisite, and a tone and thought process that were more mature and graver than those found in his satires.

The *Epitres* earned Boileau the favor of Louis XIV, who requested his presence at court. During an encounter with the king, Boileau was asked to identify his best verses. He diplomatically responded by selecting some as yet unprinted lines written in honor of the grand monarch, describing them as his "least bad" work. After reciting them, he was granted a pension of 2000 livres.

## Major Works and Literary Theory
In 1674, Boileau published *Oeuvres diverses du sieur D....*, which included earlier works and two of his masterpieces: *Le Lutrin* and *L'Art poetique*. 

*L'Art poetique* was written in imitation of Horace's *Ars Poetica* and established the code for all future French verse, occupying a position in French literature parallel to that of its Latin prototype. In this work, Boileau does more than establish rules for poetic language; he provides a careful analysis of various kinds of verse composition and the principles specific to each. The work is divided into four books:
* The first and last books focus on general precepts, primarily emphasizing the rule of *bon sens*.
* The second book addresses the ode, the epigram, the satire, the elegy, and the pastoral.
* The third book focuses on epic and tragic poetry.

While these rules are considered of value, they have been noted for a tendency to make poetic efforts too mechanical and to hamper them.

## Influence and Critical Standing
Boileau's maxims had a significant influence on English literature, transmitted through a translation revised by Dryden and a "magnificent imitation" in Alexander Pope's *Essay on Criticism*. Additionally, his mock heroic poem *Le Lutrin*, four cantos of which appeared in 1674, served as a model for Pope's *The Rape of the Lock*, although the English poem is regarded as superior in its richness of imagination.

As a critic in verse, Boileau is described as great, though not infallible. He provided a significant service by destroying the exaggerated reputations of mediocrities of his era, although his judgment was occasionally at fault. Despite his critical contributions, he is not considered a great poet.
