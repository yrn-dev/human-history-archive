# The Deutsche Bassons, Fagotte, and Bombardi

The *Deutsche Bassons*, also referred to as *Fagotte* or *Bombardi*, were musical instruments used by German ancestors prior to the period when music adopted French and Italian styles. By 1738, Eisel noted that these specific instruments were no longer in use, stating that it was unnecessary to "waste paper on them."

## Identification and Characteristics
The *Deutsche Bassons* are identified as the bombard or bass pommer. These instruments were characterized by being extraordinarily long. The bass pommer, a direct ancestor of the modern bassoon, was a straight instrument measuring 6 feet in length. 

The modern bassoon (*basson* in French, *Fagott* in German, and *fagotto* in Italian) differs from its ancestor in that it was the first instrument of its kind to be doubled back upon itself. This physical structure led to the German and Italian names, which were derived from a perceived resemblance to a bundle of sticks. In contrast, the English and French names for the instrument refer to its pitch as the bass of the wood-wind.

## Evolution and Related Instruments
The development of the bassoon involved several transitional instruments designed to improve portability and reduce unwieldy proportions.

### The Single Curtail
To shorten the tenor pommer in C, builders created an instrument constructed from a block of wood with a diameter more than double that of the pommer. This design featured two bores that communicated at a flat bottom, with the bell and the double reed mouthpiece crook positioned side by side at the top. This instrument featured two keys and seven holes (six in front and one in the back). It was known by various names: *dulceian*, *dolcian*, *doucaine*, *courtaud* (in France), and *curtail*, *curtal*, or *curtoll* (in England). Records from 1582 mention the "Curtoll" alongside the "Drone" and "Hobius (Hautboy)."

### The Double Curtail
The subsequent evolutionary step was the double curtail, which served as a converted bass pommer. It was pitched an octave below the single curtail, making its construction and pitch identical to the early *fagotto* in C. A description from a manuscript of *The Academy of Armoury* by Randle Holme, written before 1688, describes the double curtail as being "double the bigness" of the single version and played eight notes deeper. It consisted of two pipes fixed within one thick bass pipe, with one pipe being significantly longer than the other.

## Notable Makers and Usage
Sigmund Schnitzer, a craftsman in Nuremberg, was highly successful in making the extraordinarily long bass pommers. Later historical accounts from the 18th century, specifically by Doppelmaier, describe Schnitzer as renowned "almost everywhere" for producing *fagotte* of pure intonation, easy speech, and skillful workmanship. His instruments were appreciated across Germany, Italy, and France. However, an earlier chronicler from 1549, Johann Neudorfer, referred to Schnitzer simply as a *Pfeifenmacher und Stadtpfeifer* (pipe maker and town piper), without noting him as an inventor of a new instrument form.

## The Bassoon in Classical Music
The bassoon became a favorite of many great musical masters, though it was an exception for Handel. Notable uses of the instrument include:
* **Beethoven:** Utilized the bassoon extensively in his symphonies for original and beautiful independent parts.
* **Bach:** Included parts for two bassoons in his mass in B min.
* **Mozart:** Composed a concerto in Bb for bassoon and orchestra (Kochel, No. 191).
* **Weber:** Wrote a concerto for bassoon in F (op. 75) for full orchestra.

Additionally, the wind-instrument-maker W. Heckel of Biebrich-am-Rhein, at the instigation of Wagner, produced bassoons with an additional key to extend the downward compass to A.
