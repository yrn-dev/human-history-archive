# Teachers and Preachers in Historical Context

The role of the teacher and preacher has manifested across various eras and cultures, ranging from the founders of spiritual movements and academic professors to colonial leaders and diplomats. These figures have utilized their positions to influence the thoughts of their contemporaries, provide spiritual guidance, or advance specific philosophical and political ideologies.

## Spiritual and Religious Teachers

In the early history of Buddhism, Gotama (the Buddha) served as a teacher who attracted large numbers of hearers. During his time at the Veluvana (Bamboo-grove), a garden presented to him by King Bimbisara of Rajagaha, he preached many of his most complete discourses. His teachings focused on the miseries of the world resulting from passion and the possibility of release through a way of salvation. One of his disciples, Kassapa, highlighted the Buddha's teachings by stating that Nirvana is a state of rest attainable only through a change of heart, rejecting the efficacy of sacrifices.

The Buddha also provided guidance to those wishing to spread his teachings. When a merchant from Sunaparanta sought permission to preach to his relations, the Buddha questioned how the mendicant would handle violence, as the people of that region were considered exceedingly violent. The mendicant responded that he would not reply to revilement, would not strike back if struck, and would accept death without attempting to hasten or delay it, noting that death is not an evil in itself.

In later contexts, other figures focused on religious teaching for specific audiences. One such author produced works including *A Child's Book of Religion* (1866) and *Stories from the Life of the Teacher* (1863), as well as expositions on a "new faith." Similarly, Kingsley was described as a stimulating teacher and preacher who was vivid and earnest. Whether preaching to the village poor or a fashionable congregation, Kingsley's influence was characterized by inducing others to think for themselves rather than forcing them to adopt his own views. His published discourses include the *Twenty-five Village Sermons* and a sermon titled *The Message of the Church to Working Men*.

## Academic and Philosophical Teachers

The university system provided a platform for teachers of logic, medicine, and history. Francis Burgersdyk (1590–1629), a Dutch logician, held chairs in logic, moral philosophy, and natural philosophy at the university of Leiden. His work *Logic* was widely used, and he also wrote *Idea Philosophiae Moralis* (1644).

Gaspard Bartholinus (1585–1629) began his career as a professor of medicine at the university of Copenhagen in 1613. Following a dangerous illness, he fulfilled a vow to devote himself to the study of divinity, subsequently becoming a professor of divinity at Copenhagen and a canon of Roskilde.

In the realm of history and law, Isaac Dorislaus (1595–1649) was appointed to a lectureship in history at Cambridge. However, his tenure there was cut short by his early resignation, which resulted from his attempts to justify the Dutch revolt against Spain.

## Colonial and Community Leadership

In the American colonies, William Brewster (c. 1566–1644) combined the roles of community leader and teacher. A founder of the Plymouth Colony and a ruling elder, Brewster also served as a "teacher." He preached "both powerfully and profitably" to the contentment and edification of his hearers. He performed this role regularly until 1629, when the first pastor, Ralph Smith, arrived, and continued to do so irregularly thereafter.

## Preaching and Public Discourse in England

Preaching often intersected with political and ecclesiastical appointments in England. Fuller, who was admitted as a D.D. at Cambridge and served as chaplain in extraordinary to Charles II, lectured at the Savoy. While Samuel Pepys heard him preach there, it is noted that Fuller preferred his books or conversation to his sermons. Fuller's public discourse also extended to political arguments, such as his 1660 work *An Alarum to the Counties of England and Wales*, in which he argued for a parliament free from force and previous engagements.
