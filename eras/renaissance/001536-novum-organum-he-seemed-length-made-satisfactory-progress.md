# Novum Organum

The *Novum Organum* is the most celebrated work of Francis Bacon, published in 1620. It serves as a cornerstone of his broader project, the *Instauratio*, which aimed to reorganize the sciences and restore humanity's command over nature—a command Bacon believed had been lost following the fall.

## Role within the Instauratio
The *Instauratio* was designed as a comprehensive scheme of the sciences consisting of six divisions. The *Novum Organum* represents the second division, titled *Interpretatio Naturae*. This section was intended to provide a new method to train and direct the human mind toward the renovation of science. While the *Novum Organum* (particularly book ii) represents this division, it is noted to do so only imperfectly.

The work is closely linked to other parts of the *Instauratio*. For instance, the *Partitiones Scientiarum* (represented by *De Augmentis Scientiarum*) provided a survey and inventory of the possessions of the human mind. Following the method of the *Novum Organum*, the third division, *Historia Naturalis et Experimentalis*, sought to collect the materials of the *Phaenomena Universi*. Bacon appended a tract called *Parasceve* to the *Novum Organum*, which provides a short introductory sketch of the requisites for such a natural history. Bacon viewed the *Organum* and the *Natural History* as correlative; he believed that while the *Organum* provided the method, it would be valueless and inapplicable without the duly collected materials of a natural history.

## Core Concepts and Methodology
Bacon ascribed the qualities of mechanical simplicity and absolute certainty to the method devised in the *Novum Organum*. A central aim of his philosophy was the discovery of "forms." Bacon defined the "form of a nature" as something that, if given, ensures the nature infallibly follows, and if removed, ensures the nature infallibly vanishes. He further described the true form as that which deduces a given nature from a source of being inherent in more natures.

The *Novum Organum* also incorporates ideas developed in Bacon's earlier, minor philosophical writings. For example, the *Redargutio Philosophiarum* contains much of the detail that later appears in book i of the *Novum Organum* regarding the *Idola Theatri*. Similarly, the *Cogitata et Visa* contains the substance of the first book of the *Organum*.

## Influence and Legacy
Bacon is regarded by many as the father of English psychological speculation, famously stating that he "rang the bell which called the wits together." His influence is seen in several fields:

*   **Philosophy:** The fundamental principle of Locke's *Essay*—that sensation and reflection produce all ideas—is briefly stated in the first aphorism of the *Novum Organum*.
*   **Ethics:** Bacon is viewed as a forerunner of the empirical method in ethics. The inductive method discussed in the *Novum Organum* is considered the root of theories that construct moral codes through the inductive examination of human consciousness and the results of actions, most notably utilitarianism.

However, this influence was not universal. Hobbes, who served as Bacon's secretary, made no critical reference to Bacon in his works nor mentioned Baconian induction.

## Historical Context and Evaluation
The *Novum Organum* emerged at a time when scholasticism was decaying and the authority of school doctrines and the church was being discarded. Bacon gathered the scattered efforts of devoted experimenters into a unified system. 

Modern evaluations suggest that while the specific logical rules Bacon devised have not been found to be of practical service and may be logically defective, his fundamental ideas remain valuable. These include the necessity for a critical analysis of experience and the rejection of rash generalization. While scientific discovery now relies primarily on the employment of "scientific imagination" and hypothesis—for which no fixed code of rules exists—Bacon is still credited, alongside Descartes, with inaugurating modern philosophy.

## Personal Context
The publication of the *Novum Organum* coincided with a period of significant professional ascent for Bacon. By January 1620/1, he had been created Viscount St Albans, having previously been named Baron Verulam in July 1617 and Lord Chancellor on January 4, 1617/8. At this time, he appeared to have made satisfactory progress toward his cherished aims, having attained a high state rank and partially completed the method essential for his *Instauratio*.
