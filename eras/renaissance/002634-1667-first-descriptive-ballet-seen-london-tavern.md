# The Development of Ballet in England

## Origins and Early History
The modern ballet is a theatrical performance involving music, pantomime, and dancing, where a story is conveyed through gesture. The term originates from the Greek *ballizein* (to dance), passing through Medieval Latin *ballare* and Italian *ballare* and *ballata* before becoming the French *ballet*. In Old French, the term signified "to dance, to sing, to rejoice," a meaning that eventually split into the modern distinct words "ballet," "ball," and "ballad."

The earliest recorded modern ballet took place in 1489 at Tortona to celebrate the marriage of the duke of Milan (Duke Galeazzo). While the art form was developed and perfected in France, it was closely linked to the history of the opera. In France, Catherine de' Medici introduced ballet entertainments, spending significant sums to distract her son from state affairs. A landmark event occurred in 1581 with the "Ballet Comique de la Reyne," composed by Baltasarini (also known as Beaujoyeulx), which was the first instance of music and dance being arranged to display coherent dramatic ideas.

## Evolution of the Art Form
The development of ballet is categorized into three distinct stages. Initially, it was exclusive to the court for princely celebrations, where high-ranking ladies performed. Figures such as Henry IV, Louis XIII, and Louis XIV were lovers of the ballet and performed various characters. Richelieu utilized the ballet for political expression. Lully furthered the art by composing ballet music and insisting that women be admitted as dancers, replacing the previous practice of men dressing as women for feminine roles.

The second stage began when the ballet became unpopular at court after Louis XIV became too fat to dance. It was then adopted by colleges for prize distributions, frequently featuring the ballets of Quinault and Lully. The third stage marked the transition of ballet to the public stage. Jean Georges Noverre is credited as the father of modern ballet dancing and the inventor of the *ballet d'action*, emphasizing that dancing must be a direct expression of a specific attitude of mind rather than mere display.

## Ballet in England
Ballet arrived in England much later than the opera and was not introduced until the 18th century; early Italian operas performed in London lacked ballet entirely. During the regency of Lord Middlesex, a *corps* of dancers was formed and a ballet-master was appointed.

The English language first adopted the word "balette" in 1667, used by Dryden. However, the first descriptive ballet seen in London was *The Tavern Bilkers*, performed at Drury Lane in 1702. Following this, English ballet remained largely exotic, following French developments.

The "palmy days" of English ballet occurred during the first half of the 19th century, supported by royal revenue. Notable dancers of this era included Fanny Elssler, Carlotta Grisi, Mdlle Cerito, Mdlle Taglioni—who is said to have turned the heads of an entire generation—Mdlle Carolina Rosati, Miss Lucile Grahn, and Miss P. Horton. Sir Michael Andrew Agnus Costa, a British conductor and composer, wrote several ballets during this period, including *Kenilworth* (1831), *Une Heure a Naples* (1832), *Sir Huon* (1833) for Taglioni, and *Alma* (1842) for Cerito.

## Modern Transitions and Decadence
In later years, Kate Vaughan introduced a new type of grace to English dancing and popularized skirt-dancing. While a beautiful exhibition, skirt-dancing is considered a departure from true dancing because the steps are of little importance, often evolving into the exhibition of whirling draperies under multi-coloured lime-lights. Vaughan's art was imitated by Miss Letty Lind and Miss Sylvia Grey, while Adeline Genee became the finest exponent of the older classical school in London.

More recently, ballet has shifted toward becoming a spectacle that demands less intellectual appreciation and more visual stimulation. This modern trend involves spending thousands of pounds on costumes for large groups of women who primarily march and group themselves by color and mass on stage, making the chief interest of the performance independent of the dancing itself.
