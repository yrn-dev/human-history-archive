# Choral Music and Traditions

Choral music encompasses a wide range of historical forms, from the public lyric poetry of ancient Greece to the complex ecclesiastical and secular compositions of the Baroque and Romantic eras. It has functioned as a vehicle for religious worship, theatrical development, and national expression.

## Ancient Greek Choral Traditions
In ancient Greece, choral poetry was a primary feature of the Dorian school. Unlike Aeolian poetry, which was personal, Dorian lyric poetry was public in character. It consisted largely of odes sung at festivals and hymns used for the public worship of the gods, driven by Dorian pride in their social usage, government, and traditions of worship.

The form of these choral works evolved through several key figures:
* **Alcman (660 B.C.):** Credited with introducing the balanced movement of the antistrophe and strophe.
* **Stesichorus:** Added the epode, a section sung by the chorus while stationary.
* **Arion of Methymna:** Organized the "cyclic" or circular chorus that sang at the altar and gave a finished form to the "dithyramb," a choral hymn honoring Dionysus.
* **Pratinas of Phlius:** Wrote dithyrambs, tragedies, and *hyporchemata*, which were lively choral odes primarily honoring Apollo.

The epode served as the third part of an ode, completing the movement after the strophe and antistrophe. During this phase, the choirs—which had previously chanted to the left and right of the stage or altar—would either sing in unison or allow the *coryphaeus* (leader) to sing for them while standing in the center.

## Development of Drama from Choral Roots
Greek drama emerged from festivals for Dionysus, the god of wine. These festivals involved rustic worshippers singing hymns about the god's sufferings or victories. "Tragedy" (meaning "goat-song") and "Comedy" (meaning "village-song") both originated from these hymns. 

The transition to drama occurred when the leader of the chorus began reciting adventures as a messenger or as the god himself, prompting a choral response. The Attic Thespis (c. 536 B.C.) further developed this by arranging a dialogue between the leader (*koryphaios*) and a chosen member of the chorus known as the "answerer" (*hypokrites*), which later became the word for "actor."

## The Choral Works of J.S. Bach
Johann Sebastian Bach produced a vast body of choral and choral-related music, categorized by its instrumentation and purpose.

### Church Music
Bach composed 190 church cantatas. Approximately 60 of these were more or less founded on chorales, while 40 were for solo voices. The remainder were practically short oratorios, though almost all contained a chorale for congregational singing. Other major choral works include:
* **Oratorios:** The Christmas oratorio (six connected cantatas) and the Easter oratorio.
* **Passions:** The *Passion according to St Matthew* and the *Passion according to St John*. The latter underwent two revisions to reduce its scale, resulting in the transfer of its greatest figured chorale to the *Passion according to St Matthew*.
* **Masses:** The Mass in B minor and four short masses (consisting only of the Kyrie and Gloria) compiled from church cantatas.
* **Other Works:** The Magnificat in D, a funeral ode for the Duchess Eberhardine, and various ecclesiastical Latin choruses.

### Organ and Secular Choral Music
Bach's organ works include *Das Orgelbuechlein*, a planned but partially executed collection of short choral-preludes, and 18 larger chorale-preludes. He also arranged six "Schuebler" chorales from cantata movements. 

In secular vocal music, Bach wrote seven choral cantatas (three of which were later absorbed into the B minor mass and the Christmas oratorio) and seven solo cantatas. His choral style evolved over time; during his final period (1733–1750), his choral works became more numerous and terse, as seen in *Jesu, der du meine Seele*.

## Other Historical Choral Contributions
Choral elements appear in various other musical and theatrical contexts:
* **Beethoven:** The 9th symphony features a choral finale, a design foreshadowed by the Choral Fantasia and the Fantasia op. 77.
* **Karel Bendl (1838–1897):** A Bohemian composer who wrote a number of small choral works starting in 1858. His work *Poletuje holubice* (1861) became a favorite of local choral societies. Bendl served as the conductor of the choral society *Hlahoe* from 1865 to 1879. His choral output included a mass for a mixed choir, a mass in D minor for male voices, and numerous songs and choruses regarded as national possessions of Bohemia.
* **Roman Theatre:** The *Pantomimus* developed from the *canticum*, or choral singing, found in older *fabulae Atellanae* and comedies.
