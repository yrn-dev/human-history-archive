# Francis Bacon

Francis Bacon was a prominent figure in the English state, described as the most able man in the kingdom, characterized by a far-seeing, acute, and methodical intellect. In his fragment *De Interpretation Naturae Prooemium*, written around 1603, Bacon outlined his motivations for entering public life, stating he believed himself born to be of advantage to mankind. His life was guided by three primary goals: the discovery of truth for the benefit of the human race, a desire to be of service to his country, and a wish to do good for the church. To achieve these, he sought an honorable state post.

## Political Career and Relations with King James

Bacon was a devoted supporter of the royal party and served as attorney-general. While he was prudent and sagacious, his influence over King James was secondary; his practical skills were primarily used to implement the king's measures in the most effective manner possible. 

Bacon's political sympathies generally aligned with the government's home policy. He did not object to patents or monopolies that appeared advantageous to the country, though it is noted that he may have prioritized the advantages to the government over the disadvantages to the people. By November 1620, however, he urged Buckingham to withdraw the most obnoxious patents—specifically those regarding gold and silver thread and alehouses and inns—to avoid an impending political storm.

In matters of foreign policy, Bacon differed from the peace-loving King James. Bacon advocated for a war with Spain to recover the Palatinate, believing such a move would allow the king to lead the parliament and create a policy with which the people could heartily sympathize.

## Parliamentary and Legal Conflicts

Bacon's tenure in parliament was marked by various tensions. In April 1614, while representing Cambridge University, he faced objections from the House regarding his eligibility to serve as a member while holding the office of attorney-general. Although an exception was made for him, the House resolved that no future attorney-general should be eligible for a seat.

During a crisis regarding state necessities, Bacon's conduct regarding subsidies led to significant misunderstanding. While he approved of an increased triple subsidy, he opposed the short four-year period proposed for its collection, arguing that such heavy demands would cause discontent and that money should instead be raised by imposition or levy. Although his motion failed and the subsidy passed unanimously, his actions—prompted by a desire to serve the queen—caused deep offense and led to accusations that he was seeking popularity.

In legal matters, Bacon acted as a conduit between the king and the judiciary. In the case of the *commendams*, concerning the king's prerogative to grant livings, James directed Bacon to tell the judges to delay judgment until they had discussed the matter with the king. Bacon communicated this to the judges, though he hinted that he may have gone slightly beyond his specific instructions by first communicating with his rival, Coke.

## Relations with Buckingham and Coke

Bacon's relationship with the marquess of Buckingham was occasionally strained. In one instance, Bacon opposed a proposed marriage between the daughter of Lady Hatton and Sir John Villiers (Buckingham's brother), viewing the match as unsuitable. After explaining his disapproval to the king and Buckingham, he received bitter letters of reproof and rumors of disgrace. Buckingham compared this conduct to Bacon's previous unfaithfulness to Essex. Seeking to protect Buckingham's own interests, Bacon quickly changed his course, apologized, and used his influence to advance the marriage.

Bacon's views on political power included a belief that the use of favorites by princes served as a remedy against "ambitious great ones," as the favorite controlled the means of pleasing or displeasing others.

## Personal Reflections and Legacy

Facing accusations and "hunting for matters against him," Bacon maintained his integrity, stating in his letters that he possessed "clean hands and a clean heart." He observed that in times of greatness, accusation becomes a "game" and even the most just judge could seem foul under such scrutiny.
