# Brickmaking and Architectural Skill in the 16th Century

The development of architectural skill and the use of building materials during the 16th century varied significantly across different regions, with notable advancements in brickmaking in England and the construction of palaces and civic buildings in Europe.

## Brickmaking in England
Brickmaking was introduced to Britain nearly 2,000 years ago, but the art was lost following the withdrawal of the Romans. Until the 13th century, it is doubtful whether any burnt bricks were produced in England; during this interval, bricks were generally salvaged from Roman remains, such as those found at St Albans Abbey and Colchester. While Little Wenham Hall in Suffolk (built circa A.D. 1210) is one of the earliest existing brick buildings from the period of revival, bricks did not enter general use until the 15th century, and even then, they were reserved for important edifices.

During the reign of Henry VIII, brickmaking reached a state of great perfection, a development likely attributed to workmen brought from Flanders. The skill attained during this era is testified to by the older portions of Hampton Court Palace and St James's Palace. Throughout the 16th century, the use of bricks increased. However, until the Great Fire of London in 1666, smaller structures—including dwelling-houses and shops—continued to be built using timber frameworks filled with plaster and lath.

Following the Great Fire, bricks were used extensively in the rebuilding of London. From the end of the 17th century onward, they became the almost exclusive material for ordinary buildings across the country, except in areas where good brick-clay was unavailable and building stone was plentiful. Prior to 1625, English bricks lacked a recognized standard size; however, a statute passed in 1625 regulated the size to the current standard of 9 x 4½ x 3 inches. Later, from 1784 until 1850, a tax was levied on bricks, averaging approximately 4s. 7d. per thousand for ordinary bricks, with special bricks facing heavier taxation.

## 16th Century Palaces and Civic Architecture in Europe
In other parts of Europe, the 16th century saw the construction of various palaces and institutional buildings. In one region, the old ducal palace was begun in 1578 by Ammanati; it currently serves as the seat of provincial officers, the residence of the prefect, and a public picture gallery. Other 16th-century structures in the same area include the palace of the marquis Guidiccioni, which now houses archives with documents dating back to A.D. 790, as well as several other fine palaces from the late 16th century.

In France, the town of Carpentras features the church of St Siffrein, which dates from the 15th and 16th centuries and is built in the Gothic style, though it retains remains of an earlier Romanesque church. Additionally, in the town of Ornans, there is a 16th-century church and the ruins of a feudal castle. In Montbenoit, the remains of an Augustine abbey exist from the 13th to 16th centuries; its church contains fine stalls executed in the 16th century.

## Other Historical Architectural Remains
Beyond the 16th century, other eras provided evidence of high architectural skill. In Egypt, the remains of palaces, temples, obelisks, the labyrinth, and the pyramids demonstrate the perfection of sculpture, painting, and architecture. These works are noted for their beautiful symmetry, just proportion, and the liveliness of colors that have survived despite the passage of time.

In Japan, the "rough palace" (Araki-no-miya) served as a separate structure where the coffin of an Imperial personage was guarded day and night while a permanent tomb was being prepared. This period of temporary mourning was known as kari-mo-gari.
