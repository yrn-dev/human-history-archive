# Fine Printed Books in Late 15th and 16th Century England

## Binding Materials and Decoration
During the 16th century and the late 15th century in England, a number of fine printed books were bound using luxurious materials such as satin and velvet. These bindings were occasionally embroidered or set with enamels. Because these books utilized strong threads of metal freely within their construction, many have lasted better than would typically be expected. Examples of this craftsmanship produced for Henry VIII remain in a decorative and excellent state of preservation.

Throughout the Tudor period, several small bindings were created using gold and ornamented with enamels. These items are described as charming little jewels and were consistently provided with a ring at the top, which was intended for attachment to a girdle.

## Heraldic Designs and Ownership
The practice of ornamenting royal books in England with heraldic designs is considered to have begun during the reign of Edward IV and continued without interruption. This fashion eventually extended to books owned by private individuals, a trend that first appeared during the later Tudor period. Since that time, numbers of such books have been produced and continue to be made.

## Typography and Printing Trends
The 16th century saw significant shifts in printing and typography. In 1501, Aldus in Venice popularized the small octavo and introduced the italic type. While italics became the standard vernacular type in Italy by approximately 1570 and gained great popularity in France, they achieved only slight popularity in England.

In contrast, roman type—which was not used at all during the 15th century—made steady progress in England as it competed with black letter. By the end of the 16th century, black letter was rarely used, except for the production of proclamations and Bibles. Additionally, the modern usage of the letters u and v, as well as i and j, dates from around 1580, although these practices were not firmly established until the reign of Charles I.

## Preservation and Binding Customs
The survival of certain 15th-century ephemeral literature is linked to 16th-century binding practices. Many broadsides and single sheets of "catchpenny literature" and cheap romances survived only because of the custom of using such waste paper as a substitute for wooden boards to stiffen book bindings. 

This occurred during a period of great intellectual and religious upheaval in the 16th century, which caused much previously current literature to become outdated. Cumbrous early editions of books that remained in use were often superseded by handier versions.
