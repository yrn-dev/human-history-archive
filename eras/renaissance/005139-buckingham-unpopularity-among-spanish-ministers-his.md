# Buckingham's Relations with Spain and the Spanish Court

## Diplomatic Shifts and the Spanish Ambassador
In the period leading up to 1620, Buckingham's political alignment shifted significantly. While he initially aligned himself with a popular movement regarding the revolution in Bohemia and the Spanish preparation to invade the Palatinate, he later moved into close agreement with Gondomar, the Spanish ambassador. This change may have been influenced by his marriage to Lady Katherine Manners, the daughter of the earl of Rutland, who was a Roman Catholic at heart.

By the winter of 1621 and into the following year, Buckingham was described as being entirely in the hands of Gondomar. During this time, Laud had to argue with some difficulty in May 1622 to dissuade Buckingham from a resolution to declare himself a Roman Catholic. By May 1622, it is believed that an understanding had been reached between Buckingham and the Spanish ambassador for the prince of Wales to visit Madrid the following year. The Spanish court hoped this visit would lead to the prince's conversion to Roman Catholicism before he was granted the hand of the infanta Maria.

## The Expedition to Madrid
Charles and Buckingham departed on an expedition on February 17, 1623, arriving in Madrid on March 7 after passing through Paris. The mission was characterized by mutual deception: Charles and Buckingham hoped for the restitution of the Palatinate to the son-in-law of King James as a marriage gift, while the Spaniards sought extreme concessions and the conversion of Charles to Roman Catholicism.

The diplomatic mission deteriorated as political differences were exacerbated by personal disputes. Buckingham entered into conflicts with the grandees and Olivares. By the time the two men sailed from Santander in September, they had reached a final resolution to break entirely with Spain.

## Conflict with the Spanish Ministers and the Earl of Bristol
The failure of the Spanish negotiations led to significant internal friction within the English administration. The earl of Bristol, who had been created earl of Bristol on September 15 and had urged the marriage treaty, saw the negotiations wrecked by the arrival of Buckingham and Charles in March 1623.

Bristol incurred the resentment of Buckingham and Charles, primarily driven by Buckingham's "implacable jealousy." This resentment was sparked by a letter Bristol wrote to King James, which informed the king of Buckingham's unpopularity among the Spanish ministers, as well as Bristol's efforts to maintain peace with Spain after the departure of the prince and Buckingham.

Following these events, Bristol left Spain in January 1624. Upon his arrival at Dover in March, Buckingham and Charles held complete ascendancy over the king. Consequently, Bristol was forbidden from appearing at court and ordered to confine himself at Sherborne. Buckingham required Bristol to answer a series of interrogatories, though Bristol refused to inculpate himself and demanded a trial by parliament.

## Hostilities and Military Action
Following the break with Spain, Buckingham and his master worked to shift public opinion. This included abandoning engagements to France and launching a military offensive against Spain. A large fleet was dispatched to attack Cadiz and seize Spanish treasure-ships, though the fleet returned having effected nothing. 

Further tensions arose when a fleet under the earl of Lindsey (Lord Willoughby) was sent against the Spaniards but returned shattered by a storm. In 1627, the conflict expanded to include France, as Charles persisted in seizing French vessels. This led to a situation where Richelieu laid siege to La Rochelle; on June 27, 1627, Buckingham sailed from Portsmouth with a land force and a numerous fleet to relieve the city.

## Domestic Fallout and Impeachment
Buckingham's leadership and his guidance of the king's continental engagements—which involved enormous expenditure and were unpopular in the House of Commons—led to severe domestic criticism. The Commons lacked confidence in him and suspected the marriage treaty contained disapproved engagements.

When Charles's second parliament met on February 6, 1626, the House of Commons, guided by Eliot, sought Buckingham's punishment. He was impeached before the House of Lords on a long series of charges. While some of these charges were untrue or exaggerated, his primary failure was as the leader of the administration. Although Charles dissolved the parliament, the Star Chamber subsequently declared the duke innocent of the charges.
