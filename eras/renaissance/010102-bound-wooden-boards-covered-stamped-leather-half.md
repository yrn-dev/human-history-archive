# History and Evolution of Book Binding

The development of bindings and covers for written or printed materials has historically followed the shape of the medium being protected. While early inscriptions on wood or rock required no covering, the earliest protective covers appeared around the 8th century B.C. with small Assyrian tablets. These cuneiform tablets, which recorded loans, slave sales, and other small matters, were often enclosed in an outer shell of the same shape and impressed with a short title. Egyptian papyrus rolls were typically bound with papyrus tape, often sealed with Nile mud, and stored in rectangular hollows cut from wood. Other early forms included bark books—often containing medical receipts or magical formulae—which were folded like a concertina. In Pompeii, diptychs were discovered consisting of wooden leaves hollowed out and filled with blackened wax, hinged with leather thongs. These diptychs are considered the prototypes of the modern book.

## Medieval Binding Techniques

The medieval "half-binding" emerged from the need to protect the projecting bands used when sewing quires together. Strips of leather were fastened across the back of the joined quires to protect these bands. Because vellum leaves had a tendency to curl strongly, strong wooden boards were placed on each side of the book. The loose ends of the sewing bands were fastened to these boards, which hinged upon them, and the leather strip on the back was drawn over the boards to cover the hinge. In a half-binding, the leather strip covers the back and projects slightly over the boards, leaving the rest of the boards uncovered. These books were usually kept closed with front clasps.

Over time, the leather strip expanded to cover the entirety of the boards, a style known as "whole" binding. These flat leather surfaces provided a field for artistic decoration. Early ornamentation was likely created using small metal lines or points pressed into the leather, which evolved into the use of decorative stamps. English binders were noted for their excellence in "blind" stamping—impressions made without gold leaf. Many of these stamps were cut intaglio to create cameo-form impressions. This art reached perfection during the 12th and 13th centuries in centers such as London, Cambridge, Oxford, and Durham. An example of this is the 12th-century Winchester Domesday Book, which features blind-stamped dark brown morocco.

## Printing and the Transition of Materials

The introduction of printing in Europe during the middle of the 15th century caused a reaction against the precious covers and illuminated manuscripts of the past. Printing initially produced "ugly," cheap, and small books generally bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf, often ornamented with blind large panel stamps. 

However, the earliest printed books—particularly legal commentaries, Bibles, and great choir-books—remained immensely large and heavy, printed on extraordinarily thick and strong paper. These were often bound with wooden boards that were either covered with stamped leather or left half-uncovered. The cost of these works varied; a rubricated and illuminated vellum copy of the first printed Bible (Mainz, c. 1455) in two large folio volumes was said to be worth 100 florins. By 1467, the bishop of Aleria noted that printing had reduced book prices to one-fifth of their previous cost.

## The Art of Gold Tooling

A new art of gold tooling on leather emerged shortly after the advent of printing, likely brought to Venice from the East. The finest examples of this craft are found in late 15th-century Italian work. In England, Thomas Berthelet, the Royal Binder to Henry VIII, was the first to practice this art, working until approximately 1558 with a style strongly influenced by Italian masters.

Notable collectors and binders further refined the craft:
* **Jean Grolier**, treasurer of France in 1545, collected books bound in pale brown morocco with gold tooling, featuring his name and the legend *Portio mea domine sit in terra viventium*.
* **Tommaso Maioli**, an Italian collector, used a similar legend, and his books are marked with atoms of gold in the leather's irregularities.
* **Demetrio Canevari**, physician to Pope Urban VIII, used deep red or dark green morocco featuring a colored cameo stamp of Apollo driving a chariot with one black and one white horse toward a mountain with a silver Pegasus, surrounded by the Greek legend [ORTHOS KAI MAE LOXIOS].
* **Geoffrey Tory**, printer to Francis I of France, also designed fine bindings.

## Later Developments in Binding

By the 18th century, French binders like the Deromes family continued to innovate. Nicholas Denis ("Le Jeune"), born in 1731, utilized "dentelles a l'oiseau," which were dentelle borders with arabesques interspersed with small birds. Jean Charles le Monnier was skilled in using delicate inlays of colored leathers, often signing his work in gold small capitals.

In England, the "Harleian style" was developed by Eliot and Chapman for the library of Robert Harley, earl of Oxford, characterized by morocco bindings with diamond-shaped center-pieces and broad, richly gold-tooled borders. Additionally, Thomas Hollis used fine red morocco with stamps engraved by Thomas Pingo, depicting figures of Britannia, liberty, and a cap of liberty.
