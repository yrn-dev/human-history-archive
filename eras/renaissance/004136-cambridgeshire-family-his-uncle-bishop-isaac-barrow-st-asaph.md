# Isaac Barrow (1630–1677)

Isaac Barrow was an English divine and mathematician born into an old family from Cambridgeshire and Suffolk. He was the son of Thomas Barrow, who worked as a linen-draper in London. His uncle was Isaac Barrow, the Bishop of St Asaph (1614–1680).

## Early Education and Travel
Barrow's early schooling began with two or three years at the Charterhouse school, though his conduct there provided little hope for his success as a scholar. His academic trajectory improved following his move to Felsted school in Essex under the mastership of Martin Holbeach. After making significant progress in his studies, Barrow entered St Peter's College in 1643, and later Trinity College, Cambridge.

At Cambridge, he focused on science and literature, with a particular emphasis on natural philosophy. His early academic interests were broad; he initially intended to enter the medical profession and studied chemistry, botany, and anatomy. He later expanded his studies to include astronomy, geometry, and chronology.

Following his studies, Barrow traveled through Italy and France. During a voyage from Leghorn to Smyrna, he demonstrated great personal bravery when attacked by an Algerine pirate. In Smyrna, he was received by the English consul, Mr. Bretton, for whom Barrow later wrote a Latin elegy. He then traveled to Constantinople, where he was hosted by Sir Jonathan Dawes and the English ambassador, Sir Thomas Bendish. While residing in Turkey for over a year, Barrow studied the works of St Chrysostom, whom he preferred over all other Fathers. He returned to England in 1659 via Holland and Germany.

## Academic Career at Cambridge and Gresham College
Upon returning to England, Barrow received ordination from Bishop Brownrig. In 1660, he was appointed to the Greek professorship at Cambridge. Although he originally intended to lecture on the tragedies of Sophocles, he instead chose Aristotle's rhetoric; however, these lectures were lost after being lent to a friend.

In July 1662, on the recommendation of Dr. John Wilkins (the master of Trinity College and later bishop of Chester), Barrow was elected professor of geometry at Gresham College. In May 1663, he became a fellow of the Royal Society during the first election held by the council after they obtained their charter. That same year, Barrow was appointed as the first professor of the mathematical chair at Cambridge, which had been founded by the will of Henry Lucas. To avoid conflict between his two roles, Barrow resigned from Gresham College on May 20, 1664.

## Transition to Divinity and Later Life
In 1669, Barrow resigned his mathematical chair to his pupil, Isaac Newton, as he decided to abandon the study of mathematics in favor of divinity. At the time of this resignation, he held only a fellowship at Trinity College. To support him, his uncle provided him with a small sinecure in Wales, and Dr. Seth Ward, the bishop of Salisbury, granted him a prebend in that church.

Barrow was created a doctor in divinity by mandate in 1670. On February 13, 1672, by king's patent, he was appointed master of Trinity College, succeeding Dr. Pearson, who had been promoted to the see of Chester. In 1675, he was chosen as the vice-chancellor of the university.

Barrow is remembered in religious literature for his strong, eloquent, and colored preaching, which is described as a survival of the older, larger manner of the Church, balancing emotion, learning, and logic.

## Death
Isaac Barrow died on May 4, 1677. He was interred in Westminster Abbey, where his friends contributed to the erection of a monument topped with his bust.
