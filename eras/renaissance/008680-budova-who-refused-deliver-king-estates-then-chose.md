# The Diet of 1609 and the Bohemian Religious Conflict

The Diet of 1609 stands as one of the most significant events in the history of Bohemia, marking a period of intense religious and political struggle between the Bohemian estates and King Rudolph. This conflict was characterized by a clash over religious liberties, the legal status of faith-based documents, and the personal instability of the monarch.

## The Reign of King Rudolph
Rudolph succeeded Maximilian as King of Bohemia without opposition. Having been educated in Spain, there were initial expectations that he would treat the reformers of the Bohemian church with severity. However, the early part of his reign was marked by apathy and indifference toward both matters of state and religious controversy. Rudolph suffered from fits of melancholia and periods of actual insanity.

By 1600, his mental impairment became so serious that the Habsburg princes considered the future of the state, as Rudolph had no legitimate descendants. His brother Matthias suggested the appointment of a coadjutor and arrangements for succession, suggestions which Rudolph indignantly rejected. This tension was further exacerbated by a letter from Pope Clement VIII, who formally pointed out the negative results of Rudolph's neglect of his royal duties and urged him to appoint a successor for the thrones of Bohemia, Hungary, and the imperial crown.

## Political Instability and the Conflict with Matthias
The relationship between Rudolph and his younger brother Matthias was defined by strife and treachery. Matthias attempted to supplant Rudolph by utilizing the political and religious controversies of the era. The formal rupture occurred when Rudolph refused to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey that Matthias had concluded as his representative in Hungary.

This led to a period of fragmentation: the Hungarians accepted Matthias as their ruler, and the estates of Moravia, led by Charles, lord of Zerotin, renounced their allegiance to Rudolph. Although Matthias invaded Bohemia and invited the estates to join him at Caslav, the Bohemians declined. In March 1608, the estates met at Prague; while they submitted demands regarding ecclesiastical matters to Rudolph, they authorized him to levy troops for the defense of the kingdom. Eventually, on June 25, 1608, an understanding was reached: Rudolph retained his rights as King of Bohemia but ceded the government of Upper and Lower Austria, Moravia, and Hungary to Matthias.

## The Diet of 1609 and the Twenty-Five Articles
Following a temporary settlement, the estates of Bohemia brought their religious demands before Rudolph again. The resulting diet, which met on January 20, 1609, was heavily influenced by personal enmities. Rudolph had previously declined to discuss religious matters while his brother's troops occupied parts of Bohemia, and he remained resentful of his brother's treachery. Simultaneously, Rudolph was secretly negotiating with Christian of Anhalt, the leader of the German Protestants, a fact known to the Bohemians and the Spanish court.

The estates sought the recognition of the *Confessio Bohemica*—a profession of faith drawn up by the Bohemian brotherhood and advanced Utraquists that aligned with Calvinist views on the Lord's Supper—as one of the fundamental laws of the kingdom. In March of the previous year, the estates had formulated these demands into a document known as the "twenty-five Articles." Their primary requests included:
* The granting of complete religious liberty to all classes.
* Equal rights for Roman Catholics and Protestants (a term then used to collectively describe Lutherans, Utraquists, and Bohemian Brethren) to hold all offices of state.
* Limitations on the ability of the Jesuits to acquire land.
* Redress for grievances resulting from Rudolph's misrule.

## The Role of Budova and the Negotiations
King Rudolph viewed the twenty-five Articles as illegal and demanded that the document be delivered to him for destruction. However, the document remained in the possession of Budova, who refused to deliver it to the king.

In response to this impasse, the estates chose twelve members of their own number to negotiate with the king and his councillors. Among these negotiators was Count Henry Matthias Thurn. Despite protracted discussions, the negotiations reached a stalemate on March 31, when the king stated that he could grant no concessions regarding matters of religion.
