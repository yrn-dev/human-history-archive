# The Establishment of Serfdom in Bohemia

## The Decree of 1487 and Social Shift
During the reign of King Vladislav, the political landscape of Bohemia underwent a significant transformation characterized by a decrease in royal prerogative and an unprecedented increase in the power of the nobility. This shift occurred at the expense of both the royal authority and the rights of peasants and townsmen. A pivotal moment in this process was a decree issued in 1487, which practically established serfdom in Bohemia. Prior to this measure, serfdom had been almost unknown in the region.

The implementation of this decree had profound implications for the future of the country. Historically, the rulers of Bohemia had been able to rely on a sturdy and independent peasantry, which had formed the primary composition of the armies led by the Prokops and Zizka. Following the establishment of serfdom, this independent class was eliminated, and the peasantry ceased to be a dangerous political or military force.

## Impact on Towns and Civil Discord
Concurrent with the subjugation of the peasantry, the rights of Bohemian townsmen were curtailed through various enactments. These changes were codified in a set of laws known as the "regulations of King Vladislav." This codification enumerated the rights of knights and nobles but entirely ignored the rights of the towns. The regulations operated on the tacit assumption that townsmen possessed no inherent rights, holding only those privileges that the sovereign might grant them with the consent of the knights and nobles.

These legal changes led to inevitable civil discord. The towns were excluded from several meetings of the diet. In response to this marginalization, the towns formed a confederacy in 1513 to defend their rights, selecting Prince Bartholomew of Munsterberg—a grandson of King George—to lead them.

## Consolidation under Ferdinand I
By the time of Ferdinand I, the social restructuring initiated under Vladislav was complete. The power of the cities had been thoroughly undermined, and the peasantry was no longer a threat due to the established system of serfdom. This allowed Ferdinand to focus his political efforts exclusively on the nobles and knights. He sought to make the nobility amenable to the royal will through the influence of his court and the establishment of the Jesuits in Bohemia.

Ferdinand's success in managing the estates culminated in the "Krvavy'sneem" (bloody diet) on August 22, where four principal leaders of a revolt—two citizens of Prague and two knights—were decapitated outside the Hradcany palace. Later in his life, in 1562, Ferdinand ensured the succession of the Bohemian throne by obtaining the coronation of his eldest son, Maximilian.

## Later Constitutional and Social Developments
In the centuries following the establishment of serfdom, Bohemia experienced various shifts in its relationship with the Habsburg empire. By 1871, there were efforts by the Austrian prime minister, Count Karl Hohenwart, to re-establish the ancient constitution of Bohemia in a modernized form. Although an imperial message on September 14, 1871, recognized the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia, the attempt to reconcile Bohemia with Austria failed due to the rise of a government with a pronounced German tendency in 1872.

Later, under the government of Count Eduard Taaffe in 1879, Bohemian representatives returned to the parliament of Vienna. In exchange for this, the government removed some of the grossest anomalies in the electoral system, which had previously favored the German minority, and consented to the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague to aid the intellectual development of the country.
