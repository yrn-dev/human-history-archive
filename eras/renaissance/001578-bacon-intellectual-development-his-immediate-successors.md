# Bacon and the Intellectual Development of His Successors

The influence of Francis Bacon, Lord Verulam (1561–1626), on the intellectual development of those who followed him is viewed as a catalyst for the general trend of English thought. While some scholars argue that his actual contributions to the stock of scientific knowledge were practically inconsiderable, his impact is seen in the logical fulfillment of his principles across natural science, as well as mental, moral, and political philosophy.

## Philosophical and Scientific Influence
Bacon is frequently described as the "Father of Experimental Philosophy," though some, such as Sir R. A. Gregory, characterize him not as the founder but as the "apostle" of the scientific method. His most significant service to science is found in the work *The New Atlantis*, in which he used fanciful language to plan a "palace of invention" and a "great temple of science" designed to organize the pursuit of knowledge with the highest efficiency.

In the realm of logic, Bacon's *Organum* serves as a stimulating introduction to logical study. Although its contents have been more instructively analyzed by Aristotle or in modern works, its terse and epigrammatic style provides a warning against immature and crude generalization. Bacon’s broader intellectual contribution lay in his ability to sum up the faults of medieval thought that had been revealed by the widening of knowledge. He argued against the academic aims of unpractical dialectic, the vagaries of unfettered imagination, and the tyranny of authority.

## Impact on Psychology and Ethics
Many regard Bacon as the father of the most characteristic elements of English psychological speculation. This is evidenced by the influence of the *Novum Organum* on John Locke's *Essay*. Specifically, the fundamental principle of Locke's work—that all ideas are the product of reflection and sensation—is briefly stated in the first aphorism of the *Novum Organum*.

In ethics, Bacon is viewed as a forerunner of the empirical method. The inductive method discussed in the *Novum Organum* and the spirit of the *De Augmentis* (book vii) are considered the roots of theories that construct moral codes through the inductive examination of human consciousness and the results of actions. Utilitarianism is cited as a natural result of applying the Baconian experimental method to the phenomenon of conduct.

## Divergences and Context
Despite these influences, the record of Bacon's immediate impact is not uniform. For example, Thomas Hobbes, who served as Bacon's secretary, makes no critical reference to Bacon in his works, nor does he mention Baconian induction, suggesting that in some instances, Bacon's influence was not immediate.

Bacon's own intellectual development was influenced by predecessors. Dr. Gilbert of Colchester (1540–1603), who was one of the first to practice experiment and worked chiefly on magnetism, helped form the ideas of Francis Bacon.

Bacon himself was part of a larger intellectual awakening in England. This movement began with figures such as Colet, More, and Linacre, and culminated in an age that included Shakespeare, Jonson, Spenser, Harvey, and Harriott.
