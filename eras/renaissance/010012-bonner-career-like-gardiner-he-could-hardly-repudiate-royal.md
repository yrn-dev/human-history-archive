# Bonner's Career

## Early Career and Diplomatic Missions
Bonner began his career as a graduate in law rather than theology. He served as the king's chaplain and was regarded as an unscrupulous and coarse tool of Thomas Cromwell. During this period, he did not protest the changes implemented by his masters and frequently referred theological inquiries to divines, professing that he was no theologian himself.

In the late 1530s, Bonner was involved in several diplomatic missions. Towards the end of 1535, he was sent to North Germany to further "the cause of the Gospel." In 1536, he wrote a preface to Gardiner's *De vera Obedientia*, a work that denied papal supremacy and asserted royal supremacy, which was well-received by Lutherans. Following a brief embassy to the emperor in the spring of 1538, Bonner replaced Gardiner in Paris. He began this mission by providing Cromwell with a detailed list of accusations against Gardiner. While in Paris, Bonner displayed bitterness toward Wyatt and Mason, whom he denounced as a "papist"; his conduct was so violent that Francis I threatened him with a hundred strokes of the halberd. However, he maintained the favor of Henry VIII and Cromwell, likely due to his efforts in overseeing the printing of the king's English "Great" Bible in Paris.

## Episcopal Appointments and the Six Articles
Bonner's diplomatic service was accompanied by ecclesiastical promotion. He was first appointed to the see of Hereford, but before he could take possession of that see, he was translated to the bishopric of London in October 1539.

During the final years of Henry VIII's reign, Bonner became a staunch Conservative. His views were reflected in the Six Articles, and he spent much of this period enforcing these regulations, a role described as brandishing the "whip with six strings." His activities during this time included the examination of religious dissidents. In March 1545, he examined Anne Askew, the bishop of London at the time, and drew up a form of recantation in his register. Later, on June 18, 1546, Bonner sat on a commission—alongside the lord mayor, the duke of Norfolk, St John, and Heath—that condemned Anne Askew to be burnt based on her confession.

## Conflict Under Edward VI
The accession of Edward VI marked a new chapter in Bonner's career. While Bonner had been an active agent in establishing royal supremacy and could not repudiate it, he began to doubt that supremacy when it was utilized by a Protestant council. He and Gardiner developed a theory that royal supremacy was in abeyance during a royal minority. This position was legally and constitutionally untenable, as both men had sought licenses to exercise ecclesiastical jurisdiction from the young king.

This theoretical conflict led to direct clashes with Edward's government. Bonner resisted a visitation in August 1547 and was committed to the Fleet prison. After withdrawing his opposition and being released, he took an active part against the government in the parliament of November 1547. Between November 1548 and March 1549, he emerged as a leading opponent of the Book of Common Prayer and the first Act of Uniformity.

## Deprivation and Imprisonment
Following the legalization of the Act of Uniformity and the Book of Common Prayer, Bonner neglected to enforce them. On September 1, 1549, the council required him to maintain at St Paul's Cross that the royal authority was as great as if the king were forty years of age. Bonner failed to comply. Following a seven-day trial, an ecclesiastical court presided over by Cranmer deprived him of his bishopric, and he was sent to the Marshalsea.

Bonner hoped for a reversal of his fortunes following the fall of Somerset in October 1549 and appealed to the council. However, the Protestant faction prevailed. On February 7, 1550, the council, sitting in the Star Chamber, confirmed Bonner's deprivation and condemned him to perpetual imprisonment.

## Later Activities
Following the repeal of Edward VI's legislation and the revival of the heresy acts in December 1554, Bonner returned to a role of ecclesiastical authority. On January 29, 1555, he was involved in the condemnation of several individuals, including Hooper, Rogers, and Rowland Taylor. While Gardiner condemned them, Bonner performed the act of degrading them before their executions.
