# De vera Obedientia and the Conflict of Supremacies

The term *De vera Obedientia* refers to a work by Gardiner, for which a preface was written in 1536. This text served as a vehicle for asserting royal supremacy while simultaneously denying papal supremacy. Due to its stance, the work was received with delight by the Lutherans.

## Royal and Papal Supremacy in England
The assertion of royal supremacy was a central point of contention in 16th-century English ecclesiastical and political life. Figures such as Gardiner and Bonner were active agents in the establishment of this royal supremacy. However, the application of this power shifted with different administrations. During the accession of Edward VI, Bonner and Gardiner began to doubt the royal supremacy as they observed how it was utilized by a Protestant council. 

This led to the development of a theory suggesting that royal supremacy was in abeyance during a royal minority. This position was neither constitutionally nor legally tenable, as both Bonner and Gardiner had sought licenses to exercise ecclesiastical jurisdiction from the young king. The conflict over this issue culminated in 1549, when Bonner was required by the council to maintain at St Paul's Cross that royal authority remained as great regardless of whether the king was forty years of age or a minor. Bonner's failure to comply led to a seven-day trial, his deprivation of his bishopric by an ecclesiastical court presided over by Cranmer, and his subsequent imprisonment in the Marshalsea. His deprivation was later confirmed by the council in the Star Chamber on February 7, 1550.

## Theological Challenges to Papal Supremacy
The concept of papal supremacy—the belief in the pope's absolute supremacy in all ecclesiastical matters—was a cornerstone of the Roman church. Opponents of the Reformation argued that the power to issue Indulgences was a specific instance of universal papal jurisdiction and that attacking Indulgences was equivalent to defying the whole church of Christ.

Martin Luther, who initially held the belief in papal supremacy as one of his oldest inherited convictions, eventually challenged this view. After studying the Decretals, Luther concluded that they were full of frauds. This discovery led to a direct confrontation regarding papal supremacy, most notably during the challenge issued by Eck for a dispute at Leipzig.

## Historical and Global Perspectives on Supremacy
The struggle between centralized papal authority and other forms of supremacy appeared in various historical contexts:

*   **France:** Louis IX enforced the supremacy of royal justice over his clergy and strongly opposed papal exactions for much of his reign, though he later joined forces with the pope to extort the clergy.
*   **The Episcopacy:** In various ancient churches, those who rejected papal supremacy still viewed bishops as the stewards of the gifts of the Holy Ghost and guardians of apostolic orthodoxy. In the West, the Old Catholics separated from the Roman communion following the dogma of papal infallibility, representing a revolt of the system of Episcopacy against Papalism. Similarly, Gallicans and Febronians maintained the high doctrine of episcopacy against the claims of the Papacy.
*   **The Collective Episcopate:** Before the Reformation, the collective episcopate attempted to assert its supremacy in the Western Church at the councils of Constance and Basel, though these efforts failed.

## Other Uses of Obedientia
Separate from the political and ecclesiastical disputes over supremacy, the term *obedientia* appears in the context of religious military orders. In 1540, Pope Paul III released the Knights of Alcantara from the strictness of Benedictine rule, allowing them to marry (though forbidding second marriages). Following this change, the three vows of the order became *conversio morum*, *castitas conjugalis*, and *obedientia*.
