# Michel de L'Hôpital and 16th Century Judicial Reform

## Judicial Purification in France
During the 16th century, France experienced a significant impulse toward the purification of the judicial bench. This movement focused on the eradication of judicial bribery. This effort was led by the high-minded chancellor, Michel de L'Hôpital. The process of removing bribery from the bench in most European countries is described as having been gradual.

## Comparative Judicial Corruption
The efforts seen in France contrast with the judicial history of England, where judicial corruption was a crime of remarkable rarity. Aside from a statute from 1384, which was later repealed by the Statute Law Revision Act of 1881, England lacked legislation specifically relating to judicial bribery. 

Recorded instances of judicial misconduct in England include the 1351 case of Sir William Thorpe, who was removed from office and fined for accepting bribes. Other notable cases occurred later, such as those involving the chancellor of England, Michael de la Pole, in 1387; Lord Chancellor Bacon in 1621; Lionel Cranfield, the earl of Middlesex, in 1624; and Sir Thomas Parker, the 1st earl of Macclesfield, in 1725.

## Official and Administrative Corruption
Beyond the judicial bench, corruption existed in higher official quarters in England, particularly from the period of the Revolution to the end of the reign of Queen Anne. Examples from this era include the expulsion of a speaker of the House of Commons for bribery and the pecuniary dishonesty of the great Marlborough. 

Administrative corruption also manifested in the appointment to office, a form of corruption that has proven difficult to eradicate. During the administration of Walpole, members of parliament were paid in cash for their votes, reflecting a government method characterized by the saying that every man has his price. While official bribery among humbler revenue officers has become extremely rare, it persisted longer among those whose power was constitutional rather than official, as they were viewed as less responsible to the public.

## Other 16th Century Developments
The 16th century saw various other developments across Europe:
*   **Ecclesiastical Dress:** In both England and on the continent, the *biretum* (originally a round cap with a knob) began to emphasize the ridges of its sutures, leading to a square shape.
*   **Architecture and Institutions:** In Italy, the 16th century saw the construction of the palace of the marquis Guidiccioni and several other fine palaces. An academy of sciences was established in 1584, and the collegiate church of San Stefano dates from this century.
*   **Regional Conflict:** In the Polish province of Pokutia, the town of Kolomea suffered severely during the 15th and 16th centuries due to attacks by the Tatars and the Moldavians.
*   **Governance:** In England, by the end of the 16th century, the Lord High Treasurer had become so occupied with general national policy that he could no longer personally supervise departmental details; consequently, Lord Burleigh employed a secretary for that purpose.
*   **Legal Figures:** In the middle of the 16th century, Simon Carpzov served as the burgomaster of Brandenburg.
