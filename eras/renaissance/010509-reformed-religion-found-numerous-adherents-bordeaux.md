# The Reformed Religion in Bordeaux and Related Historical Contexts

## Religious Development in Bordeaux
The reformed religion gained a significant following in the city of Bordeaux, located in south-western France. However, this period of growth was marked by severe violence; following the massacre of St Bartholomew, nearly three hundred inhabitants of the city lost their lives due to their adherence to the reformed faith.

During the 16th century, Bordeaux also served as a destination for scholars fleeing religious persecution elsewhere. George Buchanan, who had been arrested in 1539 during a bitter persecution of Lutherans in Scotland, escaped to London and Paris before proceeding to Bordeaux. He was appointed professor of Latin at the newly founded college of Guienne through the efforts of André de Gouvéa. While residing in Bordeaux, Buchanan produced several of his primary works, including the dramas *Baptistes (sive Calumnia)* and *Jephthes (sive Votum)*, as well as translations of *Alcestis* and *Medea*. His pupil in Bordeaux was Montaigne, who performed in Buchanan's tragedies.

## Theological Foundations of the Reformation
The Reformation emerged as a violent protest against the prevailing state of Catholicism. A central point of contention was the role of the individual conscience. While Catholicism increasingly suggested that a man imperilled his soul by thinking for himself, Protestantism asserted that a man could lose his soul if he left his thinking to another. This perspective held that God speaks to the individual conscience, and through these individual struggles, a stable Christian character is built.

This emphasis on individual responsibility was echoed by Milton in his 1644 work *Areopagitica*, where he argued that a man could be a "heretic in the truth" if he believed things solely because an Assembly or a pastor determined them without the man knowing the reason. Milton criticized those who treated religion as a "traffic" and resigned the "warehouse of his religion" to a divine of note, effectively making the person of that man his religion rather than keeping it within himself.

## Liturgical and Calendar Changes
The Reformation led to specific changes in religious practice and the calendar. Because the reformers rejected the doctrine of transubstantiation, the festival of Corpus Christi was suppressed in the reformed Churches. Martin Luther, despite his belief in the Real Presence, abolished the festival, viewing it as the most harmful of all medieval festivals. This rejection was further intensified by a hatred for the traffic in indulgences associated with the event. Consequently, the procession of the Host on Corpus Christi day became a public demonstration of Catholic orthodoxy against Protestantism and religious Liberalism.

The adoption of the Gregorian calendar also followed religious lines. While France received the calendar in December of the year it was introduced in Rome, the Protestant states of Germany adhered to the Julian calendar until 1700. At that time, the diet of Regensburg decreed the adoption of the new style and Gregorian correction. However, these states initially attempted to determine the paschal moon and equinox via astronomical computation from the Rudolphine tables rather than using epacts and golden numbers. This method was abandoned in 1774 at the instance of King Frederick II of Prussia due to numerous inconveniences.

## Broader Impact and Other Centers of Reform
The influence of reformed movements extended beyond France. In Eperjes, a royal free town founded by a German colony, the Reformation found many early adherents. The town became a focal point during the religious wars of the 17th century and was the site of the "butchery of Eperjes" in 1687. This was a tribunal established by the Austrian general Caraffa, which confiscated the property of and condemned to death many citizens accused of Protestantism.

Earlier religious reforms, such as the Reform of Cluny originating in French Burgundy in 1004, also created conflict. The monks of Cluny sought to reintroduce severe monastic discipline and resisted the interference of temporal power in religious affairs. This put them in opposition to the imperial and feudal church of Germany, contributing to the eventual break of Lotharingia away from German influence in both religious and political matters.
