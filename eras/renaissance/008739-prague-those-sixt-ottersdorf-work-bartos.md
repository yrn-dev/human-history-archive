# Prague and the Historians Bartos and Sixt of Ottersdorf

Prague, the capital of Bohemia, has served as a central hub for academic, religious, and political activity throughout various eras of European history. The city has been the site of significant military conflicts, the center of influential universities, and the home of various scholars and artists.

## 16th Century Historians
During the 16th century, Bohemia experienced a period of comparative tranquillity that allowed native literature to develop. While Wenceslas Hajek of Libocan is the most well-known historian of this era, other writers provided critical historical records of the region.

Bartos, also known as Bartholomew and surnamed "pisar" (the writer), served for many years as a secretary for the city of Prague. He died in 1539. His work, the *Chronicle of Prague*, is regarded as having great historical value. In this text, Bartos describes the general troubles that affected Bohemia and the city of Prague during the reign of King Louis, who is characterized as a weak and absentee sovereign.

Sixt of Ottersdorf (1500–1583) produced a later work titled *Memorials of the Troubled Years 1546 and 1547*. This text focuses on a short but important episode: the unsuccessful rising of the Bohemians against Ferdinand I of Austria. The historical value of Sixt's account is enhanced by the fact that he took a considerable part in the movement he describes.

## Academic and Religious Life
Prague has long been a center for higher learning. The Dominican order, following the policy of St Dominic to establish houses in great university towns, occupied chairs in Prague as part of their influence on medieval theological development.

In later centuries, the city continued to attract intellectuals. Bernhard Bolzano (1781–1848), an Austrian philosopher and priest, was born in Prague. After his ordination in 1805, he became a professor of the philosophy of religion at Prague University. Bolzano sought to demonstrate that reason and Catholic theology were in complete harmony. Despite interest from younger thinkers, he faced opposition and was eventually compelled to resign in 1820 following accusations of connections to revolutionary student societies. He spent the remainder of his life in literary work in Prague until his death in 1848.

## Military and Political Conflict
Prague has frequently been the focal point of imperial and regional struggles. In the early 17th century, the city was central to the conflict between the Bohemian estates and the Habsburgs. In 1619, King Frederick and Queen Elizabeth of England were crowned at St Vitus's cathedral in Prague. However, by November 8, 1620, imperial forces attacked the Bohemian army at the plateau of "Bila Hora" (White Hill). Following the Bohemian defeat, the imperialists occupied the port of Prague on the left bank of the Vltava (Moldau), leading King Frederick to flee the city the next morning.

Earlier, Ferdinand had occupied Prague with Spanish and Walloon mercenaries on July 20, treating the nobility with forbearance but dealing severely with the towns. In Prague, Ferdinand revised ancient privileges, resulting in a broad confiscation of municipal lands. He also imposed royal representatives known as town-captains to maintain unlimited control over municipal affairs.

In the 18th century, the city was again a site of military tension. In June 1742, French forces under Broglie were nearly surrounded in Prague by the army of Prince Charles. The siege of Prague was eventually raised on September 14, after which Marshal Belleisle took charge of the army remaining in the city.

## Cultural and Scientific Contributions
Prague has been a site of significant artistic and scientific production. Karel (or Karl) Bendl (1838–1897), a Bohemian composer born in Prague, was a prominent figure in the local music scene. He served as the conductor of the choral society *Hlahoe* from 1865 to 1879. Bendl composed numerous operas, including *Lejla*, *Bretislav a Jitka*, and *Dite Tabera*, as well as various masses and songs that became regarded as national possessions of Bohemia.

The city also hosted scientific publications. Works published in Prague include *Die Gesch. u. d. Wurzel d. Satzes von d. Erhaltung d. Arbeit* (1872) and *Leitfaden d. Physik für Studierende* (1881). Additionally, the *Fontes Rerum Bohemicarum*, a valuable collection of historical documents, was published in Prague during the latter part of the 19th century.
