# THE MOMO-YAMA EPOCH

The Momo-yama epoch represents the final period of the Ashikaga shogunate. Its name is derived from "Peach Hill," the Momo-yama palace built by Hideyoshi in the Fushimi suburb of Kyoto.

## The Construction and Grandeur of Momo-yama
The project for the Momo-yama residence was conceived in 1593 during the Korean campaign. The scale of construction was colossal; foundations were laid by September of that year, and within two months, a mansion of extraordinary magnificence with elaborate interior decoration was completed. The residence was surrounded by a spacious park featuring the choicest elements of Japanese landscape gardens. Records indicate that 50,000 men were employed to complete the work in this brief period.

Hideyoshi was known for a desire to surpass his contemporaries and predecessors in the celerity and magnitude of his designs. This extended to his pastimes. In 1594, while his armies were engaged in an unprecedented oversea campaign in Korea, he organized a sumptuous flower-viewing fete at the monastery of Koya and the cherry-clad hills of Yoshino. During this event, Hideyoshi presented a sum equivalent to $68,000 (or £14,000) to a shrine on Koya-san dedicated to his mother. Four years later, he organized an even more lavish party to view cherry blossoms at Daigo. This required the rebuilding of the Sambo-in Buddhist temple as a temporary resting place and the installation of silk-curtain fences enclosing the roads from Momo-yama to Daigo. Hideyoshi spent this event among gorgeously apparelled ladies in various tea pavilions, viewing art and listening to music.

## Economic and Cultural Developments
During this era, the social status of the merchant improved, aided by the liberal views of the Taiko (Hideyoshi). He encouraged commercial voyages to Annam, Cambodia, and Macao, with nine ships engaging in this trade annually. These ships departed from Sakai, Osaka, and Nagasaki, carrying licenses bearing the Taiko's vermilion stamp.

Hideyoshi's personal wealth increased significantly because the government owned the mines at Ikuno and Sado. Gold and silver became the standards of value, with the *oban* (an oval plate of gold and silver) and the *koban* (one-tenth the value of an *oban*) serving as currency. The extent of Hideyoshi's riches was evidenced in September 1596, when a terrible earthquake destroyed the great structures of Momo-yama Castle. Despite a loss of "three million pieces of gold," Hideyoshi treated the incident with indifference and ordered immediate reparations. Similarly, Osaka Castle, which had been rendered uninhabitable by the quake, was sumptuously refitted within six weeks, with expenses shared by Hideyoshi and the great feudatories.

## Influence of the Korean Campaign
The Japanese campaign in Korea yielded several indirect results. The memory of the soldiers' achievements served to protect Japan from foreign aggression decades later when the Tsing dynasty subjugated Korea and destroyed the Ming in China. 

Additionally, Hideyoshi ordered returning generals to bring back Korean art-artisans. These experts introduced technical improvements to the ceramic industry, influencing seven different kinds of Japanese pottery and porcelain, which was particularly timely due to the vogue for utensils used in the tea ceremonial. The period also saw the introduction of movable type; the first book printed with this method appeared in the Bunroku era (1592-1595).

## The Fall of Momo-yama and Transition of Power
Following the death of the Taiko, the splendid edifices of Momo-yama were dismantled and destroyed. This led to an exodus of experts who had settled at Fushimi; they moved to the fiefs of powerful provincial nobles, sparking an inter-fief rivalry of artistic production that refined Japanese manners and every branch of art.

The political transition culminated around the Battle of Sekigahara in 1600. Ieyasu, anticipating that Momo-yama would be attacked if he moved east to Yedo, left a garrison of 2,000 men under Torii Mototada to defend the castle. While Ieyasu mobilized allies to attack Uesugi from five directions—including forces from Mogami, Date, Hori, Maeda, and Satake—the western army pushed eastward. 

The western army captured and burned Momo-yama Castle. Torii Mototada, then 62 years old, defended the castle for eleven days against a force of 40,000 before traitors within the garrison set fire to the castle on September 8, leading Mototada to commit suicide. Ieyasu's castle was subsequently taken in 1600.
