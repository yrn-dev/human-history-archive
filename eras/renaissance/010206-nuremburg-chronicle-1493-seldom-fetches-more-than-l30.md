# Nuremberg Chronicle (1493)

The *Nuremberg Chronicle*, published in 1493, is an example of an illustrated book from the period of incunabula. It is characterized by the use of primitive woodcuts.

## Market Value and Collectibility
In the market for illustrated books, certain works have seen inordinate price increases, though this is often avoided by books that achieved early celebrity and thus did not become rare. The *Nuremberg Chronicle* is noted as a work that seldom fetches more than L30. This stands in contrast to other rare books of the era, such as the *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili* (Venice, 1499), which fetches more than L120, and other rarer volumes that are priced in the hundreds.

The appreciation of primitive woodcuts in incunabula is currently viewed as being almost too high. Generally, books that appeal to a collector's imagination are those associated with famous events or persons, such as those owned by poets, soldiers, great statesmen, or royalty. While first editions are often valued for their associations or their superiority for study, the value placed on such items can be fanciful.

## Historical Context of Nuremberg
The city of Nuremberg held a specific legal status known as *Reichsfreiheit*, meaning it held directly of the Empire. By the decision of the imperial deputation of 1803, Nuremberg was one of only six cities—alongside Hamburg, Lübeck, Bremen, Augsburg, and Frankfort-on-Main—permitted to maintain this status. This arrangement changed in 1806 upon the dissolution of the Empire, at which point Nuremberg, along with Augsburg, passed under the sovereignty of Bavaria.
