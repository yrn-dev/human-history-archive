# Religious and Political Conflict in Bohemia under Maximilian

## Accession and Early Influence
Maximilian succeeded his father as the king of Bohemia without opposition. His early years were marked by an education provided primarily by Protestant tutors, leading him to openly avow a strong sympathy for church reform. This inclination earned him the support of Bohemian church reformers, while the Romanists and retrograde Utraquists remained traditionally aligned with the house of Habsburg.

Despite these early sympathies, Maximilian's reign did not fulfill the expectations of the reform party. While he generally refused to sanction measures against Protestants—despite the advice of Jesuits who were gaining influence in Bohemia—he also published new decrees against the Bohemian Brethren. Over time, he began attending functions of the Roman church after a long absence. His approach to the ecclesiastical disputes of his reign was characterized by an indifference that may have been rooted in religious scepticism.

## The Diet of 1567 and Financial Tensions
In 1567, Maximilian visited Bohemia for the first time since his accession. Like other Habsburg princes of the era, he faced the challenge of raising funds for warfare against the Turks. When he requested subsidies from the Bohemians, the estates responded by presenting demands regarding religious matters. 

The estates expressed a desire for the celebrated Compacts to cease being part of the country's laws. While the Compacts had granted freedom of worship to moderate Utraquists (who differed from the Roman church primarily in their claim to receive communion in both kinds), Ferdinand I. had used these same enactments to justify the oppression of advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian Brethren.

## The Diet of 1575 and the Confessio Bohemica
Maximilian returned to Bohemia in 1575 after a long absence, prompted by the estates' refusal to grant subsidies to an absentee monarch. The resulting sittings of the diet were very prolonged. During these proceedings, the king's attitude was vacillating; he was influenced alternately by the threats of the Bohemians and the advice of the papal nuncio who had followed him to Prague. The papal nuncio strongly warned Maximilian that making concessions to the Protestants of Bohemia would create great difficulties in his other dominions.

The Bohemians' primary demand was the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg," a summary of the teachings of Luther. Additionally, they renewed a demand from the diet of 1567: that the estates, rather than the king, should have the right to appoint members of the consistory. The consistory was the ecclesiastical body ruling the Utraquist church, which had lacked an archbishop since the death of John of Rokycan.

Following the king's final refusal to recognize the Confession of Augsburg, a majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drafted a profession of faith known as the *Confessio Bohemica*. While identical to the Augsburg confession in most points, it differed regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper, aligning with the views of Calvin rather than Luther, a shift attributed to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren.

## Royal Response and Succession
Maximilian gave verbal approval to the *Confessio Bohemica* but refused to allow it to be made public or included among the kingdom's charters. He also rejected the demand that the estates appoint the members of the consistory. However, he did consent to exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the consistory's jurisdiction, allowing them to choose fifteen defenders (five from each of the estates).

The estates viewed the results of the 1575 diet as generally favourable. Consequently, they recognized Maximilian's eldest son, Rudolph, as his successor and agreed to his coronation. Maximilian died the following year.

## The Reign of Rudolph
Rudolph succeeded his father without opposition. Although educated in Spain, leading to fears that he would be severe with church reformers, Rudolph exhibited the same apathy and indifference toward religious controversy and state matters as he did toward his royal duties. His reign was marked by fits of melancholia and periods of insanity. 

By 1600, Rudolph's impaired mental state led his brother Matthias to suggest the appointment of a coadjutor and arrangements for succession. Rudolph indignantly rejected these suggestions. His anger increased following a letter from Pope Clement VIII, which formally but forcibly noted the evil results of Rudolph's neglect of his duties and called for the appointment of a Habsburg successor to the thrones of Bohemia, Hungary, and the imperial crown. This pressure from the pope eventually induced the previously indifferent Rudolph to act.
