# Private Education and Preparation of Youth

The preparation of youth for various roles in society—whether as private citizens, members of the nobility, or specialists in a trade—has historically varied across different cultures and eras, ranging from severe state-mandated discipline to private tutoring and specialized vocational training.

## Spartan Discipline and the Private Life
In Lacedæmon, the preparation of youth was characterized by a severe school designed to instill obedience. This system of training was intended to render citizens well-behaved and amenable to discipline, a process compared by the poet Simonides to horses being broken to harness early in life. While the direct heirs to the throne were typically exempt from this training, the case of Agesilaus provides a notable exception. As the younger son of King Archidamus and not the immediate heir, Agesilaus was prepared for the life of a private man through this rigorous Spartan school. This early education in obedience later contributed to his popularity as king, as it allowed him to sympathize with the people he ruled while maintaining the haughty spirit appropriate for a monarch.

## Educational Debates in Tudor England
In 16th-century England, the methods used by schoolmasters to educate youth were a subject of significant debate among the ruling class. In December 1563, during a dinner at Windsor with Secretary of State Sir William Cecil and other ministers, the issue of school discipline was raised. Cecil reported that several scholars from Eaton had run away from their school due to a fear of beating, leading him to express a desire for more discretion in the correction of students. 

Roger Ascham emerged as a champion for the "anti-floggers" during this debate. The necessity for a more discretionary approach to education was further emphasized by Sir Richard Sackville, the treasurer, who noted that the brutality of a "fond schoolmaster" had caused him to hate learning, resulting in a personal loss. Consequently, Sackville sought a different approach for the upbringing of his own young son.

## Intellectual Influence and Access to Knowledge
The transmission of ideas to youth was not always confined to formal schooling. In ancient Athens, the trial of Socratês highlighted the availability of philosophical texts. Socratês argued against the accusation that he was the source of certain teachings for the youth, pointing out that the books of Anaxagoras were readily available for purchase for a drachma in the theatre. He suggested that youth could acquire such doctrines independently, even using them to mock him if he were to claim such views as his own.

## Social Status and Language
The upbringing of gentlemen in England also involved the acquisition of specific linguistic standards. In certain historical periods, it was noted that while the common people of every shire spoke their local dialects, gentlemen and learned clarkes often condescended to these speakers. However, gentlemen in various shires were expected to write in "Southerne" English, the speech of the Court and London, which was considered more "Courtly" and "currant" than the speech of Northern-men or those from the far West.
