# Military Transitions in the 17th Century

The 17th century served as a period of significant transition for military organization and equipment in England, specifically regarding the relationship between weaponry, protective gear, and communication.

## Improvements in Firearms and Infantry
During the 17th century, advancements in firearms led to a fundamental shift in the equipment used by infantry. These improvements in weaponry resulted in the gradual abandonment of armour by infantry forces. This shift in equipment was the catalyst for further organizational changes within the military, specifically the formation of light infantry.

## Evolution of Signal Instruments
The change in infantry structure led to a corresponding evolution in the instruments used for duties and signals. In England, the bugle was introduced to the light infantry, where it gradually superseded the drum. This transition occurred by degrees following the adoption of light infantry units.

An example of this transition can be seen in Prince Albert's 1st Somerset Light Infantry, one of the oldest light infantry regiments. Formed in 1685 by the earl of Huntingdon, the regiment still employed a drummer at that time, who was paid a shilling per day. However, by the end of the 18th century, the bugle had become the recognized signal instrument for the light infantry, while the trumpet remained the standard for the cavalry.

## Later Developments of the Bugle
The integration of the bugle continued into the 19th century. On December 28, 1814, a general order introduced the bugle as a minor badge for the light infantry. By 1856, the Royal Kent bugle (or keyed bugle) had reached its peak of popularity within the army.

In the Royal Artillery, a bugle-band was established to replace the drum and fife band. Under the leadership of Trumpet-major James Lawson, a skilled cornet player, the band reached a high standard of excellence. Because Lawson found the scale of the service bugle too restricted, he received permission to add a valve attachment. This modification transformed the bugle into a chromatic instrument, effectively making it a saxhorn. This development led to the addition of other copper instruments, including bass tubas, euphoniums, tenor horns in B flat, and horns in E flat. In 1869, this ensemble was renamed the R.A. Brass Band, and it was eventually merged into the Mounted Band in 1877.

Throughout much of the 19th century, the bugle served as the nucleus for brass bands across all countries, developing via valves into tubas and saxhorns, and via keys into the ophicleide and the Royal Kent bugle. Similarly, the Flügelhorn became the signal instrument for infantry in both England and Germany, and it continued to be used alongside the keyed bugle in the military bands of Austro-Hungary.
