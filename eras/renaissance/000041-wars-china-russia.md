# Wars with China and Russia

The history of East Asia during the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries was marked by significant military conflicts involving Japan, China, and Russia. These struggles were driven by territorial ambitions, the quest for ice-free ports, and the strategic control of Manchuria and Korea.

## Russian Expansion in Asia
Russia's eastward movement was characterized by a "great trans-Asian march" from Lake Baikal to the Maritime Province and Saghalien. In 1860, Russia took advantage of China being "beaten to her knees" by France and England to secure the final cession of the Usuri region, which became the Maritime Province of Siberia. Following this, Russia shifted its Pacific naval basis to Vladivostok. Russian ambitions extended further with an attempt to establish a settlement at Tsushima, though this was interrupted by Great Britain, and the acquisition of Saghalien in 1875.

To secure an unobstructed avenue to the Pacific and an ice-free port, Russia looked toward Korea, viewing its government as corrupt and its self-defensive capacities as insignificant. However, because Japan and China were unlikely to accept aggressions against Korea, Russia sought an alternative communication line via a railway across Asia. To avoid a long detour along the northern bank of the Amur, Russia desired a shorter route through Chinese territory in Manchuria.

## The First Sino-Japanese War (1894–1895)
War broke out between Japan and China in 1894. This conflict demonstrated Japan's successful Westernization, as she utilized a sound fleet and an efficient Westernized army. As a result of the war, Japan passed into possession of the southern littoral of Manchuria. 

The aftermath of the war had significant economic and geopolitical consequences. Japan obtained an indemnity from China, providing a stock of gold that allowed Japan to adopt gold monometallism in 1897. However, the victory also created tension with other powers. Russia, France, and Germany combined to prevent Japan from reaping the full fruits of the war, particularly regarding the establishment of positions on the mainland commanding the Japan Sea.

## Escalation and the Road to the Russo-Japanese War
The Japanese possession of the Manchurian littoral became a primary motive for Russian aggression, as Russia felt it could not get nearer to the Pacific than Vladivostok unless it "swept Japan from her path." In 1898, following Germany's annexation of a portion of the Shang-tung province, Russia seized the Liao-tung peninsula and forced China to allow the extension of the trans-Siberian railway to Port Arthur. By 1900, Russia occupied Manchuria.

Attempts at a pacific resolution were made. The United States, Great Britain, and Japan worked to strengthen China's position, leading Russia to sign a treaty pledging to withdraw troops from Manchuria in three installments. Additionally, new commercial treaties opened three places in Manchuria to foreign trade. However, Russia ignored these promises and instead proposed new conditions to strengthen its grasp on the region.

## The Russo-Japanese War (1904–1905)
War was formally declared on February 10, 1904. The Russian Czar claimed pacific intentions regarding the revision of agreements on Korean affairs and accused Japan of a sudden attack on the squadron at Port Arthur. Conversely, the Japanese Mikado asserted that the integrity of Korea was essential to Japan's safety and accused Russia of violating treaty pledges to China by occupying and attempting to annex Manchuria.

Strategically, Japan's primary challenge was securing a safe avenue for troops over the sea. Russia possessed a formidable fleet in the East; at the outbreak of hostilities, Russian squadrons comprised 59 fighting ships with 1,350 guns and 18,000 men (though this included a Mediterranean squadron that did not reach the Pacific). Japan sought to anticipate the combination of these sea forces to avoid being cut off from the continent.

The conflict involved the transportation of large Japanese armies to Korea and Port Arthur, while Russia sent "endless train-loads of Russian peasants" via the Siberian railway. The war is noted as an epoch in Asian history, marking the end of the period of European arrogance.

## Economic Impact and Legacy
The combined costs of the wars with China (1894–1895) and Russia (1904–1905), alongside the nationalization of railways, left Japan with a national debt of 2,300 million yen by 1907. This debt was subsequently paid off at an annual rate of fifty million yen.
