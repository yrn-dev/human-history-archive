# England and the Trials of Margaret

The history of England and its associated figures includes diverse narratives ranging from the social crises of the 19th century to the political upheavals of the 15th and 16th centuries. These accounts detail the growth of systemic abuses in infant care and the complex personal and political struggles of various women named Margaret.

## Baby-Farming in 19th Century England
Prior to 1871, the practice of "baby-farming"—the taking in of infants to nurse for payment—had grown to an alarming extent in England. This practice often involved improper treatment and was closely linked to the issue of illegitimacy. Many baby-farms accepted children without question in exchange for a lump sum; because these children were nearly all illegitimate, it was to the pecuniary advantage of the baby-farmer to hasten the child's death.

Additionally, factory operatives and mill-hands frequently placed their children out by the day. Because these children were often viewed as a drain on resources or a burden, parents rarely inquired into the quality of care provided, sometimes paying ridiculously insufficient sums for maintenance.

In 1871, the House of Commons appointed a select committee to investigate the destruction of infants put out to nurse for hire. The committee identified several causes of high mortality, including wilful neglect, crowded rooms, bad air, lack of cleanliness, drugs, opiates, and improper or insufficient food, which led to wasting away, convulsions, and diarrhoea. Evidence from the Magdalene home and the Foundling hospital suggested that hand-nursing rather than breast-nursing contributed to this mortality, noting that children nursed by their mothers in the workhouse generally survived. This led to the act of 1872, which mandated the compulsory registration of any house receiving more than one child under the age of one year for longer than twenty-four hours. The trials of Mary Hall and Margaret Waters specifically highlighted the infamous relationship between London's baby-farming houses and lying-in houses.

## Margaret of Anjou
Margaret of Anjou (1430–1482), the daughter of René of Anjou, became the queen of England after marrying Henry VI on April 23, 1445. Initially, her only friends in England were William de la Pole, duke of Suffolk, and his wife. Margaret's French sympathies and her imperious, self-willed nature made her unpopular. While she supported her husband's educational schemes and the foundation of Queen's College, Cambridge, she was the opposite of the gentle Henry VI.

Her political activity increased after the fall of Suffolk in 1450. She supported Edmond Beaufort, duke of Somerset, against Richard of York and sought pecuniary benefits for herself and her associates. Her influence was limited until the birth of her son, Edward, on October 13, 1453, which coincided with her husband being stricken with insanity. Margaret became the ardent champion of the rights of her husband and son. While her energy sustained the cause of Lancaster, her implacable spirit contributed to its eventual failure. Following the recovery of Henry VI in January 1455, Margaret pushed her politics to extremes, resulting in the defeat of the Lancastrians at St Albans.

## Margaret, Queen of Scotland
Margaret, the sister of Henry VIII of England, was crowned at Edinburgh in March 1504. Her early marriage to James IV was marked by the loss of three children in infancy between 1507 and 1510. She later gave birth to James V in 1512 and a posthumous son, Alexander, in 1514, who died the following year.

A dispute between Margaret and her brother Henry VIII over a legacy contributed to the war that ended at Flodden on September 9, 1513, where James IV was killed. Margaret was appointed sole guardian of the infant King James V. During this period, Scotland was split between those favoring an alliance with France, led by John Stewart, duke of Albany, and those favoring England, a faction Margaret generally supported.

In August 1514, Margaret privately married Archibald Douglas, earl of Angus. This marriage alienated powerful nobility, including the earls of Home and Arran, and made her dependent on the house of Douglas. In July 1515, the council used this as a pretext to remove her from the regency and guardianship of the king in favor of Albany, who had to blockade her in Stirling Castle before she surrendered her sons. Margaret fled to England in September 1515, where she gave birth to a daughter, Margaret, who became the countess of Lennox.

Margaret's later years were marked by shifting alliances. She quarreled with Angus over money and sought a divorce, briefly aligning with Albany. After obtaining her divorce from Angus in 1527, she married Henry Stewart, Lord Methven. Together, they influenced the counsels of James V until 1534, when the council and clergy frustrated her attempt to arrange a meeting between James and Henry VIII.
