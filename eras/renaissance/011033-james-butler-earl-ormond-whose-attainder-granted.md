# James Butler, Earl of Ormond

The history of the Butler family, who served as the *Pincerna Hiberniae* (butlership of Ireland) starting with Theobald Walter, is characterized by a tradition of loyalty to English custom and the English crown. Among the various figures bearing the name James Butler, two distinct individuals are noted in the archive's records: the 5th Earl of Ormond (also Earl of Wiltshire) and the later, more famous James Butler, the 1st Duke of Ormonde.

## James Butler, 5th Earl of Ormond
James Butler, the 5th Earl, was granted the English earldom of Wiltshire in 1449 as a reward for his Lancastrian zeal. He was a knight of the Garter, with a stall plate located in St George's chapel. His military career included fighting at St Albans in 1455, where he fled the field by casting his harness into a ditch, and leading a wing at Wakefield. 

Following defeats with the earl of Pembroke at Mortimer's Cross and his capture after Towton, his ultimate fate remained a subject of uncertainty. However, reports suggested he was beheaded at Newcastle, and a letter from May 1461 indicated that the head of the Earl of Wiltshire had been placed on London Bridge. In the 15th century, this James Butler held the manor of Ashby-de-la-Zouch; following his attainder, the manor was granted in 1461 to Lord Hastings.

## James Butler, 1st Duke of Ormonde
A later grandson of the line, James Butler, is described as perhaps the most famous of the Ormondes. He reunited the Ormonde estates and titles through his marriage to his cousin, Elizabeth Preston. A statesman and soldier, he served the king in Ireland, navigating conflicts between Protestant parliamentarians and Catholic rebels. He remained in Ireland to proclaim Charles II in 1649, but after being defeated at Rathmines and seeing his garrisons broken by Cromwell, he left the country at the end of 1650.

Upon the Restoration, he was appointed lord-lieutenant. His estates were restored to him, and he received the county palatine of Tipperary, which James I had previously taken from his grandfather. His titles expanded over time: he was created a marquess in 1632, received the English earldom of Brecknock in 1660, and was granted an Irish dukedom of Ormonde in 1661. In 1682, he obtained a patent for an English dukedom of the same title.

## Political Struggles and Later Life
The 1st Duke's tenure as lord-lieutenant was interrupted for seven years due to the intrigues of Buckingham. In 1670, he survived a violent assassination attempt in St James's Street when ruffians dragged him from his coach to take him to the gallows at Tyburn. It is suggested that he was saved by his son Ossory, who threatened to shoot Buckingham—even if he were positioned behind the king's chair—should harm befall his father.

With the accession of James II, the Duke was removed from active employment. He died in 1688 at his house in Dorsetshire. His life spanned several generations of his family; he had known his great-great-uncle, the Black Earl (born 1532), and was attended by a great-grandson shortly before his death. His son Ossory had predeceased him by eight years.

## Succession and Forfeiture
The 1st Duke was succeeded by his grandson, James, the second duke of Ormonde. The second duke was a recognized leader of the London Jacobites and was attainted in 1715. This resulted in the forfeiture of his estates and honours. He spent thirty years in exile, primarily in Avignon, where he died in 1745 without surviving issue. He was succeeded in the family line by his younger brother, Charles, who was created earl of Arran in the Irish peerage and Lord Butler of Weston in the English peerage by King William.
