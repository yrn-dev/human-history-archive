# Vladislav of Bohemia and Hungary

## Accession and Early Reign
Vladislav, the son of King Casimir of Poland, was elected as the king of Bohemia at Kutna Hora on May 27, 1471. This election was viewed as a victory for the national party and reflected the strong anti-clerical sentiment prevalent in Bohemia at the time. This was contrasted by the alternative claimant, Matthias of Hungary, who was an unconditional adherent of Rome. In contrast, Polish envoys representing Vladislav promised that he would maintain the Compacts.

The early years of Vladislav's reign were characterized by a struggle with Matthias of Hungary over the Bohemian throne. This period of desultory warfare lasted until 1478, when the treaty of Olmutz was concluded. Under this agreement, Bohemia was secured for Vladislav, while Matthias retained the "lands of the Bohemian crown"—specifically Lusatia, Silesia, and Moravia—for the remainder of his life, after which they were to be restored to Bohemia.

## Domestic Policy and Social Impact
Vladislav's policy regarding state matters and religious controversy is described as reactionary. While he remained faithful to his promise to maintain the Compacts and did not prevent Bohemians from receiving communion in both kinds, he appointed government officials in Prague from the section of the Utraquist party closest to Rome. This period saw the severe persecution of the Bohemian Brethren, extreme Hussites, which led to serious riots in Prague and the storming of the city's three town halls. In response, nobles of the same faith formed a league to protect themselves. Peace was temporarily restored following a meeting of all estates at Kutna Hora in 1485.

In terms of state governance, Vladislav's reign was marked by a decrease in royal prerogative and an unprecedented increase in the power of the nobility. This shift occurred at the expense of the peasants and townsmen. A decree issued in 1487 practically established serfdom in Bohemia, a condition previously almost unknown in the region. This measure significantly impacted the future of the country, as rulers could no longer rely on the independent and sturdy peasantry that had formed the core of the armies of the Prokops and Zizka.

Furthermore, the rights of Bohemian townsmen were curtailed through various enactments, most notably a decree known as the "regulations of King Vladislav." This codification enumerated the rights of knights and nobles but entirely ignored those of the towns. It was tacitly assumed that townsmen possessed no inherent rights, only privileges granted by the sovereign with the consent of the nobility and knights.

## Expansion to Hungary and Later Life
On July 15, 1490, the diet elected Vladislav as the king of Hungary. Following this, many events of his later life are tied to Hungarian history. During this period, Janos Corvinus, the illegitimate son of Matthias Hunyadi, did homage to Vladislav to save his fortunes. Janos eventually relinquished three important fortresses—Tata, Komarom, and Pressburg—into Vladislav's hands during an invasion of Hungary by Maximilian.

In 1502, Vladislav married Anna de Candale, who had connections to the royal family of France. They had two children: a daughter, Anna, who later married Archduke Ferdinand of Austria, and a son, Louis. Vladislav died in Hungary in 1516.

## Succession and the Reign of Louis
Vladislav was succeeded by his son, Louis, who had been crowned king of Bohemia at the age of three. Per Vladislav's instructions, the emperor Maximilian I and Sigismund, king of Poland, served as guardians for the young king. While the Bohemian estates accepted this arrangement, they refused to grant the guardians any right to interfere in Bohemian affairs. Consequently, the country was ruled almost without control by great Bohemian nobles, specifically the supreme burgrave, Zdenek Leo, lord of Rozmital.

The nominal reign of Louis saw a significant constitutional development in 1517 at the diet of St Wenceslas. During this meeting, the estates settled long-standing discords. The citizens renounced certain claimed privileges, while the other two estates recognized municipal autonomy and sanctioned the presence of towns at the diet meetings—a practice to which they had been informally readmitted since 1508. On October 24, the three estates agreed to live together in friendly intercourse as members of the same race and country.

In 1522, Louis arrived in Bohemia from Hungary, where he had also been elected king. Upon reaching Prague, he dismissed all Bohemian state officials, including Leo of Rozmital. He appointed John of Wartenberg as burgrave and Charles of Munsterberg as regent of Bohemia during his absences.
