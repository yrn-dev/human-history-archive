# Roger Ascham

Roger Ascham (c. 1515–1568) was an English writer and scholar born in Kirby Wiske, a village in the North Riding of Yorkshire. He was the third son of John Ascham, who served as steward to Lord Scrope of Bolton. Much of the available information regarding his early life is derived from Edward Grant, the headmaster of Westminster, who edited Ascham's letters and delivered a panegyrical oration on his life in 1576.

## Education and Early Academic Career
Ascham did not receive a traditional school education; instead, he was raised in the house of Sir Humphry Wingfield, a barrister and 1533 speaker of the House of Commons. Under the guidance of a tutor named R. Bond, Ascham and other children in the household engaged in archery, a sport Sir Humphry encouraged by bringing bows and shafts from London during term times. This early influence led Ascham to write his first English work, *Toxophilus*, and to advocate for the inclusion of archery in educational establishments, including the statutes of Harrow, St Albans, and other Elizabethan schools.

Around 1530, at approximately fifteen years of age, Ascham entered St John's College, Cambridge. He was influenced by Sir Thomas Smith and John Cheke, the latter of whom became a fellow during Ascham's first year. Ascham also befriended Robert Pember, a scholar of great learning and facility in Greek. Following Pember's advice, Ascham accelerated his mastery of the Greek language by teaching it to boys while he was still a boy himself. His Latin studies focused specifically on Caesar and Cicero.

Ascham earned his B.A. on February 18, 1534/5. Despite the college master, Dr. Nicholas Metcalfe, being a "papist," he praised students who excelled in the "new learning." Metcalfe helped Ascham secure a fellowship, though Ascham notes that after he spoke against the Pope, he faced punishment and a rebuke, and other fellows were warned not to vote for him during the election. Ascham regarded the day of his election as his "birthday."

## Tutorship of Princess Elizabeth
Shortly after the reign of Edward VI began, Ascham publicly professed Protestant opinions during a disputation on the doctrine of the Mass. The debate began at his college and moved to the university's public schools before being stopped by the vice-chancellor. Ascham wrote a letter of complaint regarding this to Sir William Cecil.

Through Cecil and the request of Princess Elizabeth, Ascham was selected as her tutor in January 1548, following the death of her previous tutor, Grindal. Ascham taught the sixteen-year-old Elizabeth for two years, primarily at Cheshunt. He praised her industry, wisdom, stature, and beauty, noting her proficiency in English, French, and Italian, as well as her ability to speak Latin and Greek. Under his tutelage, Elizabeth read most of Cicero and a large portion of Titus Livius, as well as the Greek Testament, the tragedies of Sophocles, select orations of Isocrates, and the works of Melanchthon and St Cyprian. Ascham's tenure ended in 1550 following a quarrel with the princess's steward.

## Diplomatic and Court Service
Following his return to Cambridge, John Cheke secured Ascham a position as secretary to Sir Richard Morrison, an ambassador to Charles V. During this embassy, which visited Venice, Innsbruck, and Louvain, Ascham read Greek with the ambassador several days a week. His letters from this period were published in 1553 as a "Report" on Germany.

Upon the accession of Queen Mary, Ascham was appointed Latin secretary to the Queen through Bishop Gardiner, receiving a pension of £20 per year. During this time, he quietly suppressed his Protestantism, though he mentioned to the schoolmaster Sturm that some attempted to block Gardiner's benevolence toward him because of his religion. On June 1, 1554, he married Margaret Howe, the niece of Sir R. Wallop, with whom he had two sons. Ascham frequently complained of poverty, though he held various lucrative leases of crown and ecclesiastical property.

In 1555, he resumed his studies with Princess Elizabeth, focusing on the Greek orations of Demosthenes (*De Corona*) and Aeschines. After Elizabeth ascended the throne, Ascham was granted the prebend and canonry of Wetwang in York minster on October 5, 1559, despite being a layman.

## Literary Contributions and Educational Theory
Ascham served as a letter-writer for his college and the university starting in 1541 or earlier. In 1546, he was elected the university's public orator. 

In 1563, during a great plague in London, Ascham dined with Sir William Cecil and other ministers at Windsor. During the meal, Cecil mentioned that scholars at Eaton had fled their school to avoid beatings, sparking a debate on school discipline. Ascham championed the "anti-floggers," arguing for more discretion in correction. This experience, combined with a conversation with treasurer Sir Richard Sackville—who claimed a brutal schoolmaster had made him hate learning—inspired Ascham to write *The Scholemaster*. This work is considered a typical English book of Renaissance culture.

In the realm of literary criticism, Ascham raised a theory regarding the system of Latin quantity in metrics, which later influenced a group known as the "Areopagus," including Spenser, Sidney, Dyer, and Harvey.
