# Cesare Borgia

Cesare Borgia, also known as the Duke of Valentinois, was a prominent figure in Italian politics at the turn of the 16th century. His career was characterized by ambitious territorial conquests in Romagna, the use of military force and diplomatic intrigue, and a complex relationship with the papacy.

## Early Career and Transition from Priesthood
In July 1497, Cesare served as a papal legate in Naples, where he crowned Frederick of Aragon as king. During this period, the pope sought to utilize Cesare to execute various political schemes and attempted to secure a wealthy marriage for him. Cesare desired to marry Carlotta, the daughter of the king of Naples, but both Carlotta and her father refused the alliance, citing his status as "a priest, the bastard of a priest." Consequently, in August 1498, Cesare requested and received permission from the pope and the cardinals to renounce his priesthood "for the good of his soul."

On October 1, 1498, Cesare traveled to France as a papal legate to Louis XII. His mission was to deliver a papal bull annulling Louis's marriage to Jeanne of France, as Louis intended to marry Anne of Brittany. In return for this service, Cesare received military assistance for his own enterprises and the duchy of Valentinois. While in France, he again unsuccessfully attempted to win over Carlotta of Naples, eventually marrying Charlotte d'Albret, the sister of the king of Navarre, in May 1499.

## Conquests in Romagna
With the support of the French king, the pope planned for Cesare to subdue turbulent local despots in Romagna to carve out a personal principality. Establishing his headquarters at Cesena, Cesare led an army composed of Italian troops, 4,000 Swiss and Gascons, and 300 French lances. He quickly captured Imola and subsequently besieged Forli. The city was held by Caterina Sforza, the widow of Girolamo Riario; after a gallant defense, she surrendered on January 22, 1500, and later entered a convent.

In October 1500, aided by Venetian friendship and an army of 10,000 men, Cesare returned to Romagna. Giovanni Sforza of Pesaro and Pandolfo Malatesta of Rimini fled, allowing their cities to be taken. Faenza surrendered in April 1501 after Cesare promised to spare the life of its lord, Astorre Manfredi. However, Cesare broke this promise, sending Manfredi to Rome as a prisoner, where he was outraged and killed. Following the capture of Castel Bolognese, the pope created Cesare the duke of Romagna. He further expanded his power by conquering Urbino and Camerino.

## Political Methods and the Conspiracy of La Magione
Cesare was regarded as a terror throughout Italy due to his insatiable avidity, lack of scruple, and cruelty. He was described as morose, silent, and unsympathetic. His power was challenged by the conspiracy of La Magione, where deposed princes, the Orsinis, and several of his own captains—including G.P. Baglioni, Oliverotto da Fermo, and Vitellozzo Vitelli—plotted against the Borgias. This led to risings in Romagna and Urbino and the defeat of papal troops.

Cesare managed to resolve the crisis when the French king promised assistance, which intimidated the confederates. While trusting no one, Cesare outmaneuvered his rivals. He decoyed the *condottieri* Vitelli, Oliverotto, Paolo Orsini, and the duke of Gravina to the town of Senigallia, where he had them arrested.

## Influence on Niccolò Machiavelli
In October 1502, Niccolò Machiavelli was sent as an envoy to Cesare's camp in Romagna. Machiavelli observed Cesare's intricate intrigues, including the murder of his disaffected captains. Machiavelli developed a strong admiration for Cesare's combination of diplomatic prudence and audacity, his self-reliance, his firm administration of conquered provinces, and his adroit use of fraud and cruelty. 

Machiavelli particularly noted Cesare's method of substituting native troops from his own duchy for mercenaries and aliens. This observation influenced Machiavelli's own efforts to establish a national militia for Florence. In his writings, including *The Prince*, Machiavelli idealized Cesare as "Valentino," viewing his ability to cement a new state from scattered elements as a model for the unification of Italy.

## Decline and Fall
Cesare returned to Rome in early 1503 to help reduce the last rebel Orsinis. However, in the summer of 1503, both he and his father were seized by fever. The pope died on August 18, 1503, while Cesare was still incapacitated. This event triggered the collapse of his power; enemies rose in Rome and the deposed princes in Romagna sought to regain their territories.

After the brief reign of Pius III, Giuliano della Rovere was elected as Pope Julius II on November 1, 1503. Although Julius II initially showed no ill-will, he insisted that Cesare's territories be restored to the church. To prevent Venice from establishing a protectorate over the principalities in Romagna, Julius II arrested Cesare, effectively ending Borgia's power. Cesare was forced to surrender his castles in Romagna.
