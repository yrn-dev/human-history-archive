# Roger Ascham

Roger Ascham (c. 1515–1568) was an English scholar and writer born in the village of Kirby Wiske in the North Riding of Yorkshire. He was the third son of John Ascham, who served as steward to Lord Scrope of Bolton. Much of the available information regarding his early life is attributed to Edward Grant, the headmaster of Westminster, who edited Ascham's letters and delivered a panegyrical oration on his life in 1576.

## Education and Early Academic Career
Ascham did not receive a traditional school education; instead, he was educated in the home of Sir Humphry Wingfield, a barrister and 1533 speaker of the House of Commons. Under the guidance of a tutor named R. Bond, Ascham and other children in the house engaged in archery as their primary sport. Sir Humphry would personally bring bows and shafts from London during term times to oversee their shooting in the field. This early experience influenced Ascham's first English work, *Toxophilus*, and his belief in the importance of archery within educational establishments, which likely led to its inclusion in the statutes of Elizabethan schools such as Harrow and St Albans.

Around 1530, at approximately fifteen years of age, Ascham entered St John's College, Cambridge. During his time there, he was influenced by Sir Thomas Smith and John Cheke, the latter of whom became a fellow during Ascham's first year. Ascham was also guided by Robert Pember, a scholar noted for his facility with the Greek tongue. Following Pember's advice, Ascham taught Greek to boys to accelerate his own learning and focused his Latin studies on the works of Caesar and Cicero. He earned his B.A. on February 18, 1534/5.

While at Cambridge, Ascham was elected to a fellowship. This occurred during the mastership of Dr. Nicholas Metcalfe, a "papist" who nevertheless praised students who excelled in "the new learning." Despite his election, Ascham faced rebuke and punishment after speaking against the Pope among his companions, which led to a warning that no fellow should vote for him during that election.

## Public Service and Tutorship
Ascham's academic skills led him to serve as a letter-writer for his college and the university starting in 1541 or earlier. In 1546, he was elected public orator for the university. In 1548, he wrote a letter to the protector Somerset on behalf of Sedbergh school regarding the confiscation of its endowment under the Chantries Act.

Early in the reign of Edward VI, Ascham publicly professed Protestant opinions during a disputation on the doctrine of the Mass. Although the vice-chancellor stopped the disputation, Ascham's subsequent letter of complaint to Sir William Cecil proved beneficial. Following the death of Grindal, the tutor to Princess Elizabeth, Ascham was selected as her tutor through the influence of Cecil and the princess's own request. For two years, primarily at Cheshunt, Ascham taught the sixteen-year-old Elizabeth. He praised her industry and wisdom, noting her proficiency in French, Italian, and English, as well as her ability to speak Latin and Greek. Their curriculum included the Greek Testament, the tragedies of Sophocles, select orations of Isocrates, and the works of Melanchthon and St Cyprian. In Latin, Elizabeth drew her knowledge from Titus Livius and Cicero.

In 1550, after a quarrel with Elizabeth's steward, Ascham returned to Cambridge. He subsequently became the secretary to Sir Richard Morrison, the ambassador to Charles V. During this embassy, Ascham traveled to Venice, Innsbruck, and Louvain—finding the latter university inferior to Cambridge—and spent several days a week reading Greek with the ambassador.

## Later Life and Career
Following the accession of Mary, the embassy was recalled. Through Bishop Gardiner, Ascham was appointed Latin secretary to Queen Mary with an annual pension of £20. To maintain his safety, he quietly suppressed his Protestantism, though he noted that some attempted to hinder Gardiner's benevolence toward him because of his religion. On June 1, 1554, he married Margaret Howe, the niece of Sir R. Wallop, with whom he had two sons. Despite obtaining lucrative leases of crown and ecclesiastical property, Ascham frequently complained of poverty.

After Elizabeth's accession, Ascham resumed his studies with her in 1555, reading the orations of Aeschines and Demosthenes' *De Corona* in Greek. On October 5, 1559, despite being a layman, he was granted the prebend and canonry of Wetwang in York minster.

## Literary Works and Influence
Ascham is most famous for *The Scholemaster*, a work he began in 1563. The impetus for the book occurred on December 10, 1563, during a "great plague" in London while the court was at Windsor. During a dinner with Sir William Cecil and other ministers, Cecil mentioned that scholars at Eaton had fled their school to avoid beatings. Ascham championed the "anti-flogger" position in the resulting debate. This was further reinforced when Sir Richard Sackville shared that a "fond schoolmaster" had caused him to hate learning through brutality. *The Scholemaster* is regarded as a typical English book of Renaissance culture.

Ascham's letters were published in 1576 and saw various editions through 1611. His English works were edited by James Bennett in 1771, and his select letters, *Toxophilus*, and *The Scholemaster* were published by Dr. Giles in 1864-1865. *The Scholemaster* saw reprints in 1571 and 1589, with later editions by J. Upton, Prof. J.E.B. Mayor, and Prof. Edward Arber. *Toxophilus* was republished in 1571, 1589, 1788, and by Prof. Edward Arber in 1868 and 1902.
