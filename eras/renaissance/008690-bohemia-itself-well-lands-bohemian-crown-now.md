# Bohemia and the Lands of the Bohemian Crown

Bohemia, along with the associated lands of the Bohemian crown, has a history defined by shifts in dynastic rule, religious conflict, and a fluctuating struggle for autonomy against imperial powers, particularly the house of Habsburg.

## Early Rulers and Territorial Expansion
The introduction of Christianity to Bohemia occurred via Moravia through the Greek monks Cyrillus and Methodius; Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius around 873. Early notable rulers included Wenceslas the Holy, who was murdered by his brother Boleslav in 935 and later canonized. Under Boleslav and Boleslav II, Bohemia expanded its frontiers to include Moravia, a large part of Silesia, and portions of Poland (now Galicia). However, these gains were temporary; Boleslav III lost foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great of Poland and was eventually dethroned due to his cruelty, leading to the rule of the Polish prince Vladivoj.

By 1198, Premysl Ottakar was crowned king, beginning an uninterrupted line of royal titles. His successor, Premysl Ottakar II, significantly expanded the realm to include the archduchies of Austria, Styria, Carinthia, Istria, and parts of northern Italy, extending his reach from the Giant Mountains to the Adriatic. Known as "the man of gold" for his wealth and "the man of iron" for his military power, Ottakar II encouraged German immigration to provide a counterpoise to the power of the Bohemian nobility.

## Dynastic Transitions and the Hussite Era
Following the Premysl line, the Luxemburg dynasty ruled until 1437. King John of Luxemburg, who allied closely with France and died at the battle of Crecy in 1346, was noted for the proverb that nothing in the world could be done without the help of God and the king of Bohemia. 

The 15th century was marked by religious strife and the rise of the Hussites. After the death of King Sigismund, rioting in Prague led to the expulsion of many Germans. Sigismund attempted to reconquer the city, which was viewed as a heretical community for its use of the sacred chalice. Despite a crusade proclaimed by Pope Martin V in 1420, the Taborites, led by Zizka, repulsed the crusaders at Vitkov hill, and Sigismund was defeated at the battle of the Vysehrad on November 1, 1420. Later, Prince Vladislav of Poland was elected king in 1471. A treaty at Olmutz in 1478 secured Bohemia to Vladislav, while the "lands of the Bohemian crown"—consisting of Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia—were held by Matthias of Hungary for the remainder of his life before being restored to Bohemia.

## The Habsburg Conquest and the White Hill
The relationship between Bohemia and the Habsburgs became volatile in the early 17th century. In 1611, Archduke Leopold of Austria invaded Prague but was defeated by troops raised by the Bohemian estates. Subsequently, the estates deposed King Rudolph, and Matthias occupied Bohemia. Although Matthias had previously allied with Bohemian Protestants, he later adopted policies favoring the Church of Rome. In 1615, Matthias convoked a general diet including representatives from the lands of the Bohemian crown, where it was decreed that Bohemian would be the official language of the country.

Following the death of Matthias, the Bohemian estates deposed Ferdinand and elected Frederick, elector palatine, as king in 1619. This led to a military confrontation. On November 8, 1620, imperial forces under the duke of Bavaria, Tilly, and Bouquoi defeated the Bohemian army at the battle of the White Hill (Bila Hora). This event marked a turning point; King Frederick fled, and Bohemia submitted to Ferdinand.

## Suppression and Loss of Autonomy
The aftermath of the battle of the White Hill saw the execution of national movement leaders on June 21, 1621, and the wholesale confiscation of lands. Much of the ancient nobility was exiled, replaced by foreign adventurers and imperial army veterans. Catholicism was re-established through "terrible cruelty," and those refusing the creed of the Roman church were expelled.

In 1627, Ferdinand published a decree suppressing Bohemia's ancient free constitution. The new constitution established the Bohemian crown as hereditary within the house of Habsburg and added a fourth "estate"—the clergy—which took precedence over all others. The diet lost its legislative power, which was vested solely in the sovereign, and while the estates could still vote on taxes, they were forbidden from attaching conditions to grants of money. Additionally, the German language was granted equal right with Bohemian in law courts and government offices.

## Later Imperial Rule and 19th Century Developments
Bohemia remained a theater of the Thirty Years' War, suffering occupations by Saxons and pillaging by Swedish troops, including the taking of part of Prague in 1648. In 1720, the estates recognized the Pragmatic Sanction, allowing female succession to the throne for Archduchess Maria Theresa. Despite this, Charles, elector of Bavaria, invaded in 1741 and occupied Prague before Maria Theresa recovered the territory.

Under Maria Theresa and Joseph II, Bohemian autonomy was further curtailed. A 1749 decree abolished separate Bohemian law courts, and an Austro-Bohemian chancellor was later appointed to oversee the administration of Bohemia and the German domains.

By 1871, attempts were made by Austrian prime minister Count Karl Hohenwart to reconcile Bohemia with the empire by recognizing the rights of the kingdom. While these efforts initially failed, the government of Count Eduard Taaffe (starting in 1879) persuaded Bohemian representatives to return to the parliament in Vienna. In exchange, the government removed electoral anomalies that favored the German minority and established a Bohemian university at Prague. The Bohemians participated in this parliament while maintaining the view that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary.
