# Semper Virginis

In the history of early printing and bibliography, the phrase *Semper Virginis* appears in the title of one of the earliest known examples of a dated title-page. This specific work, titled *Sermo ad populum predicabilis in festo presentacionis Beatissime Marie Semper Virginis*, represents a significant transition in the physical production of books.

## The First Dated Title-Page
During the early period of printing, the general appearance of printed copies closely resembled manuscripts written in formal book-hand. Printers typically based their type on handwriting deemed appropriate for the class of work being produced. In these early volumes, decorations such as paragraph marks, initial letters, headlines, and chapter headings were often added by hand to mimic manuscript styles. Most of these early books lacked a title-page and provided no statement regarding when, where, or by whom they were produced. When such information was provided, it was typically reserved for a colophon—a paragraph at the end of the book that sometimes included the printer's arms or a fanciful design.

The work *Sermo ad populum predicabilis in festo presentacionis Beatissime Marie Semper Virginis* is distinguished as the first known book to feature a dated title-page. This title-page consisted of a nine-line paragraph on an otherwise blank page. In addition to the title of the book, the page included words in praise of the subject and the date 1470 written in roman numerals. It also contained a reference directing the reader to further information on the following page. This book was printed in Cologne by Arnold ther Hoernen.

## Development of Printing in Cologne
The printer Arnold ther Hoernen was active in Cologne during the late 15th century. Beyond the introduction of the dated title-page in the *Semper Virginis* text, ther Hoernen is credited with the first use of headlines and pagination in 1470 and 1471. These innovations contributed to the rapid development of book completion independent of manual handwork. Other advancements in the region followed shortly after, such as the introduction of printed signatures in 1472 by Johann Koelhoff in Cologne to assist binders in the correct arrangement of quires.

## Context of the Term "Semper Virginis"
The phrase *Semper Virginis* (meaning "always Virgin") appears in various historical and ecclesiastical contexts within the archive's records:

*   **Ecclesiastical Architecture:** In the history of Rome, the *Liber Pontificalis* records that a temple known as the Pantheon was converted into a church dedicated to *Sanctæ Mariæ semper Virginis* and all martyrs. This occurred after a request was made to the prince Phocas. Other accounts suggest the Pantheon, originally vowed by Agrippa to Neptune and Cybele, was dedicated to the Virgin on the calends of November by Boniface IV.
*   **Theological Literature:** The term is used in the title of a three-volume collection of Patristic passages regarding the doctrine and festival of the Conception of the Blessed Virgin, titled *De immaculato Deiparae semper Virginis conceptu Caroli Passaglia sac. S.J. commentarius*, published in Rome between 1854 and 1855.

## Evolution of the Title-Page
Following the 1470 publication of the *Semper Virginis* sermon, the use of title-pages remained slow to develop. For approximately twenty years after 1470, title-pages—when used—usually only featured a short title and occasionally a woodcut or a printer's/publisher's device. Decoration was more frequently applied to the first page of the actual text, sometimes utilizing an ornamental border. A further development occurred six years after the *Semper Virginis* publication, when Erhard Ratdolt and partners in Venice printed their names, the date, and descriptive verses on the title-page of a Latin calendar, enclosing the text within a four-piece border.
