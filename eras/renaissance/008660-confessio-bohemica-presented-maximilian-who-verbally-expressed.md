# Confessio Bohemica

The *Confessio Bohemica* was a profession of faith drafted by the estates of Bohemia during the 16th century. It emerged as a result of prolonged religious disputes and political negotiations between the Bohemian estates and their monarch, Maximilian.

## Origins and Development
In 1575, King Maximilian returned to Bohemia after a long absence, prompted by the estates' refusal to grant subsidies to a monarch who resided away from the kingdom. During the subsequent sittings of the diet, the Bohemian estates presented several principal demands. The primary request was for the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg"—a summary of the teachings of Luther—within Bohemia. Additionally, the estates renewed a demand first expressed at the diet of 1567: that they should possess the right to appoint members of the consistory, the ecclesiastical body responsible for ruling the Utraquist church. This demand was particularly pressing as the church had lacked an archbishop since the death of John of Rokycan.

Maximilian's response to these demands was vacillating. He was influenced by the threats of the Bohemians on one side and the advice of the papal nuncio on the other. The nuncio warned the king that making concessions to the Protestants of Bohemia would create significant difficulties in his other dominions. Ultimately, Maximilian refused to recognize the Confession of Augsburg.

In response to this final refusal, a majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drew up their own profession of faith, which became known as the *Confessio Bohemica*.

## Theological Content
The *Confessio Bohemica* was largely identical to the Augsburg Confession in most of its points. However, it differed specifically regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. In this matter, the Bohemian profession aligned with the views of Calvin rather than those of Luther, a divergence attributed to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren.

## Reception by Maximilian
When the *Confessio Bohemica* was presented to Maximilian, the king verbally expressed his approval of the document. Despite this verbal endorsement, he refused to allow the confession to be made public. Furthermore, he denied consent for the *Confessio* to be included among the charters of the kingdom.

Maximilian also rejected the estates' demand to appoint the members of the consistory. However, he did reach a temporary settlement by consenting to exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the jurisdiction of the consistory. He allowed these groups to choose fifteen defenders—with five belonging to each of the estates—to serve as supreme representatives.

## Later Political Significance
The *Confessio Bohemica* remained a point of contention in later years. By the early 17th century, the groups known as the Bohemian Brethren, Lutherans, and Utraquists were collectively referred to as Protestants. In March of the year preceding 1609, the estates drafted a document containing twenty-five "Articles" to formulate their religious demands. Among these articles was the wish that the *Confessio Bohemica* be recognized as one of the fundamental laws of the kingdom. 

The estates also sought complete religious liberty for all classes and equal rights for Protestants and Roman Catholics to hold all offices of state, while requesting limits on the Jesuits' ability to acquire land. King Rudolph considered these articles illegal and demanded their destruction. Despite negotiations led by twelve members of the estates, including Count Henry Matthias Thurn, Rudolph stated on March 31, 1609, that he could grant no concessions regarding matters of religion.
