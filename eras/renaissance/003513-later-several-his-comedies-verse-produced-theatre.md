# Verse Comedies and Theatre Productions

The production of comedies, particularly those written in verse, has been a significant element of dramatic history across various European traditions. These works often balanced artistic experimentation with the demands of public performance and the preferences of the theatrical venues that hosted them.

## French Verse Comedies and Dramatists

In France, the intersection of poetry and theatre produced several notable works. Julien Auguste Pélage Brizeux, a poet of Irish origin settled in Brittany, produced a one-act verse comedy titled *Racine* in 1827, which was performed at the Théâtre Français in collaboration with Philippe Busoni. 

During the 18th century, the dramatist Gresset contributed significantly to the genre. After producing the tragedy *Edouard III* in 1740, he wrote the comedy *Sidnei* in 1745. He also authored *Le Mechant*, a play that continued to be performed and was characterized by Brunetiere as the finest verse comedy of the French 18th-century theatre, surpassing even the *Metromanie* of Alexis Piron.

Earlier, in the 17th century, Jean Desmarets (Sieur de Saint-Sorlin) began writing for the theatre at the request of Richelieu. His work in this field included the comedy *Les Visionnaires* (1637), which was long considered a masterpiece, as well as the verse tragedy *Scipion* (1639) and the prose-tragedy *Érigone* (1638).

## English and Irish Dramatic Works

In the English-speaking world, the production of comedies often varied between prose and verse. Hugh Kelly, an Irish dramatist, produced his first comedy, *False Delicacy*, in prose. It was produced by Garrick at Drury Lane on January 23, 1768, as a moral and sentimental "sermon preached in acts" intended to rival Oliver Goldsmith's *Good-Natured Man*. Kelly's second comedy, *A Word to the Wise*, was produced at Drury Lane on March 3, 1770, though it was abandoned following riots during its first two performances.

William Davenant's career in the early 17th century saw a transition from tragedies and masques to the legitimate drama. In 1636, he published the famous comedy *The Wits*, which had been licensed in 1633. Davenant later became the manager of the new theatre in Drury Lane in 1639.

## German Comedy and the Middle Class

In Germany, Julius Roderich Benedix focused his comedic works on the lives of the upper middle class. While managing the theatre at Wesel, he produced the comedy *Das bemooste Haupt* (1841), which achieved great success. Benedix's farces were noted for their bright, natural dialogue and clear plots. His best-known comedies include *Dr Wespe*, *Die Hochzeitsreise*, *Der Vetter*, *Das Gefangnis*, *Das Lugen*, *Ein Lustspiel*, *Der Storenfried*, *Die Dienstboten*, *Aschenbrodel*, and *Die zartlichen Verwandten*.

## Other Notable Theatre Productions

The late 19th and early 20th centuries saw a shift toward didacticism and social commentary in French drama. Eugène Brieux wrote plays aimed at social iniquities, such as *Les Hannetons* (1906), a comedy in three acts. Other works by Brieux addressed the dowry system (*Les Trois Filles de M. Dupont*), the injustices of law (*La Robe rouge*), and the effects of betting among workmen (*Le Résultat des courses*).

Charles Maurice Donnay also produced several comedies, including the four-act *Lysistrata*, which was produced at the Grand Theatre in 1892. His play *Amants* (1895), produced at the Renaissance theatre, was hailed by Jules Lemaitre as the *Berenice* of contemporary French drama.

In the United States, William Evans Burton, an English actor and playwright, achieved success as a manager and actor in New York, Philadelphia, and Baltimore. He performed in low comedy parts based on the novels of Dickens and authored numerous plays, including *Ellen Wareham* (1833), which was produced simultaneously in five London theatres.
