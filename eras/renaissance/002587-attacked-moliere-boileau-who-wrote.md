# Nicolas Boileau-Despreaux

Nicolas Boileau-Despreaux (1636–1711) was a prominent French poet and critic who is recognized as the first writer to reduce French versification to a specific rule and method. He emphasized the value of workmanship for its own sake, introducing a level of terseness, vigour of expression, and perfect regularity of verse structure that had previously been absent in French poetry, with the exception of Malherbe.

## Early Life and Education
Born on November 1, 1636, in the rue de Jerusalem, Paris, Boileau was the fifteenth child of Gilles Boileau, a clerk in the parlement. His mother died when he was two years old, and he is noted to have had a delicate constitution. He was educated at the College de Beauvais and later studied theology at the Sorbonne. He eventually transitioned to the study of law and was called to the bar on December 4, 1656. However, he soon recoiled from the legal profession in disgust due to the chicanery he encountered. Following the death of his father in 1657, which left him a small fortune, Boileau devoted himself entirely to letters.

## Literary Career and Satires
Boileau's early poetic work did not immediately signal his future success. His "peculiar powers" first became evident in his first satire (1660), written in imitation of Juvenal's third satire, which depicted a poet's farewell to Paris. He eventually produced twelve satires. These works served two primary purposes: they allowed him to parody and attack high-ranking writers of the era—including Philippe Quinault, Jean Chapelain, the abbe Charles Cotin, and Georges de Scudery—and they demonstrated the poetical capabilities of the French language.

In 1664, Boileau wrote the prose *Dialogue des heros de roman*, a satire targeting the elaborate romances of the period. This work is credited with effectively abolishing the "lucubrations" of Mlle de Scudery and La Calprenede. Although widely read in manuscript, the book was not published until 1713, reportedly out of regard for Mlle de Scudery.

## Relationships with Contemporaries
Boileau maintained close ties with other leading literary figures of his time. He was a constant friend to Moliere and Racine, supporting their interests and meeting with them, along with Antoine Furetiere and Chapelle, at the *Pomme du Pin* and the *Moulon Blanc* to discuss literary matters. Boileau expressed his admiration for Moliere in the second satire (1664) and in stanzas addressed to him in 1663. Regarding the great men of the reign of Louis XIV, Boileau once claimed to know only three: himself, Moliere, and Corneille. While he viewed Racine as an "extremely clever fellow," he claimed to have taught Racine the art of "elaborate rhyming" (*rimer difficilement*).

Boileau's relationship with Pierre Corneille was more complex. While he admired Corneille whenever his principles allowed, his testimony was of a "chequered character." Nevertheless, Boileau invalidated a story that he had offered to resign his own pension to provide financial aid to Corneille.

## Critical Views and the Ballade
As a critic, Boileau was known for his "hatred of stupid books" and a hard, unsympathetic outlook. He and Moliere both attacked the ballade, a poetic form that had culminated in the 17th century with poets such as La Fontaine, Voiture, Sarrazin, and Madame Deshoulieres. Boileau criticized the form, writing that the ballade, "asservie a ses vieilles maximes, Souvent doit tout son lustre au caprice des rimes."

## Later Life and Controversies
In 1677, Boileau was appointed historiographer to the king, after which his literary output decreased. His later works include the ode *Sur la prise de Namur*, the epistles *Sur l'amour de Dieu* and *A mes vers*, and the satires *Sur l'homme* and *Sur les femmes*. The latter provoked an *Apologie des femmes* by Charles Perrault and was condemned by the bishop Bossuet as being subversive of morality and incompatible with the spirit of Christianity.

Boileau was admitted to the Academy on April 15, 1684, through the wish of the king. In 1687, he retired to a country-house in Auteuil, which Racine referred to as his *hotellerie d'Auteuil*. He returned to Paris in 1705, living in the cloisters of Notre Dame with his confessor. Around 1705, he wrote the 12th satire, *Sur l'equivoque*, which attacked the Jesuits. The Jesuit fathers subsequently pressured Louis XIV to withdraw the publication privilege for Boileau's works and demanded the suppression of this satire. These conflicts are said to have hastened his death, which occurred on March 13, 1711.
