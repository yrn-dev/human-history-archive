# Khair-ed-Din and the Barbarossa Family

The Barbarossa family consisted of Turkish sea rovers and admirals during the 16th century. The name "Barbarossa," meaning "Redbeard," was applied by Christians to the family, likely due to their tawny or red beards. While some historians suggested the name was a mispronunciation of "Baba Arouj," contemporary Arab chronicles explicitly state that the nickname was applied to Khair-ed-Din.

## Origins and Early Life
The family was founded by Yakub, a Roumeliot probably of Albanian blood who settled in Mitylene following the Turkish conquest of the area. Yakub worked as a skipper and coasting trader. He had four sons born after 1482: Elias, Isaak, Arouj, and Khizr. While Khizr became a potter and Isaak a trader, Elias and Arouj pursued a life of sea roving.

During an encounter with a galley of the Knights of Saint John based at Rhodes, Elias was killed and Arouj was captured. Arouj was eventually ransomed by a Turkish pasha and returned to the sea. He spent time serving the Mamelukes in Egypt, but during the conflict between the Mamelukes and Sultan Selim I, Arouj moved to Tunis.

## Activities in North Africa
In North Africa, Arouj found employment as a mercenary amidst the constant conflicts of Berber princes, supplementing this work with piratical raids against Christian trade. He was eventually joined in Tunis by his brother Khizr, who took the name Khair-ed-Din, and later by their brother Isaak.

Arouj and Khair-ed-Din collaborated with Moors who had been exiled from Granada following the Spanish conquest of 1492. Together, they conducted raids on the Spanish coast and sought to increase their power by supplanting or fighting native African princes. The brothers established their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes. In 1512, they attempted to capture Bougie from the Spaniards but were unsuccessful.

## The Rise of Khair-ed-Din
The Spanish, acting in self-defence against these piratical attacks, began conquering coast towns including Tunis, Algiers, and Oran. Arouj fell in battle against the Spaniards in 1518. Following this loss, his brother Khair-ed-Din appealed to the sultan of Turkey, Selim, who provided him with troops.

Using these forces, Khair-ed-Din drove the Spaniards from a fort located on a rocky island in front of Algiers in 1529. This action established him as the founder of Turkish power in the region.

## Governance of Northern Africa
From 1518 until the death of Uluch Ali in 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli. Following 1587 and until 1659, the region was governed by Turkish pashas sent from Constantinople for three-year terms. 

This system ended in 1659 when a military revolt in Algiers stripped the pashas of their power. From that point forward, these African cities functioned as anarchical military republics. Although they remained nominal parts of the Turkish empire, they chose their own rulers and sustained themselves through plunder.
