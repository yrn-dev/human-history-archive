# The Works of Francis Bacon

The writings of Francis Bacon, excluding his occasional writings and letters, are categorized into three primary classes: Professional, Literary, and Philosophical.

## Professional Works
Bacon's professional legal writings include the *Maxims of Law*, the *Reading on the Statute of Uses*, and a treatise on the *Use of the Law*, though the latter is possibly spurious. Bacon expressed hope that posterity would debate whether he or Sir Edward Coke was the greater lawyer once his rules and decisions were compared to Coke's reports. 

While Coke's reports may demonstrate a more dogged grasp of the letter, a greater knowledge of precedent, and a more complete mastery of technical details, Bacon's legal works are noted for their richness, ethical fullness, and a more comprehensive intelligence of the abstract principles of jurisprudence. Bacon is characterized as a lawyer of the first order who possessed a power of generalization and a keen scientific insight into the bearings of isolated facts. These qualities made him well-suited for his self-imposed, though ultimately uncompleted, task of codifying or digesting the chaotic mass of English law.

## Philosophical Works
The majority of Bacon's writings consist of fragments or treatises. These were either closely connected to his grand comprehensive scheme or formed integral parts of it. These philosophical works are further classified into three specific categories:

1. Writings that were originally intended to be part of the *Instauratio* but were later thrown aside or superseded.
2. Works that are connected with the *Instauratio* but were not directly included in its plan.
3. Writings that actually formed part of the *Instauratio Magna*.
