# Baconian Method and Natural History

## The Role of Natural History
The Baconian method is considered valueless and inapplicable unless it is supported by materials that have been duly presented and collected. To function, the method requires the formation of a competent natural history of the *Phaenomena Universi*. Bacon describes this natural history as the *basis totius negotii*, or the basis of the entire undertaking. 

A short introductory sketch regarding the requirements for such a natural history is provided in the tract *Parasceve*, which is appended to the *Novum Organum*. To build this history, Bacon intended several principal works, some of which were published and others left in manuscript. These include the *Historia Ventorum*, *Historia Vitae et Mortis*, and *Historia Densi et Rari*, as well as a comprehensive collection of observations and facts titled *Sylva Sylvarum*.

## Inductive Process and Axioms
Bacon's approach to induction involves the establishment of axioms. He asserts that when axioms are established through this kind of induction, it is necessary to examine whether the axiom is framed to the measure of the particulars from which it was derived, or if it is wider and larger. If the axiom is wider, one must observe if it confirms that wideness by indicating new particulars. This serves as a collateral security to ensure the thinker does not loosely grasp at abstract forms or shadows, but instead focuses on things realized in matter and solid.

## Logic and the Syllogism
While Bacon is associated with induction, he did not propose to abandon the syllogism entirely. He viewed the syllogism as incompetent for "principal things" rather than useless for generality, noting that there is no reason why it should not be employed in mathematics. 

In the field of physics, Bacon argued that induction is required because of the inconstancy of the physical body and the flux of matter. Induction allows for the formation of well-defined notions. Once the axioms and notions of the first class have been defined through induction, the syllogism may then be utilized.

## Theoretical and Practical Frameworks
Bacon's method involves a scale of nature that corresponds with a scale of ascending axioms. In his theoretical analysis, the final step provides the first means for practical operation. 

Regarding the study of physics, Bacon maintained that the consideration of final causes must be rigidly excluded, as the knowledge of final causes does not lead to works. However, he noted there is no opposition between physical and final causes; ultimately, the mind is led to refer facts to Providence and God, viewing the universe as the work of reason. He also noted that the idea of final cause remains fruitful in sciences dealing with human action.

## Methodological Consciousness and Influence
Bacon was not ignorant of deductive methods or the use of hypotheses. This is evidenced by his admission of the syllogism into physical reasoning, his hints regarding the testing of axioms, his use of the *Vindemiatio*, and what he termed *Experientia Literata*. 

His terminology also reflects external influences. The word *Idola*, which he uses to describe false appearances in contrast to Platonic Ideas, is borrowed from Plato. Additionally, his notion of "form" and his classification of causes, as well as the phrase "better known in the order of nature," are derived from Aristotle. Despite his structured approach, Bacon seemed to anticipate that the natural progress of science would eventually render his specific method antiquated.
