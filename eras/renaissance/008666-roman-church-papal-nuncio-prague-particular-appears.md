# Roman Church in Bohemia and Imperial Affairs

The Roman church's relationship with the Bohemian lands and the Holy Roman Empire was characterized by prolonged religious conflict, diplomatic tension, and struggles over ecclesiastical jurisdiction. These dynamics were often mediated by the presence of papal nuncios and the directives of the popes.

## Religious Conflict in Bohemia
The influence of the Church of Rome in Bohemia began to weaken during the reign of King Charles due to attacks on clerical immorality by priests such as Waldhauser and Milic. This alienation grew during the church schism, as rival pontiffs engaged in theological strife. Consequently, much of the Bohemian nation supported Huss, who served as confessor to Queen Sophia. After Huss was declared a heretic and burnt on July 6, 1415, by the council of Constance, the nobles of Bohemia and Moravia issued the *Protestatio Bohemorum* on September 2, 1415. This document protested Huss's execution and declared that those claiming heresy existed in Bohemia were "liars" and "sons of the devil." This protest served as a declaration of war against the Roman church and initiated the Hussite wars.

While the Hussites eventually came to terms with the church in 1436, tensions persisted. The Church of Rome refused to recognize John of Rokycan, who had been chosen by the estates as archbishop of Prague. By the 15th century, the Bohemian nobility was divided into a national party and a Roman or Austrian division, the latter led by Ulrich of Rosenberg.

## The Papal Nuncio and the Bohemian Diet
In 1575, King Maximilian returned to Bohemia after the estates refused to grant subsidies to an absentee monarch. During the subsequent prolonged sittings of the diet, Maximilian's attitude was vacillating, influenced by both the threats of the Bohemians and the advice of the papal nuncio who had accompanied him to Prague. 

The papal nuncio strongly cautioned the king against making concessions to the Protestants of Bohemia, representing the significant difficulties Maximilian would face in his other dominions if he did so. This influence contributed to the king's final refusal to recognize the "Confession of Augsburg," a summary of Luther's teaching. While the Bohemian estates eventually produced the *Confessio Bohemica*—which aligned with Calvin regarding the sacrament of the Lord's Supper—Maximilian refused to make this profession public or include it among the kingdom's charters.

## Papal Influence on the Habsburg Succession
The Roman church also exerted pressure on the imperial succession. Emperor Rudolph, a patron of the arts who resided in Prague, suffered from melancholia and periods of insanity. By 1600, his mental impairment led his brother Matthias to suggest the appointment of a coadjutor and arrangements for succession. 

Rudolph initially repelled these suggestions, but his anger was further provoked by a letter from Pope Clement VIII. In a manner that was formally courteous but forcible, the pope highlighted the evil results that Rudolph's neglect of royal duties would bring upon his subjects. Pope Clement VIII called upon Rudolph to appoint a Habsburg prince as his successor to the thrones of Hungary, Bohemia, and the imperial crown. It is probable that the fear of the threats contained in the pope's letter induced the previously indifferent Rudolph to act.

## Papal Jurisdiction and Imperial Criticism
Beyond Bohemia, the Roman Curia's interference in the internal affairs of the Empire remained a point of contention. Johann Nikolaus von Hontheim, a German theologian and official to the archbishop-elector in Coblenz, studied the effects of this interference during the elections of emperors Francis I and Charles VII. Hontheim observed the "extreme claims" of the papal nuncio and his interference in the electoral college. These experiences led Hontheim to critically examine the basis of papal pretensions, which he later published under the pseudonym "Febronius."

In other contexts, the Roman church's claims of absolute supremacy were challenged by reformers. Martin Luther, after studying the Decretals and finding them full of frauds, questioned the papal jurisdiction that underpinned the system of Indulgences. Additionally, in 1613, an attempt to introduce the Gregorian calendar into Germany was frustrated by anti-papal prejudice.
