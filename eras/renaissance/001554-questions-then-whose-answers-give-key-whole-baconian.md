# The Key to Baconian Philosophy

The core of Baconian philosophy is centered on the relationship between human power and human knowledge. According to Bacon, the aim and work of human power is to generate or superinduce a new nature (or natures) upon a given body. Conversely, the aim and work of human knowledge is to discover the "form" or "true specific difference" of a given nature. This is further described as the "source of emanation" or "nature-engendering nature" (*natura naturans*).

## The Concept of Forms
The central questions that provide the key to the entire Baconian system are: "What are forms?" and "How is it that knowledge of them solves both the theoretical and the practical problem of science?" 

Bacon experienced significant difficulty in providing an exact and adequate definition of a "form." However, in the *Novum Organum*, he provides a general description based on three criteria:
1. If the form is given, the nature infallibly follows.
2. If the form is taken away, the nature infallibly vanishes.
3. The true form deduces the given nature from a source of being that is inherent in more natures and is better known in the natural order than the form itself.

## The Baconian Method and Induction
Bacon's theory of induction differs from the argument of moving from particulars to a general proposition. Instead, he viewed the essence of induction as elimination, specifically the process of exclusion and rejection. This approach is a necessary consequence of his doctrine of forms.

There are noted resemblances between the Baconian inductive method and the Socratic search for essence through the analysis of instances, which concludes in a definition. Bacon also acknowledged that Plato utilized a method somewhat similar to his own.

## Theoretical and Practical Applications
In the Baconian system, the final step of theoretical analysis provides the first means for practical operation. This creates a link between the discovery of forms and the ability to superinduce new natures.

Regarding the study of physics, Bacon asserted that the consideration of final causes must be rigidly excluded, as knowledge of final causes does not lead to works. However, he noted there is no opposition between physical and final causes; ultimately, the mind is led to refer facts to Providence and God, viewing the universe as the work of reason. Additionally, the idea of final cause remains fruitful in sciences dealing with human action.

## Criticisms and Limitations
The Baconian method has faced several formidable objections:
* **Efficiency and Practice:** Some, such as Macaulay, argued that subjecting an operation to analysis does not make a naturally performed operation more efficacious.
* **Scientific History:** Critics point out that scientific progress has not actually followed the Baconian method, and no single discovery can be definitely attributed to his rules.
* **Causal Conception:** Logicians have argued that Bacon held a radically false conception of the causal relation, treating scientific problems as matters of co-existence and assuming each phenomenon has only one cause.
* **Inductive Scale:** The process of forming axioms through a gradually ascending scale is viewed by some as a route no science has ever followed or could follow to make progress. Instead, true scientific procedure is seen as involving hypothesis followed by verification and the deductive method.

## Influence on Subsequent Thought
Bacon is regarded by some as the father of English psychological speculation. For example, the principle in Locke's *Essay*—that sensation and reflection produce all ideas—is briefly stated in the first aphorism of the *Novum Organum*. 

In ethics, Bacon is seen as a forerunner of the empirical method. The inductive method discussed in the *Novum Organum* and the spirit of *De Augmentis* are considered the roots of theories that construct moral codes through the inductive examination of human consciousness and action results, most notably utilitarianism. However, the immediacy of this influence is debated, as Hobbes, who served as Bacon's secretary, made no critical reference to Bacon or Baconian induction in his works.
