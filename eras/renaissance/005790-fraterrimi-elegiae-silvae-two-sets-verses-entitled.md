# Fraterrimi, Elegiae, and Silvae

The titles *Fraterrimi*, *Elegiae*, and *Silvae* appear within the archive's records in the context of classical and neo-classical poetry, specifically associated with the works of the Scottish scholar George Buchanan and the Roman poet Statius.

## The Works of George Buchanan

George Buchanan is recognized as one of the most significant scholars of Scotland, noted for a mastery of the Latin language that was seldom surpassed by any modern writer. His writing style is characterized by a certain elasticity and freshness, avoiding rigid modeling after any single classical author; it is noted that he wrote Latin as if it were his mother tongue.

Beyond his command of language, Buchanan possessed originality of thought and a rich vein of poetical feeling. His poetic output was diverse, including:

*   **Major Poetic Works:** Among his other poems are *Fratres Fraterrimi*, *Elegiae*, and *Silvae*.
*   **Verse Sets:** He produced two sets of verses titled *Iambon Liber* and *Hendecasyllabon Liber*.
*   **Other Poetic Contributions:** His work includes three books of *Epigrammata*, a book of miscellaneous verse, and translations of Greek plays and the Psalms. He also wrote two tragedies, *Jephthes* and *Baptistes*, which have maintained a European reputation for academic excellence.
*   **Scientific Poetry:** He authored *De Sphaera* in five books. This work was suggested by the poem of Joannes de Sacrobosco and served as a defense of the Ptolemaic theory against the Copernican view.

In addition to his poetry, Buchanan wrote several prose works, including *Libettus de Prosodia* (1640), *Vita ab ipso scripta biennio ante mortem* (1608), a Latin translation of Linacre's Grammar (1533), and *Chamaeleon*, a satire written in the vernacular against Maitland of Lethington.

## Silvae in Roman Poetry

The term *Silvae* also appears in the context of the Roman poet Statius. Specifically, the archive references Statius's *Silvae* (ii. 7), which contains a poem entitled *Genethliacon Lucani*. This poem was addressed to Polla Argentaria, the widow of the Roman poet Lucan. 

Lucan (Marcus Annaeus Lucanus, A.D. 39-65) was a poet of the Silver Age and the grandson of the rhetorician Seneca. After being recalled to Rome by Nero and later becoming an accomplice in the conspiracy of Piso, Lucan died by opening his veins. The poem in Statius's *Silvae* provides information regarding Lucan's career and his body of work.
