# England and the European Peasantry

## The Spanish Armada and English Defense
During the late 16th century, Philip II of Spain, who viewed himself as the absolute defender of Catholicism in Europe, sought to maintain his power and faith against the rise of international Protestantism. Queen Elizabeth of England resolutely supported the Protestant cause, assisting and directing attacks against Philip II. Between 1587 and 1592, Philip II sacrificed the resources of Spain to pursue the invasion of England and the conquest of the French throne. To achieve the invasion of England, he forced Alexander Farnese to suspend campaigns in Flanders to assist in transporting troops. These efforts failed utterly with the defeat of the Spanish Armada.

During the period of the Armada's approach, England utilized bonfires as a signal of alarm to warn the population of the fleet's arrival.

## European Peasant Customs and Bonfires
The practice of lighting bonfires has been observed by peasants throughout Europe from time immemorial, typically occurring on certain days of the year. These customs, which involve dancing around or leaping over the fires, can be traced back to the middle ages and likely originate in the early days of heathen Europe. 

Early Christian synods attempted to suppress these practices as pagan. For example, the third council of Constantinople in A.D. 680 ordered the cessation of fires kindled on new moons before houses and shops. Similarly, in A.D. 742, the Synodus Francica under Pope Zachary forbade "sacrilegious fires" known as *Nedfri* (or bonefires). These traditions are also reflected in Ovid's *Fasti*, which describes shepherds leaping over heaps of burning straw during the feast of Pales.

Later, the early Church compromised with these heathen traditions by incorporating bonfires into Christian festivals. In Cornwall, bonfires are lit on the eve of St. Peter's day and St John the Baptist. In Cornish, midsummer is called *Goluan*, meaning both "festivity" and "light." Some of these fires involved the burning of effigies or the pretense of burning living persons, and it is believed that human sacrifices were actually made in bonfires in ancient times.

## Social and Economic Conditions of the Peasantry
The experience of the European peasantry has been marked by periods of extreme misery and social instability. In the fifteenth century, a great pestilence caused significant social disorganization across Europe. This led to peasant risings against the wealthy and landlords in France and England. Following the Hussite Wars, such insurrections increased in gravity in Germany and took on a religious character.

The introduction of printing with movable type in the mid-fifteenth century—appearing in the Rhineland and Holland before spreading to Italy and England (where Caxton printed in Westminster in 1477)—further influenced these developments. The resulting increase in the distribution of Bibles and accessible information provided facilities for widespread popular controversies at a time when the church was divided and unable to defend itself effectively.

## Peasant Dress and Cultural Evolution
The clothing of the peasantry has historically been shaped by race, wealth, and climate. Unlike the rich, whose fashions change frequently, the garments of the poor have changed slowly over centuries. For instance, a Lincolnshire peasant wearing leathern gaiters and a smock frock might have appeared unchanged for centuries. Similarly, some Breton peasants in holiday dress resembled men of the late 17th century, and some Irish peasants retained the tailed coats, shoes, and breeches of the early 19th century.

However, these provincialisms have been swept away by the railway and the sewing machine. Ready-made clothing has replaced hand-stitched items, such as skirts with a year's stitching in the hem. In England, the distinction between classes in dress has blurred; for example, a labourer or journeyman painter may wear a frayed morning coat similar to the cut of a coat worn by a rich man for a London morning call.
