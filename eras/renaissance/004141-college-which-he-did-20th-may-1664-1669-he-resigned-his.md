# Academic and Professional Appointments in the 17th and 19th Centuries

The history of academic and professional appointments across Europe and North America reveals a pattern of shifting roles between mathematics, divinity, and classical studies, often intertwined with ecclesiastical and political service.

## Mathematical and Scientific Professorships

In the 17th century, several scholars held prominent positions in mathematics and physics. Giovanni Alfonso Borelli, an Italian physicist and physiologist born in 1608, served as a professor of mathematics at Messina in 1649 and later at Pisa in 1656. Borelli is recognized as the founder of the iatrophysical school, primarily through his work *De motu animalium*, which applied mechanical principles to the movements of the animal body.

In England, the academic landscape saw figures like Barrow, who was appointed to the Greek professorship at Cambridge in 1660. While he initially intended to lecture on the tragedies of Sophocles, he instead chose Aristotle's rhetoric. Barrow's career expanded in July 1662 when he was elected professor of geometry at Gresham College, following a recommendation from Dr. John Wilkins. In 1663, he was appointed as the first professor of a mathematical chair at Cambridge founded by Henry Lucas. Due to these overlapping roles, Barrow resigned his position at Gresham College on May 20, 1664. He eventually resigned his mathematical chair in 1669 to his pupil, Isaac Newton, as he decided to shift his studies from mathematics to divinity.

## Divinity and Ecclesiastical Appointments

The transition from scientific study to divinity was a noted path for some scholars. After leaving his mathematical professorship, Barrow became a fellow of Trinity College and received a prebend in Salisbury from Dr. Seth Ward, as well as a small sinecure in Wales from his uncle. By 1670, he was created a doctor in divinity. In 1672, by the king's patent dated February 13, he succeeded Dr. Pearson as master of Trinity College, and he later served as vice-chancellor of the university in 1675.

Other figures focused primarily on divinity and church governance. Burnet, who returned to England in 1664, served as a minister at Saltoun, East Lothian, from October 1664, though he was not formally inducted until June 1665. In 1669, Burnet resigned his parish to become a professor of divinity at the university of Glasgow. His tenure was marked by efforts to compromise between presbyterian and episcopal principles, though he eventually opposed the absolutism of Lauderdale.

Similarly, Henry King (1591–1669) followed an ecclesiastical path, becoming the bishop of Chichester in 1642. Despite being imprisoned by the Parliamentary army in December of that year, he was restored to his benefice during the Restoration.

## Classical and Oriental Studies

The study of languages and classical texts also occupied significant academic chairs. Edmund Castell, an English orientalist born in 1606, studied at Emmanuel College and St John's, Cambridge. He was appointed professor of Arabic at Cambridge and served as a prebendary of Canterbury. Castell is best known for his *Lexicon Heptaglotton*, a massive work involving seven languages that took eighteen years to compile.

In the 19th century, Thomas Hewitt Key (1799–1875) contributed to classical scholarship. After studying at Trinity and St John's Colleges, Cambridge, he served as professor of mathematics at the university of Virginia from 1825 to 1827. Upon returning to England in 1828, he was appointed professor of Latin at the university of London. In 1842, he transitioned to the professorship of comparative grammar and became the undivided headmaster of the university's school.

## Other Academic and Professional Roles

Beyond traditional professorships, various individuals held specialized roles in chemistry and public service. Johann Kunkel von Lowenstjern, a German chemist, managed the royal laboratory at Dresden for the elector of Saxony, Johann Georg II. After resigning in 1677, he lectured on chemistry at Wittenberg and Annaberg before becoming the director of the glass works and laboratory of Brandenburg in 1799.

In the United States, Jeremy Belknap (1744–1798) was educated at Harvard College, graduating in 1762. He later served as an overseer of Harvard in 1792 and founded the Massachusetts Historical Society.

In the 19th century, John Ball (1818–1889) combined academic training at the Roman Catholic College at Oscott and Christ's College, Cambridge, with a career in politics and natural science. While he served as a parliamentary candidate and under-secretary for the colonies, he used his influence to further natural science, particularly regarding colonial floras and the Palliser expedition in Canada.
