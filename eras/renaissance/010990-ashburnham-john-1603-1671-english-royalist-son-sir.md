# ASHBURNHAM, JOHN (c. 1603-1671)

John Ashburnham was an English Royalist and a trusted advisor to the monarchy during the mid-17th century. He was the son of Sir John Ashburnham of Ashburnham in Sussex.

## Early Career and Parliamentary Service
Ashburnham entered the service of the king early in his life. In 1627, he was dispatched to Paris by a relative, the duke of Buckingham, to make peace overtures. The following year, in 1628, he prepared to join an expedition to Rochelle, though this was interrupted by the assassination of the duke of Buckingham. 

In 1628, Ashburnham was appointed groom of the bedchamber and elected as a member of parliament for Hastings. He continued to represent this borough in the Long Parliament of 1640, during which time he provided services to the king by reporting on the proceedings of parliament. Through his activities, he acquired a considerable fortune and succeeded in recovering the Ashburnham estates that had been alienated by his father.

## Role in the Civil War
As one of the king's chief advisers, Ashburnham enjoyed the full confidence of Charles. He accompanied the king to York at the start of the war with Scotland. During the Civil War, he served as the treasurer of the royal army. In this role, he caused remonstrances and jealousy from Hyde, as Ashburnham infringed upon Hyde's province as chancellor of the exchequer. In 1644, he served as a commissioner at Uxbridge.

In April 1646, Ashburnham accompanied Charles in his flight from Oxford to the Scots. He subsequently escaped to the continent, where he joined the queen in Paris. He later resided at Rouen and was sent to the Hague to seek aid from the prince of Orange.

## Later Conflicts and Controversy
Following the army's seizure of Charles, Ashburnham joined the king at Hampton Court in 1647. While there, he participated in several conferences with other army officers and Cromwell. When Charles escaped from Hampton Court on November 11, Ashburnham provided advice that opposed that of Sir John Berkeley, who had urged the king to go abroad. Following Ashburnham's counsel, the king took refuge in the Isle of Wight, where Ashburnham placed him in the care of the governor, Robert Hammond.

Upon realizing the situation, the king exclaimed, "Oh, Jack, thou hast undone me!" Ashburnham responded with a great passion of weeping and offered to kill Hammond. This action led to Ashburnham being charged with disloyalty and treachery. While Clarendon censured his conduct, he absolved Ashburnham of any crime other than excessive self-confidence and folly. Ashburnham was eventually acquitted by both Charles I and Charles II.

## Descendants and Legacy
John Ashburnham had eight children. His eldest son, William, had a son named John (1656-1710), who was created Baron Ashburnham in 1689. The second son of that John, also named John (1687-1737), became the 3rd Baron Ashburnham in 1710 and was later created earl of Ashburnham and Viscount St Asaph in 1730. The 5th earl (born 1840) was a direct descendant of this line. Bertram, the 4th earl (1797-1878), was the collector of the Ashburnham library, which was dispersed in 1884 and 1883.

Several writings are associated with John Ashburnham's legacy. In 1648, *A Letter from Mr Ashburnham to a Friend* was published to defend his conduct regarding the king. He also wrote *A Letter to W. Lenthall* (1647), which repudiates the claim that the king violated his parole. His longer *Narrative* was published in 1830 by George, the 3rd earl of Ashburnham, although the earl's championship of his ancestor is described as unconvincing and entirely uncritical.
