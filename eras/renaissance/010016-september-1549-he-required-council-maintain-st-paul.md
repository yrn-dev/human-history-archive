# The Deprivation of Bishop Bonner (1549)

## Conflict with the Government of Edward VI
Following the accession of Edward VI, Bishop Bonner initially continued his career, having previously been an active agent in establishing royal supremacy. However, as a Protestant council began to utilize this supremacy, Bonner developed doubts regarding its application. He and Gardiner formulated a theory suggesting that royal supremacy was in abeyance during a royal minority. This position was neither constitutionally nor legally tenable, as both men had sought fresh licenses from the young king to exercise ecclesiastical jurisdiction.

Bonner's opposition to the government manifested in several stages. In August 1547, he resisted a visitation and was committed to the Fleet prison. Although he later withdrew this opposition and was released, he remained an active opponent of the government during the parliament of November 1547. Between November 1548 and March 1549, Bonner emerged as a leading opponent of the Book of Common Prayer and the first Act of Uniformity.

## The Events of September 1549
Once the Act of Uniformity and the Book of Common Prayer became law, Bonner neglected to enforce them. Consequently, on September 1, 1549, the council required Bonner to maintain a specific position at St Paul's Cross: that the royal authority was as great as if the king were forty years of age.

Bonner failed to comply with this requirement. This defiance led to a trial lasting seven days. Following the trial, an ecclesiastical court presided over by Cranmer deprived Bonner of his bishopric. He was subsequently sent to the Marshalsea.

## Appeals and Perpetual Imprisonment
The fall of Somerset in the month following Bonner's deprivation initially provided him with hope, leading him to appeal from Cranmer to the council. However, the Protestant faction eventually gained the upper hand in the struggle. On February 7, 1550, the council, sitting in the Star Chamber, confirmed Bonner's deprivation. Additionally, he was condemned to perpetual imprisonment.

## Related Ecclesiastical Context (1549)
The year 1549 was marked by broader religious activity and disputes within the English church. During the visitation of 1549, Edmund Grindal—who had been a proctor and Lady Margaret preacher in 1548-1549—was selected as one of the Protestant disputants, likely through the influence of Ridley. Grindal later became a chaplain to Ridley and was granted the precentorship of St Paul's.

Additionally, in May 1549, Hooper returned to England after publishing works in Zurich. Hooper became a champion of Swiss Protestantism and was appointed chaplain to Protector Somerset. Hooper had taken a prominent role against both Gardiner and Bonner, and he later faced his own conflicts with the Council and the legal ceremonies of consecration.
