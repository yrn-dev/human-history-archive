# Orux, Harrach, and Ordiche

The names "Orux," "Harrach," and "Ordiche" are the Spanish or Italian forms of the name Arouj, a 16th-century Turkish admiral and sea rover. Arouj was part of a family of sea rovers known to Christians by the name "Barbarossa" (Redbeard), a nickname likely derived from the Latin word for beard (*barba*) due to the family's red or tawny beards.

## Family Origins and Early Life
The Barbarossa family was founded by Yakub, a Roumeliot who was likely of Albanian blood. After the Turks conquered Mitylene, Yakub settled there, working as a skipper and coasting trader. He had four sons, all born after 1482: Elias, Isaak, Arouj, and Khizr. While Khizr became a potter and Isaak a trader, Elias and Arouj pursued lives as sea rovers.

During an encounter with a galley belonging to the Knights of Saint John, who were established at Rhodes at the time, Elias was killed. Arouj was taken prisoner but was eventually returned to the sea after being ransomed by a Turkish pasha.

## Career and Mercenary Activities
Arouj initially served the Mamelukes in Egypt. However, during the conflict between the Mamelukes and Sultan Selim I, Arouj moved to Tunis. In northern Africa, he found employment as a mercenary amidst the constant conflicts between Berber princes, alternating this work with piratical raids against Christian trade.

In Tunis, Arouj was joined by his brother Khizr, who adopted or was given the name Khair-ed-Din. Their brother Isaak eventually joined them as well. Arouj and Khair-ed-Din collaborated with the exiled Moors of Granada to conduct raids on the coast of Spain. They furthered their ambitions by supplanting or fighting native African princes, establishing their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes. In 1512, they attempted to capture Bougie from the Spaniards, though they were beaten off.

## The "Barbarossa" Designation
There has been historical debate regarding the origin of the name "Barbarossa." In 1840, Captain Walsin Esterhazy suggested that the term was a mispronunciation of "Baba Arouj." However, this theory is contested by the fact that contemporaries did not apply the prefix "Baba" to Arouj. Furthermore, a contemporary Arab chronicle published in 1837 explicitly states that "Barbarossa" was the name applied by Christians specifically to Khair-ed-Din.
