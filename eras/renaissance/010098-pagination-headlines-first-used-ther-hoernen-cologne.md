# Early Printing Innovations at Cologne

## Development of Pagination and Headlines
In the early history of printing, the transition from manuscript styles to printed books involved a gradual introduction of structural elements. While early printed copies often mirrored the appearance of formal book-hand manuscripts—where chapter headings, headlines, and initial letters were added by hand—certain printers began to implement these features through printing. 

Pagination and headlines were first used by Arnold ther Hoernen at Cologne in 1470 and 1471. Although printed leaf numeration appeared as early as 1470 and became common approximately ten years later, printed pagination did not become a common practice until nearly the middle of the 16th century.

## Other Printing Advancements in Cologne
Beyond pagination and headlines, other technical guides for book production were developed in Cologne. In 1472, Johann Koelhoff introduced printed signatures, which served as guides for binders to ensure that quires were arranged correctly.

Arnold ther Hoernen is also associated with the first known dated title-page. This title-page consisted of a nine-line paragraph on an otherwise blank page for the book *Sermo ad populum predicabilis in festo presentacionis Beatissime Marie Semper Virginis*. It included the date 1470 in roman numerals, words of praise for the work, and a reference to further information on the following page.

## Comparison with Other Printing Centers
The evolution of book components varied across different regions and time periods:

*   **Illustrations:** Rude woodcuts first appeared in small books produced by Albrecht Pfister at Bamberg around 1461. By the early 1470s, illustrations were abundant in books printed at Augsburg, and by the 1480s, they were common in France, Germany, and the Low Countries. In Italy, full development of illustrations occurred around 1490. While experiments with copper engraving were attempted in France and Italy during the 15th century, they were not successful.
*   **Title-Pages:** Following the 1470 example in Cologne, Erhard Ratdolt and partners in Venice printed a Latin calendar six years later that featured their names, the date, descriptive verses, and a four-piece border on the title-page. For the next twenty years, title-pages remained simple, often consisting only of a short title and occasionally a woodcut or a printer's/publisher's device.
*   **Colophons:** Before the standardization of title-pages, information regarding the producer, date, and location of a book was often reserved for a paragraph at the end of the volume known as a colophon. Printers frequently attached a device to the colophon, such as a fanciful design or the arms of the printer or the town.
