# Works Connected with the Instauratio but Not Included in Its Plan

The philosophical writings of Francis Bacon are categorized based on their relationship to his grand comprehensive scheme known as the *Instauratio*. While some of his works formed integral parts of the *Instauratio Magna* and others were originally intended for it but later superseded or discarded, a specific group of treatises consists of subjects connected with the *Instauratio* without forming a direct part of its plan.

## The New Atlantis
Among the works connected to but not included in the *Instauratio*, the most remarkable and interesting is the philosophic romance titled *New Atlantis*. This work provides a description of an ideal state. In this imagined society, the principles of the new philosophy are implemented through state guidance and political machinery, allowing Bacon to imagine the attainment of many results he contemplated. The work remained incomplete, as a planned second part concerning the laws of a model commonwealth was never written.

## Other Philosophical Tracts
Several other important tracts fall into this category of connected works:

*   ***De Principiis atque Originibus secundum Fabulas Cupidinis et Caeli***: In this unusually interesting tract, Bacon uses two old mythological stories as a disguise to uncover deep truths. He discusses the nature of primitive matter, the origin of things, and the limits of science. Additionally, the text provides full notices of the modern philosopher Telesio and the ancient philosopher Democritus.
*   ***Cogitationes de Natura Rerum***: This work was likely written early, possibly in 1605.
*   ***De Fluxu et Refluxu Maris***: A treatise focusing on the theory of the tides, written probably around 1616.

## Context of the Instauratio
To understand the position of these connected works, it is necessary to view them alongside the broader *Instauratio* project. The *Instauratio* was a reorganization of the sciences intended to restore man's command over nature, which had been lost by the fall. In its final form, it consisted of six divisions. The first division, *Partitiones Scientiarum* (represented by *De Augmentis Scientiarum*), served as an inventory of the human mind's possessions and a survey of the sciences. The second division, *Interpretatio Naturae* (represented imperfectly by the *Novum Organum*, specifically book ii), focused on the new method for training the human mind toward the renovation of science.

Bacon viewed the *Organum* and "Natural History" as correlative and equally necessary components of a true philosophy. He noted that even a completed *Organum* would not significantly advance the "Instauration of the Sciences" without Natural History, whereas Natural History alone would advance it "not a little."
