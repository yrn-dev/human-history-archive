# Basset Horn

The basset horn (known in French as *cor de basset* or *cor de bassette*, in German as *Bassethorn* or *Basshorn*, and in Italian as *corno di bassetto*) is a wood-wind instrument. Despite its name, it is not a "horn" but is instead the tenor member of the clarinet family.

## History and Development
The basset horn was developed during the 16th and 17th centuries due to a prevailing desire to create complete families of instruments for concert performance. Its archetypes are the same as those of the clarinet. The invention of the instrument in 1770 is attributed to a clarinet maker from Passau named Horn, from whom the instrument derived its name. The Italian and French names are considered misnomers.

The instrument underwent several modifications over the following decades. In 1782, Theodore Lotz of Pressburg introduced changes, which were later improved by two Viennese instrumentalists, Johann and Anton Stadler. Further development occurred in 1812 by the famous clarinettist Iwan Mueller. Mueller used the basset horn as a basis to invent the alto clarinet in Eb by applying a construction and fingering analogous to the clarinet in Bb, rather than the clarinet in C.

## Construction and Design
The basset horn consists of a nearly cylindrical tube made of wood, typically box-wood or cocus, featuring a cylindrical bore. It terminates in a metal bell that is wider than the bell found on a clarinet. 

The physical shape of the instrument has evolved for the convenience of the player in reaching the holes and keys:
* **Modern models:** These are usually curved or bent either at the bell (which is turned upwards) or near the mouthpiece.
* **Older models:** These featured a bend at an obtuse angle in the middle. Near the bell, at the bottom of the lower joint, these models contained a wooden block where the bore was reflexed and bent down upon itself.

## Technical Specifications and Range
The basset horn utilizes the same fingering as the clarinet. It is pitched a fifth below the clarinet in C. While the alto clarinet in Eb is sometimes used as a substitute—particularly in military bands—the basset horn differs in three specific ways:
1. It possesses a metal bell, whereas the alto clarinet has a contracted, pear-shaped bell.
2. Its bore is wider than that of the tenor clarinet in F or the alto clarinet in Eb.
3. Its tube is longer than that of the clarinet and includes four additional long keys operated by the right thumb. In a standard clarinet, the right thumb is used only to steady the instrument.

These additional keys extend the compass of the basset horn two tones downward to F (Notation: F2), whereas the F clarinet only extends to A (Notation: A2) and the Eb clarinet to G (Notation: G2). Consequently, the basset horn has a total range of four octaves, from actual sounds F2 to F6 (Notation: C3 to C7).

## Comparative Context
In the organization of wood-wind families, the basset horn bears the same relationship to the clarinet as the cor anglais (English horn) does to the oboe. While the cor anglais is the tenor of the oboe family, the basset horn serves as the tenor of the clarinet family.
