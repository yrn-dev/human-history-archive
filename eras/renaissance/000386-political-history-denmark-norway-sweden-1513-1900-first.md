# Political History of Denmark, Norway and Sweden, 1513-1900

The political history of the northern kingdoms during this period is characterized by efforts toward unification, the influence of absolute monarchy, and the shifting dynamics of Scandinavian power.

## The Ambitions of Christian II
Following the succession of Christian II (1513-1523), the son of Hans, there appeared to be a possibility that the dream of a unified Scandinavian empire could be realized. Elected as the king of Norway and Denmark, Christian II succeeded in subduing Sweden through military force. However, this triumph was undermined by the "Stockholm massacre," a crime and blunder that transformed the Swedish nation into an irreconcilable enemy of the unional government.

Christian II was a patriot who sought to create a powerful state independent of foreign influences, particularly the commercial tyranny of the Hansa League and German influence. While he was progressive and enlightened in his intentions—as seen in his *Landelove* (National Code)—his actions were often marked by revolting brutality and a double-mindedness that created general distrust. In Denmark, he aligned himself with the people against the aristocracy, though this contributed to his own undoing. His political approach to religion was opportunistic; he was Lutheran or Papist depending on the circumstances, and while he was frequently at war with the Curia and treated the Church as a foe, he maintained Catholic worship and did not question papal supremacy.

## The Reign of Christian IV
By 1588, Christian IV ascended the throne during a period of uncertainty. An energetic monarch, he guided the nation for half a century. His domestic policies focused on expansion and modernization, which included enlarging the harbours of Elsinore and Copenhagen. He abolished several decaying towns and established new ones, most notably founding Christiania in August 1624 on the ruins of Oslo.

To improve industry and trade, Christian IV promoted the immigration of well-to-do and skilful Dutch handicraftsmen and traders. He also worked to abolish the privileges of Hanseatic towns, opened the Arctic seas for fisheries, and established joint-stock chartered companies in the West and East Indies. Copenhagen was specifically embellished and fortified to serve as the capital of a great empire.

In foreign policy, Denmark's position was distinct from Sweden's, as it remained outside the major religious-political movements resulting from the Catholic reaction. Denmark utilized its possession of the Sound to close the Baltic to Western powers and maintained monopolies over rich fisheries in Norway, which caused irritation for Holland and England.

## Absolute Monarchy and the Enevaelde
The introduction of the *Enevaelde*, or absolute monarchy, had a significant impact on the Danish state by increasing the reserve of native talent. This system opened state appointments to the middle classes, who came to monopolize the energy and intellect of the nation. 

Under the administration of Chancellor Griffenfeldt, Denmark attempted to regain its status as a leading international power. However, these efforts were complicated by the instability of Europe and the ambitions of Louis XIV of France. In April 1672, France and Sweden concluded a treaty stating that France would not include Denmark in its alliances without Sweden's consent, indicating that Sweden held more weight in French diplomatic balances than Denmark did. During this time, Denmark was invited by the elector of Brandenburg to join an alliance with Emperor Leopold, while simultaneously negotiating a renewal of the Dano-Dutch alliance.

## Ecclesiastical Structure
The state churches of Norway, Sweden, and Denmark are Lutheran and have retained the episcopate. In these nations, the king is recognized as the *summus episcopus*, the supreme authority in ecclesiastical matters, although in Sweden and Norway, this power is somewhat limited by parliament. The king's authority is exercised through a minister overseeing education and religion.

The role of bishops varies by country:
* **Denmark:** Bishops serve as inspectors of schools and the clergy.
* **Sweden:** Bishops preside over local consistories consisting of lay and clerical members.
In all three countries, the rite of ordination remains in the hands of the bishops.
