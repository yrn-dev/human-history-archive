# The Popularization of the Small Octavo by Aldus

The transition of book production in the 16th century was significantly influenced by the innovations of Aldus Manutius, a great Venetian printer. In 1501, Aldus popularized the small octavo, a format that created "handy books" with far-reaching consequences for the industry. This development was accompanied by the introduction of a new type known as italic, which was likely cut from the handwriting of Aldus's friend, Bembo.

## Evolution of Typography and Book Size
The italic type grew steadily in popularity throughout much of the 16th century. By approximately 1570, it had become nearly the standard vernacular type in Italy. It also found great popularity in France, where it successfully resisted the introduction of a rival French cursive type called *lettres de civilite*. In England, italics gained only slight popularity; however, roman type—which had not been utilized at all during the 15th century—made steady progress against black letter. By the end of the 16th century, black letter was used primarily for proclamations and Bibles. Additionally, the modern usage of the letters u and v, and i and j, originated around 1580, though these practices were not firmly established until the reign of Charles I.

Following the success of the Aldine octavos, French printers in Lyons and Paris halved the size of these volumes in the second quarter of the 16th century, creating small sextodecimos. While these found a ready market, they were eventually ousted by printers in Leiden and Antwerp, who produced even smaller books in small twelves or 24mo. These smaller formats utilized paper that was much thinner than previous standards.

## Binding and Material Changes
The physical construction of books shifted during this period to reduce size and weight, notably through the substitution of wooden sides with pasteboards. Gold tooling became a feature of bindings, and France saw a trend of very elaborate decoration in the second half of the century, which continued until it was checked by a sumptuary law.

In England, fine printed books of the 16th century and late 15th century were sometimes bound in satin and velvet, occasionally embroidered or set with enamels. Some of these works, including those made for Henry VIII, have lasted well due to the free use of strong metal threads. During the Tudor period, small bindings of gold ornamented with enamels were produced; these often featured a ring at the top for attachment to a girdle. 

Aldus Manutius utilized a distinct binding style for several of his books, using dark morocco with small dolphins and "Aldine" knot leaves in gold and blind tooling. A similar style was used by the Florentine printer Giunta, though without the dolphins. Some early Venetian bindings also featured recessed panels created by using double boards with a pierced upper board.

## Printing Challenges and Piracy
The rise of the octavo and other popular formats led to issues with piracy and spurious imprints. The octavo editions of the classics printed by Aldus at Venice were subject to glaring counterfeits produced in Lyons. Similarly, unauthorized editions of Luther's works claimed to be printed at Wittenberg. 

Some printers used fictitious imprints to evade legal privileges. For example, in 1499, a printer in Brescia used the spurious imprint "Florentiae" for an edition of Politian to evade a privilege granted to Aldus. In England, controversial books of the 16th century often purported to be issued in German towns or, humorously, "at Rome before the castle of S. Angel at the sign of S. Peter."

## Illustration and Production Costs
The 16th century saw a shift in book illustration. While early Italian art produced fine outline illustrations—such as those in the *Poliphili Hypneratomachia* printed by Aldus at the end of the 15th century—the later part of the century saw a decline in paper quality. This decline, combined with a move toward more ambitious designs, made it difficult for printers to execute woodcuts effectively. Consequently, copper-plates printed separately from the text gradually superseded woodcuts, starting in the Low Countries and later spreading to England.

The cost of printed materials in England varied widely during the century. A Christmas carol or ballad sold for a halfpenny, and thin quarto chapbooks were priced at 4d. More substantial works were more expensive: the Great Bible of 1541 cost 10s. in sheets and 12s. bound, while Edward VI's prayer-book (1549) was 2s. 2d. unbound and 3s. 8d. in boards or paste. By 1598, works such as Sidney's *Arcadia* sold for 9s.
