# Philip of Hesse and the German Religious Wars

Philip of Hesse, also known as Philip the Magnanimous or Philip the Generous, was a prominent landgrave of Hesse and a central figure in the political and religious upheavals of 16th-century Germany. He is noted for his efforts to unify Protestant forces, his contributions to education, and his role as a primary antagonist to the Holy Roman Emperor.

## Efforts Toward Protestant Unity
Following the Imperial Diet of Spires in 1526, which decreed that states observe the Edict of Worms banning Martin Luther and his followers, the reform movement grew in strength. By 1529, the Catholic party attempted to prevent the Reformation from maintaining its existing ground. This led to a protest by the Evangelical states, giving rise to the term "Protestants."

Landgrave Philip recognized that the Evangelical cause lacked political significance without the united force of all subscribing princes and states. He became the most powerful and zealous champion of peace, viewing the union of all German Protestants as an absolute political necessity. To achieve this, Philip sought to resolve the intense feud regarding the doctrine of the Lord's Supper. 

Philip's efforts toward conciliation included an invitation to Martin Luther for a religious conference as early as 1527, though Luther refused. Later, on April 22, 1529, Philip invited Zwingli to a colloquy. This resulted in a famous conference in 1529 held in the Rittersaal of the Schloss in Marburg, where Luther and Zwingli debated the subject of Transubstantiation.

## Contributions to Hesse and Education
Philip the Magnanimous left a significant mark on the infrastructure and intellectual life of his territories. In 1527, he founded the university of Marburg. This institution was the first university established without papal privileges and quickly gained a high reputation throughout Protestant Europe.

In the city of Cassel, Philip, along with his son William IV, worked during the 16th century to restore the town's fortifications. His influence extended to the religious landscape of Marburg; driven by Protestant zeal, Philip removed the body of St. Elizabeth of Hungary from its silver-gilt sarcophagus in the Elisabethenkirche to an unknown location within the church.

## The German Religious War and Imprisonment
The German religious war broke out in 1546, shortly after the death of Martin Luther. During this conflict, the Protestant Saxon army suffered a severe defeat at Lochau. Philip of Hesse, who remained the chief antagonist to the Emperor, was caught and imprisoned in what is described as a breach of faith.

Following these events, Emperor Charles V attempted to establish a settlement and peace by 1547. However, stability remained elusive. By 1552, Germany was again at war, leading to the treaty of Passau and a subsequent unstable equilibrium. Eventually, Charles V abdicated, resigning his sovereign rights in Germany to his brother Ferdinand and his rights in Spain and the Netherlands to his son Philip.

## Legacy and Commemoration
The legacy of Philip the Magnanimous is preserved through various honors and historical sites in Hesse. The grand duke Louis II founded the Order of Philip the Magnanimous in 1840; the order's badge features an effigy of Philip surrounded by the motto *Si Deus vobiscum quis contra nos*. 

Historically, the landgraves of Hesse utilized the Schloss in Marburg as a residence, a building that now houses the archives of Hesse. The city of Cassel, which became a principal residence for the house of the landgraves in the 15th century, continues to reflect the architectural and political development of the region Philip once led.
