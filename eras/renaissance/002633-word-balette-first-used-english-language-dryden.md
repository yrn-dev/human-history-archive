# John Dryden and the English Language

John Dryden was a significant figure in the development of English literature and language, contributing to various forms of prose and poetry while introducing specific terminology into the English lexicon.

## Linguistic Contributions and Terminology
Dryden is credited with the first use of several words in the English language. In 1667, he first used the word "balette." Additionally, no earlier employment of the word "biography" has been traced in English than Dryden's use of it in 1683. In that context, he used the term to describe the literary work of Plutarch, defining it as "the history of particular men's lives."

## Prose Style and Diction
Dryden's prose is noted for its balance and harmony between native and learned constituents. He exhibited a sensitive tact in "Gallicizing" and maintained a balance in his sentence structure, avoiding both excessive curtness and ungainly lengthiness. His style varied according to the purpose of the writing: he employed a "rolling period" for compliment and ceremony, while using a "short sharp stroke without the gloves" for invective. He is described as an absolute master of rhythm who achieved a sense of delusive ease. His *Preface* to his *Fables* (1700) is cited as an example of admirable and lasting prose.

## Literary Works and Genres
Dryden worked across multiple literary forms, including epigrams, tragedies, and translations.

### Epigrams and Poetry
Dryden is listed among the writers who were successful in their epigrammatical attempts, though he is not as closely identified with the spirit of the epigram as Alexander Pope.

### Drama and Tragedy
In the realm of tragedy, Dryden brought the rehandling of Shakespeare in blank verse and the rhymed heroic play to their utmost. His heroic tragedies, such as *Tyrannic Love* and *The Conquest of Granada* (1669-1670), were hybrid works influenced by French tragedy and heroic romance. Dryden also "revived" Shakespeare by vamping his works in a specific style; he handled *Troilus and Cressida* and *The Tempest*, and converted *Antony and Cleopatra* into *All for Love* (1678). The latter is regarded as loftier and stronger than any of his original plays.

### Translations
Dryden was involved in the publication of "Plutarch's Lives from the Greek by several hands," published in London between 1683 and 1686. This work was dedicated by Dryden to James Butler, the first Duke of Ormond, in a panegyric described as fulsome. While it is said that forty-one translators worked on the project, Dryden did not translate any of the Lives himself; instead, he wrote the *Life of Plutarch* which serves as a prefix to the translation.
