# BOOK-PLATES

The book-plate, also known as the *ex-libris*, is a printed label used to indicate the ownership of individual volumes. This practice is nearly as old as the printed book itself, serving a similar purpose to the symbolical personal devices or hand-painted armorials found in medieval manuscripts.

## Origins and Early Development
The earliest known examples of book-plates originated in Germany. According to the authority Friedrich Warnecke of Berlin, the oldest movable *ex-libris* are hand-painted woodcuts depicting a shield of arms supported by an angel. These were pasted into books gifted to the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim by Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach around the year 1480.

The practice spread to other nations at different intervals. In France, the oldest discovered *ex-libris* belongs to Jean Bertaud de la Tour-Blanche, dated 1529. In England, the earliest example is a gift-plate from Sir Nicholas Bacon for books presented to the university of Cambridge. Other early examples include a 1597 plate from Holland belonging to Anna van der Aa, an Italian plate attributed to 1622, and the first known American example, a plain printed label for John Williams in 1679.

In France, the movable *ex-libris* did not become common until the 17th century, as book-owners previously preferred the more luxurious method of stamping personal devices directly onto covers. The term *ex-libris*, used as a substantive, originated in France and eventually became the recognized term for book-plates across the continent.

## German Influence and Artistry
Germany is central to the history of the book-plate, as the fashion began there and produced works of high artistic interest long before spreading elsewhere. Between 1503 and 1516, Albrecht Durer engraved at least six plates and provided designs for many others; a notable example is the 1515 plate of Lazarus Spengler. Other significant artists associated with German plates include Lucas Cranach, Hans Holbein, and the "Little Masters," such as the Behams, Jost Amman, Virgil Solis, Matthias Zundt, Georg Hupschmann, and Saldorfer. While the heavy, over-elaborated German style did not affect neighboring countries, these draughtsmen influenced German decorative styles for centuries.

## English Styles and Evolution
English book-plates reflect the prevailing decorative tastes of their respective epochs. Early English examples, such as that of Sir Thomas Tresham (1585), were remarkably simple and plainly armorial, featuring symmetrical mantling and occasional wreaths or palms.

Following the Restoration, book-plates became a standard accessory for well-ordered libraries. This period introduced two distinct characteristics: the use of tincture lines and dots (a method devised by Petra Sancta in *Tesserae Gentilitiae*) and more elaborate mantling that resembled the periwigs of the era. From this point until the French Revolution, English decoration generally followed French tastes with a delay of several years.

## Classification of Styles and Composition
The general classification of styles was established by Lord de Tabley (the Hon. Leicester Warren) in his 1880 work, *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates*. These styles include:
*   **Early Armorial:** Previous to the Restoration (e.g., the Nicholas Bacon plate).
*   **Jacobean:** A term including the heavy decorative manner of the early Georgian, Queen Anne, and Restoration days (e.g., the 1698 plate of Francis Gwyn of Lansanor).
*   **Chippendale:** Also described as *rococo* (e.g., the 1762 French plate of Convers).
*   **Wreath and Ribbon:** Associated with the period of the urn.

Beyond these, various pictorial compositions were prevalent. These include "library interiors" and "book-piles" (exemplified by the 1699 plate of William Hewer), portrait-plates (such as those of John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys), landscapes by the Bewick school, and allegories engraved by George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth.

## Modern Developments and Collecting
The taste for collecting and studying book-plates as objects of interest began around 1875, gaining momentum with Lord de Tabley's 1880 guide. This led to the formation of collector societies in England, Germany, France, and the United States, which publish journals such as *The Journal of the Ex-libris Society* (London), the *Ex-libris Zeitschrift* (Berlin), and the *Archives de la societe francaise de collectionneurs d'ex-libris* (Paris).

In recent years, the design of book-plates has evolved from the routine work of heraldic stationers into a recognized minor branch of higher art. Modern designs tend to emphasize symbolic or allegorical elements over purely heraldic ones. Notable modern English artists include Charles Ricketts, Robert Anning Bell, G.W. Eve, C.W. Sherborn, and others. In America, recognized engravers include E.D. French, K.W.F. Hopson, and J.W. Spenceley. While most modern plates are reproduced via process work to make them more affordable and popular, a few artists continue to use the graver.
