# Carthusian Rules of Fasting and Dress

The Carthusian order is a religious institute characterized by a life of isolation and solitude, designed to resemble the practices of early Egyptian monks. The order was formally constituted by a papal act in 1170. Its customs were first reduced to writing in 1130 by Guigo, the fifth prior of the Grande Chartreuse, with subsequent modifications made in 1259, 1367, 1509, and 1681.

## Dietary Regulations and Fasting
To mortify the flesh and combat the "artifices of the Evil One," the Carthusians adopted a regime of nearly continuous fasting. Their dietary laws are strict and based on the day of the week:

*   **Mondays, Wednesdays, and Fridays:** Monks consume only bread and water.
*   **Tuesdays and Saturdays:** The diet consists of cooked vegetables.
*   **Sundays and Thursdays:** Cheese and eggs are permitted.

The order maintains a permanent prohibition on the consumption of meat, regardless of whether a monk is in good health or ill. While they do not purchase fish, they will accept it if it is provided through charity. Their bread is made of unbolted meal, and they dilute their wine with so much water that the flavor of the wine is hardly remaining.

Under normal circumstances, the monks eat only once per day. However, this rule is relaxed during the octaves of Epiphany, Pentecost, Easter, and Christmas, as well as during other solemnities. On feast days, they eat twice and take their meals in the refectory.

## Dress and Penance
The Carthusians chose a dress intended to be "more poor and contemptible than that of any other religious body" as a remedy against vain-glory and pride. Historically, these garments were described as coarse, dirty, short, and scanty. In the present day, the habit has evolved into an ordinary religious habit made of white serge; the habit for both nuns and monks remains white.

As a specific method of mortifying the flesh, the monks always wear hair shirts of the severest kind.

## Monastic Life and Structure
The Carthusian conception of monasticism emphasizes a life wholly cut off from the outer world. Monks live in separate small houses, spending their time in prayer, reading, and manual labor, specifically the writing of books. While they assemble in the church for vespers and matins, they recite the prayers for minor canonical hours in their own dwellings upon hearing the church bell. Mass is only said on Sundays and festivals.

To prevent avarice, the order limits its possessions. Each cell is enclosed by a specific quantity of land, and the order refuses to accept additional land regardless of the offer. They similarly limit the number of animals they keep, such as goats, sheep, asses, and oxen. To further discourage the augmentation of possessions, the number of inhabitants in each monastery is restricted to twelve monks, one prior (the thirteenth), eighteen lay brothers, and a few paid servants.

## Administration and Expansion
The order is governed by priors rather than abbots. The superior general of the order is the prior of the Grande Chartreuse, where a general chapter of priors is held annually. 

The order has primarily flourished in France, though it established houses across western Europe, including renowned *Certose* in Naples, Florence, and Pavia. The number of Charterhouses grew from approximately 50 in the middle of the 13th century to 170 by the beginning of the 18th century, with 75 of those located in France. Since the middle of the 13th century, a small number of convents for Carthusian nuns have existed; while there were no more than ten historically, only two or three have existed in recent times. The rules for nuns are similar to those of the monks, though the requirements for silence, solitude, and isolation are less stringent.
