# James I.

The reign of James I. was marked by various administrative actions, the granting of royal charters to boroughs, and policies that impacted both domestic industry and the legal rights of the English population.

## Impact on Trade and Industry
In Buckinghamshire, a county historically noted for its agriculture, corn, and cattle, the lace-making industry was established by Fleming refugees fleeing the Alva persecution. While this industry became highly profitable—with the parish of Hanslape reportedly earning an annual profit of £8,000 to £9,000—it was significantly harmed by the monopolies of James I. The resulting decay of bone lace-making led to a petition being addressed to the high sheriff of Buckinghamshire in 1623 to represent the distress of the local people.

## Royal Charters and Municipal Governance
James I. issued several charters to various English towns, altering their governance and legal status:

*   **Barnstaple:** Through a charter dated 1610, James I. increased the number of chief burgesses to twenty-five. He also instituted a recorder, justices of the peace, a clerk of the market, and other officers. This charter was subsequently confirmed in 1611 and 1689.
*   **Lyme Regis:** Following the incorporation of the town by Elizabeth in 1591, James I. provided further charters to the borough.
*   **Ashford:** In 1607, acting upon a petition from the inhabitants of Ashford, James I. granted Sir John Smith, Kt., the right to hold a court of record in the town every third Tuesday.

## Peerage and Political Appointments
On May 4, 1605, James I. created Thomas Arundell of Wardour, the 1st Baron Arundell of Wardour, as a baron. Thomas Arundell had previously served with imperial troops against the Turks in Hungary and had been created a count of the Holy Roman Empire by Rudolph II. Despite this elevation, Arundell later fell under temporary suspicion during the events of the Gunpowder Plot.

## Billeting and Civil Grievances
The reign of James I. was characterized by repeated petitions against the practice of billeting, which involved providing board and lodgings for soldiers. Under this system, a civil magistrate acted under the king's orders to superintend the process; however, these magistrates frequently spared their personal or political friends at the expense of their opponents. These grievances, which also occurred during the reigns of Elizabeth and Charles I., eventually culminated in the Petition of Right in 1628, which declared the practice illegal.
