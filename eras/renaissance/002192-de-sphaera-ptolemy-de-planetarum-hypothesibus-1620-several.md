# De Sphaera and De Planetarum Hypothesibus

The texts *De Sphaera* and *De Planetarum Hypothesibus* are astronomical works that were translated into English in the early 17th century by the English astronomer John Bainbridge.

## John Bainbridge
John Bainbridge (1582–1643) was born in Ashby-de-la-Zouch, Leicestershire. His professional background was diverse; he practiced as a physician for several years, operated a school, and studied astronomy. In November 1618, he was admitted as a licentiate of the college of physicians. He gained recognition following a publication regarding the comet of 1618, which led Sir Henry Savile to appoint him in 1619 to the Savilian chair of astronomy at Oxford, a position recently founded by Savile.

During his tenure at Oxford, Bainbridge was incorporated into Merton College. He later served as the junior reader of Linacre's lectures in 1631 and the senior reader in 1635. In addition to his translations, Bainbridge authored *An Astronomical Description of the late Comet* (1619) and *Canicularia* (1648). Several of his manuscript works are preserved in the library of Trinity College, Dublin. He died at Oxford on November 3, 1643.

## Translations and Astronomical Context
In 1620, Bainbridge produced translations of two significant works: Proclus' *De Sphaera* and Ptolemy's *De Planetarum Hypothesibus*. 

The work by Ptolemy belongs to a tradition of astronomy that was heavily influenced by the geocentric theory of the universe. This theory was established by Hipparchus, who elaborated the system by introducing "eccentrics" to explain changes in the orbital velocity of the moon and sun. This was achieved by displacing the earth from the center of the circles these bodies were assumed to describe, resulting in the "equation of the centre." Ptolemy's work at Alexandria, which occurred approximately 250 years after Hipparchus, utilized the observations of his predecessor, including a catalogue of 1,080 stars divided into six magnitudes of brightness. This catalogue was substantially incorporated into Ptolemy's *Almagest*.

## Related Spherical and Planetary Studies
The study of the "sphaera" (sphere) appears in various historical and technical contexts within the archive's records:

*   **Educational Apparatus:** Celestial globes were used in monastic schools during the time of Bede. Gerbert of Aurillac created such globes in 929, and their manufacture was later described by Alphonso the Wise in 1252 and by G. Campanus of Novara in his 1303 work, *De sphaera solida*.
*   **Ancient Athletics:** The term *sphaerae* also refers to a type of formidable boxing-glove used by ancient pugilists. These were sewn with leather-covered small metal balls.
*   **Arabian Astronomy:** The works of Ptolemy were translated into Arabic in Bagdad, a center of learning. Arabian astronomers such as Al Batani, Ibn Yunis of Cairo, Zarkala, and Abul Hassan further refined astronomical measurements. For example, Zarkala determined the meridian distance between Bagdad and his observatory in Toledo to be 51° 30´, which was significantly more accurate than Ptolemy's error of 18°.
