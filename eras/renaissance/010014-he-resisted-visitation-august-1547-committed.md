# The Conflict of Bishop Bonner and the Royal Supremacy

## Conflict with the Government of Edward VI
The accession of Edward VI marked a new phase in the career of Bonner. Having previously been an active agent in the establishment of royal supremacy, Bonner found himself unable to repudiate it. However, he began to harbor doubts regarding that supremacy upon observing how it was utilized by a Protestant council. 

In response, Bonner—or possibly Gardiner—developed a theory suggesting that royal supremacy was in abeyance during the minority of a king. This position was neither constitutionally nor legally tenable. Both Bonner and Gardiner had sought fresh licenses from the young king to exercise their ecclesiastical jurisdiction; logically, if the king possessed the authority to confer such jurisdiction, he also possessed the authority to issue injunctions and order visitations. Furthermore, if a royal minority created an abeyance of supremacy in ecclesiastical matters, the same would apply to the temporal sphere, which would result in anarchy.

## Resistance and Imprisonment
This disagreement over the nature of royal authority led to a direct conflict between Bonner and the government of Edward VI. In August 1547, Bonner resisted a visitation, an act of defiance that resulted in him being committed to the Fleet. He eventually withdrew his opposition and was released, which allowed him to participate actively against the government during the parliament of November 1547.

## Opposition to the Act of Uniformity
During the parliamentary session spanning November 1548 to March 1549, Bonner emerged as a leading opponent of the Book of Common Prayer and the first Act of Uniformity. Once these measures were enacted into law, Bonner neglected to enforce them. 

Consequently, on September 1, 1549, the council required Bonner to maintain at St Paul's Cross that the royal authority remained as great as it would be if the king were forty years of age. Bonner failed to comply with this requirement. Following a trial that lasted seven days, an ecclesiastical court presided over by Cranmer deprived Bonner of his bishopric and sent him to the Marshalsea.

## Final Deprivation and Sentence
The subsequent fall of Somerset provided Bonner with a glimmer of hope, prompting him to appeal from Cranmer to the council. However, the Protestant faction ultimately prevailed. On February 7, 1550, the council, sitting in the Star Chamber, confirmed Bonner's deprivation. Additionally, he was condemned to perpetual imprisonment.
