# Portuguese Overseas Expansion and Presence in Asia and Africa

## Early Voyages and the Route to India
The Portuguese were among the first Europeans to push overseas enterprises southward and eastward. In 1497, Vasco da Gama sailed from Lisbon, rounding the Cape and visiting Zanzibar before crossing the Indian Ocean to reach Calicut in India. This voyage spurred further Portuguese attempts to utilize the South African route to reach India. By 1498, the Portuguese had arrived at Calicut, and their earliest possession in that region was the island of Anjidiv.

The Portuguese successfully won the Indian sea trade from the Red Sea Arabs through a series of sea fights, aided by ships that were larger and more heavily armed. For a period, Lisbon surpassed Venice as the primary mart for oriental spices.

## Establishments in the Indian Ocean and Asia
Following their arrival in India, the Portuguese established and fortified trading stations along the coasts of the Indian Ocean. In 1510, they captured Goa, followed by the capture of Malacca in 1511 and Ormuz in 1515. By 1515, Portuguese ships had also reached Java and the Moluccas. 

Taking advantage of the decay of the kingdom of Gujarat, the Portuguese expanded their holdings to include Chaul (1531), Diu (1535), Daman (1559), and Bassein with its dependencies, which included Bombay (1534). Other long-term Portuguese possessions included Macao in China and a part of Timor.

In the Pacific, the Portuguese sailor Magellan, while in the employment of the Spanish King in 1519, coasted south of South America and passed through the "Strait of Magellan" to enter the Pacific Ocean. In East Asia, the Portuguese reached Japan in 1542 via a Chinese junk. In 1549, the Jesuit missionary Francis Xavier began teaching in Japan. While Japan initially welcomed this intercourse and many converts to Christianity were made, conflicts eventually arose between Portuguese Jesuits, Spanish Dominicans, and Protestant English and Dutch. These tensions, combined with feudal conflicts, led the Japanese to view Europeans as a nuisance. Consequently, a persecution of Christians occurred, and Japan was absolutely closed to Europeans in 1638.

## African Possessions
The Portuguese navigator Pedro Alvares Cabral was the first European to visit Kilwa, anchoring there in 1500. In 1502, Kilwa submitted to Vasco da Gama. However, after the sultan failed to pay an imposed tribute, the Portuguese occupied the city in 1505 and built a fort, which was the first they erected on the east coast of Africa. Despite fighting and a period of abandonment in 1512 due to fever, the Portuguese presence in the region was established. Mozambique also became a Portuguese possession.

## Cultural and Linguistic Influence in India
The Portuguese were the earliest students of the Marathi language, though they were only familiar with Konkani and the standard dialect of the northern Konkan spoken on the coast. Under Roman Catholic auspices, a new literature emerged in Goa. An Englishman named Thomas Stephens, who arrived in Goa in 1579, wrote the first Konkani grammar and a metrical paraphrase of the New Testament. This period saw a growth of Christian literature authored by native converts and Portuguese missionaries. However, it is noted that the Inquisition at Goa destroyed earlier Konkani literature, including the burning of manuscripts and temples.

## Decline and Competition
The Portuguese monopoly on Indian trade was challenged in the seventeenth century by the Dutch, who established numerous fortified stations across the East Indies, Ceylon, Persia, and India. Simultaneously, the rise of English trade and factories in the Indian seas led to constant broils. 

The Portuguese possession of Hormuz had long given them a monopoly on trade with Persia, which embittered English rivals. In 1622, English vessels joined with the governor of Fars (the "duke of Shiraz") to attack the Portuguese. After a ten-week defense, the Portuguese surrendered Hormuz on May 1, 1622. The archive notes that the power of the Portuguese was further undermined by the "inherent vices of their intolerant system" even before the arrival of their Dutch and English rivals.

## Global Territorial Divisions
In 1494, the Pope divided the new continent of America between Spain and Portugal. Portugal was granted Brazil and all territories east of a line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands, while Spain was granted the remainder.
