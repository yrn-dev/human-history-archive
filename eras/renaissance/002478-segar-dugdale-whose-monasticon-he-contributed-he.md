# Sir William Dugdale

Sir William Dugdale (1605–1686) was a prominent English antiquary. Born on September 12, 1605, at Shustoke, near Coleshill in Warwickshire, he was the son of a country gentleman of old Lancashire stock and received his education at Coventry.

## Personal Life and Early Career
To please his father, who was infirm and old, Dugdale married at the age of seventeen. He resided with the family of his wife until his father's death in 1624, at which point he moved to Fillongley, near Shustoke, to live on an estate his father had purchased for him. In 1625, he purchased the manor of Blythe, Shustoke, and relocated there in 1626.

Dugdale developed an early interest in antiquarian studies. In 1635, he met Sir Symon Archer, a learned antiquary who was gathering materials for a history of Warwickshire, and accompanied him to London. While in the city, he became acquainted with Thomas, the earl marshal of England and earl of Arundel, as well as Sir Christopher Hatton, the comptroller of the household.

## Heraldic and Public Service
Dugdale entered the field of arms in 1638 when he was created a pursuivant of arms extraordinary under the name of Blanch Lyon. In 1639, he became a rouge croix pursuivant in ordinary. This position provided him with lodging in the Heralds' Office, allowing him to spend significant time examining the Cottonian and Tower records, among other manuscript collections.

In 1641, Sir Christopher Hatton commissioned Dugdale to create exact drafts of monuments in the principal churches of England, including those in Norwich, Lincoln, Ely, Peterborough, York, Selby, Chester, Lichfield, Tamworth, Warwick, Southwell, Beverley, Newark, and Westminster Abbey, as Hatton feared the coming war would lead to the spoliation of the Church.

During the English Civil War, Dugdale was summoned to attend the king at York in June 1642. King Charles deputed him to summon the surrender of strongholds, including the castles of Warwick and Banbury. Dugdale accompanied Charles to Oxford, where he remained until its surrender in 1646. He also witnessed the battle of Edgehill, later conducting an exact survey of the field to mark the graves of the slain and note the movements and positions of the armies.

## Major Works and Contributions
Dugdale is described as the father of genealogical science. His most significant published works include:
*   *Monasticon Anglicanum* (1655–1673), a three-volume work used as a source for the history of foundations such as Bolton Priory.
*   *Antiquities of Warwickshire* (1656).
*   *History of St Paul's Cathedral* (1658).
*   *Baronage of England* (1675–1676).

In his *Baronage*, Dugdale refers to his own *Monasticon* and provides details on families such as the de Ripuariis (also known as de Redvers or de Rivers), noting that the family ended in the time of Edward the Fifth with Isabella de Fortibus.

Dugdale also contributed to the study of knighthood. He is cited as a leading authority who translated or provided an ancient French tract regarding the ceremonies of creating knights. This work, alongside contributions by Segar, Nicolas, and Byshe, details the "order and manner of creating Knights of the Bath in time of peace according to the custom of England."

## Professional Associations
Dugdale maintained professional connections with other antiquaries and annalists. He was well acquainted with Sir William Segar and Sir James Balfour. Balfour, a Scottish annalist and baronet of Kinnaird, contributed to Dugdale's *Monasticon*.

Dugdale's own life was documented in a *Life* written by himself up to 1678, which included his correspondence, diary, and an index to his manuscript collections; this was edited by William Hamper and published in 1827.
