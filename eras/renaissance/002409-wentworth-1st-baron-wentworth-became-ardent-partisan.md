# Thomas Wentworth, 1st Baron Wentworth

## Influence on the Reformation
Thomas Wentworth, 1st Baron Wentworth, was a figure of influence at Cambridge during the early 16th century. While at the university, he, along with Cranmer, influenced John Bale (1495–1563). Bale, who had previously been a member of the Carmelite monastery at Norwich and the house of "Holme," became an ardent partisan of the Reformers under the influence of Wentworth and Cranmer. This shift led Bale to abandon his monastic habit and marry Dorothy.

## The Barony of Wentworth and Successions
The barony of Wentworth has a complex history of succession involving various heirs and titles. In one line of descent, the title passed to a first cousin as the 7th baron. In another instance, the barony passed to the heirs of Ada (d. 1852), the daughter of a poet. Ada was the first wife of Baron King, who was created the 1st earl of Lovelace in 1838. 

Baron King and Ada had a daughter, Lady Anne, who married Wilfrid S. Blunt, and two sons. The younger son (born 1839, died 1906) became the 2nd earl of Lovelace. Upon the death of the 2nd earl, the earldom of Lovelace passed to his half-brother (the son of the 1st earl's second wife), while the barony of Wentworth passed to the 2nd earl's only child, his daughter.

Additionally, Anna Isabella Milbanke (1792–1860), the daughter of Sir Ralph Milbanke and the Hon. Judith (daughter of Lord Wentworth), became Baroness Wentworth in 1856. Anna Isabella, a mathematician and poetess, married the poet Byron on January 2, 1815.

## Thomas Wentworth, Earl Strafford
A separate figure, Thomas Wentworth, Earl Strafford, served as the lord deputy of Ireland. In 1632, he was appointed to this position, and the town of Belfast benefited from his "enlightened policy," which included the acquisition of certain fiscal rights from the corporation of Carrickfergus.

However, Wentworth's administration was characterized by a policy of subjecting the powerful. This brought him into conflict with Lord Boyle (the earl of Cork and Viscount Dungarvan), who had previously been the most powerful subject in Ireland. Although Wentworth and Cork shared similar talents for practical statesmanship and views on administration, Wentworth sought to diminish Cork's influence. He subjected Cork to various humiliations, including the arbitrary deprivation of much of the revenues of Youghal and forcing him to remove his wife's tomb from the choir in St Patrick's at Dublin.
