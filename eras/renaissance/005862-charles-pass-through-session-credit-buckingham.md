# Charles I and the Influence of Buckingham

## Relationship with Buckingham
Following the death of King James on March 27, 1625, King Charles I and Buckingham shared identical aims and objects. Both men were young and inexperienced, driven by a desire to recover the Palatinate and chastise Spain. While Charles was described as obstinate once his mind was decided, he was also sluggish in action and lacked fertility in his ideas. Consequently, Charles long submitted his mind to Buckingham, a versatile and brilliant favorite who provided visions of future possibilities and direction.

## Diplomatic Missions and the Spanish Expedition
Under the guidance of Buckingham, efforts were made to secure active co-operation from the French court. However, this was complicated by the rebellion of the Huguenots of La Rochelle. Although James had promised English ships to help suppress the rebellion, Buckingham sought to mediate peace between the king of France and his subjects to prevent Charles from compromising his standing with parliament by attacking Protestants. During his time in France, Buckingham also made love to the queen of France.

Earlier, in 1622 and 1621, Buckingham had been under the influence of Gondomar. In May 1622, Laud managed to argue Buckingham out of a resolution to declare himself a Roman Catholic. In February 1623, Charles and Buckingham embarked on an expedition to Madrid, arriving on March 7. The trip was marked by mutual deception: Charles and Buckingham hoped for the restitution of the Palatinate as a marriage gift for Charles, while the Spanish hoped for extreme concessions and the conversion of Charles to Roman Catholicism before providing the hand of the infanta Maria. Personal disputes between Buckingham and the grandees and Olivares eventually led the two young men to sail from Santander in September with a resolution to break entirely with Spain.

## Conflict with Parliament
Charles faced significant difficulties during his first parliamentary session. Under Buckingham's guidance, he had entered into engagements that required enormous expenditure and involved a war on the continent, a venture that lacked popularity in the House of Commons. The Commons also suspected the marriage treaty contained disapproved engagements. Consequently, they voted very little money and demanded the full execution of laws against Roman Catholics.

Tensions escalated when English ships fell into French hands to be used against La Rochelle. When the Commons reassembled at Oxford on August 1, they expressed a lack of confidence in Buckingham and refused to vote any money until trusted persons were admitted to the king's council. Because Charles stood by his minister, he dissolved his first parliament on August 12.

## The Second Parliament and Impeachment
Charles's second parliament met on February 6, 1626. Under the guidance of Eliot, the House asked for Buckingham's punishment, and he was impeached before the House of Lords on numerous charges. While some charges were untrue or exaggerated, his primary failure was as the leader of the administration. Because Charles refused to acknowledge his minister's incompetency, the Commons utilized criminal charges as their only means of recourse. Charles dissolved the second parliament in a similar manner to the first. Subsequently, the Star Chamber declared the duke innocent, and Buckingham was elected chancellor of Cambridge University on June 1.

## The Case of the Earl of Bristol
The Earl of Bristol, created on September 15, had urged for a marriage treaty to benefit Frederick. However, the arrival of Charles and Buckingham in March 1623 wrecked negotiations. Buckingham's jealousy toward Bristol was fueled by a letter Bristol wrote to James regarding Buckingham's unpopularity with Spanish ministers. 

After arriving in Dover in March 1624, Bristol was forbidden from appearing at court and ordered to confine himself at Sherborne by Charles and Buckingham, who then held complete ascendancy over the king. Following the death of James, Charles I removed Bristol from the privy council and ordered him to absent himself from the first parliament. In January 1626, Charles angrily refused Bristol's request to attend the coronation, accusing him of attempting to pervert his religion in Spain. 

Despite a letter from Lord Keeper Coventry requesting he not use his writ, Bristol took his seat in the Lords in March 1626 to demand justice against Buckingham. Charles attempted to obstruct this by having the attorney-general accuse Bristol of high treason on May 1. The Lords ordered both charges be investigated simultaneously, but proceedings stopped when parliament was dissolved on June 15. Charles then ordered a prosecution in the Star Chamber, and Bristol was sent to the Tower until March 17, 1628, when the peers of Charles's third parliament insisted on his liberation.
