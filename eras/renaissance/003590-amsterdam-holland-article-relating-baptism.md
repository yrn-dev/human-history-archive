# Amsterdam in Holland

Amsterdam, a leading city in international dealings and the head of the republic of the United Provinces, served as a primary center for commerce, finance, and the arts in Holland. Its influence extended across the Low Countries, particularly during a period when the intellect of the region was concentrated in Holland and Zealand.

## Commerce and Finance
The city's role in international trade led to the establishment of the Amsterdamsche Wisselbank (the Bank of Amsterdam) by a city ordinance on January 31, 1609. This exchange bank allowed individuals to deposit money or bullion and withdraw the equivalent value at their pleasure. To regulate commerce, the bank required that all bills of 600 gulden (£50) or more be paid through the bank via the transfer of credits, a process known as "bank money." In 1643, this threshold was lowered to 300 gulden (£25).

The bank operated without its own capital, intending to hold the full amount of specie for all outstanding bank money in its vaults, though this regulation was not strictly followed. The bank provided loans to the Dutch East India Company and, by 1795, a report indicated that the city of Amsterdam was heavily indebted to the bank, which held obligations of West Friesland and the states of Holland as security. Despite the repayment of this debt, the bank could not be revived and ceased to exist in 1820.

## Literary and Intellectual Life
Amsterdam became the center of all literary enterprise in Holland toward the end of the transition period. In 1585, the "White Lavender" and "Fig Tree" chambers of rhetoric fled Flanders to settle in Amsterdam alongside the "Eglantine." The Eglantine institution encouraged intellectual reform, spreading its influence throughout Zealand and Holland.

The city's intellectual life was further enriched by the presence of one of the six great universities of the region, alongside others in Harderwijk, Franeker, Groningen, Utrecht, and Leiden. A significant cultural hub formed in the salon of Roemer Visscher and his daughters, where a new school of thought emerged. This circle provided the first admirers and critics for the odes of Huygens, the comedies of Bredero, the farces of Coster, and the tragedies of Vondel. While scholars at Leiden, such as Hugo Grotius, Vossius, and Drusius, insisted on a pure Latinity, the writers in Amsterdam helped the Dutch language achieve a high level of melody and finish.

## Language and Dialect
A notable distinction existed between the written language and the dialect spoken on the banks of the Y. While writers like Coster, Bredero, and Roemer Visscher utilized their native idiom, others like Cats or Huygens used a language where southern elements predominated, mixed with the dialects of Holland and Zeeland. Vondel was initially influenced by the idiom of Brabant, only becoming familiar with the Amsterdam dialect after 1625. Over time, the written language moved from the south to the north, was modified in Amsterdam, and was adopted by other provinces in the 17th century following the Union of Utrecht.

## Religious Movements
Amsterdam served as a refuge for those facing persecution. John Smith (Smyth), a separatist who had been a preacher in Lincoln, emigrated to Amsterdam with the Gainsborough Independents. After joining the "Ancient" church and encountering Mennonite teaching in 1609, Smyth separated from the Independents and baptized himself. Along with Thomas Helwys and others, he formed the first English Baptist Church.

In 1611, this group published a "declaration of faith of English people remaining at Amsterdam in Holland." This document taught that:
* Every church must receive members via baptism upon the confession of their sins and faith, as wrought by the preaching of the gospel.
* Baptism, described as washing with water, is an outward manifestation of walking in newness of life and dying unto sin; consequently, it does not appertain to infants.
* No church should claim any prerogative over another.
* The magistrate should not compel individuals into any form of religion nor meddle with matters of conscience or religion.

## Arts and Notable Figures
The city has been a significant site for the arts, housing six works by De Hooch in the Amsterdam Museum. In the realm of scholarship, Jan van Broekhuizen (1649–1707), a poet and classical scholar born in Amsterdam, produced editions of Tibullus and Propertius. After a career that included service in the army and a period in America under Admiral de Ruyter, Broekhuizen retired to a country house near Amsterdam to pursue literary studies.

Additionally, the city's connection to the royal house is noted through Louis Bonaparte, King of Holland. Louis maintained a zoological collection at a royal country-seat in Soestdyk (near Baarn), which was later moved to Amsterdam in 1809.
