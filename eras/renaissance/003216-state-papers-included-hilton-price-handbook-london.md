# The State Papers in F. G. Hilton Price's Handbook of London Bankers

The state papers contained within F. G. Hilton Price's *Handbook of London Bankers*, published in 1876, serve as a repository for some of the earliest available records concerning the establishment of banking within England. These documents detail early attempts to introduce formal banking structures to the English state.

## The Petition of Christopher Hagenbuck
One of the primary records included in the handbook is a petition addressed to Queen Elizabeth in November 1581. This document, printed in its original Italian, was submitted by Christopher Hagenbuck and his partners. In the petition, Hagenbuck asserted that he had discovered a "method and form" that would allow for the institution of an office capable of receiving a "very large sum of money" every year. 

Hagenbuck claimed that this office could operate without expense to the Queen. He argued that the system would ensure the Queen was always provided with any "notable sum of money" she might desire, while simultaneously benefiting the state and the people. Furthermore, he proposed that this institution would maintain abundance in the country and eliminate the "extreme usuries" that were devouring both the people and the Queen.

## Terms and Outcome of the 1581 Proposal
As a condition for explaining his plan, Christopher Hagenbuck requested to receive 6% of the total mass of money received by the office annually for a period of twenty years. Queen Elizabeth agreed to a modified term, granting Hagenbuck and his partners 4% for twenty years and confirming this grant under the great seal. Although the document bears the signature of Francis Walsingham, the records indicate that nothing further appears to have resulted from this proposal.

## Historical Context and Subsequent Proposals
The timing of Hagenbuck's petition suggests a connection to continental banking developments. Specifically, the date of the document aligns with the establishment of the Banco della Piazza di Rialto in Venice. This suggests that the concept of establishing a bank may have been familiar to Hagenbuck through his commercial dealings with Venice and was a concept circulating among those involved in business at the time.

The *Handbook of London Bankers* also contains other state papers from later periods that present similar proposals for banking. These documents date from 1621 and 1622, and again from 1662 and 1666. However, like the petition of Hagenbuck, these subsequent proposals were never carried into practice.
