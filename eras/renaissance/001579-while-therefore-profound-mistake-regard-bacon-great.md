# Francis Bacon and Roger Bacon

The archive contains records regarding two distinct figures named Bacon: the 13th-century thinker Roger Bacon and the later philosopher and statesman Francis Bacon.

## Roger Bacon
Roger Bacon is described as one of the highest-ranking figures of the 13th century, comparable to Thomas Aquinas, Bonaventura, and Albertus Magnus. While he was not as renowned in his own time as these contemporaries, modern criticism suggests he was in advance of his age. His writings are characterized as a "long tirade against ignorance," in which he boldly challenged the dogmas and methods of his time, which he viewed as infantile and clumsy.

Roger Bacon's work is noted for its passionate insistence on the collection of knowledge and the necessity of experiment. This approach is seen as a revival of the spirit of Aristotle. While some view him as a modern thinker whose conceptions of science were clearer than those of his namesake, he remained a systematic thinker working within the tracks of his own national and contemporary surroundings.

## Francis Bacon: Philosophy and Method
Regarding his philosophical contributions, it is noted that viewing Francis Bacon as a "lonely pioneer of modern thought" or a "great constructive philosopher" is a mistake; however, it is considered unfair to call him a trifler. His primary achievement lay in summing up the faults of medieval thought revealed by the expansion of knowledge.

Bacon developed a method of induction that he believed offered mechanical simplicity and absolute certainty. He distinguished his induction from the Aristotelian *epagoge* and the Socratic search for essence; for Bacon, the essence of induction was elimination, exclusion, and rejection. This theory was a consequence of his doctrine of forms and his view of science.

Bacon also emphasized the necessity of a "complete natural history"—the tabulation and arrangement of the phenomena of the universe. He believed that his *Organum* and the *Natural History* were correlative and together produced a "new philosophy." In a letter to Jean Antoine Baranzano, he argued that a store of instances must be procured for the business of science to proceed, regardless of how many volumes such a history might fill.

## Influence of Francis Bacon
Bacon's influence was immediate and lasting in both natural science and philosophy. In natural science, his work was referenced by principal scientists across Italy, Germany, France, and England. In France, Voltaire and the encyclopaedists hailed him as "le pere de la philosophie experimentale," and d'Alembert described him as the most eloquent, universal, and greatest of philosophers. In England, his work *New Atlantis* provided the impetus for the foundation of the Royal Society.

In the realm of philosophy, Bacon is regarded by many as the father of English psychological speculation. The fundamental principle of Locke's *Essay*—that ideas result from reflection and sensation—is briefly stated in the first aphorism of Bacon's *Novum Organum*. In ethics, he is seen as a forerunner of the empirical method; the inductive method discussed in the *Novum Organum* and the spirit of *De Augmentis* are considered the roots of theories that construct moral codes via the examination of human consciousness and action, specifically utilitarianism. Notably, however, Hobbes, who served as Bacon's secretary, made no critical reference to Bacon or Baconian induction in his works.

## Political Career and Relations with King James
As the first officer of the crown, Francis Bacon was a sagacious and devoted member of the royal party. Despite his abilities, his influence was secondary, and his practical skills were primarily used to execute the will of King James.

Bacon generally sympathized with the government's home policy, including the use of patents and monopolies, though he later recognized that some were oppressive. In November 1620, he advised Buckingham to withdraw the most obnoxious patents—specifically those regarding inns, alehouses, and the monopoly of gold and silver thread—to avoid an "impending storm," though this advice was rejected.

On foreign policy, Bacon disagreed with the king. He favored a war with Spain to recover the Palatinate, believing such a move would allow the king to lead the parliament and create a policy the people could sympathize with. King James, described as peace-loving and vacillating, neglected this advice and replaced Bacon's proffered proclamation with a "featureless production."

## Bibliography and Related Figures
The classical edition of Bacon's works was produced by R. L. Ellis, J. Spedding, and D. D. Heath (1st ed. 1857, 2nd ed. 1870). Other editions include a 17-volume set by B. Montagu (1825-1834) and a 1905 reprint of philosophical works by J. M. Robertson.
