# Rubrics and Information Storage

The term "rubric" appears across various historical, liturgical, and cartographic contexts, often referring to specific directions, headings, or instructional notes. Additionally, the storage of information—ranging from 17th-century liturgical texts to modern digital records—serves as a primary method for preserving data for legal, religious, or academic purposes.

## Liturgical and Religious Rubrics

In religious contexts, rubrics provide directions for the conduct of services and the use of vestments. In the office for Holy Communion during the second year of Edward VI in England, a rubric directed the "helpers" of the priest to wear "albes with tunacles." This relates to the use of the dalmatic, an ornament of the minister that was later restored in many Anglican churches during the 19th-century ritual revival, based on the "ornaments rubric" of the Book of Common Prayer.

Other historical examples include the old *ordo Romanus* from the age of Pepin. Under the rubric of the Vigil of the Theophany, this text asserts that the "second birth" of Christ during Baptism is more honored than the first birth from Mary, as it is distinguished by mysteries such as the miracle of Cana.

## Liturgical Works of John Bona

John Bona (1609–1674), an Italian cardinal, author, and former general of the Congregation of Feuillants, produced numerous writings focused on devotional and liturgical subjects. His works *Divina Psalmodia* and *De Libris Liturgicis* are noted for containing stores of interesting rubrical information, which are interspersed with prayers and verses. While recent advances in liturgical studies have somewhat reduced the value of these specific works, Bona's *De Sacrificio Missae* remains a classical treatise in its field. Notable editions of Bona's collected works were published in Paris (1677), Turin (1747), and Antwerp (1777).

## Cartographic and Literary Rubrics

Rubrics also appear as annotations on historical maps. The Peutingerian Tables contain a rubric in the northeast of Asia stating, “*Hic Alexander Responsum accepit*.” This is interpreted as a possible allusion to the tale of the Oracular Trees, suggesting the antiquity of Alexandrian Legends, although it is noted that the rubric could be an interpolation.

In a literary context, the works of Plutarch contain incidental allusions interspersed throughout his writings. Plutarch's dramatic dialogue, *De Genio Socratis*, provides statements regarding the character of Epaminondas. These were likely derived from materials Plutarch collected for a now-lost life of Epaminondas, potentially including information from Spintharus the Tarentine, which may have been communicated via his son Aristoxenus.

## Modern Information Storage Systems

In contemporary administrative and legal frameworks, the storage of information is handled through specialized digital databases.

### Law Enforcement and Victim Data
The FBI collects victim contact information during investigations and stores it in a case management system. A Victim Specialist then exports this data into the VNS database, where it is linked to a VNS case number. This system allows for the generation of seven types of notification letters:
1. Initial notification
2. Case under investigation
3. Arrest of the defendant
4. Declination of prosecution
5. Other
6. Advice of victim rights
7. Investigation closed

Once the prosecution stage begins, the USAO's Victim Witness Specialist takes over, importing data from its own case management system into the VNS to notify victims of events such as arraignments, charges filed, sentencing hearings, proposed plea agreements, and the results of those hearings.

### Telecommunications Records
MetroPCS utilizes a Billing System to store account information entered by personnel. For the purpose of complying with legal processes mandated by CALEA, call detail records are maintained. Because MetroPCS offers unlimited service, these records are not needed for billing but are stored to assist government agencies with lawful subpoena requests. MetroPCS employs Verisign, a secure third-party provider and consultant to the Office of Homeland Security and federal agencies, to store these call detail records. The data is electronically transmitted to Verisign and retrieved via a dedicated secure circuit using a static 313 address. These files are generally maintained for approximately six months before being automatically purged.
