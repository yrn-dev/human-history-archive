# Samuel Mearne

Samuel Mearne (d. 1683) was a prominent 17th-century English bookbinder who served as the royal binder to King Charles II. His work is regarded as being of the highest merit and represents some of the daintiest leather bindings produced in England during that period.

## Artistic Style and Innovations
Mearne is credited with the invention of the cottage style of decoration, a design that has persisted into the modern era. This specific style is characterized by an inner rectangle that runs parallel to the edges of the book, with the upper and lower lines broken outwards to create the outline of a gable roof. The enduring nature of this style is evidenced by its use on the Bible upon which Edward VII took his coronation oath.

In addition to the cottage style, Mearne utilized a technique of painting the fore-edge of books. This method ensured that the artwork remained invisible while the book was shut, becoming visible only when the edges were fanned out. This specific 17th-century approach was later followed in the 18th century by John Edwards of Halifax.

## Influences and Materials
Mearne's leather bindings were, to some extent, imitated from the work of Le Gascon, a great French binder. An example of Mearne's craftsmanship is a 1678 edition of the Common Prayer, which features smooth red morocco gold-tooled with black fillets.

## Historical Legacy and Collectibility
Interest in the work of Samuel Mearne has increased in England, particularly following an exhibition of bindings at the Burlington Fine Arts Club in 1891. Consequently, bindings attributable to Mearne from the time of Charles II have become more sought after by collectors than they were previously.
