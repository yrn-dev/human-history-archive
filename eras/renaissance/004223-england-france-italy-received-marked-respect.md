# England, France, and Italy

The historical relationship between England, France, and Italy is characterized by intersecting military conflicts, diplomatic missions, cultural exchanges, and the shared development of legal and architectural systems.

## Political and Military Relations
France and England have a long history of conflict and diplomatic negotiation. In the 14th century, Louis I, duke of Anjou and count of Maine, served in the French army at the battle of Poitiers and was sent to England as a hostage following the treaty of Brétigny in 1360. After breaking his parole in 1363, he participated in the renewed war against England starting in 1369. Later, in the 16th century, the French soldier and diplomat Michel de Castelnau was sent by King Henry II to England in 1559 to treat with Queen Elizabeth regarding her claims on Calais, a matter settled at the congress of Cateau-Cambresis.

In the late 18th and early 19th centuries, France under Napoleon Bonaparte engaged in extensive European campaigns. Bonaparte served as commander-in-chief in Italy in 1795 and later destroyed the Republic of Venice via the Peace of Campo Formio in 1797. In 1800, the Battle of Marengo took place in Italy. By 1801, preliminaries of peace were signed between France, England, and Austria.

During the 19th century, these nations were involved in "balance-of-power" wars. England and France, along with Sardinia, assailed Russia during the Crimean war to defend Turkey. France also liberated North Italy from Austria, though this came at the cost of Savoy.

## Legal and Architectural Development
The study of Roman law, revived in Italy, exerted a significant influence across Europe. It was propagated by the Glossators and became respected in England from the time of Stephen to Edward I. In France and Italy, Roman law maintained a direct or indirect influence.

Architecturally, the period between the 11th and 15th centuries saw the rise of Gothic architecture, particularly in England and France, where numerous abbeys and cathedrals were constructed. During this same era, Italy and Spain also began to build "freely and beautifully." This period coincided with a revival of town life and trade, with prominent urban centers including London, Oxford, Cambridge, Southampton, and Dover in England; Paris, Tours, Orleans, Bordeaux, and Boulogne in France; and Venice, Genoa, Verona, Bologna, Pisa, Florence, Naples, and Milan in Italy.

## Cultural and Intellectual Exchange
Italy served as a primary destination for European scholars, artists, and musicians. Gaspard Bartholinus, a Swedish physician, traveled through Italy, France, and England, receiving respect at the universities he visited. Similarly, the French diplomat Michel de Castelnau completed his education with travels in Italy and a long stay in Rome. In the arts, the Flemish painter Pieter Breughel spent time in both Italy and France before settling in Antwerp and Brussels.

Musical influence also flowed from Italy to France and England. In 1647, Cardinal Mazarin brought an orchestra, singers, and mise-en-scene from Italy to France to create a taste for Italian opera. While this did not fully make Paris appreciate Italian music, it fostered a demand for French opera. The orchestral horn was introduced to France from Italy; this instrument later became known as the "French horn" in England.

In the field of geographical science, the "Yule method," developed by Yule, exerted a stimulating influence on the literatures of Italy, France, and Germany.
