# BONNER, EDMUND (1500?-1569)

Edmund Bonner was a high-ranking English cleric who served as the Bishop of London. His life was marked by service to the English crown during the Tudor period and subsequent imprisonment during the reign of Elizabeth I.

## Early Life and Education
The parentage of Edmund Bonner is a subject of historical dispute. One account, printed by Strype, suggests he was the natural son of Elizabeth Frodsham and George Savage, the rector of Davenham, Cheshire; Elizabeth later married a sawyer of Hanley in Worcestershire also named Edmund Bonner. However, Sir Edmund Lechmere contested this, asserting that Bonner was born legitimately, though the evidence for this claim is described as not very satisfactory.

Bonner received his education at Broadgates Hall (now Pembroke College), Oxford. He graduated as a bachelor of civil and canon law in June 1519 and was ordained around the same time. He later attained the degree of Doctor of Civil Law (D.C.L.) in 1525.

## Service to Wolsey and the Crown
In 1529, Bonner served as chaplain to Cardinal Wolsey and was present with the cardinal at Cawood during the time of Wolsey's arrest. Following this, Bonner entered the service of the king, a transition potentially influenced by Cromwell. 

His diplomatic duties for the crown included a mission to Rome in January 1532, where he was tasked with obstructing judicial proceedings against Henry in the papal curia. In October 1533, while Pope Clement VII was a guest of Francis I at Marseilles, Bonner was entrusted with the task of informing the pope of Henry's appeal to a general council. While Burnet recounted a story that Clement threatened to burn Bonner alive for this, there is no good authority to support that claim. As a reward for these services, Bonner was granted several livings and was appointed archdeacon of Leicester in 1535.

## Episcopal Career and Religious Inquisitions
Bonner eventually rose to the position of Bishop of London. In this capacity, he was involved in the examination of those accused of heresy. In March 1545, he examined Anne Askew, a Protestant who had been committed to the Counter prison. Bonner drew up a form of recantation for Askew and entered it into his register. Although Catholic historians such as Parsons used this fact to claim she recanted, Askew refused to sign the form without qualification.

## Later Years and Imprisonment
Upon the accession of Elizabeth I, Bonner's standing shifted. Elizabeth refused to allow him to kiss her hand, though he continued to vote in the convocation and parliament of 1559. In May of that year, Bonner refused to take the oath of supremacy. 

This refusal led to his imprisonment in the Marshalsea. A few years later, he was indicted on a charge of praemunire after again refusing the oath when it was tendered to him by his diocesan, Bishop Horne of Winchester. Bonner responded by challenging the legality of Bishop Horne's consecration. This resulted in a special act of parliament to address the point of consecration, and the charge against Bonner was subsequently withdrawn.

Edmund Bonner died in the Marshalsea on September 5, 1569. He was buried at midnight in St George's, Southwark, a timing chosen to avoid the risk of a hostile demonstration. Following his tenure, the Bishopric of London was filled by the successor to the recusant Dr Thomas Young.
