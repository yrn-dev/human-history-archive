# Bermuda

## Governance and Political Structure
Bermuda is characterized as a partially self-governing community of British origin. Its political system features an appointed executive and an elected legislature. Specifically, Bermuda possesses two legislative bodies: one that is elected and one that is nominated by the crown. 

In colonies with this type of representative institution—where there is no responsible government—the crown generally does not legislate by order in council. Instead, laws are created by the governor with the concurrence of the legislative body or bodies. In instances where a second chamber exists that possesses a preponderance of elected representatives, the governor must have the concurrence of at least one of these bodies.

## Historical Events
Bermuda appears in historical records as a location for political imprisonment during the 19th century. Following the quelling of Papineau's rebellion in Canada, an ordinance was issued on June 28, 1838 (the day of Queen Victoria's coronation). This ordinance provided for the transference of eight individuals, who had been excepted from a general proclamation of amnesty, from Montreal to Bermuda. These individuals were to be imprisoned in Bermuda without trial.

## Connection to Other Settlements
Bermuda is linked to the colonization of other Caribbean islands. In 1628, Charles Wolferstone, a native of Bermuda, was appointed as the governor of Barbados. This appointment occurred during the early stages of Barbados's colonization, the same year that sixty-four settlers arrived at Carlisle Bay to found the present capital. Additionally, records indicate that New Providence was colonized in 1666 by settlers arriving from the Bermudas.
