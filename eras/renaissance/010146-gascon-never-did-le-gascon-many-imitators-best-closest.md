# Le Gascon

Le Gascon was a master French bookbinder whose innovations in the 16th and 17th centuries left a lasting impact on the craft of binding. He is specifically credited with the invention of "dotted work," a technique that has remained in use to a varying degree since its inception.

## Technical Innovations
Le Gascon achieved the dotted work effect by modifying his gilding tools. He used small tools designed for creating arabesques and curves, which he scored across. This modification ensured that when the tools were used to make impressions on the book's surface, a dotted line appeared instead of a solid right line.

## Influence and Imitators
The style developed by Le Gascon was widely adopted and continuously followed into the present day. He had numerous imitators, among whom the Dutch binders Poncyn and Magnus are noted as the closest and best. 

Beyond the borders of France, Le Gascon's influence extended to England during the second half of the 17th century. The leather bindings produced by Samuel Mearne, which are regarded as the daintiest produced in England up to that time, were imitated to some extent from the work of Le Gascon.

## Comparison with Contemporaries
Le Gascon's work is often compared to that of Florimond Badier, who operated in a very similar style. A distinguishing difference between the two craftsmen was their approach to attribution; while Badier sometimes signed his work, Le Gascon never did.

Le Gascon was part of a broader tradition of French binding masters during the 16th and 17th centuries. Other notable figures of this era included Nicholas Eve, who bound the *Livre des Statuts et Ordonnances de l'ordre du Benvist Sainct Esprit* for Henry III, and Clovis Eve, the inventor of the "fanfare" style characterized by delicate tracery and leafy stem spirals over the boards. Additionally, the family of Padeloup le Jeune produced excellent workmen in the 17th and 18th centuries, known for inlaid bindings and "dentelle," a type of lace-like gold tooling.
