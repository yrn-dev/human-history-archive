# Bacon's Motive for Founding the Sciences

Francis Bacon, Lord Verulam (1561–1626), who served as Lord Chancellor to James I of England, sought to found the sciences anew based on a profound conviction regarding the utility of human knowledge. His primary motive was the belief that the knowledge possessed by man at the time was of little service to him. Bacon asserted that the existing knowledge of the world, particularly concerning nature, did not extend to the certainty and magnitude of works.

## The Recovery of Human Sovereignty
Bacon believed that man's sovereignty over nature is founded solely on knowledge. He argued that this sovereignty had been lost, replaced by blind experiments and vain notions rather than a free relationship between the human mind and things. Consequently, the overarching object of all science, according to Bacon, is to recover the *imperium hominis* and restore the original commerce between humanity and nature.

## The Practical Aim of Philosophy
Bacon identified the cause of previous failures in this direction as a lack of appreciation for the real aim and nature of philosophy. He explicitly rejected the idea that philosophy is the search after truth or the science of things human and divine. He observed that those who sought knowledge for its own sake—for satisfaction, ostentation, or "Truth"—had set a wrong mark.

To Bacon, philosophy is altogether practical. He argued that abstract notions regarding the principles and nature of things are of little consequence to the fortunes of humanity. Instead, he posited that the true aim of all science is to extend the limits of the power and greatness of man and to endow human life and condition with new works or powers. He questioned whether a view of nature should be one of mere delight and contentment rather than one of discovery and benefit, suggesting that the "riches of nature's warehouse" should be utilized to produce worthy effects and infinite commodities for human life.

## Influence and Legacy
Bacon's vision for the organization of science is most notably expressed in his work *The New Atlantis*. In this text, he planned a "palace of invention" and a "great temple of science" where the pursuit of knowledge across all branches would be organized according to principles of highest efficiency. This work provided significant impetus for the foundation of the Royal Society. Dr. Thomas Sprat, the society's first historian, noted that Bacon possessed the "true imagination of the whole extent" of such an enterprise and provided the best arguments for the experimental method of natural philosophy.

Bacon's influence extended across Europe, with his work referenced by principal scientists in Italy, Germany, and France. In France, the encyclopaedists and Voltaire hailed him as the "father of experimental philosophy" (*le pere de la philosophie experimentale*). The division of sciences used in the great French *Encyclopedie* was founded upon Bacon's classification. Other thinkers, such as Leibnitz, described him as a man of divine genius (*divini ingenii vir*), while Huygens noted his "bonnes methodes."

While some, such as Sir R. A. Gregory, suggest that Bacon was the "apostle" rather than the founder of the scientific method, his insistence on the practical application of knowledge and the experimental method remains a cornerstone of his intellectual legacy.
