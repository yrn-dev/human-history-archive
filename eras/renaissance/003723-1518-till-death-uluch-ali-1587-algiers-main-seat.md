# The Beylerbeys of Algiers (1518–1587)

From approximately 1518 until the death of Uluch Ali in 1587, the city of Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa. In this capacity, Algiers was the center of authority for a region that included Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli.

## Establishment of Turkish Power
The origins of this period are linked to the aftermath of the 1492 conquest of Granada by the Catholic sovereigns of Spain, which forced many Moors into exile. These exiles engaged in piratical attacks against the Spanish coast, aided by Moslem adventurers from the Levant. Among the most successful of these were brothers Arouj and Khair-ed-Din, natives of Mitylene, who were known to Christians by the nickname "Barbarossa" or "Redbeard."

In response to these activities, Spain began conquering coastal towns, including Tunis, Oran, and Algiers. After Arouj fell in battle against the Spaniards in 1518, his brother Khair-ed-Din sought assistance from the sultan of Turkey, Selim, who provided him with troops. In 1529, Khair-ed-Din succeeded in driving the Spaniards from a fort located on a rocky island in front of Algiers, thereby establishing Turkish power in the region.

## Role and Nature of the Beylerbeys
During the period from 1518 to 1587, the beylerbeys functioned as admirals of the sultan. They commanded extensive fleets and conducted significant military operations for political purposes. While they were known as slave-hunters and employed ferocious methods, the archive notes that their Christian enemies were neither more chivalrous nor more humane.

The maritime activities of this era were carried out by a class or corporation of captains known as *reises*. These cruisers were funded by capitalists. To maintain the state treasury, ten per cent of the value of all prizes captured by these vessels was paid to the treasury of the pasha or his successors.

## Infrastructure and Naval Evolution
Bougie served as the primary port for shipbuilding, utilizing timber sourced from the surrounding countryside. For a significant period, the pirates relied on galleys; however, these vessels were unfit for the high seas and were consequently restricted to the coast and the Mediterranean.

The naval capabilities of the region expanded when a Flemish renegade named Simon Danser introduced the advantages of sailing ships. This technological shift allowed the pirates to range further into the Atlantic, reaching as far as Iceland or the Canaries. The strength of these operations was bolstered by renegades from various parts of Christendom, including an English gentleman from the Buckinghamshire Verney family.

## Regional Influence and Legacy
Algiers became the most formidable of the pirate ports, to the extent that the term "Algerine" became synonymous with "Barbary pirate." While similar trade was conducted from Tunis, Tripoli, and towns in the empire of Morocco (most notably Salli), it was performed with less energy than in Algiers.

The era of the beylerbeys ended in 1587 with the death of Uluch Ali. Following this, the region was governed by Turkish pashas sent from Constantinople for three-year terms until 1659. This later period marked a shift in objective, as the political operations of the beylerbeys were replaced by a sole focus on plunder directed at both the sea and native tribes on land.
