# The Inner Temple and the Inns of Court

The Inner Temple is one of the four ancient societies in London known as the Inns of Court. These societies—which also include the Middle Temple, Lincoln's Inn, and Gray's Inn—serve as the mandatory professional bodies for barristers in England.

## History and Purpose of the Inns of Court
The rise of the English Inns of Court as educational institutions can be traced back to the 13th century. This development is attributed to a clause in the Magna Carta that fixed the Common Pleas at Westminster rather than having them follow the king's court. As a result, professors of law were brought together in London. 

Associations of lawyers established their own houses where students were educated in the common law. Within these institutions, specific degrees were conferred: the degree of barrister, which corresponds to a bachelor or apprentice, and the degree of sergeant, which corresponds to a doctor. In the English and Irish legal systems, the term "barrister" refers to the highest class of lawyers, who possess exclusive audience in all superior courts. While the English barristers belong to the four Inns of Court, those in Ireland belong to the King's Inns.

## Notable Residents and Members
The Inner Temple has been home to a variety of figures, including legal professionals, historians, and men of letters.

### Edmund Bolton
Edmund Bolton (born 1575) was an English poet and historian who resided at the Inner Temple after being educated at Trinity Hall, Cambridge. A Roman Catholic and retainer of the duke of Buckingham, Bolton secured a position at the court of James I. He is noted for formulating a scheme to establish an English academy, a project that was viewed favorably by the king but failed following the king's death. 

Bolton's literary contributions include *Hypercritica* (1618?), a critical treatise on contemporary authors, and *Nero Caesar, or Monarchic Depraved* (1624), which included notes on British affairs. He also wrote a *Life of King Henry II* for Speed's *Chronicle*; however, this work was replaced by a life written by Dr. John Barcham because Bolton's Catholic sympathies were evident in his treatment of Thomas Becket. In 1600, Bolton contributed to the work *England's Helicon*.

### Eustace Budgell
Eustace Budgell (1686–1737) was an English man of letters who joined the Inner Temple after matriculating at Trinity College, Oxford, in 1705. Although he joined the society, Budgell did not study law, instead devoting his full attention to literature. He was befriended by his mother's first cousin, Addison, and served as a clerk in the office of Lord Wharton, the lord-lieutenant of Ireland, starting in 1710. 

Budgell became a contributor to several periodicals, including the *Spectator* (where his papers were marked with an X), the *Guardian* (where they were marked with an asterisk), and the *Tatler*, which he co-wrote with Steele and Addison. He held various official posts in Ireland, including deputy-clerk of the council, chief secretary to the lords justices, and accountant and comptroller-general of the revenue. His career in Ireland ended after he wrote a lampoon against E. Webster, the secretary to the duke of Bolton. Budgell later suffered significant financial losses in 1720 due to the South Sea scheme and spent further funds in unsuccessful attempts to enter parliament.

## Related Legal Education in London
Other legal figures have utilized the Temple for their studies. Paul Dudley (1675–1751), the son of Joseph Dudley, studied law at the Temple in London after graduating from Harvard in 1690. Following his studies, Dudley returned to Massachusetts, where he served as attorney-general from 1702 to 1718, and later as associate justice and chief justice of the province's superior court.
