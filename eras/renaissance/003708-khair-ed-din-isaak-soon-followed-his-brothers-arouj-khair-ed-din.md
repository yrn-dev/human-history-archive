# Khair-ed-Din

The archive contains records of two distinct historical figures named Khair-ed-Din: a 16th-century Turkish admiral and a 19th-century Turkish statesman.

## Khair-ed-Din (The Admiral)

Khair-ed-Din, also known as Khizr, was a 16th-century Turkish admiral and sea rover. He was one of four sons born after 1482 to Yakub, a Roumeliot likely of Albanian blood who had settled in Mitylene following the Turkish conquest. While his brothers Elias and Arouj took to sea roving, Khizr initially became a potter.

Khizr eventually joined his brother Arouj in Tunis, where he took or was endowed with the name Khair-ed-Din. He was joined there by another brother, Isaak, who had been a trader. Together, Arouj and Khair-ed-Din collaborated with exiled Moors from Granada to conduct raids on the Spanish coast. They also sought to increase their fortunes by supplanting or fighting native African princes, establishing their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes. In 1512, they attempted to capture Bougie from the Spaniards but were unsuccessful.

To the Christians, Khair-ed-Din and his brother were known by the nickname "Barbarossa" (meaning "Redbeard"), likely due to their tawny or red beards. While some later historians suggested "Barbarossa" was a mispronunciation of "Baba Arouj," contemporary Arab chronicles explicitly state the name was applied by Christians to Khair-ed-Din.

Following the death of Arouj in battle against the Spaniards in 1518, Khair-ed-Din appealed to the Turkish sultan, Selim, who provided him with troops. In 1529, Khair-ed-Din drove the Spaniards from a fort located on a rocky island in front of Algiers, an action that established Turkish power in the region. From 1518 until 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli.

## Khair-ed-Din (The Statesman)

A separate figure, Khair-ed-Din (meaning "Joy of Religion"), was a Turkish statesman of Circassian race who died in 1890. In his early childhood, he was sold by a Tunisian slave-dealer to Hamuda Pasha, the bey of Tunis. Hamuda Pasha granted him his freedom and a French education. Upon completing his schooling, Khair-ed-Din served as the steward of the bey's estates and eventually rose to the position of minister of finance.

Khair-ed-Din was dispatched to France by Hamuda Pasha to secure the extradition of the prime minister, Mahmud ben Ayad, who had fled to France with the beylic's treasure-chest. Although the mission failed, the six years spent in France allowed Khair-ed-Din to study French administrative methods and political ideas. Upon his return to Tunisia, he became highly valued by three successive beys: Ahmet (1837), Mohammed (1855), and Sadok (1859). His influence led to a series of liberal measures during their reigns, and he secured the Turkish sultan's confirmation of the reigning family's right to succeed to the beylic.

Despite his liberal views, Khair-ed-Din opposed the French plan to establish a protectorate over Tunisia in the early 1870s. This opposition made him an enemy of Sadok's prime minister, Mustapha ben Ismael, who undermined the bey's confidence in him. Consequently, Khair-ed-Din's position in Tunisia became untenable.

After the accession of Abdul Hamid, Khair-ed-Din expressed his desire to enter Turkish service. In 1877, the sultan summoned him to Constantinople and appointed him to the Reform Commission at Tophane. In early 1879, the sultan appointed him grand vizier, and he subsequently prepared a scheme for constitutional government.
