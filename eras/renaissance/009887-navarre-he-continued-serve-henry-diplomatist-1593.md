# Henry of Navarre

Henry of Navarre, who later became Henry IV of France, was a central figure in the political and religious conflicts of the late 16th century. His trajectory from the kingship of Navarre to the throne of France involved extensive diplomatic efforts, military campaigns, and complex relationships with the papacy and other European powers.

## Diplomatic and Political Efforts
Henry utilized a network of diplomats and scholars to secure his position and expand his influence. One such figure was Jacques Bongars, a French scholar and diplomatist born in Orleans. Bongars entered the service of Segur Pardaillan, a representative of Henry. In 1587, Bongars was dispatched on a mission to various princes in northern Europe and subsequently visited England to seek assistance for Henry from Queen Elizabeth. 

Bongars continued to serve Henry as a diplomatist, and by 1593, he became the representative of the French king at the courts of the imperial princes. In this capacity, Bongars worked vigorously to support Henry's goal of curtailing the power of the house of Habsburg, dedicating significant personal wealth and health to the cause until Henry's murder in 1610.

## Military and Domestic Affairs
Henry's rise was marked by both support and opposition. From 1589, he was supported by figures such as Biron, who had previously served as a marshal of France and grand master of artillery. However, Biron was suspected of prolonging the civil wars for his own benefit before being killed by a cannon-ball at the siege of Epernay on July 26, 1592. Additionally, around 1591, the English poet and politician Fulke Greville, 1st Baron Brooke, served for a short period in Normandy under Henry of Navarre.

In terms of territorial and financial management, Henry sold the independent lordship of Epernon—which had been attached to the crown of Navarre—to Jean Louis de Nogaret in the 16th century. This sale led to the area being raised to the rank of a duchy in 1581.

## Relations with the Papacy
Henry's path to the French throne was complicated by religious tensions. Pope Gregory XIV (Nicolo Sfondrato), who served from 1590 to 1591, was subservient to Philip II and acted against Henry. Gregory XIV excommunicated Henry of Navarre and threatened his followers with the ban. However, this papal intervention proved counterproductive, as it served to rally moderate Catholics to support Henry and accelerated his conversion.

## Personal Life and Marriage
Henry was married to Marguerite de Valois, the daughter of Henry II and Catherine de' Medici. The marriage took place on the eve of St Bartholomew's Day. Both were noted for their licentious manners, though they often lived together for long periods and generally remained on good terms. Eventually, Marguerite was established at the castle of Usson in Auvergne, and the pope dissolved the marriage after Henry's accession. Despite the dissolution, the two remained friends; Marguerite continued to hold the title of queen and was frequently consulted by the king on important matters, although Henry's parsimonious nature was often strained by her extravagance.

## Legacy and Death
Henry's reign ended with his murder in 1610. Following his death, the regency was given to Marie de' Medici, a process in which Jean Louis de Nogaret (the duke of Epernon) played an instrumental role.
