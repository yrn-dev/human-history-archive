# Martin Luther

Martin Luther was a religious reformer and professor of theology whose work significantly impacted the evangelical movement and the religious landscape of Germany. He is identified as one of the great religious reformers—alongside figures such as Moses, Buddha, Paul, Mani, Mahomet, and St Francis—who sought to enlighten the ascetic instinct, directing it toward higher aims by substituting true personal holiness for ritual purity.

## Early Career and Academic Work
Luther was ordained after attaining inward peace and continued his theological studies, focusing on the "experimental" portions of Gerson, Bernard, and Augustine. In 1508, he was sent to assist the university at Wittenberg, which had been established in 1502 by Frederick the Wise, the elector of Saxony. At Wittenberg, Luther began preaching, first to monks of his order in a small chapel and later as a substitute for a member of the town's clergy.

Following a mission to Rome on behalf of the German Augustinian Eremites, Luther was sent to Erfurt in 1512 by his vicar-general, Staupitz, to pursue higher graduation in theology. He became a Doctor of the Holy Scripture, took an oath to vigorously defend the evangelical truth, and joined the Wittenberg Senate. Shortly thereafter, he succeeded Staupitz as the professor of theology in Wittenberg.

## Theological Controversies and the Papacy
In April 1518, at a chapter of the Augustinian Eremites in Heidelberg, Luther discovered that his positions were not universally accepted by his fellow monks. In response, he published his *Resolutions*, a work in which he discarded scholastic explanations from Thomas Aquinas and Bonaventura, as well as medieval ideas and practices regarding Indulgences dating from the beginning of the 13th century. This led to a decrease in the sale of Indulgences in Germany, causing alarm within the Papal Curia.

Luther was summoned to Rome, but the summons was cancelled by Pope Leo after the emperor Maximilian and the elector of Saxony viewed it as an attack on the university of Wittenberg. Luther instead met with Cardinal Cajedtan, the papal Legate, at Augsburg. Following this unsuccessful interview, Luther published an account titled *Acta Augustana* and issued appeals to a General Council and to the pope.

## Conflict with the Empire
Luther faced significant opposition from Emperor Charles, who was persuaded by Aleander to place Luther under a ban. While the diet of Worms initially suggested Luther be invited under a safe-conduct to be examined and to speak on papal claims, the emperor eventually attempted to have him condemned without appearing. An edict was published on March 10th ordering the seizure of Luther's books. Despite these efforts to intimidate him, Luther arrived at Worms on April 16th.

## Relations with Other Reformers
Luther's efforts to unite evangelical Christians were complicated by fierce controversies, particularly with the Swiss reformer Zwingli. The two differed on the true doctrine of the sacrament of the Supper; Luther proposed a theory of Consubstantiation, which required the doctrine of "Ubiquity" (the omnipresence of the body of Christ extended in space). Zwingli, a thorough democrat, disagreed with Luther's exegesis and his distrust of the "common man." Luther also suspected Zwingli's friends of helping organize a Peasants' War to link social revolution with religious awakening.

At a conference in Philip of Hesse's castle at Marburg, Luther and Zwingli failed to reach an agreement on whether the body and blood of Christ are corporeally present in the bread and wine. However, they did sign the *Articles of Marburg*, which established agreement on the personality of Christ, the Trinity, baptism, faith, justification, the Scriptures, and the rejection of the sacrifice of the Mass.

## Later Life and Influence
In his final years, Luther worked to unite the evangelical movement, advise evangelical princes, and establish a good system of schools for his countrymen. He maintained close ties with the electors John and John Frederick. He remained vigilant against attempts by the Roman Curia to regain control over Germany.

One notable controversy in his later career involved Philip of Hesse, who requested Luther's sanction to take a second wife. Luther, along with Melanchthon and Martin Bucer, signed a document sanctioning this bigamy in an exceptional case of "direst need." The document noted that while the original commandment of God and the law of the land mandate marriage between one man and one woman, the Gospel does not expressly prohibit bigamy.

Luther's influence extended to other languages; for example, Tyndale used Luther's Bible freely for marginal notes and prefaces, sometimes translating or paraphrasing long passages from it for his English translation.
