# Attempts on the English Throne and the Career of Sir Nicholas Bacon

## The Attempt to Install Lady Jane Grey
In 1553, an attempt was made to place Lady Jane Grey on the English throne. This effort was supported by John Dudley, the duke of Northumberland, and William, 13th Lord Grey de Wilton. Lord Grey de Wilton was a soldier of great fame who had served in France during the final years of Henry VIII's reign and had been a leader of the English army at the battle of Pinkie in 1547. Following the attempt to install Lady Jane Grey, Lord Grey was imprisoned in 1551 as a friend of the fallen protector, the duke of Somerset. However, he was eventually pardoned by Queen Mary and given the responsibility of defending Guines. Despite receiving indifferent support, he defended the town with gallantry until he was forced to surrender in January 1558, after which he remained a prisoner in France for a period.

## Career of Sir Nicholas Bacon
Sir Nicholas Bacon (1509–1579) served as the lord keeper of the great seal of England during the reign of Queen Elizabeth. Born at Chislehurst, he was the second son of Robert Bacon of Drinkstone, Suffolk. He received his education at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, where he graduated with a B.A. in 1527, and spent time in Paris. After returning to England and entering Gray's Inn, he was called to the bar in 1533.

Bacon entered public life in 1537 as the solicitor of the court of augmentations. He acquired several estates, primarily in the eastern counties, following the dissolution of the monasteries. His rise in official status continued with his appointment as a member of parliament for Dartmouth in 1545 and his appointment as attorney of the court of wards and liveries in 1546. By 1550, he had become a bencher, and in 1552, he served as the treasurer of Gray's Inn.

## Service Under Queen Elizabeth
Although Bacon held Protestant sympathies, he maintained his office in the court of wards during the reign of Mary, though an order was issued to prevent him from leaving the country. His career reached a significant peak upon the accession of Elizabeth in 1558. Due to a close and long friendship with his brother-in-law, Sir William Cecil (later Lord Burghley), Bacon was appointed lord keeper of the great seal in December 1558. He was subsequently knighted and named a privy councillor.

In his official capacity, Bacon presided over the House of Lords during the opening of Elizabeth's first parliament and helped secure the archbishopric of Canterbury for Matthew Parker. He did, however, find himself in opposition to Cecil regarding the policy of waging war on France to assist the enemies of Mary queen of Scots, citing England's poverty as the reason for his objection. Later, he supported a closer union with foreign Protestants, recognizing the danger posed by the aggressive religious policies of Scotland and France. In 1559, he was granted the full jurisdiction of lord chancellor.

## Style and Legacy
Bacon's contemporaries noted the peculiarities of his style. Sir Tobie Matthews described him as a man of rare and diverse knowledge who possessed a "facility and felicity" of expression, using metaphors and allusions in an elegant and abundant way.
