# Johann Sebastian Bach and the School of St Michael

## Early Life and Education
Johann Sebastian Bach was born in Eisenach and baptized on March 23, 1685. Following the death of his parents during his tenth year, he was placed under the care of his elder brother, Johann Christoph, who served as an organist at Ohrdruf. Johann Christoph provided Sebastian with music instruction, though he is reported to have been jealous of his younger brother's talent. This jealousy led him to forbid Sebastian from accessing a manuscript volume containing the works of great organists, including Buxtehude and Froberger.

In response, Sebastian spent six months waking up every night to copy the forbidden volume by moonlight, reaching through the lattice of the bookcase to do so. This effort resulted in the permanent ruin of his eyesight, a condition evident in his later blindness and reflected in all extant portraits of him from a later age. Once the copying was complete, his brother discovered the duplicate and confiscated it.

## Appointment at St Michael
In 1700, at the age of fifteen, Sebastian was forced to rely on his own resources following the death of his brother. He traveled to Lueneburg, where he was appointed as a chorister at the school of St Michael, a position he secured due to his beautiful soprano voice. 

While he held the position of chorister, it appeared that Bach focused his efforts more on instrumental music than on vocal music. Aside from the routine duties required of the choristers, his role at the school provided for his general education. There is little known regarding his specific musical instructors during this period.

## Musical Development and Influences
Bach's musical growth was primarily the result of his own incessant study of contemporary and classical composers. His studies included the works of:
* Frescobaldi (c. 1587)
* Caspar Kerl (1628-1693)
* Buxtehude
* Froberger
* Muffat the elder
* Pachelbel
* Johann Joseph Fux (1660-1741), the author of *Gradus ad Parnassum*, a work used for the training of all later classical composers.

Bach also undertook several holiday expeditions on foot to Hamburg to hear Reinken, a great Dutch organist. During one such trip, an anecdote recounts that Bach, unable to afford dinner while sitting outside an inn, found two ducats inside two herring-heads that had been thrown out of a window. He used the money to return to Hamburg rather than going home. While in Hamburg, he encountered the work of Keiser, who was establishing German opera on a splendid scale. Although this did not directly influence Bach's style, it served to fire his imagination.
