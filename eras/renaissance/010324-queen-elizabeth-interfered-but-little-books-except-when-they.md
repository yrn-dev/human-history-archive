# Press and Publication under Queen Elizabeth

## Censorship and the Book Trade
During the Tudor and Stuart eras, the English government did not tolerate a free press, and various measures were implemented to curb it. This trend began under Henry VIII, who in 1529 granted the first patent for the office of king's printer to Thomas Berthelet, stipulating that only licensed books could be printed. At that time, possessing or purchasing an unlicensed book was a punishable offense. In 1556, the Company of Stationers was incorporated and given extensive powers to repress obnoxious books.

While the Star Chamber exercised an effectual censorship in subsequent reigns, the high demand for polemical books led many to be printed abroad and smuggled into England. During the reign of Queen Elizabeth, interference with books was generally limited; she intervened primarily when works emanated from Roman Catholics or touched upon her royal prerogatives. Consequently, bookselling flourished toward the end of her reign and continued to do so under her successor, James.

## Religious Texts and Bibles
Following the accession of Queen Elizabeth, an effort was made to challenge the increasing popularity of the Calvinistic Genevan Bible. This led to the creation of the Bishops' Bible. The initiative was led by Archbishop Parker between 1563 and 1565. Parker organized the work by dividing the Bible into parcels and distributing them to learned men and bishops, who were tasked with perusing and collating the text and adding short marginal notes for correction or illustration.

## Literary Works and Dedications
The publication of literary works during this period often included accessories beyond the bare text, such as prefaces, indexes, complimentary verses, and dedicatory letters. An example of this is the 1590 first edition of the first three books of Spenser's *Faerie Queene*. The work included a dedication to Elizabeth, addressed to her as "The Most Mightie and Magnificent Empresse Elizabeth." The edition also featured a letter to Sir Walter Raleigh and various complimentary sonnets inscribed to figures such as the earls of Essex, Oxford, Northumberland, and Ormond, as well as other ladies and lords of the court.

## Administrative and Household Records
The administration of the royal household during Elizabeth's reign is documented in the *Household Book* of Queen Elizabeth. This record identifies the "lord steward" and the "master of the household and clerk of the green cloth." The lord steward was a peer, privy councillor, and the first dignitary of the court, presiding at the Board of Green Cloth—a committee responsible for examining household accounts and punishing offenders within the palace jurisdiction.
