# The Confessio Bohemica and the Articles of the Bohemian Estates

The *Confessio Bohemica* was a profession of faith developed by the estates of Bohemia during the 16th century. Its creation and subsequent attempts to secure its legal status were central to the religious and political conflicts between the Bohemian estates and their monarchs.

## Origins of the Confessio Bohemica
The *Confessio Bohemica* emerged from a period of prolonged deliberation during the sittings of the diet in 1575. At this time, the Bohemian estates were seeking the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg," which served as a summary of the teachings of Luther. Additionally, the estates sought the right to appoint members of the consistory, the ecclesiastical body responsible for ruling the Utraquist church, which had lacked an archbishop since the death of John of Rokycan.

Following the final refusal of King Maximilian to recognize the Confession of Augsburg, a majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drafted their own profession of faith, known as the *Confessio Bohemica*. While this document was identical to the Augsburg confession in most respects, it differed regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. Influenced by the Bohemian Brethren, the *Confessio Bohemica* aligned with the views of Calvin rather than those of Luther.

## Initial Reception under Maximilian
Upon its presentation to King Maximilian, the monarch provided verbal approval of the *Confessio Bohemica*. However, he refused to allow this approval to be made public and denied the request to include the *Confessio* among the charters of the kingdom. Maximilian also rejected the estates' demand to appoint the members of the consistory, though he did eventually allow the advanced Utraquists and Lutherans to be exempt from the consistory's jurisdiction and permitted them to choose fifteen defenders.

## The Twenty-Five Articles and King Rudolph
In the early 17th century, religious controversies in Bohemia were often influenced by personal enmities. King Rudolph, who had a vacillating nature, had previously declined to discuss religious matters while his brother's troops occupied portions of Bohemia. In March of the year preceding 1609, the estates drafted a document consisting of twenty-five "Articles" to formulate their religious demands.

The "articles" expressed the specific wish that the *Confessio Bohemica* be recognized as one of the fundamental laws of the kingdom. Beyond this, the articles demanded:
* The granting of complete religious liberty to all classes.
* Equal rights for Roman Catholics and Protestants (a term then used to collectively describe the Bohemian Brethren, Lutherans, and Utraquists) to hold all offices of state.
* Limitations on the ability of the Jesuits to acquire land.
* Redress for various grievances resulting from the misrule of King Rudolph.

## Conflict and Negotiation
King Rudolph considered the document of the twenty-five articles to be illegal and demanded that it be delivered to him for destruction. However, the document remained in the possession of Budova, who refused the king's demand. 

In response, the estates appointed twelve representatives to negotiate with the king and his councillors, a group that included Count Henry Matthias Thurn. Despite these protracted discussions, the conflict reached a stalemate on March 31, when the king stated that he could grant no concessions regarding matters of religion.
