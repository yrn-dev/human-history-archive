# Lutherans and Advanced Utraquists in Bohemia

In the religious landscape of Bohemia during the 16th and early 17th centuries, the Lutherans and advanced Utraquists emerged as prominent groups seeking reform and legal recognition. These groups, often aligned with the Bohemian Brethren, frequently clashed with the Roman Catholic Church and the restrictive legal frameworks of the Habsburg monarchs.

## The Utraquist Church and the Consistory
The Utraquist church was governed by an ecclesiastical body known as the consistory. Following the death of John of Rokycan, the church lacked an archbishop. By 1575, the Bohemian estates sought the right to appoint the members of the consistory themselves, rather than leaving the appointments to the monarch.

Historically, the Utraquists were divided. Moderate Utraquists differed little from the Roman church, except for their claim to receive communion in both kinds; they were granted freedom of worship under the "celebrated Compacts." However, advanced Utraquists held more progressive views. By the mid-16th century, some advanced Utraquists differed very little from German Lutherans. Because Ferdinand I used the Compacts as a tool to oppress both the Bohemian Brethren and the advanced Utraquists, the estates eventually expressed a desire for the Compacts to cease being part of the country's laws.

## The Confessio Bohemica and Lutheran Influence
A central point of contention for the Bohemian Protestants was the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg," a summary of Martin Luther's teachings. While the Augsburg Confession is a symbol of the Lutheran church recognized by all its members, King Maximilian initially refused to recognize it in Bohemia.

In response, a majority of the diet—comprising advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drafted a profession of faith known as the *Confessio Bohemica*. This document was largely identical to the Augsburg Confession, with one primary exception: the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. In this specific point, the *Confessio Bohemica* aligned with the views of Calvin rather than Luther, a shift attributed to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren. Although Maximilian verbally approved the *Confessio Bohemica*, he refused to make this approval public or include the document among the kingdom's charters.

## Legal Status and Jurisdiction
The relationship between the Bohemian reformers and the Habsburg monarchy was often defined by the king's need for financial subsidies for warfare against the Turks. The estates frequently leveraged these requests to demand religious concessions.

Under King Maximilian, a significant legal shift occurred regarding ecclesiastical authority. While the king rejected the estates' demand to appoint the consistory's members, he eventually consented to exempt the advanced Utraquists and the Lutherans from the jurisdiction of the consistory. As part of this arrangement, they were permitted to choose fifteen defenders—with five members drawn from each of the estates—to serve as their supreme representatives.

## Later Developments and the "Protestants"
By the early 17th century, the terms "Lutherans," "Utraquists," and "Bohemian Brethren" were collectively referred to as "Protestants." During the reign of Rudolph, these groups continued to seek comprehensive religious liberties. 

In March 1608, the estates drew up a document of twenty-five Articles. These articles demanded that the *Confessio Bohemica* be recognized as one of the fundamental laws of the kingdom and that complete religious liberty be granted to all classes. Furthermore, they sought an equal right for Protestants and Roman Catholics to hold all offices of state and requested limitations on the ability of the Jesuits to acquire land. Despite these efforts, Rudolph stated on March 31, 1609, that he could grant no concessions in matters of religion.
