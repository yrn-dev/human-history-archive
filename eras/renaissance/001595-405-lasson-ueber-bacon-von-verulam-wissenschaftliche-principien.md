# Lasson, Ueber Bacon von Verulam's wissenschaftliche Principien

## Overview
*Ueber Bacon von Verulam's wissenschaftliche Principien* is a work authored by Ad. Lasson and published in Berlin in 1860. It is categorized within the archive's records as a source for the study of the philosophy of Francis Bacon (Baron Verulam).

## Context within Baconian Scholarship
Lasson's work is situated among several 19th-century scholarly examinations of Francis Bacon's life and philosophy. Other contemporary texts include Kuno Fischer's *Fr. Bacon* (1856), G. L. Craik's *Lord Bacon, his Writings and his Philosophy* (1846-1847), and J. v. Liebig's *Ueber F. B. v. Verulam* (1863). These works collectively analyze Bacon's role as a philosopher, statesman, and essayist.

## Relation to the Criticisms of Liebig
The archive notes a significant scholarly conflict regarding the interpretation of Bacon's scientific methods, specifically involving the work of J. v. Liebig. Liebig criticized Baconian methods and results, basing his claims on the *Sylva Sylvarum*. However, these criticisms were severely commented upon by Fischer and Sigwart. 

Liebig's attacks included accusations that Bacon:
* Made induction start from the undetermined perceptions of the senses.
* Utilized imagination.
* Applied arbitrary interpretations to phenomena.

Critics of Liebig, including Fischer, argued that Liebig's own proposed "true scientific method" was actually the same Baconian doctrine he claimed to attack. Furthermore, Liebig was accused of ignoring Bacon's own descriptions of the nature of the *Sylva Sylvarum* and exaggerating errors while missing the core points of Bacon's arguments.

## Subject of Study: Francis Bacon
The subject of Lasson's study, Francis Bacon (1561-1626), was the youngest son of Sir Nicholas Bacon and was born at York House in London. During his time at Trinity College, Cambridge, Bacon concluded that the current Aristotelian philosophy was fruitless and served only for disputation. This led him to believe that a new method for philosophy had to be devised.

Bacon is described in various archive sources as the "apostle" of the scientific method and the "Father of Experimental Philosophy," though some sources suggest his actual share in the development of scientific work has been overstated. He is noted for his influence on systematic scientific publication and for his work *The New Atlantis*, in which he envisioned a "palace of invention" and a "temple of science" organized for the efficient pursuit of knowledge. His ideas were likely influenced by the work of Dr. Gilbert of Colchester, an experimental philosopher who focused on magnetism.
