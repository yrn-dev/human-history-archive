# Translation of Malvezzi's Discourses upon Cornelius Tacitus (1642)

The translation of Malvezzi's *Discourses upon Cornelius Tacitus*, published in 1642, is a work attributed to Sir Richard Baker, Knight. 

## Authorship and Context
Sir Richard Baker produced the translation of Malvezzi's discourses during a period of his life spent primarily in writing while imprisoned. This work was published in 1642, a year during which Baker was prolific, releasing several other titles including *Motives for Prayer upon the seaven dayes of ye weeke* and *Theatrum Redivivum, or The Theatre Vindicated*, the latter of which served as a response to William Prynne's *Histrio-Mastix*.

Baker's broader literary output included various religious and historical texts. His chief work, the *Chronicle of the Kings of England from the Time of the Romans' Government unto the Death of King James*, was published in 1643 and saw many subsequent editions, including a Dutch translation in 1649. While popular for many years, the *Chronicle* is noted for having slight historical value due to numerous inaccuracies. Other works by Baker include *Cato Variegatus* (1636), *Meditations on the Lord's Prayer* (1637), a translation of *New Epistles by Moonsieur D'Balzac* (1638), and an *Apologie for Laymen's Writing in Divinity* (1641). He also wrote meditations on several psalms of David.

## Subject of the Discourses: Cornelius Tacitus
The subject of the translation, Cornelius Tacitus, was a Roman historian whose works have been preserved and analyzed across various historical records. In the Roman Empire, the value of Tacitus's writing was recognized to the extent that an emperor once ordered ten copies of the historian's work to be transcribed annually for placement in public libraries. However, many of these Roman libraries perished, and a significant portion of Tacitus's work was preserved in a single manuscript discovered in a Westphalia monastery.

Tacitus's writings provide accounts of various Roman figures and events, though these accounts are sometimes contested by other historians. For example, Tacitus wrote that the procurator Felix exercised the authority of a king with the disposition of a slave, relying on his brother Pallas's power at court to commit wicked practices. Tacitus also claimed that before the death of Cumanus, Felix was procurator over Samaria only; however, this is noted as being contradicted by Josephus, who lived in Judea at the time. Additionally, Tacitus recorded that Caius commanded the Jews to place his effigies in their temple, though it is noted he may have been mistaken in claiming the Jews took arms in response.

Tacitus's influence extended to later historians. For instance, Pieter Cornelissen Hooft (1581-1647), a member of the Amsterdam school and author of the *History of Holland*, considered himself a disciple of Tacitus in his noble prose work.
