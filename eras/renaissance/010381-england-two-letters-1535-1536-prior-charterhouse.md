# England: The Charterhouse and Andrew Boorde (1535–1536)

## The Carthusian Order in England
The Carthusians are a religious order characterized by a life of almost entire isolation, with members living wholly cut off from the outer world. This manner of life, which resembles that of early Egyptian monks under the guidance of St. Anthony, is described as being half eremitical and half cenobitical. The order is governed by priors rather than abbots, with the prior of the Grande Chartreuse serving as the superior general.

The first English Charterhouse was established in 1178 at Witham, near Selwood Forest. By the time of the Dissolution, nine such houses existed in England, the most prominent being those located at Sheen in Surrey and at Smithfield in London. The Carthusians were distinguished as the only order to offer corporate resistance to the ecclesiastical policies of King Henry VIII. Consequently, the community at the London Charterhouse remained firm in its opposition, leading to the execution of the prior and several monks in 1535 under circumstances described as barbarous cruelty.

## Andrew Boorde and the Charterhouse
Andrew Boorde (born 1490?–1549) was an English author and physician born at Boord's Hill, Holms Dale, Sussex. Educated at Oxford, he entered the Carthusian order while still under age. Although he was "dispensed from religion" in 1521 to serve as suffragan bishop of Chichester—an office he never actually filled—he was not fully freed from his monastic vows until 1529. Boorde attributed his departure to an inability to endure the "rugorosite off your relygyon."

Following his release from vows, Boorde studied medicine abroad, visiting universities in Toulouse, Poitiers, Montpellier, Orleans, and Wittenberg. He also observed surgical practices in Rome and went on a pilgrimage to Compostella in Navarre. Despite his previous departure, Boorde was again in London at the Charterhouse in 1534.

## Correspondence of 1535 and 1536
During the period of 1535 and 1536, Boorde engaged in a series of communications regarding his status within the monastic order. He wrote two letters to the prior of the Charterhouse in these years, in which he argued anxiously for his complete release from his monastic vows. 

By 1536, Boorde also wrote to Thomas Cromwell, complaining that he was in a state of "thraldom" at the Charterhouse. Cromwell subsequently set him at liberty and entertained him at his home in Bishops Waltham, Hampshire. Following this, Cromwell appears to have tasked Boorde with a mission to investigate public sentiment abroad regarding the English king. While in Catalonia, Boorde sent Cromwell seeds of rhubarb, a plant that would not be generally cultivated in England for another two hundred years.

## Subsequent Activities of Andrew Boorde
After his release, Boorde continued his medical studies in Glasgow in 1536, where he recorded observations on the "devellyshe dysposicion" of Scottish men and their lack of favor toward Englishmen. Around 1538, he undertook an extensive journey to Jerusalem and nearly every European country except Turkey and Russia. He documented these travels in a full itinerary, which was sent to Cromwell but unfortunately lost.

Boorde eventually settled in Montpellier. Before 1542, he authored his *Dietary*, his *Brevyary*, and the *Fyrst Boke of the Introduction of Knowledge*, which is regarded as the earliest continental guide book. He likely returned to England in 1542, residing in Pevensey and Winchester.
