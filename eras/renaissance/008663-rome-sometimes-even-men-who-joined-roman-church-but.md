# Rome and the Roman Church

The history of Rome is characterized by a transition from a secular imperial dominion to a centralized religious authority. While the Latin-speaking Roman Empire died in the fifth century, the Latin-speaking half of the Catholic Church survived, utilizing the prestige and tradition of the empire to establish a universal dominion across Europe.

## The Early Roman State and Foundations
The origins of the Roman people are traced to Lavinium, the first town founded by Aeneas, which housed the most ancient sacred things and images of Rome's tutelary deities. In the early history of the city, Rome experienced periods of severe internal disorder, marked by daily seditious speeches and internal quarrels. During one such period of instability, the general Marcius gained immense renown throughout Italy after capturing Bollae, a town twelve miles from Rome, where he put nearly all adult inhabitants to the sword.

## The Rise of the Papacy
As the Roman Empire decayed, Christianity spread across Europe, eventually conquering the barbarians. The Patriarch or Pope of Rome emerged as the head of the entire Christian church. Following the disappearance of the emperors, the Pope began to annex imperial titles and claims, including the title of *pontifex maximus*, the most ancient title held by the emperors as the head sacrificial priest of the Roman dominion.

The Pope of Rome became the only Western patriarch, leading a vast region where Latin was the ruling tongue. This distinguished him from the patriarchs of the Orthodox Church, who spoke Greek. A final rupture between the Byzantine Christians and Rome occurred in 1054, triggered by the addition of the words *Filioque* to the Latin creed.

## Temporal and Spiritual Ambitions
Throughout the Middle Ages, the Church at Rome sought to ensure that the Pope himself, rather than any temporal prince, functioned as the emperor in effect. While the Popes often lacked armies, they possessed a vast propaganda organization through priests across the Latin world and exercised influence over the souls of men by holding the "keys of heaven and hell" in their imaginations.

The guiding influence in Rome was the grand idea of a divine world dominion and a "peace of Christ" throughout the earth, a vision expressed by St. Augustine. This ambition was maintained by an assembly of cardinals, priests, and educated officials, even when individual Popes were characterized by corruption, greed, or sensuality. For example, the Lateran was a den of uncleanness during the days of John XII.

## The Roman Church and European Politics
The Roman Church played a significant role in the political structure of Europe, acting as a "magician" that kept the corpse of the Roman Empire alive. Through Charlemagne, the tradition of the Roman Caesar was revived. For eleven centuries following Charlemagne, the Church was preoccupied with reviving Roman ascendancy on earth, treating it as its inheritance and using the faith of simple men to forward political schemes.

The centralized authority of Rome eventually overshadowed the episcopal system. In the high theory of episcopacy, bishops were seen as successors of the apostles and the essential ties of Catholic unity. However, this system failed to preserve unity even when the world was united under the Roman Empire. Within the Roman Catholic Church, groups such as the Febronians and Gallicans attempted to maintain the high doctrine of episcopacy against the claims of the Papacy.

## Challenges and Reformation
By the period of the Hussite wars, the Church of Rome faced strenuous opposition from independent religious writers such as Peter Chelcicky (died 1460), who expressed a horror of bloodshed and a determination to accept the decrees of worldly authorities while remaining an enemy of the Roman Church.

In Bohemia, tensions persisted regarding the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg" (a summary of Luther's teaching). While King Maximilian was influenced by the advice of the papal nuncio in Prague—who warned that concessions to Protestants would create difficulties in other dominions—the Bohemian estates sought the right to appoint members of the consistory, the ecclesiastical body ruling the Utraquist church.

Ultimately, the reign of the Latin mind, which had prioritized organization over curiosity and innovation, began to end between the thirteenth and sixteenth centuries. The rediscovery of Greek classics and the influence of Semite and Mongol cultures led Europeans to break away from the Latin tradition, resulting in the dismemberment and renewal of the Latin Church.
