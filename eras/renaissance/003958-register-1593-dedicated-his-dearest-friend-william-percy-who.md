# Percy

The name Percy is associated with various historical figures across different eras and regions, ranging from medieval nobility and military leaders to literary figures and explorers.

## Medieval and Early Modern Figures
In the 14th century, the Percy family was involved in the conflicts between Scotland and England. During a skirmish near Newcastle in 1388, the 2nd Earl of Douglas and Mar captured the pennon of Hotspur Percy. In August of that same year, Percy sought revenge at the Battle of Otterburn. Although the battle resulted in a victory for the Scots and the capture of Hotspur and his brother, the 2nd Earl of Douglas fell during the fight. This struggle was later celebrated in Scottish and English ballads, including "The Battle of Otterburn" and "Chevy Chase." The poet Sir Philip Sidney noted that the "olde song of Percy and Douglas" moved his heart more than a trumpet.

## Literary and Academic Connections
In the late 16th century, William Percy was a close associate of the English poet Barnabe Barnes. Barnes, the fourth son of Dr. Richard Barnes (the bishop of Durham), published a work titled *Parthenophil and Parthenophe, Sonnettes, Madrigals, Elegies and Odes*, which was entered on the Stationers' Register in 1593. Barnes dedicated this work to William Percy, referring to him as his "dearest friend." Percy also contributed a sonnet to the eulogies that were prefixed to a later work by Barnes titled *Offices*.

## Military and Political Figures
In the 17th century, Percy Kirke (c. 1646–1691) served as an English soldier. The son of George Kirke, a court official to Charles II and Charles I, he received his first commission in 1666 in the Lord Admiral's regiment and later served in the Blues. His military career included campaigns on the Rhine with Turenne and service with Monmouth at Maestricht in 1673. 

Kirke rose to the rank of lieutenant-colonel in 1680 and subsequently became colonel of one of the Tangier regiments. In 1682, he was appointed governor of Tangier. While he distinguished himself in this role, he was known for his wild life and rough manners. Following the evacuation of Tangier, his regiment—known as "Kirke's Lambs" due to their badge—returned to England. Kirke later served as a brigadier in Faversham's army and participated in the Revolution, leading to a promotion by William III. He commanded the relief of Derry and conducted his final campaign in Flanders in 1691, dying as a lieutenant-general in Brussels in October of that year. He was succeeded as colonel of the "Lambs" by his eldest son, Lieut.-General Percy Kirke (1684–1741).

## Other References
The name also appears in later academic and geographical contexts. Earl Percy is credited as the author of the 1901 work *Highlands of Asiatic Turkey*.
