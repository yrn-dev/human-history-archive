# Bohemia and Hungary

The histories of Bohemia and Hungary are closely linked through shared monarchs, territorial conflicts with the Ottoman Empire, and their eventual relationship with the House of Austria.

## Early Dynastic Ties and Governance
In the 13th century, the estates of Austria, seeking stability after a period of disorder, chose Ottakar, son of Wenceslaus I, king of Bohemia, as their duke in 1251. Ottakar, who became king of Bohemia in 1253, expanded his influence into Styria. Although Bela IV, king of Hungary, initially conquered Styria, a 1254 treaty confirmed Ottakar's possession of the land. Following a subsequent war, a treaty in March 1261 saw Bela recognize Ottakar as the duke of Styria.

By the late 15th century, the two crowns were again united. Vladislav was elected king of Hungary in 1490. His son, Louis, was crowned king of Bohemia at the age of three. Following Vladislav's death in 1516, Louis succeeded him. During Louis's minority, the Bohemian estates refused to allow his guardians—the emperor Maximilian I and Sigismund, king of Poland—to interfere in Bohemian affairs. This period saw the rise of powerful nobles, such as the supreme burgrave Zdenek Leo, lord of Rozmital.

A significant constitutional event occurred in Bohemia in 1517 at the diet of St Wenceslas. On October 24, the three estates agreed to live together in friendly intercourse. This settlement involved citizens renouncing certain privileges in exchange for the recognition of their municipal autonomy and their presence at the diet. When Louis arrived in Bohemia from Hungary in 1522, he dismissed state officials, including Leo of Rozmital, and appointed John of Wartenberg as burgrave and Charles of Munsterberg as regent.

## The Reign of Louis II and the Battle of Mohacs
Louis II (1506–1526) was the son of Vladislav II and Anne of Candale. Despite a precocious intellect and the ability to speak six languages, his constitution was feeble. His reign was characterized by instability; he was often a puppet of magnates and suffered such penury that he pawned his jewels for food and clothing. His guardians, Count George of Brandenburg-Anspach and Cardinal Bakócz, were noted for neglecting him and squandering royal revenues.

Internal strife intensified after the death of Bakócz, as figures such as János Zapolya, István Báthory, and István Verböczy fought for power. Simultaneously, the Turkish peril grew acute after Louis arrested the Ottoman envoy Berham in 1521 and refused a league with Suleiman the Magnificent against the Habsburgs.

The crisis culminated on August 28 or 29, 1526, at the battle of Mohacs. Louis had collected an army of 25,000 men at Tolna, but they were utterly defeated by Suleiman. King Louis was killed during the battle, drowning in a swollen river.

## Habsburg Succession and Ottoman Conflict
Following the death of Emperor Maximilian in 1519, his grandson Charles V succeeded him. Charles resigned his Austrian lands and his claims on Bohemia and Hungary to his brother, Ferdinand. This division separated the Spanish and Austrian branches of the Habsburgs.

The victory at Mohacs allowed the Turks to conquer and retain the greater part of Hungary until the peace of Karlowitz in 1699. Ferdinand spent much of his life combating the Turks and attempting to secure Hungary, facing an active rival in John Zapolya, who was supported by Suleiman. After the Turks besieged Vienna in 1530 and conducted several invasions, Ferdinand eventually agreed to pay an annual tribute to Suleiman for the small portion of Hungary he held, approximately 12,228 square miles.

Under later rulers, the relationship between the territories and the central authority fluctuated. Emperor Maximilian II (1564–1576), who was also king of Bohemia and Hungary, maintained a liberal policy but remained a tributary to the Turk. His successor, Rudolph II (1576–1612), was a fanatical Catholic who encouraged the Jesuits in the counter-Reformation. During his reign, Bohemia existed in a state of semi-independence, and Hungary preferred the Turk to the emperor. By the time of Matthias's death in 1619, Bohemia was practically independent. Ferdinand II, elected king of Bohemia in 1618, sought to establish Jesuit rule, an attempt that led to the Thirty Years' War.

## Later Imperial Struggles and Dissolution
The struggle to drive Ottoman power from Hungary continued under Leopold I. After the peace of Oliva (1657–60) safeguarded the Hungarian frontier, campaigns between 1662 and 1699 eventually expelled the Turks. However, internal unrest persisted. In 1683, invited by Hungarian malcontents and encouraged by Louis XIV, the Turks overran Hungary and reached the walls of Vienna, where they were defeated on September 12 by John Sobieski.

In the 19th century, constitutional conflicts arose as the Czechs of Bohemia and the Magyars of Hungary refused to recognize a common parliament, arguing it violated the historic rights of their respective crowns. This led to the superseding of the 1861 constitution in 1865.

Following the events of the World Wars, the Republic of Austria was required to recognize the complete independence of Hungary. Additionally, Austria had to recognize the independence of Czecho-Slovakia, with the boundaries of Bohemia and Moravia forming the border between Austria and Czecho-Slovakia. Property of predominant historic interest to the former kingdoms of Bohemia and Hungary could be transferred without payment.
