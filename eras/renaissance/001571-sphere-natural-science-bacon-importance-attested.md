# Francis Bacon and Natural Science

The importance of Francis Bacon in the sphere of natural science is evidenced by the extensive references to his work among the principal scientists of England, France, Germany, and Italy. He is regarded by many as a primary catalyst for the new scientific movement, with some hailing him as "le pere de la philosophie experimentale" (the father of experimental philosophy).

## International Influence and Recognition
Bacon's influence extended across Europe, as documented by T. Fowler, who collected quotations from leading figures such as Gassendi, Descartes, and Jean Baptiste du Hamel. In France, the *Encyclopedie* described him as the most eloquent, universal, and greatest of philosophers, while d'Alembert and Voltaire contributed to raising him to the pinnacle of his fame. Condillac further noted that no one understood the causes of human errors better than Bacon.

In Germany and other regions, critical yet valuable testimony was provided by Leibnitz, who referred to Bacon as "divini ingenii vir" (a man of divine genius) and grouped him with Campanella. Similarly, Huygens praised Bacon's "bonnes methodes." In France, Sorbiere credited Bacon as the person who most powerfully solicited the interests of physics and excited the world to conduct experiments.

## Impact on English Science and the Royal Society
Within England, one of Bacon's most significant achievements was the impetus his work *New Atlantis* provided for the foundation of the Royal Society. Dr. Thomas Sprat, the society's first historian and Bishop of Rochester, asserted that Bacon possessed the "true imagination of the whole extent" of the scientific enterprise. Sprat further maintained that Bacon's writings contain the best arguments for the experimental method of natural philosophy. This influence is also reflected in the works of Dr. John Wallis and Cowley's *Ode to the Royal Society*.

## Scientific Concepts and Methodology
Bacon's approach to science was rooted in the inductive method, which he discussed in the *Novum Organum*. He viewed the sciences as organically connected, arguing against the "disincorporation" of particular arts and sciences from general knowledge. He proposed a fundamental doctrine called *Prima Philosophia* (first philosophy) to display the unity of nature by connecting the highest axioms of various sciences into a single body of truth.

In his view, "forms" were not abstractions or ideas, but highly general physical properties. He held a firm grasp of the physical character of natural principles, though he regarded nature in a purely statical aspect. For Bacon, "cause" meant *causa immanens* (underlying substance), and effects were seen as manifestations rather than consequents.

Bacon emphasized two primary methods of knowledge: argument and experience. He argued that mere argument is insufficient for certainty, which can only be achieved through immediate inspection or intuition provided by experience. He specifically advocated for a "true natural history" consisting of instances, observations, connections, and canons. He believed that his *Organum* and the *Natural History* were correlative and equally necessary to produce a new philosophy.

## Critical Evaluations and Limitations
Despite his influence, Bacon's method has faced significant criticism. Some logicians and scientists, including Liebig and Brewster, have argued that the actual progress of science did not follow the Baconian method. Critics point out that no single discovery can be definitely attributed to his rules and that celebrated scientists often ignored his precepts in practice.

Major objections include:
* **Causal Misconception:** Critics argue Bacon had a false conception of the causal relation, treating scientific problems as matters of co-existence and assuming each phenomenon has only one cause.
* **Inductive Process:** The idea of forming axioms through a gradually ascending scale is viewed by some as a route that no science has ever followed or could follow to make progress.
* **Lack of Hypothesis:** It is argued that Bacon failed to recognize the deductive method and the power of framing hypotheses, which are central to true scientific procedure.

Bacon himself appeared to anticipate that the progress of science would eventually render his specific method antiquated.
