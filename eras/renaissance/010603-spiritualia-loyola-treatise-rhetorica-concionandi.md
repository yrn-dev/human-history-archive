# Spiritualia of Loyola and Rhetorica Concionandi

## The Spiritual Exercises of St. Ignatius of Loyola
The *Spiritual Exercises* (also referred to as *Exercitia Spiritualia*) are a central body of literature associated with St. Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556), the founder of the Society of Jesus. Born Inigo Lopez de Recalde in the province of Guipuzcoa, Loyola transitioned from a career in arms and service at the court of Ferdinand and Isabella to the foundation of the Jesuits.

The literature surrounding the *Spiritual Exercises* is extensive. Key texts and editions include:
* The official Latin text found in the third volume of the Avignon edition of the *Constitutions* (1830).
* A version by Roothaan, titled *Exercitia spiritualia S. P. Ignatii de Loyola, cum versione litterali ex autographo Hispanico, notis illustrata* (1841), which includes a literal translation from the Spanish autograph.
* *A Book of Spiritual Exercises* written by Garcias de Cisneros (published in London, 1876).
* *Historia exercitiorum S. P. Ignatii de Loyola* by Diertino (1887).
* *La Genèse des exercices de Saint Ignace de Loyola* by P. Watrigant, which was republished from *Les Études* in 1897.

## Works of St. Francis Borgia
St. Francis Borgia produced several published works, most notably a treatise titled *Rhetorica Concionandi*. Additionally, Borgia authored a series of *Exercises* that are described as being similar to the *Exercitia Spiritualia* of Loyola. The collected works of St. Francis Borgia, the *Opera Omnia*, were published in Brussels in 1675.

## Related Rhetorical Treatises
While *Rhetorica Concionandi* is a specific work by Borgia, the archive contains records of various other historical treatises on rhetoric. These include the *Rhetorica* (also known as *Auctor ad Herennium*), a work dedicated to Q. Herennius. This text is divided into four books: the first and second cover *inventio* and forensic rhetoric; the third addresses *dispositio*, *pronuntiatio*, *memoria*, and deliberative and demonstrative rhetoric; and the fourth focuses on *elocutio*. The author of this work aimed for clearness and conciseness (*breviter et dilucide scribere*), avoiding Greek rhetorical subtleties in favor of Roman terminology.

Other mentioned rhetorical works include Aristotle's *De Rhetoricâ* and *Rhetorica ad Alexandrum*, as well as various Greek and Roman traditions regarding the art of rhetoric and funeral orations.
