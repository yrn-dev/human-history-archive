# Wit's Trenchmour, The Wil of Wit, and A Poste with a Packet

The works *Wit's Trenchmour* (1597), *The Wil of Wit* (1599), and *A Poste with a Packet of Mad Letters* (1603) are prose tracts authored by the English poet and writer Nicholas Breton.

## Author Biography
Nicholas Breton (1545?–1626) was born into an old family from Layer-Breton, Essex. His father, William Breton, was a tradesman who died in 1559. Following this, his mother, Elizabeth Bacon, married the poet George Gascoigne. Breton was likely born in the parish of St Giles without Cripplegate at a "capitall mansion house" in Red Cross Street.

While there is no official record of his university attendance, the diary of the Rev. Richard Madox indicates that Breton was "once of Oriel College" and was present in Antwerp in 1583. In 1593, he married Ann Sutton and started a family. Breton's career was supported by a patron, Mary, countess of Pembroke, for whom he wrote extensively until approximately 1601. He is believed to have died shortly after the publication of his final work, *Fantastickes*, in 1626.

## Literary Style and Beliefs
Breton was a popular and facile writer during his own time, though he was forgotten by the following generation. His body of work is diverse, comprising satires, pastoral and religious poems, and various miscellaneous prose tracts. While his religious poetry is noted for its sweetness and fluency—reflecting a devout and earnest mind—his prose writings provide evidence that he was an ardent Protestant, despite suggestions of Catholicism based on his references to Mary Magdalene and the Virgin.

## Prose Works and Autobiographical Elements
Among Breton's twenty-two prose tracts are *Wit's Trenchmour* (1597), *The Wil of Wit* (1599), and *A Poste with a Packet of Mad Letters* (1603). The latter work was later enlarged in 1637. 

*A Poste with a Packet of Mad Letters* is considered a source for biographical information, as some of the letters signed "N.B." are viewed as autobiographical. In the 19th letter of the second part, the author expresses a series of personal grievances, claiming to have been wounded in wars, imprisoned, plagued by an unquiet life, long sick, and burdened by debts of "hearts griefe" that fame cannot pay.

## Other Works
In addition to his prose, Breton produced a wide array of poetical works, including:
* *The Workes of a Young Wit* (1577)
* *A Floorish upon Fancie* (1577)
* *The Pilgrimage to Paradise* (1592)
* *Marie Magdalen's Love: a Solemne Passion of the Soules Love* (1595)
* *A Divine Poem* (1601), which includes "The Blessed Weeper" and "The Ravisht Soul"
* *The Soules Heavenly Exercise* (1601)
* *An Excellent Poem, upon the Longing of a Blessed Heart* (1601)
* *The Soules Harmony* (1602)
* *The Mother's Blessing* (1602)
* *Olde Madcappe newe Gaily mawfrey* (1602)
* *A True Description of Unthankfulnesse* (1602)
* *The Passionate Shepheard* (1604)
* *The Honour of Valour* (1605)
* *The Soules Immortall Crowne* (1605)
* *An Invective against Treason; I would and I would not* (1614)

Breton also contributed to other miscellanies of verse, such as *England's Helicon*. Some works are only partially his, such as *The Arbor of Amorous Devises* (1594). Other titles, including *The Passion of a Discontented Mind* (1601), *Mary Magdalen's Lamentations* (1604), and *Sir Philip Sidney's Ourania by N.B.* (1606), have been erroneously attributed to him.
