# Harrow and Elizabethan Education: The Influence of Roger Ascham

The educational landscape of the Elizabethan era was characterized by a mix of private tuition, university scholarship, and the development of public schools. A central figure in this period was Roger Ascham, whose own unconventional early education and subsequent pedagogical theories influenced the statutes of several prominent institutions.

## The Early Education of Roger Ascham
Roger Ascham did not receive his primary education at a formal school. Instead, he was educated through private tuition in the home of Sir Humphry Wingfield, a barrister who served as speaker of the House of Commons in 1533. Sir Humphry was known for his fondness for having many children brought up in his house, where they were supervised by a tutor named R. Bond.

A distinctive feature of this private tuition was the emphasis on physical activity, specifically archery. Sir Humphry Wingfield would personally bring bows and shafts from London during term times to accompany the students into the field to shoot. This experience deeply impacted Ascham, leading him to attribute great importance to archery within educational establishments. This influence is evident in his earliest English work, *Toxophilus*, and is reflected in the provision for archery found in the statutes of Elizabethan schools, including Harrow and St Albans.

## University Training and Academic Development
Around 1530, at the age of fifteen, Ascham entered St John's College, Cambridge, which was regarded at the time as the most learned and largest college in either university. During his tenure, he was influenced by Sir Thomas Smith and John Cheke, the latter of whom was admitted as a fellow during Ascham's first year. Ascham also benefited from the guidance of Robert Pember, noted for his facility in the Greek tongue.

Under Pember's advice, Ascham adopted a specific method for mastering Greek: while still a boy, he taught Greek to other boys to accelerate his own learning. His Latin studies focused specifically on the works of Caesar and Cicero. Ascham earned his B.A. on February 18, 1534/5. Despite the college master, Dr. Nicholas Metcalfe, being a "papist," he provided praise and exhibition to students who excelled in the "new learning." However, Ascham's election to a fellowship was marked by conflict; after he spoke against the Pope, he faced rebuke and punishment, and other fellows were warned not to vote for him.

## Tutorship and Pedagogical Views
Ascham's career extended to the instruction of high-profile students. Through the influence of Sir William Cecil and the wishes of Princess Elizabeth, Ascham was selected as her tutor, replacing Grindal. For two years, primarily at Cheshunt, he taught the sixteen-year-old princess. Her curriculum included the Greek Testament, select orations of Isocrates, the tragedies of Sophocles, Melanchthon's Commonplaces, and St Cyprian. In Latin, she drew her knowledge from Titus Livius and almost all of Cicero. Ascham praised the princess for her industry, wisdom, and her ability to speak French, Italian, English, Latin, and moderately in Greek.

Ascham's views on school discipline were formalized in his famous work, *The Scholemaster*, begun in 1563. The impetus for this work occurred during the "great plague" in London, while the court was at Windsor. During a dinner with Sir William Cecil and other ministers, Cecil reported that several scholars from Eaton had run away from their school to escape beatings. This sparked a debate between "floggers and anti-floggers," with Ascham acting as the champion for those opposed to corporal punishment. This stance was further reinforced by Sir Richard Sackville, who noted that a "fond schoolmaster" had used brutality to make him hate learning, a loss he wished to avoid for his own son.

## Institutional Connections and Legacy
Ascham maintained strong ties to academic institutions. From 1541, he served as a letter-writer for his college and the university. In 1548, he wrote a notable letter to the protector Somerset on behalf of Sedbergh school, an institution attached to St John's College by Dr. Lupton in 1525, whose endowment had been confiscated under the Chantries Act. By 1546, Ascham had been elected public orator by the university.

The influence of these Elizabethan educational standards persisted into later centuries. For example, George Butler, who served as the head master of Harrow from 1805 to 1829, was noted for a successful administration attributed to his tact, all-round knowledge, and skill as an athlete.
