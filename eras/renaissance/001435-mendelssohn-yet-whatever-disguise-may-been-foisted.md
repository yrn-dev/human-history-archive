# Mendelssohn

The name Mendelssohn is associated with significant contributions to the fields of music and aesthetic theory, most notably through the rediscovery of the works of J.S. Bach and the development of theories regarding human sentiments.

## Influence on Music and the Bach Revival

Mendelssohn played a pivotal role in the history of music by rediscovering the art of J.S. Bach. Prior to this, Bach's work had been neglected by younger contemporaries who viewed it as crabbed and old-fashioned; it had survived only as the subject of unintelligent and desultory academic study. Mendelssohn was among the first to proclaim the powers of Bach's genius through both word and deed.

Alongside Schumann and others, Mendelssohn's enthusiastic efforts helped rapidly increase the circle of Bach's worshippers. This revival is noted as being obviously connected to certain tendencies within the "Romantic" movement in music.

## Musical Development and the St Matthew Passion

Mendelssohn's own musical trajectory was significantly shaped by his discovery of Bach's *Passion according to St Matthew* at the age of twelve. This work influenced Mendelssohn's approach to the general design of oratorios. By applying these insights, he was able to rescue the oratorio from the operatic influences that had ruined Beethoven's *Mount of Olives*.

Additionally, Mendelssohn is noted for his early musical proficiency. When comparing the early works of various composers, the pianoforte quartets written by Beethoven at age fifteen are described as clumsy and childish in execution, a quality that contrasts remarkably with the works produced during the boyhood of Mendelssohn, Mozart, or Schubert.

## Contributions to Aesthetic Theory

In the realm of philosophy and aesthetics, Moses Mendelssohn developed influential theories on the sentiments. These theories were informed in part by the work of Burke; specifically, Mendelssohn borrowed at least one of his most fruitful and important ideas from Burke's *Philosophical Inquiry into the Origin of our Ideas on the Sublime and Beautiful*. This text, published in 1756, had attracted the attention of the rising aesthetic school in Germany and was translated and annotated by Lessing.
