# Richard Masters and the Case of Elizabeth Barton

## Introduction
Richard Masters was a parish priest who played a pivotal role in bringing the activities of Elizabeth Barton to the attention of the ecclesiastical authorities in early 16th-century England. Through his reports, the case of Barton, known as "the maid of Kent," reached the highest levels of the church hierarchy, specifically the Archbishop of Canterbury.

## The Discovery of Elizabeth Barton
Elizabeth Barton was born in 1506 in Aldington, Kent. In her nineteenth year, she suffered from an illness that resulted in religious mania and hysteria. At the time of this illness, Barton was employed as a servant for Thomas Cobb, who served as the steward of an estate near Aldington. This estate was owned by William Warham, the Archbishop of Canterbury.

During her period of convalescence, Barton entered into trances that could last for several days. While in these states, she delivered ravings characterized by "marvellous holiness in rebuke of sin and vice." These utterances led the local country folk to believe that Barton was inspired. Thomas Cobb reported these occurrences to Richard Masters, the parish priest, who subsequently acquainted Archbishop Warham with the matter.

## The Growth of Barton's Influence
After her recovery, Barton found herself the object of admiration within her local community. She later confessed during her trial that she was cunning enough to feign trances to continue her prophecies. As her fame grew, Archbishop Warham took further action in 1526, instructing the prior of Christ Church, Canterbury, to dispatch two monks to conduct an inquiry into her case.

One of these monks, Edward Bocking, facilitated Barton's admission as a nun to the convent of St Sepulchre in Canterbury. Under the instruction of Bocking, Barton's prophecies became more remarkable. She asserted that she was in direct communication with the Virgin Mary, a claim that attracted many pilgrims.

## Political Utterances and Downfall
Barton began to direct her utterances toward political matters, creating a widespread and profound sensation. Most notably, she declared that if King Henry persisted in his intention to divorce Catherine, he would die a "villain's death" and would no longer be the king of the realm. Her influence was such that high-ranking figures, including Sir Thomas More and Fisher, the bishop of Rochester, corresponded with her.

In 1532, while passing through Canterbury upon his return from France, King Henry is said to have allowed Barton to force herself into his presence in an attempt to terrify him into abandoning his marriage. Following the solemnization of the marriage in May 1533, Barton's utterances became increasingly treasonable. She was eventually examined and confessed before Cranmer, who had succeeded Warham as archbishop following Warham's death in March.
