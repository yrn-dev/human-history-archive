# The Buccaneers of the West Indies

## Origins and the Butcher Trade
The emergence of the buccaneers in the West Indies was rooted in the colonial conditions of the 16th century. The island of Santo Domingo had been almost depopulated due to the oppressive colonial policies of Spain. Spanish officials presided over isolated coastal establishments, but the mother country's imposed monopolies deprived these settlers of convenient markets for their produce. Consequently, vessels from France, Holland, and England were welcomed, and their cargoes were bought.

The island's depleted population left behind immense herds of wild cattle, making Santo Domingo a provisioning point for smugglers. The remaining natives possessed skills in preserving flesh at small establishments known as *boucans*. Adventurers adopted this practice of "boucanning" from the natives, which led to the development of an extensive and illicit butcher trade on Hispaniola.

## The Rise of the Buccaneer
The buccaneer emerged from these conditions as a figure who functioned alternately as a hunter, sailor, and occasionally a planter. These individuals were characterized as bold, unscrupulous, roving, and often savage. They harbored an intense detestation of Spain, a sentiment fueled by Spanish monopolies and the refusal of Spaniards to recognize the right of other races to trade or establish settlements in the West Indies.

Because of this Spanish intransigence, the governments of England, France, and Holland did not control their subjects who invaded the islands, leaving them to establish settlements at their own risk. Various nations contributed bands of colonists to the West Indies, including the island of St Kitts (also known as St Christopher), where English and French settlers were planted simultaneously. Tensions existed between the English and French; in 1629, after some English settlers moved to an adjoining island, a Spanish fleet of thirty-nine sail surprised and partly dispersed the remaining colonists. However, the scattered bands returned after the fleet departed, and encouragement was provided to their countrymen in Santo Domingo.

## Decline and Extinction
The community of buccaneers eventually faced a period of decline. While they continued to operate along the coasts of Santo Domingo and Jamaica, their influence waned. They appeared significant one final time during the siege of Carthagena, though they served merely as auxiliaries to French regular forces rather than as the architects of the expedition. Despite a temporary reawakening of "despairing energy" and unity following the treachery of the French commander of that expedition, the community's extinction approached rapidly.

The bond of unity that had lasted for three-quarters of a century—keeping the subjects of England and France together in aggressive schemes against a common foe—was broken in 1689. This occurred because the French and English buccaneers were forced to take sides in the war that arose between their respective home countries.

Between 1697 and 1700, during a short period of peace, France and England attempted to gain favor with the king of Spain through their influence in both the New and Old Worlds. The career of the buccaneers was effectively closed in 1700 with the resumption of hostilities and the rise of Spain following the accession of the French claimant to the throne.

## Related Maritime Activity
Beyond the buccaneers, the West Indies remained a site of various international maritime interests. In 1688, Davis arrived in the West Indies after clearing Cape Horn. His arrival followed a period of activity in the Pacific, where a "mysterious band of corsairs" had ravaged the region until the Frenchmen and their companions crossed New Spain to reach the West Indies, bringing peace to the Pacific.

In later centuries, the region continued to be a strategic naval area. During later conflicts, the German cruiser *Karlsruhe* operated in the West Indies, providing a challenge to hunting vessels. Additionally, British naval dispositions included maintaining the ship *Essex* in the West Indies.
