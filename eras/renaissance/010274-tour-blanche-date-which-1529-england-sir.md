# Book-plates and Early Ex-Libris

The book-plate, also referred to as an *ex-libris*, is a printed label used to indicate the ownership of individual volumes. This practice is nearly as old as the printed book itself. In terms of its function and relationship to the books it marks, the book-plate is comparable to the symbolical personal devices or hand-painted armorials found in medieval manuscripts, just as the printed page is related to the work of a scribe.

## Early German Examples
The earliest known examples of book-plates originated in Germany. According to Friedrich Warnecke of Berlin, a leading authority on the subject, the oldest movable *ex-libris* are specific woodcuts. These woodcuts depict a shield of arms supported by an angel and were hand-painted to imitate devices used in old manuscripts. These particular plates were pasted into books that Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach presented to the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim around the year 1480, a date established by the record of the gift.

## European Development
Following the German examples, book-plates appeared in other European nations:

*   **France:** The most ancient *ex-libris* discovered in France belonged to Jean Bertaud de la Tour-Blanche and is dated 1529.
*   **England:** An early English example is a gift-plate used by Sir Nicholas Bacon for books he presented to the university of Cambridge.
*   **Holland:** A plate belonging to Anna van der Aa dates to 1597.
*   **Italy:** An Italian example is attributed to the year 1622.

## American Examples
The earliest known example of a book-plate in America is a plain printed label belonging to John Williams, dated 1679.
