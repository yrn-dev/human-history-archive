# Francis Bacon's Philosophical Concepts

Francis Bacon's philosophical framework incorporates various concepts regarding the nature of reality, the structure of science, and the methodology of theoretical analysis. His work frequently interacts with classical Greek philosophy, specifically the ideas of Plato and Aristotle.

## The Concept of Idola
Bacon utilizes the term *Idola*, a word manifestly borrowed from Plato. In the context of Platonic thought, the *eidolon* represents a fleeting and transient image of a real object. Bacon uses this term in connection with Platonic Ideas, contrasting the *Idola* with them as "the false appearance." This conceptualization is further elaborated in his work *Advancement*, bk. i. Scholars have noted comparisons between Bacon's *Idola* and the *Offendicula* of Roger Bacon.

## Influence of Aristotle and the Order of Nature
Certain elements of Bacon's theory are derived from Aristotle, including his classification of causes and his notion of "form." Specifically, Bacon refers to that which is "better known in the order of nature," a concept found in Aristotle's *An. Post.* 71 b 33, *Topic* 141 b 5, and *Eth. Nic.* 1095 a 30. There is scholarly debate regarding the precise phrasing of this concept, with some writers arguing for the phrase *notiora natura* and others for *notiora naturae*.

## Prima Philosophia and the Scale of Nature
Bacon's conception of *Prima Philosophia* involves a scale of nature that corresponds with a scale of ascending axioms. However, Bacon does not detail how such a science is to be constructed or how it can maintain an independent method while serving as a receptacle for generalizations from various other sciences. Because its content varies based on the progress of those sciences, the construction of *Prima Philosophia* remains an open question in his work.

## Final Causes and Physics
Bacon maintains a strict distinction between physical causes and final causes. He asserts that the consideration of final causes must be rigidly excluded from physics because the knowledge of final causes does not lead to practical works. Despite this exclusion, he posits that there is no inherent opposition between the two. In ultimate resort, the mind is led to view the universe as the work of reason and to refer facts to Providence and God. Additionally, Bacon suggests that the idea of the final cause remains fruitful in sciences that deal with human action.

## Theoretical Analysis and Practical Operation
In his methodology, Bacon suggests that the final step of theoretical analysis provides the initial means for practical operation. This approach is compared to a principle in Aristotle's *Eth. Nic.* iii. 3. 12, which states that the last thing in the analysis is the first in the genesis.
