# Marin Mersenne and Musical Instrumentation

Marin Mersenne was a figure of significant scientific and musical interest in the 17th century, known for his detailed descriptions and illustrations of various musical instruments. A member of the order of Minim friars, Mersenne maintained a close intellectual relationship with the philosopher René Descartes, serving as Descartes' literary and scientific representative in Paris.

## Analysis of the Phagotus and Bassoon
Mersenne provided critical analysis regarding the origins of the bassoon (or fagotto). He examined the work of Theseus Ambrosius Albonesius, who attributed the invention of the bassoon to a canon of Ferrara named Afranio. While Albonesius suggested a relationship between Afranio's "phagotus" and the bassoon, Mersenne disagreed. 

Mersenne did not consider Afranio the inventor of the bassoon, but rather the inventor of a different type of fagotto. He classified this instrument with the Neapolitan *sourdeline*, which is a complex variety of musette. According to Mersenne, Afranio's instrument consisted of two *bassons* interconnected by tubes and powered by bellows. He noted that, similar to the *sourdeline*, these instruments only produce sound when the springs (keys) are open. Furthermore, Mersenne dismissed the etymology of the name "phagotus" proposed by Theseus Albonesius, characterizing it as fanciful.

## Descriptions of Bag-pipes and Musettes
Mersenne provided comprehensive descriptions and illustrations of various bag-pipes used in France, Italy, and the Netherlands. He detailed the *cornemuse* and *chalemie*, which preceded the advent of the musette. He identified two distinct types of *cornemuse* used during the 16th and 17th centuries:

*   **The Shepherd's Cornemuse:** Used as a solo instrument, this variety featured a leather bag inflated by a valved blow-pipe, a chaunter with a conical bore and double reed, and two drones (a *gros bourdon* and a *petit bourdon*) both tuned to C.
*   **The Concert Cornemuse:** Played with a family of instruments called *Hautbois de Poitou*, this version featured a single drone with a double reed, rather than a beating-reed.

Mersenne also provided a full description of the musette, an instrument inflated by a small arm-worked bellows. The musette was characterized by a compact barrel droner containing several drones and a chaunter with a narrow cylindrical bore and a double reed.

Additionally, Mersenne described the *surdelina* (or *sampogna*), also known as the *musette de Naples*. He attributed its invention to Dom Julio, Vincenze, and Jean Baptiste Riva, though Mersenne appears to have made his own alterations to the original design. This complicated instrument featured two drones with ten keys each and two chaunters with narrow cylindrical bores, all utilizing double reeds and inflated by bellows.

## Observations on Other Instruments
Mersenne's writings extended to a wide array of other musical devices:

*   **The Cornet:** Mersenne wrote a eulogium of the *dessus* cornet, which was frequently used in vocal concerts and to accompany the organ. He described the tone of the *dessus* as resembling "the brilliance of a sunbeam piercing the darkness" when played in cathedrals, chapels, or churches. He further asserted that the serpent serves as the true bass of the cornet, stating that the two together are like "body" and "soul."
*   **The Dulcimer:** Mersenne described and illustrated the dulcimer, which he referred to as the *psalterion*. He noted that the instrument featured thirteen courses of pairs of octaves or unisons, using steel wire for most strings and brass wire for the first. He observed that the use of a curved stick, which rebounds many times upon the strings, creates an effect similar to a tremolo.
