# Le Lys dans la vallee

## Overview
*Le Lys dans la vallee* is a literary work authored by the writer Balzac. Within the context of Balzac's creative output, the work features a character known as Mme de Mortsauf.

## Inspiration and Origins
The character of Mme de Mortsauf in *Le Lys dans la vallee* was based on a real-life figure from Balzac's life, Madame de Berny. Madame de Berny was a woman of a higher social rank than Balzac and was connected with the court of the *ancien regime*. She was the mother of nine children and was introduced to Balzac in 1821. 

Balzac referred to Madame de Berny as *La dilecta*. She was one of at least three women of higher rank who served as "Egerias" to the author; the archive notes that it is hardly extravagant to suggest that Balzac would not have been the writer he became without the influence of these women.

## Context of the Author's Work
*Le Lys dans la vallee* exists alongside a vast body of work produced by Balzac. His output during the early 1830s was particularly prolific:
* **1830:** This "epoch year" saw the publication of *La Maison du chat qui pelote* and part of *La Peau de chagrin*, as well as numerous shorter works including *Gobseck*, *La Vendetta*, and *Une Passion dans le desert*.
* **1831:** *La Peau de chagrin* was completed and published alongside *Le Chef-d'oeuvre inconnu* and *Maitre Cornelius*.
* **1832:** Works produced included *Le Colonel Chabert*, *Louis Lambert*, and *Les Marana*.
* **1833:** This year saw the publication of the masterpiece *Eugenie Grandet*, *L'Illustre Gaudissart*, and *Le Medecin de campagne*.

## Authorial Background
The creation of works like *Le Lys dans la vallee* occurred during a period of lifelong financial pressure for Balzac. His debts were not primarily the result of extravagant spending, but rather financial irregularities. These included speculations, travel, the collection of curiosities, costly production methods—such as writing novels on a series of printed revises—and the anticipation of earnings. Despite these pressures and a lifestyle that occasionally rendered him a vagrant or a hermit, Balzac was regarded as one of the most good-natured men of genius and maintained positive relationships with most famous men of letters of his era, including Hugo.
