# Cowley and the Royal Society

## Influence on the Royal Society
The works of Francis Bacon provided a significant impetus for the establishment of the Royal Society. Dr. Thomas Sprat, the first historian of the society and bishop of Rochester (1635–1713), asserted that Bacon possessed the "true imagination of the whole extent" of the society's enterprise. Sprat further noted that Bacon's writings contain the most effective arguments for the experimental method of natural philosophy. In this context, Cowley's *Ode to the Royal Society* is cited as a relevant reference, alongside remarks by Dr. John Wallis.

## Poetic Contributions and Style
Cowley is noted for his contributions to the evolution of English poetry between the eras of Spenser and Milton. He experimented with the formal ode, specifically utilizing an irregular "Pindaric" approach to strophe-grouping. 

In addition to his odes, Cowley authored the *Davideis* (1656). Along with the works of Denham (specifically *Cooper's Hill*, 1642) and Ogilby's *Aeneid*, Cowley's writing helped clear the path for Dryden, who became the primary master of the rhetorical couplet as blank verse declined.

## Literary Transition and Reputation
Cowley is identified as a figure marking the transition in poetry from the "poetry of the imagination" to the "poetry of the intelligence." In this regard, he is compared to Davenant, though the archive notes that Cowley possessed far more genius than Davenant.

Cowley's reputation for wit was recognized by his contemporaries. Sir John Denham once remarked that if Cowley had never spoken and Killigrew had never written, the two combined would have constituted a "matchless wit."
