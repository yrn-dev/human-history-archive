# The Barbarossa Family and Naval Conflict in North Africa

## Origins and the Barbarossa Name
The name "Barbarossa" (meaning "Redbeard" in Latin, from *barba*) was a nickname applied by Christians to a family of 16th-century Turkish sea rovers and admirals. This family included Arouj, his brother Khizr (also known as Khair-ed-Din), and Hassan, the son of Khair-ed-Din. While some later historians, such as Captain Walsin Esterhazy in 1840, suggested the name was a mispronunciation of "Baba Arouj," contemporary evidence contradicts this; the prefix "Baba" was not used for Arouj by those living at the time. Furthermore, an Arab chronicle published in 1837 explicitly states that the name Barbarossa was applied by Christians specifically to Khair-ed-Din. In Spanish or Italian records, Arouj is referred to as "Orux," "Harrach," or "Ordiche."

The family was founded by Yakub, a Roumeliot likely of Albanian descent who settled in Mitylene following the Turkish conquest. Yakub worked as a skipper and coasting trader and had four sons born after 1482: Elias, Isaak, Arouj, and Khizr. While Khizr became a potter and Isaak a trader, Elias and Arouj pursued a life of sea roving.

## Rise of Arouj and Khair-ed-Din
Arouj's early career was marked by capture and service to various powers. During an engagement with a galley of the Knights of Saint John based at Rhodes, Elias was killed and Arouj was taken prisoner. After being ransomed by a Turkish pasha, Arouj returned to the sea and served the Mamelukes in Egypt. However, during the conflict between the Mamelukes and Sultan Selim I, Arouj moved to Tunis. In northern Africa, he found employment as a mercenary amidst the constant conflicts between Berber princes, supplementing this work with piratical raids against Christian trade.

In Tunis, Arouj was joined by his brother Khizr, who adopted the name Khair-ed-Din, and later by their brother Isaak. The brothers collaborated with exiled Moors from Granada—who had been driven into exile by the Catholic sovereigns of Spain in 1492—to conduct raids on the Spanish coast. Arouj and Khair-ed-Din also sought to increase their power by supplanting or fighting native African princes. They established their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes.

## Conflict with Spain and Turkish Influence
The activities of the Barbarossa brothers and the exiled Moors prompted Spain to take defensive measures, leading to the Spanish conquest of several coast towns, including Tunis, Algiers, and Oran. In 1512, Arouj and Khair-ed-Din attempted to seize Bougie from the Spaniards but were beaten off. 

Arouj eventually fell in battle against the Spaniards in 1518. Following this loss, Khair-ed-Din appealed to the Turkish Sultan Selim for assistance. The Sultan provided troops, which enabled Khair-ed-Din to drive the Spaniards out of a fort located on a rocky island in front of Algiers in 1529. This victory established the foundation of Turkish power in the region.

## Governance of Northern Africa
From 1518 until 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli. Following the death of Uluch Ali in 1587, the region was governed by Turkish pashas dispatched from Constantinople for three-year terms. This system lasted until 1659, when a military revolt in Algiers stripped the pashas of their power. From 1659 onward, these African cities functioned as anarchical military republics. Although they remained nominally part of the Turkish empire, they lived by plunder and elected their own rulers.
