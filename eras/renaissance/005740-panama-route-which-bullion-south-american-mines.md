# Panama

Panama is a region characterized by its strategic geography as an isthmus, serving as a critical transit point between the Atlantic and Pacific Oceans. Historically, it functioned as a vital route for the transport of bullion—gold and silver from mines brought to a standard of purity—from South American mines to Porto Bello, where it was then shipped to Spain.

## Early Exploration and Colonial Conflict
Spanish explorers were among the first to cross the Isthmus of Panama, sighting the Pacific Ocean prior to the voyage of the Portuguese sailor Magellan. Magellan, sailing for the Spanish King in 1519, eventually entered the Pacific via the Strait of Magellan after coasting south of South America.

By the 17th century, the route through Panama became a target for buccaneers. In 1671, a force of 2,000 buccaneers, operating under commissions granted by Thomas Modyford, the governor of Jamaica, launched a major attack on the Isthmus. After seizing Chagres and completing a difficult journey via canoes and on foot, the force engaged and routed the defenders of Panama city. The city was subsequently taken and burnt, an event marked by great barbarities. Although the city was sacked, the Spaniards had managed to remove the treasure before the buccaneers arrived. The expedition ended poorly for many participants; many died of starvation during the return to Jamaica, and the leader, Morgan, was accused of defrauding his followers. Morgan was imprisoned in the Tower in 1672, though he later returned to Jamaica as lieutenant-governor in 1674. In his final years, Morgan worked to suppress the buccaneers.

## Political and Territorial Aspirations
In the mid-19th century, Panama featured in the territorial ambitions of the American South. During the period leading up to the American Civil War, Southerners—fearing the predominance of the anti-slavery North in Congress and the rise of the Abolitionist movement—dreamed of annexing territories in the West Indies and Mexico. Their vision included the creation of a great slave state that would detach from the North and extend as far as Panama.

In later history, the region became central to American world politics. The history of Panama is described as purely American history, reflecting a tradition that rejects the idea of "subject peoples," a spirit similar to that which created the Dominions of Australia, Canada, and South Africa.

## The Panama Canal and Naval Strategy
The construction of the Panama Canal transformed the isthmus into a strategic naval chokepoint. During the World Crisis, the canal was a key consideration in naval movements in South American waters. For example, the British monitored the movements of Admiral von Spee, who had the option of steaming northward along the West Coast of South America toward the Panama Canal. 

To counter such a move, the British deployed the West Indian Squadron to the canal. Had von Spee passed through the Panama Canal, he would have encountered the French *Conde* as well as the *Berwick*, *Lancaster*, and *Princess Royal*.
