# Balthasar Hubmaier and Hans Denck

Balthasar Hubmaier (ca. 1480–1528) and Hans Denck (ca. 1495–1527) were scholarly figures who joined the Anabaptist movement during its early development in the 16th century. They are associated with a religious community that sought to model itself after primitive Christianity, rejecting traditions and accretions that appeared after the records of the New Testament.

## The Anabaptist Movement
The Anabaptist movement began in Zurich, initiated by individuals including Felix Manz, Konrad Grebel, Simon Strumpf, and Wilheld Reubli. This group separated from Zwingli to establish a separate church based on brotherly love. Their core tenets included the repudiation of the use of force and the advocacy of a scriptural communism of goods. They believed that Christians should remain independent of worldly tribunals by exercising patience and love toward one another.

The most radical doctrine of the movement was the rejection of infant baptism (paedobaptism), which they viewed as unscriptural. This stance led to the condemnation and banishment of several members by Zwingli. Following this banishment, the movement was formally instituted when Joerg Blaurock, Konrad Grebel, and others baptized one another and shared the Lord's Supper.

## Contributions of Hubmaier and Denck
Balthasar Hubmaier and Hans Denck are described as "scholarly saints" who joined the rapidly growing community of Anabaptists. Hubmaier is noted for having baptized 300 converts in 1525, though he brought no new adherents with him. The method of baptism practiced by Hubmaier involved sprinkling or pouring from a single bucket, rather than immersion.

While Hubmaier is remembered for his teaching and martyrdom, Hans Denck is characterized by his piety and "gentle learning." The archive notes that Denck was among those of whom "the world was not worthy."

## Core Beliefs and Practices
Hubmaier, Denck, and their fellow Anabaptists held several central articles of faith:

*   **Inner Light:** They believed in religious feeling, conscience, or "inner light" as the sole true ground or beginning of religion.
*   **Non-Resistance:** They maintained that true Christians are "like sheep among wolves" and must not defend themselves.
*   **Baptism of Believers:** They rejected infant baptism in favor of baptizing believers only.
*   **The Lord's Supper:** They practiced the simple breaking of bread. This act was performed in remembrance of the death of Christ to renew the bond of brotherly love, which served as the basis for neighborly life.
*   **Justification:** They rejected the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith, viewing it as one-sided.

## Persecution and Legacy
The Anabaptists faced significant persecution, which prevented the development of a homogeneous community or the adoption of a common confession. This persecution also removed noble leaders who preached non-resistance. 

The archive contrasts the teaching and martyrdom of figures like Hubmaier, Grebel, and Blaurock with later, more violent events. Specifically, the movement is often remembered for the "grossest licence and cruelty" that occurred in Muenster between 1533 and 1535. In that instance, after Jan Matthisson prophesied the end of the world and the establishment of the kingdom of heaven, those who did not embrace Anabaptism were driven from the town. This period of rule, which included the leadership of Bernt Knipperdolling and John of Leiden, ended in 1535 when the town was taken by the besieging bishop.
