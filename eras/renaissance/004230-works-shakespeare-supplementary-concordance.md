# Works of Shakespeare; with a Supplementary Concordance to the

The study and indexing of the works of William Shakespeare have been the subject of extensive scholarly effort, resulting in various concordances, critical editions, and debates regarding the authorship of specific plays.

## Concordances and Indices
Several significant concordances have been produced to organize the words and phrases of Shakespeare's writings. Samuel Ayscough, an English librarian and index-maker known as "The Prince of Indexers," produced the first index-concordance to Shakespeare in 1790. 

In the 19th and early 20th centuries, American scholars further expanded these tools. Helen Kate Furness (1837–1883) compiled *A Concordance to the Poems of Shakespeare* in 1872. Later, John Bartlett (1820–1905), a publisher and compiler from Massachusetts, published his *New and Complete Concordance or Verbal Index to Words, Phrases and Passages in the Dramatic Works of Shakespeare; with a Supplementary Concordance to the Poems* in 1894. This work, which Bartlett had copyrighted five years prior to its publication, was noted for surpassing its predecessors in the fullness and number of its citations. Bartlett was assisted in his work by his wife, the daughter of Harvard professor Sidney Willard.

## The Variorum Edition by Horace Howard Furness
Horace Howard Furness (1833– ), an American Shakespearian scholar and Harvard graduate, devoted himself to the study of Shakespeare after being admitted to the bar in 1859. Starting in 1871, he produced a new Variorum edition intended to summarize the conclusions of the best textual, annotative, and critical authorities across all languages. 

The volumes of this scholarly work were released over several decades:
* *Romeo and Juliet* (1871)
* *Macbeth* (1873; revised 1903)
* *Hamlet* (2 vols., 1877)
* *King Lear* (1880)
* *Othello* (1886)
* *The Merchant of Venice* (1888)
* *As You Like It* (1890)
* *The Tempest* (1892)
* *A Midsummer Night's Dream* (1895)
* *The Winter's Tale* (1898)
* *Much Ado about Nothing* (1899)
* *Twelfth Night* (1901)
* *Love's Labour's Lost* (1904)

While generally accepted as a thorough work, the edition is noted for a textual inconsistency: Furness used the text of the Cambridge (Globe) editors as his foundation for several plays, but switched to the First Folio text as his basis beginning with *Othello* (1858).

## Authorship and Collaboration
The authorship of certain plays attributed to Shakespeare has been a subject of critical conjecture, particularly regarding the influence of Christopher Marlowe. It is considered nearly certain, based on collateral and cumulative evidence, that the better part of the serious scenes in *King Henry VI.* is primarily the work of Marlowe. Furthermore, Marlowe is regarded by competent judges as the principal author of the second and third plays under that name, which were first printed imperfectly as *The Contention between the two Famous Houses of York and Lancaster*.

Critics have noted that in the final text of the metrical or tragic scenes of these works, the tone is often more in the key of Marlowe at his best than of Shakespeare, who is described in this context as Marlowe's disciple. However, the authorship of the humorous prose scenes remains a point of conjecture. These scenes exhibit a high level of comic realism not found in pre-Shakespearian dramatists, yet in their original, unpurified state, they contain touches of homely ribaldry and clownish horseplay reminiscent of Shakespeare's predecessors.

Regarding other works, *A Taming of a Shrew*—the play upon which Shakespeare's comedy was based—has been attributed to Marlowe, though this is argued against because the play's humorous character does not align with Marlowe's other work and it contains passages borrowed from him. Additionally, Fleay conjectured that *Richard III.* and *Edward III.*, which are usually included in Shakespeare editions, are based on plays by Marlowe.

## Related Literary Studies
The broader context of Shakespeare's era and influences has been explored through various other scholarly works. This includes studies on John Lyly and "Euphuism," with contributions from scholars such as R. W. Bond, J. A. Symonds, and J. D. Wilson. Additionally, the works of Robert Greene have been edited and analyzed by Alexander Dyce, A. B. Grosart, and J. Churton Collins, with J. J. Jusserand providing an account of Greene's pamphlets in *English Novel in the Time of Shakespeare* (1890).
