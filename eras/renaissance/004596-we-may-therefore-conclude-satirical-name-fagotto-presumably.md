# The Origin and Evolution of the Fagotto

The bassoon, known in German as the *Fagott* and in Italian as the *fagotto*, is a double-reed woodwind instrument and the bass member of the oboe family. Its Italian and German names were derived from a perceived resemblance to a bundle of sticks, a characteristic resulting from the instrument being the first of its kind to be doubled back upon itself. This design was a departure from its direct ancestor, the bass pommer, which was a straight instrument measuring 6 feet in length.

## Development from the Pommer
During the 15th and 16th centuries, the unwieldy nature of bass pommers led to various attempts to create a more practical bass for orchestral use. By doubling the long tube of the instrument, the pommer was transformed into the *fagotto*. 

The transition of the pommer is further evidenced by the development of the *dulceian* (also known as the *dolcian*, *doucaine*, *curtail*, *curtal*, *curtoll*, or in France, the *courtaud*). To increase portability and shorten the proportions of the tenor pommer in C, makers constructed the instrument from a block of wood with two bores cut into it, communicating at a flat bottom. This evolution eventually led to the "double curtail," a converted bass pommer an octave below the single curtail. In construction and pitch, the double curtail was identical to the early *fagotto* in C.

## The Role of Afranio and Ravilius
The invention of the *fagotto* is sometimes ascribed to Afranio, a canon of Ferrara. This claim appears in a 1539 work by his nephew, Theseus Ambrosius Albonesius, titled *Introductio in Chaldaicam Linguam ... et descriptio ac Simulacrum Phagoti Afranii*. According to these records, the first instrument based on Afranio's design was constructed by Ravilius of Ferrara.

However, detailed descriptions and illustrations of Afranio's *phagotus* suggest it was not related to the bassoon. The *phagotus* resembled a *flute-a-bec a colonne* but was double and played via bellows. It consisted of two twin columns with keys and finger-holes, a shorter ornamental column in front, and a rear column with a liftable capital for the insertion of a bag-pipe or bellows. Mersenne categorized Afranio's invention not as the bassoon, but as a different kind of *fagotto* similar to the Neapolitan *sourdeline*, a complex musette.

## Etymology and Early Usage
The term *fagotto* is believed to have been bestowed in Italy, as the French equivalent *fagot* was not used for the *basson*. Evidence suggests the name was applied before 1555. The existence of the *Phagoto d'Afranio*, which featured a pretended Greek etymology, implies that another *fagotto* already existed in Italy with which Afranio was familiar.

A potential origin for the satirical name *fagotto* is found in a 1426 document from the archives published by Edm. van der Straeten. This record shows that Louis Willay, a maker of instruments in Bruges, sold a set of wood-wind instruments—including four *doucaines*, four *bombardes*, and four flutes—to Philippe le Bon as a gift for Nicolas III, the marquis of Ferrara. It is suggested that the unusual appearance of the *doucaine* may have provoked the satirical wit of a courtier, leading to the name *fagotto*.

## Regional Variations and Makers
In the 18th century, Eisel distinguished between the *Basson* and the *Deutscher Basson* (also called *Fagotte* or *Bombardi*). Eisel noted that these German ancestors of the instrument were no longer in use by 1738, suggesting that the *Deutscher Basson* referred to the bombard or bass pommer. This indicates that the modern bassoon was not of German origin.

Despite this, certain German makers were highly regarded. Sigmund Schnitzer of Nuremberg was renowned in Germany, Italy, and France for producing *fagotte* of pure intonation and extraordinary size. However, contemporary records from 1549 by Johann Neudorfer describe Schnitzer simply as a *Pfeifenmacher und Stadtpfeifer* (pipe maker and city piper), and he is not credited as the inventor of the doubled-tube design.
