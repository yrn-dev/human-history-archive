# Book-Plates and the Evolution of the Ex-Libris

The book-plate, also referred to as the *ex-libris*, is a printed label used to indicate the ownership of individual volumes. This practice is nearly as old as the printed book itself, serving a similar purpose to the hand-painted armorial or symbolical personal devices found in medieval manuscripts. While the printed page replaced the scribe's work, the book-plate replaced the hand-painted device.

## Origins and Early Examples
The earliest known examples of book-plates originated in Germany. According to the authority Friedrich Warnecke of Berlin, the oldest movable *ex-libris* are woodcuts depicting a shield of arms supported by an angel. These were pasted into books gifted to the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim by Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach around the year 1480, a date established by the record of the gift. These woodcuts were hand-painted to imitate devices found in old manuscripts.

In other European nations, the most ancient discovered examples include a 1529 plate from France belonging to Jean Bertaud de la Tour-Blanche, a 1597 plate from Holland belonging to Anna van der Aa, and a 1622 plate from Italy. In the United States, the earliest known example is a plain printed label from 1679 belonging to John Williams.

## The English Tradition and Sir Nicholas Bacon
In England, the gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon is the oldest discovered example. Sir Nicholas Bacon (1509–1579) was the second son of Robert Bacon of Drinkstone, Suffolk, and served as the lord keeper of the great seal during the reign of Queen Elizabeth. He was educated at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, and later entered Gray's Inn. His book-plate was created for books he presented to the university of Cambridge.

Following Bacon's plate, the next oldest English example is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585. Until the final quarter of the 17th century, authentic English plates remained limited in number. These early plates were characterized by a remarkable simplicity compared to German elaborateness, typically featuring plain armorials with symmetrical mantling and occasional wreaths or palms.

## Classification of Styles
The modern study and collection of book-plates began around 1875, gaining significant momentum with the 1880 publication of the *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates* by Lord de Tabley (then the Hon. Leicester Warren). This work established a general classification of styles:

*   **Early Armorial:** This style refers to plates produced previous to the Restoration, as exemplified by the Nicholas Bacon plate.
*   **Jacobean:** Though described as a somewhat misleading term, it is understood to include the heavy decorative manner of the early Georgian days, Queen Anne, and the Restoration. An example of this style is the Lansanor plate (Francis Gwyn, 1698).
*   **Chippendale:** This style is identified with the rococo, represented by the French plate of Convers.
*   **Wreath and Ribbon:** This style belongs to the period associated with the urn.

## Post-Restoration Developments
Following the Restoration, book-plates became an established accessory for most well-ordered libraries. These plates maintained the heraldic simplicity of the previous age but introduced a different "physiognomy." They began to invariably display dots and tincture lines based on a method devised in the middle of the century by Petra Sancta, the author of *Tesserae Gentilitiae*. Additionally, the mantling became more elaborate, resembling the periwigs of the era. While this style was imported from France, it developed its own English character. From this point until the French Revolution, English book-plate decoration generally followed French tastes with a delay of several years.

## Compositional Genres
Beyond general styles, certain compositions were prevalent during different periods. Many older and modern plates are essentially pictorial. Specific English genres include:

*   **Library Interiors and Book-Piles:** Exemplified by the *ex-libris* of William Hewer (1699), the secretary to Samuel Pepys.
*   **Portrait-Plates:** Notable examples include those of architect John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys.
*   **Allegories:** Engraved by artists such as George Vertue, John Pine, Bartolozzi, and Hogarth.
*   **Landscape-Plates:** Produced by wood engravers of the Bewick school.

In these pictorial styles, the armorial element typically plays a secondary role.
