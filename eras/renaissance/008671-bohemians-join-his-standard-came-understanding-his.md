# Bohemia: History, Language, and Literature

## Early History and Christianization
The early history of Bohemia became clearer following the introduction of Christianity. Initial attempts to bring the faith to the region came from Germany, but these were largely unsuccessful due to an innate Bohemian distrust of their western neighbors. Christianity found greater success when it arrived from Moravia via Cyrillus and Methodius, Greek monks from Thessalonica. Around the year 873, Prince Borivoj was baptized by Methodius, leading to a rapid adoption of the faith.

Among the early rulers, Wenceslas (surnamed the Holy) is the most famous; he was murdered in 935 by his brother Boleslav and later canonized by the Church of Rome. This murder triggered a war with Germany, in which Boleslav and his son, Boleslav II, were generally successful. Under Boleslav II, Bohemia expanded its frontiers to include Moravia, a large part of Silesia, and the area of Poland now known as the Austrian province of Galicia. However, this empire did not last; Boleslav III lost these foreign possessions to Boleslav the Great of Poland. Following a period of civil war between Boleslav III and his brothers, Jaromir and Ulrick, the Bohemians dethroned Boleslav III due to his cruelty and chose the Polish prince Vladivoj—son of the Bohemian princess Dubravka—as their ruler.

## Religious and Political Conflict (1420–1620)
In the early 15th century, the death of a king led to rioting in Prague and other cities, resulting in the expulsion of many Germans who adhered to the Church of Rome. Sigismund claimed the Bohemian crown as the successor to his brother and arrived at Kutna Hora. On March 1, 1420, Pope Martin V proclaimed a crusade against Bohemia, viewing the community as heretical for their use of the sacred chalice and acceptance of other evangelical truths. Sigismund's army occupied the castle of Prague on June 30, 1420, but failed to conquer the city after the Taborites, led by Zizka, repulsed an attack on the Vitkov hill. Sigismund was further defeated at the battle of the Vysehrad on November 1, 1420.

Later, in 1575, Maximilian returned to Bohemia after the estates refused subsidies to an absentee monarch. The Bohemian estates demanded the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg" (a summary of Luther's teaching) and the right to appoint members of the consistory, the body ruling the Utraquist church. When Maximilian refused, the majority of the diet—comprising advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian brotherhood—created the *Confessio Bohemica*. This profession of faith aligned with Calvin regarding the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. While Maximilian verbally approved the *Confessio*, he refused to make it public or include it in the kingdom's charters. He did, however, exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the consistory's jurisdiction.

## The Struggle for the Crown and the White Hill
In the early 17th century, strife broke out between Rudolph and his brother Matthias. The rupture was formally caused by Rudolph's refusal to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey that Matthias had concluded as a representative in Hungary. While the Hungarians and the estates of Moravia (led by Charles, lord of Zerotin) accepted Matthias, the Bohemians declined Matthias's overtures when he invited them to meet at Caslav. In March 1608, the estates met in Prague, submitting ecclesiastical demands to Rudolph and authorizing him to levy troops for Bohemia's defense. Matthias eventually reached an understanding with Rudolph on June 25, 1608, at Liben; Rudolph ceded the government of Upper and Lower Austria, Moravia, and Hungary to Matthias but remained king of Bohemia.

Following Matthias's death in March of a later year, the Bohemian estates deposed Ferdinand on July 31 and elected Frederick, elector palatine, as king on August 26. Frederick and his queen, Elizabeth of England, were crowned at St Vitus's cathedral in Prague. Warfare continued in 1619, with Thurn occupying Moravia and advancing on Vienna before retreating. In 1620, Ferdinand (now emperor) allied with Duke Maximilian of Bavaria, Spain, Poland, several Italian states, and the Lutheran elector of Saxony to reconquer Bohemia. An imperialist army under the duke of Bavaria, Tilly, and Bouquoi entered Bohemia in September. On November 8, 1620, the imperial forces attacked the Bohemian army at "Bila Hora" (White Hill). The Bohemians were defeated in a few hours, and the imperialists occupied the port of Prague. King Frederick fled the city the following morning.

## Language and Literary Revival
The Bohemian (or Czech) language has ancient records, including hymns, legends (such as those of St Dorothy and St Catherine), and epic fragments like the *Alexandreis*. The rhymed chronicle of Dalimil, likely written during the reign of King John (1310-1346), provides historical records though it is marked by racial antipathy toward Germans.

After a period of decline following the deaths of Comenius, Slavata, and Skala—during which the Austrian government discouraged the language—a 19th-century romantic movement sparked a revival. Joseph Dobrovsky (1753-1829) first drew attention back to the language through a grammar and a history of Bohemian literature. The revival was further advanced by four key figures:
* **Joseph Jungmann (1773-1847):** Produced a monumental German and Bohemian dictionary and a history of Bohemian literature.
* **John Kolar (1793-1852):** The revival's greatest poet, known for *Slavy dcera*.
* **Paul Joseph Safarik (1795-1861):** Author of *Starozitnosti Slovanske*, recording early Slavic history.
* **Francis Palacky (1798-1876):** Considered the greatest Bohemian historian, specifically for his history of Bohemia up to 1526.

Other notable historians include Wenceslas Tomek, who wrote on the town of Prague; Jaroslav Goll, who studied the Bohemian Brethren; and Joseph Kalousek, who studied the ancient constitution of Bohemia.
