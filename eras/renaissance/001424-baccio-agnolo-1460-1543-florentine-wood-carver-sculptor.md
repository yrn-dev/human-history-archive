# BACCIO D'AGNOLO

Baccio d'Agnolo (c. 1460–1543) was a Florentine architect, sculptor, and wood-carver. Although his family name was Baglioni, he was known by the name Baccio—an abbreviation of Bartolommeo—and d'Agnolo, indicating he was the son of his father, Angelo.

## Early Career and Sculpture
D'Agnolo began his professional life as a wood-carver. Between 1491 and 1502, he was responsible for a significant portion of the decorative carving found in the Palazzo Vecchio and the church of Santa Maria Novella in Florence. After establishing his reputation as a sculptor, he shifted his focus toward the field of architecture.

## Architectural Works and Innovations
D'Agnolo's architectural career included several prominent projects in Florence and beyond. At the start of the 16th century, he worked alongside Simon Pollajuolo on the restoration of the Palazzo Vecchio. In 1506, he received a commission to complete the drum of the cupola for the metropolitan church of Santa Maria del Fiore; however, this project remained unexecuted due to adverse criticisms from Michelangelo.

Among his other noted works is the campanile of the church of Santo Spirito, which is highly admired. D'Agnolo also designed the Villa Borghese and the Bartolini palace, in addition to various other villas and palaces. The Bartolini palace is historically significant as the first house to feature frontispieces of columns for the windows and doors—a stylistic element that had previously been restricted to churches. This innovation led to d'Agnolo being ridiculed by the people of Florence.

## Education and Professional Circle
While the exact date is uncertain, d'Agnolo appears to have studied in Rome. His studio became a gathering place for some of the most famous artists of his era, including the young Raphael, Sansovino, Michelangelo, and the brothers Sangallo.

## Death and Legacy
Baccio d'Agnolo died in 1543. He was survived by three sons who all became architects, the most prominent of whom was Giuliano.
