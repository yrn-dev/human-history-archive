# Italian Painting and Illumination

The history of Italian art is characterized by diverse schools of painting and a significant evolution in book illumination, ranging from the Byzantine-influenced styles of the Middle Ages to the historical and sacred works of the Renaissance and Baroque periods.

## Italian Illumination
Italian illumination, particularly that influenced by Byzantine elements, is noted for the speed with which it reached maturity and the splendor it achieved by the 15th century. While examples from earlier centuries are scarce, frescoes indicate that Italian artists were capable of great work as early as the 12th century. This is evidenced by two masterly paintings in the *Winchester Psalter* (Cottonian MS. Nero C. iv.) from the middle of that century, depicting the Virgin enthroned and the death of the Virgin.

By the beginning of the 14th century, many Italian specimens remained ordinary and lacked the skill found in the Northern schools of France and England. However, Italian book ornamentation adopted the general system of decoration from these schools, incorporating borders, initials, and miniatures. In the 14th century, illumination expanded with great energy, especially in Florence. Notable examples include the Add. MS. 27,428 of legends of the saints (circa 1370) and Royal MS. 6, E. ix. (1334-1342), which contains an address to Robert of Anjou, king of Sicily. As the century progressed, the art extended to choral books and large law books, such as the Decretals.

## Historical and Sacred Painters
Several prominent Italian painters are noted for their contributions to historical and religious art:

*   **Giovanni Battista Castello (Il Bergamasco):** Born near Bergamo (c. 1500 or 1509), he belonged to the school of Genoa. He worked as a sculptor and architect in addition to being a painter. His notable works include "Christ as Judge of the World" and the "Martyrdom of St Sebastian." He decorated the Pardo Palace in Spain and the Nunziata di Portoria in Genoa. He died in Madrid, where he served as the architect of the royal palaces.
*   **Federigo Barocci (1528-1612):** Born in Urbino, Barocci was inspired by Raphael and commended by Michelangelo after painting frescoes in Rome. He became famous for religious subjects, imitating the style of Correggio. He also produced highly finished, delicate etches from his own designs.
*   **Giovanni Francesco Barbieri (Guercino) (1591-1666):** Born at Cento, Guercino was a historical painter associated with Benedetto Gennari. His early style followed the Caracci, though he was deeply influenced by the shadows and coloring of Caravaggio; his later works shifted toward the lightness of Guido. He was known for his extreme rapidity, completing 144 paintings and 106 large altar-pieces, including the *St Petronilla* painted for Gregory XV in Rome.
*   **Carlo Dolci (1616-1686):** A Florentine painter and disciple of Jacopo Vignali, Dolci specialized in sacred subjects. Unlike Guercino, Dolci was remarkably slow in his execution. He primarily painted in small sizes, though some of his works were life-sized.

## Other Notable Artists and Styles
The Italian tradition includes artists who worked across borders or specialized in specific genres. Valerio Castello (1625-1659), the son of Bernardo Castello, excelled in battle-scenes and is noted for the "Rape of the Sabines." His work is described as combining the style of Paolo Veronese with the fire of Tintoretto.

Niccolo Cassana (1659-1714), also known as Nicoletto, was a Venetian painter and disciple of Giovanni Francesco Cassana. He painted portraits of the English nobility and the Florentine court, eventually traveling to England where he painted Queen Anne. One of his principal works is the "Conspiracy of Catiline."
