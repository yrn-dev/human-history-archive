# Strype, Memorials, and Cranmer

The historical record concerning the English Reformation and the figures associated with it is documented across various archival sources, including the works of Strype and accounts of Archbishop Cranmer. These records provide detailed insights into the ecclesiastical conflicts, the translation of sacred texts, and the biographies of key religious figures during the 16th century.

## The Works of Strype and Historical Documentation
The writings of Strype, specifically *Memorials* (I. i. 271) and *Cranmer*, serve as sources for detailed accounts of historical cases from this era. Strype's *Ecclesiastical Memorials* (III. i. 172-173) also provide circumstantial details regarding the parentage of Edmund Bonner, the Bishop of London. According to Strype, Bonner was the natural son of Elizabeth Frodsham and George Savage, the rector of Davenham, Cheshire. However, this account was disputed by Strype's contemporary, Sir Edmund Lechmere, who claimed that Bonner was born legitimately, though the evidence for this claim is described as not very satisfactory.

## Archbishop Cranmer and the English Bible
Archbishop Cranmer played a significant role in the effort to provide the English people with translations of Holy Scripture. Between 1525 and 1539, various translations were printed by private individuals without public authority. In a letter to Cromwell dated August 4, 1537, Cranmer expressed his views on Matthew's Bible, stating that such versions should be read until the bishops could set forth a better translation—an event he pessimistically suggested might not happen "till a day after doomsday."

This impatience reflected the slow progress of an authorized version that had been projected several years prior. Following a commission of inquiry in 1530 and petitions from the Convocation of Canterbury in 1534, an authorized translation was pursued. Under the leadership of Coverdale, a new revision based on Matthew's Bible was created. This project resulted in *The Great Bible*, which was printed in Paris by Francis Regnault before being completed in London in April 1539. The title page of this folio Bible depicts King Henry VIII giving the "Word of God" to Cromwell and Cranmer, who then distribute it to clerics and laymen. A subsequent edition in 1540 became known as *Cranmer's Bible* because it featured a long Preface written by the Archbishop.

## Theological Conflicts and the Reforming Party
The English church experienced internal friction between different reforming factions. Bishop Hooper represented the extreme reforming party and was the first of the bishops to suffer due to his Zwinglian views, which placed him further "beyond the pale" than other figures such as Cranmer, Ridley, and Latimer. 

Hooper sought to spread a form of religion in England based on the *Consensus Tigurinus* (negotiated in 1549 between Swiss Calvinists and Zwinglians), despite the opposition of more conservative theologians, including Bucer, Peter Martyr, Ridley, and Cranmer. Hooper also advocated for the reduction of episcopacy to narrow limits, a position that later influenced the Puritans during the reign of Elizabeth I.

## The Career of Edmund Bonner
Edmund Bonner's career spanned several decades of political and religious upheaval. Educated at Broadgates Hall (now Pembroke College), Oxford, he graduated in civil and canon law in 1519 and was admitted D.C.L. in 1525. He served as chaplain to Cardinal Wolsey and later entered the service of the king. 

In January 1532, Bonner was sent to Rome to obstruct judicial proceedings against Henry VIII in the papal curia. In October 1533, he was tasked with informing Pope Clement VII of Henry's appeal to a general council. For these services, Bonner was granted several livings and appointed archdeacon of Leicester in 1535. 

Later in life, Bonner faced conflict during the accession of Elizabeth I. He refused to kiss her hand and, in May 1559, refused to take the oath of supremacy. This led to his imprisonment in the Marshalsea. He was later indicted on a charge of praemunire for refusing the oath when tendered by Bishop Horne of Winchester. Bonner challenged the legality of Horne's consecration, leading to a special act of parliament to address the point, after which the charge against Bonner was withdrawn. He died in the Marshalsea on September 5, 1569, and was buried at midnight in St George's, Southwark, to prevent hostile demonstrations.
