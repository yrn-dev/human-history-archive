# Large Book-Buyers of the 16th, 17th, and 18th Centuries

During the 16th, 17th, and 18th centuries, the practice of book-collecting existed primarily as a means of enhancing the formation of fine libraries rather than as a separate pursuit. The vast majority of large book-buyers during this era operated with a public objective, or their efforts were eventually recognized as being worthy of a public resting-place.

## Trends in Public Ownership and Bequests
The general trend of the period moved steadily toward public ownership of private collections. Many prominent collectors bequeathed their libraries to academic institutions. For example, Sir Thomas Smith (who died in 1577) left his books to Queens' College, Cambridge, while the library of Archbishop Williams was given to St John's College, Cambridge. Archbishop Parker also left his collection to Corpus Christi College at the same university, although this bequest was subject to severe restrictions.

Other significant contributions were made to the university library at Oxford. Sir Thomas Bodley refounded the library during his own lifetime, and it received liberal contributions from Laud and a bequest from Selden. Later, Richard Rawlinson (who died in 1755) bequeathed the best of his own books to Oxford, despite allowing the books of his brother, Thomas Rawlinson, to be sold.

## Specialized and Institutional Collections
Many book-buyers focused on specific fields of study, and these specialized collections often transitioned into public or professional hands:
* **Scientific and Mathematical:** The grandson of Thomas Howard, earl of Norfolk (d. 1646), gave the earl's mathematical and scientific books to the Royal Society.
* **Heraldic:** The collections of Ralph Sheldon (d. 1684) were given to Heralds' College.
* **General Interest:** The library that provided such pleasure to Pepys was left to Magdalene College, Cambridge.

In Ireland, Archbishop Usher's library was purchased for Trinity College, Dublin, and Archbishop Marsh founded a library in Dublin after purchasing the printed books of Stillingfleet.

## Royal Acquisitions and National Foundations
Royal patronage also played a role in the movement of books into public spheres. George I purchased the books of Bishop Moore—which included nearly unique Caxton quartos—and presented them to the university library at Cambridge.

The culmination of these trends in the 18th century led to the establishment of major national institutions. The Harleian MSS. were offered to the nation at a price significantly below their actual value. Similarly, Sir Hans Sloane formed a great collection that included approximately 50,000 printed books. The offer of Sloane's collections, combined with the necessity of better preserving the remaining Cotton manuscripts, resulted in the vesting of these materials in trustees for public use in 1702. Although some materials were partially destroyed by fire in 1731, these events led to the foundation of the British Museum in 1753, which opened in 1757.

## Private Sales and Transitions
While the overarching trend was toward public ownership, some large collections were dispersed through private commerce. The immense accumulations of Thomas Rawlinson (d. 1725) were used as materials for a series of auctions, and the printed books of Harley were sold to a bookseller named Osbourne.
