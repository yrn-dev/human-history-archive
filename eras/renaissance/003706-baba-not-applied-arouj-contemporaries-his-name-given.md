# Barbarossa

The term "Barbarossa," meaning "Redbeard" (from the Latin *barba*), was a nickname applied by Christians to a family of 16th-century Turkish sea rovers and admirals. This family included Arouj, his brother Khizr (also known as Khair-ed-Din), and Hassan, the son of Khair-ed-Din. The nickname was given to the family due to their tawny or red beards.

## Origins and Early Life
The family was founded by Yakub, a Roumeliot likely of Albanian descent who settled in Mitylene following the Turkish conquest of the area. Yakub worked as a skipper and coasting trader. He had four sons born after 1482: Elias, Isaak, Arouj, and Khizr. While Khizr became a potter and Isaak a trader, Elias and Arouj pursued sea roving.

During an engagement with a galley from the Knights of Saint John based at Rhodes, Elias was killed and Arouj was captured. Arouj was eventually ransomed by a Turkish pasha and returned to the sea.

## Career of Arouj
Arouj initially served the Mamelukes in Egypt. However, during the conflict between the Mamelukes and Sultan Selim I, Arouj moved to Tunis. In northern Africa, he found employment as a mercenary amidst the constant conflicts between Berber princes, while also conducting piratical raids against Christian trade.

In Tunis, Arouj was joined by his brother Khizr, who took the name Khair-ed-Din, and later by their brother Isaak. Arouj and Khair-ed-Din collaborated with exiled Moors from Granada—who had been driven into exile by the Catholic sovereigns of Spain in 1492—to conduct raids on the Spanish coast. The brothers also sought to increase their fortunes by supplanting or fighting native African princes. They established their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes. In 1512, they attempted to capture Bougie from the Spaniards but were unsuccessful.

## Nomenclature and Contemporary Accounts
While the nickname Barbarossa is associated with the family, there has been historical debate regarding its specific application to Arouj. In 1840, Captain Walsin Esterhazy suggested that "Barbarossa" was a mispronunciation of "Baba Arouj," a theory that gained wide acceptance. However, contemporaries of Arouj did not apply the prefix "Baba" to him. In Italian or Spanish records, his name appears as "Ordiche," "Orux," or "Harrach." Furthermore, an Arab chronicle published in 1837 by F. Denis and S. Rang explicitly states that "Barbarossa" was the name Christians applied specifically to Khair-ed-Din.

## Establishment of Turkish Power in Africa
Arouj fell in battle against the Spaniards in 1518. Following this loss, Khair-ed-Din appealed to the Turkish sultan, Selim, who provided him with troops. In 1529, Khair-ed-Din succeeded in driving the Spaniards from a fort located on a rocky island in front of Algiers, an act that founded Turkish power in the region.

From 1518 until the death of Uluch Ali in 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli. Following this period, from 1587 to 1659, the region was governed by Turkish pashas sent from Constantinople for three-year terms. In 1659, a military revolt in Algiers stripped the pashas of their power. From that point forward, although nominally part of the Turkish empire, these African cities functioned as anarchical military republics that lived by plunder and selected their own leaders.
