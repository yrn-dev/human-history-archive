# Mar-Prelate Controversy

The Mar-Prelate controversy was a conflict of pamphlets that took place in 1588 and 1589. The dispute was waged between a puritan writer using the pseudonym "Martin Marprelate" and those defending the Established Church.

## Nature of the Pamphlets
The tracts written by "Martin Marprelate" were characterized by a plain and homely style combined with pungent wit. The author utilized violent and personal invective against Anglican dignitaries and wrote under the assumption that he possessed powerful and numerous adherents capable of enforcing demands for reform. While the writer maintained puritan doctrines in a general sense, the primary focus of his attack was the Episcopacy.

## Publication and Distribution
The pamphlets were produced at a secret press established around midsummer 1588 by Robert Waldegrave, a printer, and John Penry, a Welsh puritan. This press was created specifically to issue puritan literature that had been forbidden by the authorities. 

The first tract, titled the *Epistle*, appeared in November 1588 at Molesey. This work served as a response to *A Defence of the Government established in the Church of Englande*, written by Dr. John Bridges, the dean of Salisbury. In the *Epistle*, the author assailed the episcopal office in general and targeted specific prelates with personal abuse.

Following the *Epistle*, the press was moved to Fawsley, near Northampton, where it produced a second tract called the *Epitome*. While the *Epitome* contained more serious argument than the first tract, it remained similar in style. In March 1589, a third work titled *Hay any Worke for Cooper* was produced at Coventry; this was a reply to *An Admonition to the People of England*, a long and dull response written by Thomas Cooper, the bishop of Winchester.

## Response from the Established Church
Because Thomas Cooper's response failed to appeal to the same readership as the Marprelate pamphlets, ecclesiastical authorities decided that the only way to silence the writer was to employ a similar railing style. Consequently, the authorities secretly commissioned writers of ready wit to answer the pamphlets, including Robert Greene, Thomas Nashe, and John Lyly. This group produced works such as *An Almond for a Parrat* and *Pappe with an Hatchet* (September 1589), the latter of which was likely written by Lyly.

## Associated Figures and Legal Proceedings
The controversy and the broader puritan movement involved several key figures who faced ecclesiastical and legal pressure:

*   **Thomas Cartwright:** An English Puritan divine born in Hertfordshire. Cartwright studied at Cambridge and later held the Lady Margaret professorship of divinity. He was a bitter critic of the Anglican Church's constitution and hierarchy, comparing them unfavorably to primitive Christian organization. His conflicts with John Whitgift led to his deprivation of his professorship in 1570 and his fellowship in 1571. After periods of exile in Geneva, Antwerp, and Middelburg, and organizing Huguenot churches in the Channel Islands, he returned to London in 1585. He was imprisoned in 1590 after being summoned before the court of high commission and was committed to the Fleet again in 1591.
*   **John Penry:** The Welsh puritan who established the secret press used to print the Marprelate tracts.
*   **Richard Bancroft:** The archbishop of Canterbury (1544–1610) and a zealous champion of the church. Bancroft was known for a passionate attack on Puritans delivered in a sermon at Paul's Cross on February 9, 1589, in which he denounced private judgment and asserted the divine right of bishops. Later, while managing ecclesiastical affairs, Bancroft directed proceedings against Thomas Cartwright and his friends, as well as "Martin Mar-Prelate" and John Penry. Bancroft caused the "seditious writings" of Penry to be intercepted and delivered to the lord keeper.
