# Antipaedobaptists in Colonial America

From 1643 onward, antipaedobaptists from New England and other regions began settling in the New Netherlands, known today as New York. This movement was characterized by individuals seeking religious liberty or fleeing intolerance in other colonies and their home countries.

## Settlements in the New Netherlands
In 1643, Lady Deborah Moody departed Massachusetts for the New Netherlands due to her antipaedobaptist views. During her journey, she stopped at New Haven, where she succeeded in winning Mrs. Eaton, the wife of Governor Theophilus Eaton, to her principles. Lady Deborah eventually settled at Gravesend, which is now part of Brooklyn, after receiving a guarantee of religious liberty from the Dutch authorities.

Other Baptist figures also labored in the region. In 1656, Francis Doughty, an English Baptist who had previously spent time in Rhode Island, baptized several converts; however, this action resulted in his banishment. Later that same year, William Wickenden of Providence administered ordinances and evangelized at Flushing, but he was likewise banished and heavily fined.

Later developments in New York included the formation of a Baptist church at Oyster Bay sometime before 1724, which was likely Arminian. From 1711 onward, Valentine Wightman (1681–1747) of Connecticut, a General Baptist, made occasional missionary visits to New York. These visits occurred at the invitation of Nicolas Eyres, a businessman who had adopted Baptist views. In 1714, Wightman baptized Eyres and several others, assisting them in the organization of a church. This congregation was nearly wrecked in 1730 due to debts incurred while erecting a meeting-house.

## Baptist Growth and Persecution in Massachusetts
The Baptist presence in Massachusetts faced significant challenges. In 1663, John Myles (1621–1683), a Welsh Baptist and former one of Cromwell's Tryers, sought refuge in Massachusetts with his congregation to escape the intolerance of the government of Charles II. They were permitted to settle in Rehoboth on the condition that their meeting-place be established at a considerable distance from that of the standing order. Myles became a significant figure in promoting the growth of the Baptist Church in Boston and other areas of Massachusetts.

Other early antipaedobaptist views were held by Thomas Gould of Charlestown, who shared these views as early as 1654 and was in close contact with President Dunster. By May 1665, Gould and several other English Baptists who had settled near Boston, along with those who had been baptized in England, joined in a church covenant. This church suffered severe persecution; members were frequently fined, imprisoned, and denied the use of their own meeting-house. 

Despite a mandate from Charles II in July 1679—which rebuked Massachusetts authorities for denying others the liberty that they themselves had sought in exile—and the subsequent Act of Toleration (1689) in England, the Boston Baptists struggled to gain recognition. It required the earnest efforts of other dissenters and English Baptists before their right to exist was recognized.

## Rhode Island and the Quaker Colonies
Rhode Island served as an early center for antipaedobaptist activity. In November 1637, John Clarke, a physician, arrived in Boston seeking religious freedom. Finding the dominant party there banishing the Antinomian minority (including William Coddington, John Wheelwright, and Mrs. Anne Hutchinson), Clarke assisted the fugitives in forming a colony on the island of Aquidnek (Rhode Island). By 1641, "professed Anabaptists" were present on the island, with Clarke likely serving as their leader. Other early figures included Robert Lenthall, who arrived in 1640 from Massachusetts with antipaedobaptist convictions, and Mrs. Scott, the sister of Mrs. Hutchinson. Mark Lucar, who had been baptized by immersion in London in January 1642, reached Newport around 1644 and became an active member of the Newport church, serving as a link between English and American Baptists.

The Quaker colonies also attracted many Baptists from Wales, England, and New England due to their religious liberty. Thomas Dungan, an Irish Baptist minister who had spent time in Rhode Island, helped found a Baptist church at Cold Spring in Bucks County, Pennsylvania, around 1684. In 1688, Elias Keach (son of the English evangelist Benjamin Keach) helped form the Pennepek church. While services were held in Philadelphia under the Pennepek church from 1687, an independent organization was not established until 1698. This church eventually acquired the Keithian meeting-house after several Keithian Quakers joined.

## Organizational Development and Diversification
Baptist churches in the Quaker colonies held general meetings from nearly the beginning. In 1707, the Philadelphia Association was established as a delegated body to maintain sound doctrine, set churches in order, and consult on church needs. This body was highly influential in educational and missionary work, as well as in managing the supply of ministers. 

Diversification occurred within the association at an early date; from 1705 onward, Seventh-day Baptist churches were formed following the appearance of Sabbatarianism. Additionally, a number of Baptists settled on Block Island around 1663.
