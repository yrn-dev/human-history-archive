# Progress in Scientific Discovery

Progress in scientific discovery is driven by specific cognitive processes and methodologies, though the history of science reveals a tension between formal rules and the actual practices of successful investigators.

## The Role of Hypothesis and Imagination
Progress in scientific discovery is made mainly, and perhaps solely, through the employment of hypothesis. This process is not characterized by random guesswork or the sole use of imagination; rather, it relies on "scientific imagination." The true scientific procedure involves the formulation of a hypothesis, which is subsequently tested through verification. In this framework, the deductive method serves as the most powerful instrument for advancement.

This approach implies that the human mind is not merely a passive or recipient entity during the process of discovery, as the ability to frame a hypothesis requires an active mental engagement.

## Critique of the Baconian Method
Francis Bacon is often regarded as a leader in the reformation of modern science and a co-inaugurator of modern philosophy alongside Descartes. This position is attributed to his eloquence and his ability to organize a new mode of thinking into a connected system. His work, the *Novum Organum*, gave expression to ideas that were already emerging as scholasticism decayed and the authority of the church and school doctrines was discarded.

However, the specific logical method and code of rules devised by Bacon have not been found to be of practical service. Critics and logicians have noted that science has not actually followed the Baconian method in its progress. No single discovery can be definitely ascribed to the use of his rules, and many celebrated scientists have ignored his most cherished precepts. 

The failure of the Baconian method is attributed to a radically false conception of the causal relation. Bacon treated scientific problems as issues of co-existence and assumed that every phenomenon has only one cause. Furthermore, the inductive formation of axioms via a gradually ascending scale is a route that no science has ever followed or could ever use to make progress. Despite these flaws, Bacon's fundamental ideas—specifically the necessity for a critical analysis of experience and the rejection of rash generalization—remain valuable.

## Social and Educational Influences on Discovery
The environment in which research occurs significantly impacts the pace and nature of discovery. In Britain and France, scientific progress often occurred despite formal educational systems. British universities were characterized by educational retrogression, focusing on a pedantic and uncritical textual knowledge of Latin and Greek classics. Similarly, French education was dominated by the classical tradition of Jesuit schools. Consequently, much of British science was created by individuals working outside ordinary centers of erudition, often facing hostility from the clerical and teaching professions.

In contrast, Germans were able to organize a body of investigators that was larger in proportion to the small bands of British and French experimentalists.

Historically, a significant gap existed between the "philosopher" (the gentleman) and the artisan or trader. In Alexandria, for example, the lack of mental contact between thinkers and practical workers—such as glass and metal workers—meant that the city produced no chemistry or microscopes, and inventions like Hero's steam engine were never applied to useful work. Because the progress of science was not sustained by the excitement of practical applications, discoveries often remained in obscure manuscripts until the Renascence.

## The Economics of Discovery
There is often a disconnect between the act of discovery and the resulting economic wealth. Sincere scientific men are frequently characterized by an "unworldliness," being too preoccupied with research to plan how to monetize their findings. As a result, the economic exploitation of scientific and technical progress typically falls to a more acquisitive type of person. In Great Britain, this resulted in the creation of crops of rich men who profited from the discoveries of inventors, while the discoverers themselves were often left to starve.
