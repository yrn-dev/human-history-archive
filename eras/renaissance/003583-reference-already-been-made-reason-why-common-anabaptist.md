# The Anabaptists

The Anabaptists were a body of Christians who emerged during the spiritual movements of the 15th and 16th centuries. This era was characterized by a general revolt against authority, manifesting in the anti-papal reformation of Luther, anti-feudal peasant risings, and various anti-ecclesiastical movements. The Anabaptists are distinguished from other denominations by their specific view on the ordinance of baptism, maintaining that it should be administered to believers only.

## Doctrines and Confessions
Because of systemic persecution, the Anabaptists were unable to develop a homogeneous, self-contained community or adopt a common public confession of faith. However, a few core doctrines were generally held across the movement:
*   **Believer's Baptism:** The central tenet that baptism is for believers only.
*   **Justification:** The rejection of the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith, which they viewed as one-sided.
*   **The Lord's Supper:** The practice of the breaking of bread, where brothers and sisters in Christ partake in remembrance of Christ's death to renew the bond of brotherly love as the foundation of neighborly life.

The earliest known confession of faith for any Baptist community is found in Zwingli's 1527 publication, *Elenchus contra Catabaptistas*. Zwingli translated seven articles of the Anabaptists into Latin *ad verbum*, though he characterized them as "impious, stolid, audacious, [and] fanatical." He is noted for unfairly combining the fourth and fifth articles into a single treat on "avoiding abominable pastors in the church," despite the original texts not mentioning pastors or abominations in those specific articles.

## Persecution and Internal Conflict
The Anabaptists faced severe persecution from both Romanists and Protestants. In Zuerich, the Protestant Council passed a decree instituting cruel persecution against them, despite the Council's own recent struggle for liberty and fear of Romanist persecution.

This persecution had two primary effects. First, it prevented the formation of a unified community. Second, it removed noble leaders who preached non-resistance, which in turn provoked some adherents toward vengeance. This shift was exemplified by Jan Matthisson (or Matthyszoon), a preacher in Haarlem who prophesied the establishment of the kingdom of heaven and a speedy end to the world. 

Matthisson sent Boekebinder and de Kniper to Muenster, where they attempted to realize his ideals. In 1533, all who did not embrace Anabaptism were driven from the town, and Bernt Knipperdolling became burgomaster. Following the death of Matthisson in early 1534, John of Leiden (Johann Bockelson) took leadership. Under John of Leiden, Muenster became a site of "grossest licence and cruelty" until the town was taken by the besieging bishop in 1535.

## Relationship to Other Movements
The Anabaptists are distinct from the Zwickau prophets (Marcus Stuebner, Nikolaus Storch, and Thomas Muenzer). While the Zwickau movement shared a "daring faith in divine illumination" and an emphasis on the "inner word"—believing divine revelation came directly from the Spirit of God rather than the Bible or the church—they were not typical Anabaptists. For instance, Thomas Muenzer repudiated infant baptism in theory but continued the practice and did not insist on the re-baptism of believers.

## Anabaptism in England
Anabaptist views entered England through foreign influence, specifically Dutchmen. Records from London indicate that in 1535, ten Dutch Anabaptists were put to death while ten others repented. In 1536, King Henry VIII issued a proclamation directing the clergy to teach that Anabaptist opinions were "detestable heresies." In 1538, four Dutch Anabaptists (three men and one woman) "bare faggots" at Paul's Cross, and another man and woman were burnt in Smithfield.

By the reign of Edward VI, Anabaptists were noted as segregating themselves from other men and forming separate communities. In 1549, John Hooper reported that Anabaptists flocked to his public lectures, causing him "much trouble." By the end of the 16th century, communities existed in London, Kent, and East Anglia, with Robert Cooke as a notable representative. In Kent, Joan Boucher was burnt in 1550 for her heretical views regarding the Virgin Mary. While these groups raised the question of baptism, they were considered an "alien element," and the subsequent rise of modern Baptist churches was independent of them.

## Legacy
The history of the Anabaptists is often overshadowed by the crimes of John of Leiden and the events at Muenster. However, the archive notes they should be remembered for the piety and gentle learning of Hans Denck, as well as the teaching and martyrdom of Hubmaier, Grebel, and Blaurock.
