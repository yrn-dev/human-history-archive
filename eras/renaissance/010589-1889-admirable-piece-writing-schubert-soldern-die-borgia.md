# Cesare Borgia

Cesare Borgia was a prominent figure among the adventurers of Renaissance Italy. While he was characterized as being more clever and unscrupulous than his rivals, his success was primarily attributed to the support he received from the papacy. Following the death of his father, Borgia's career ended, and he was no longer able to maintain a prominent role in the affairs of Italy. His eventual fall demonstrated that his system had been constructed on an unsound basis.

## Methods of Rule and Conquest
Borgia's approach to conquest was marked by treachery and ferocity. However, once he had successfully conquered a territory, he governed his subjects with justice and firmness. Because of this, his administration was often preferred over the local despots and the anarchy of factions. Despite long-held imaginations to the contrary, he is not considered a man of genius.

## Relationship with Niccolò Machiavelli
In October 1502, Niccolò Machiavelli was sent as an envoy to the camp of Cesare Borgia, the duke of Valentinois, who was then located in Romagna. Although Machiavelli was sent against his will, his duty was to watch and wait upon the duke. During this time, Machiavelli observed the intricate intrigues that led to Borgia murdering his disaffected captains.

Machiavelli developed a strong admiration for Borgia's character, specifically noting his combination of diplomatic prudence and audacity. He praised Borgia's self-reliance, his avoidance of half-measures, his use of native troops, and his adroit employment of fraud and cruelty. Machiavelli further commended Borgia's firm administration of conquered provinces and his ability to cement a new state from scattered elements and deal with doubtful allies or false friends.

In his writings, including the *Principe* and letters to his friend Vettori, Machiavelli expressed that Borgia's behavior was worthy of scrupulous imitation. Machiavelli idealized Borgia as a hero and used him as the model for his conceptions of the perfect prince, referring to this ideal statesman as "Valentino." It is probable that Machiavelli separated this "radiant creature of his political fancy" from the actual Cesare Borgia, whom he later encountered in Rome as a ruined and contemptible man.
