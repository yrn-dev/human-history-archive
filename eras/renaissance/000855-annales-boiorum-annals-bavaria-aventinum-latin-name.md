# Annales Boiorum

The *Annales Boiorum*, also known as the *Annals of Bavaria*, is a historical work authored by Johann Turmair, who wrote under the name Aventinus. The name Aventinum is the Latin designation for Abensberg, the town where the author was born on July 4, 1477.

## Authorship and Development
Johann Turmair was educated at several institutions, including Paris, Cracow, Vienna, and Ingolstadt. He returned to Ingolstadt in 1507 and was later appointed in 1509 as the tutor to Ernest and Louis, the younger sons of the late duke of Bavaria-Munich, Albert the Wise. Turmair held this tutoring position until 1517, during which time he produced manuals and a Latin grammar for his students. In 1515, he traveled to Italy accompanied by Ernest.

Encouraged by William IV, duke of Bavaria, Turmair began writing the *Annales Boiorum* around 1517. He completed the work in 1521. Following the completion of the Latin text, he undertook a German version of the work titled *Bayersche Chronik*, which was finished several years later.

## Content and Methodology
The *Annales Boiorum* consists of seven books. The text examines the history of Bavaria in conjunction with general history, covering the period from the earliest times up to the year 1460. In his writing, the author expresses a strong sympathy for the Empire in its conflicts with the Papacy.

Aventinus is noted for taking immense pains with the work, and his approach is said to have anticipated the modern scientific method of historical writing. Due to this contribution, he has been referred to as the "Bavarian Herodotus."

## Publication History
The *Annales* were first published in 1554. However, this initial edition omitted many important passages because they contained reflections on Roman Catholics. A more comprehensive edition was later published in 1580 at Basel by Nicholas Cisner. A complete edition of Aventinus's works was published in Munich between 1881 and 1886, and a more recent six-volume edition has since appeared.

## Life of the Author
Beyond his historical writing, Aventinus helped found the *Sodalitas litteraria Angilostadensis*, an organization that brought several old manuscripts to light. While he did not definitively adopt the reformed faith, he sympathized with the teachings of the reformers and maintained a strong dislike for monks. These views led to his imprisonment in 1528, though he was soon released through the efforts of his friends. His later years were somewhat unsettled until his death at Regensburg on January 9, 1534.
