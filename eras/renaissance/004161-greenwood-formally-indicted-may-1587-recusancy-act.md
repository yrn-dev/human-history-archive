# The Indictment of John Greenwood and Henry Barrowe

## Legal Proceedings of May 1587
In May 1587, John Greenwood and Henry Barrowe were formally indicted for recusancy. The act under which they were charged had been originally directed against Papists. Following this indictment, the two men were ordered to provide heavy bail to ensure their conformity. They were required to remain in the Fleet Prison until such bail was provided.

## Henry Barrowe's Path to Separatism
Henry Barrowe was born around 1550 in Shipdam, Norfolk. He came from a family with marital ties to the lord keeper Bacon and likely to Aylmer, the bishop of London. Barrowe's early academic career included matriculating at Clare Hall, Cambridge, in November 1566, and earning a B.A. between 1569 and 1570. Following his graduation, he spent time "following the court," a period described as licentious or frivolous. Between 1576 and the following few years, he was a member of Gray's Inn, though he was never called to the bar.

A turning point occurred around 1580 or 1581 when Barrowe was deeply impressed by a sermon. This event led him to retire to the country, where he engaged in meditation and study that brought him to the strictest form of Puritanism. He eventually formed an intimate relationship with the Separatist leader John Greenwood, adopting Greenwood's views without reserve.

## Arrest and Imprisonment
Although Barrowe did not reside strictly in London, he was associated with a group known as "the brethren of the Separation" and participated in their secret meetings, where he became conspicuous for his eloquence and earnestness. 

While John Greenwood was imprisoned in the Clink, Barrowe traveled from the country to visit him. On November 19, 1586, Barrowe was detained by the gaoler and brought before Archbishop Whitgift. During these proceedings, Barrowe refused to give bail for future appearances and refused to take the *ex officio* oath, insisting that his arrest was illegal. As a result, he was committed to the Gatehouse.

## Later Detention and Principles
Barrowe remained a prisoner for nearly six years, the remainder of his life. His confinement varied between close confinement and periods where he had "the liberty of the prison." During his detention, he underwent several irregular examinations before the high commissioners. On March 18, 1588, following a petition to the queen, he was examined before the privy council at Whitehall. Throughout these examinations, Barrowe vigorously defended the principle of separatism and denounced the prescribed ritual of the Church.
