# The Council of Basel and the Papacy of Eugenius IV

The conflict between Pope Eugenius IV and the Council of Basel during the 15th century represented a significant struggle over the governance of the Church and the authority of councils versus the papacy. This period was marked by diplomatic ruptures, the election of an antipope, and a long-term schism.

## Conflict and the Greek Church
The tension between Eugenius IV and the fathers of Basel arose because the Pope was neither willing nor able to observe or accept all the decrees of the council. A primary source of misunderstanding was the question of union with the Greek church. Emperor John Palaeologus, facing heavy pressure from the Turks, expressed a strong desire to unite with the Catholics. He agreed to meet with principal representatives of the Greek church in the west to conclude this union in the presence of the Latin council and the pope.

This led to double negotiations. The fathers of Basel sought to hold the meeting in a location remote from the pope's influence, suggesting Savoy, Avignon, or Basel. However, these locations were rejected by both the Greeks and Eugenius IV. Consequently, Palaeologus accepted the offers of the pope. On September 18, 1437, Eugenius IV issued a bull pronouncing the dissolution of the council of Basel and summoned the fathers to Ferrara. On January 8, 1438, he opened a new synod in Ferrara, which was later moved to Florence. A momentary union between the Greek and Latin churches took place in Florence on July 6, 1439, though this union was more apparent than real.

## Suspension and Deposition of Eugenius IV
Despite being abandoned by most of its members and Cesarini, the council of Basel continued to operate under the presidency of Cardinal Aleman, maintaining that it held an oecumenical character. On January 24, 1438, the council suspended Eugenius IV. Despite the intervention of most powers, the council proceeded to pronounce his deposition on June 25, 1439.

This action resulted in a new schism when the council elected Amadeus VIII, the duke of Savoy, as pope on November 4. Taking the name Felix V, Amadeus VIII became an antipope.

## The Schism and Political Responses
The schism lasted ten years. Felix V found very few adherents outside of certain universities, the Swiss confederation, the states of Alphonso of Aragon, and his own hereditary states. Various European powers responded differently to the conflict:
* **England and Italy:** Remained faithful to Eugenius IV.
* **Germany:** Remained neutral.
* **France:** King Charles VII focused on securing the benefits of many reforms decreed at Basel for his kingdom through the Pragmatic Sanction of Bourges, which became law on July 13, 1438.

## Resolution and Aftermath
The decline of the council began in 1447 when Frederick III, king of the Romans, negotiated with Eugenius IV and ordered the burgomaster of Basel to no longer allow the council's presence in the imperial city. The remaining rump of the council moved to Lausanne in June 1448.

Eugenius IV died on February 23, 1447. His successor, Nicholas V, had already been governing the Church for two years. The fathers of Lausanne gave their support to Nicholas V, claiming that evidence proved the pontiff accepted the dogma of the superiority of the council as defined at Basel and Constance. Under pressure from France, the antipope Felix V abdicated on April 7, 1449.

The seventeen-year struggle to defend the principle of council superiority ended in defeat. While the papacy had been fundamentally shaken by the great schism of the West, it emerged victorious from this trial. This event closed the era of the great 15th-century councils, and the constitution of the Church remained monarchical.
