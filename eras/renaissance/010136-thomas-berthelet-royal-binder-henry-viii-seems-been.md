# Thomas Berthelet

Thomas Berthelet was a prominent figure in the English book trade during the 16th century, serving as both a royal binder and a royal printer under the reign of King Henry VIII.

## Contributions to Bookbinding
Berthelet is recognized as the first binder in England to practice the art of gold tooling on leather. This technique, which likely originated in the East and was brought to Venice, emerged as a sophisticated alternative to the cheap, "ugly" books bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf that became common following the introduction of printing in Europe during the mid-15th century.

The work produced by Berthelet is characterized by a strong Italian influence, particularly in his early output. Due to this stylistic connection, it is considered likely that he received his instruction in gold tooling from an Italian master. Berthelet continued his work in this field until approximately 1558.

## Role as King's Printer
Beyond his skill in binding, Berthelet held a significant position in the production of texts. In 1529, Henry VIII granted him the first patent for the office of king's printer. This appointment occurred during a period of high demand for books driven by the Reformation in England and religious dissensions on the continent.

However, this official role existed within a system of strict state control. Under the terms of the patent, Berthelet was permitted to print only those books that had first been licensed. During this era, the possession or purchase of any book that lacked a license was considered a punishable offense.
