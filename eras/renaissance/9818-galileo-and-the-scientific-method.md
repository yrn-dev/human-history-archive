# Galileo Galilei and the Birth of the Modern Scientific Method

## Overview
During the late Renaissance, the intellectual landscape of Europe was dominated by Aristotelian philosophy, which relied heavily on deductive reasoning and the authority of ancient texts. Galileo Galilei (1564–1642) fundamentally disrupted this paradigm by championing an approach that integrated mathematical description with systematic empirical observation.

While Galileo is often credited as the "father of modern science," historians debate whether he "invented" the scientific method or simply synthesized existing trends of the era. His primary contribution was the insistence that the "Book of Nature" was written in the language of mathematics. By combining controlled experiments—such as his studies on inclined planes to understand acceleration—with the use of the telescope to observe the moons of Jupiter and the phases of Venus, Galileo shifted the goal of science from asking *why* things happen (metaphysics) to *how* they happen (physics).

## Key Facts
*   **Empiricism over Authority:** Galileo challenged the Aristotelian belief that heavier objects fall faster than lighter ones. Through experimentation, he demonstrated that in a vacuum, all objects fall with the same acceleration.
*   **Mathematization of Nature:** He moved beyond qualitative descriptions (e.g., "the object is heavy") to quantitative measurements (e.g., calculating the time of descent), laying the groundwork for classical mechanics.
*   **The Telescopic Evidence:** By observing the phases of Venus, Galileo provided the first physical evidence that Venus orbited the Sun, not the Earth, rendering the Ptolemaic system untenable.
*   **The Conflict of Method:** His clash with the Catholic Church was not merely a religious dispute but a methodological one. The Church relied on scriptural interpretation and tradition, while Galileo demanded that physical evidence take precedence over traditional dogma.

## Did You Know?
1. **The Leaning Tower Myth:** While legend claims Galileo dropped two spheres from the Leaning Tower of Pisa to disprove Aristotle, many historians believe this is an apocryphal story added later by his pupil, Vincenzo Viviani.
2. **The "E pur si muove" Legend:** It is widely cited that Galileo whispered "And yet it moves" after his recantation in 1633, but there is no contemporary evidence to prove he actually said this.
3. **The Moons of Jupiter:** Galileo discovered four of Jupiter's largest moons (Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto), proving that not everything in the universe revolved around the Earth.
