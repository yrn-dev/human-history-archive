# 24mo and Small Twelves

In the history of printing, the "size" of a book is a technical term describing the relationship between individual leaves and the original sheet of paper. While standard sizes like folio, quarto, and octavo are created by folding paper in halves, books in twelves (12mo) and twenty-fours (24mo) require the paper to be folded in three at some stage of the process.

## Development of Small Formats
During the second quarter of the 16th century, French printers in Lyons and Paris produced small sextodecimos, which halved the size of Aldine octavos. While these found a ready market, it was not a lasting one, as printers from Leiden and Antwerp eventually ousted them by producing even smaller books in 24mo or small twelves. 

The production of these small formats was made possible by the use of paper that was much thinner than what had been used previously. Additionally, the overall weight and size of books were further reduced during this period by substituting wooden sides with pasteboards for the bindings.

## Technical Characteristics of 12mo
A book in 12mo is created by folding the sheet into twelve. In the early part of the 16th century, this was typically done so that the height of the sheet formed two pages (similar to an octavo), while the width was divided into six. Under this method, the "wire-lines"—white lines visible when handmade paper is held to the light—run perpendicular. 

Later printing habits changed the folding method: the height of the sheet formed the width of four pages, and the width of the sheet formed the height of three pages. This change in folding resulted in the wire-lines becoming horizontal.

## Evolution of Book Materials and Binding
The ability to produce small, portable books was dependent on the availability of paper. In the ancient world, the lack of paper made the development of paged books difficult, as materials were limited to parchment and papyrus strips joined edge to edge on unwieldy rolls. Paper originated in China, possibly as early as the second century B.C., and was learned by Arab Muslims in 751 after the capture of skilled papermakers in Samarkand. 

The manufacture of paper entered Christendom through Greece or the capture of Moorish mills during the reconquest of Spain. Although the product deteriorated under Christian Spanish influence, Italy led the world in producing good paper by the end of the 13th century. By the end of the 14th century, paper was abundant and cheap enough in Germany to make the printing of books a practicable business.

As printing evolved, binding styles shifted. Early printed books, such as Bibles, legal commentaries, and great choir-books, were immensely heavy, printed on extraordinarily thick paper and bound with wooden boards covered in stamped leather. By the second half of the 16th century, gold tooling and elaborate decoration became vogue in France, though this was eventually checked by a sumptuary law.

## Market and Accessibility
The shift toward smaller formats coincided with a broader range of book prices and types. In 15th-century Europe, very small books were almost exclusively limited to prayer-books, books of hours, and psalters. By the beginning of the 16th century in England, a variety of price points existed:
* Ballads or Christmas carols: halfpenny
* Thin quarto chapbooks: 4d.
* Edward VI.'s prayer-book (1549): 2s. 2d. unbound; 3s. 8d. in boards or paste
* The Great Bible (1541): 10s. in sheets; 12s. bound
* Sidney's *Arcadia* (1598): 9s.
