# Edmund Bonner

Edmund Bonner (1500?–1569) was the Bishop of London and a prominent ecclesiastical figure during the reigns of Henry VIII, Edward VI, and Mary I. His career was marked by shifts in religious alignment and involvement in the state-led persecutions of the 16th century.

## Early Life and Education
There are conflicting accounts regarding Bonner's parentage. One account, printed by Strype, suggests he was the natural son of Elizabeth Frodsham and George Savage, the rector of Davenham, Cheshire; Elizabeth later married a sawyer of Hanley named Edmund Bonner. However, Sir Edmund Lechmere disputed this, asserting that Bonner was born legitimately.

Bonner was educated at Broadgates Hall (now Pembroke College), Oxford, where he graduated as a bachelor of civil and canon law in June 1519. He was ordained around the same time and received his D.C.L. in 1525.

## Service under Henry VIII
In 1529, Bonner served as chaplain to Cardinal Wolsey and was present with him at Cawood during the cardinal's arrest. He later entered the service of the king, possibly through the influence of Cromwell. In January 1532, he was dispatched to Rome to obstruct judicial proceedings against Henry VIII in the papal curia. In October 1533, he was tasked with informing Pope Clement VII—then a guest of Francis I at Marseilles—of Henry's appeal from the pope to a general council. For these services, Bonner was granted several livings and appointed archdeacon of Leicester in 1535.

Later, in June 1546, Bonner was part of a commission that included the duke of Norfolk, St John, Heath, and the lord mayor. This commission condemned Anne [Parr] to be burnt based solely on her confession. She was subsequently burnt at Smithfield on July 16 in the presence of the dignitaries who had condemned her.

## Conflict under Edward VI
The accession of Edward VI brought a change in Bonner's fortunes. While he had been an active agent in establishing royal supremacy, he began to doubt that supremacy when it was utilized by a Protestant council. Bonner, or perhaps Gardiner, developed a theory that royal supremacy was in abeyance during a royal minority. This position was neither constitutionally nor legally tenable, as Bonner and Gardiner had both sought licenses to exercise ecclesiastical jurisdiction from the young king.

Bonner resisted a visitation in August 1547 and was committed to the Fleet, though he was later released. Between November 1548 and March 1549, he was a leading opponent of the first Book of Common Prayer and the Act of Uniformity. Following the passage of these laws, he neglected to enforce them. On September 1, 1549, the council required him to maintain at St Paul's Cross that the king's royal authority was as great as if he were forty years of age. Bonner failed to comply and was deprived of his bishopric by an ecclesiastical court presided over by Cranmer. He was sent to the Marshalsea, and on February 7, 1550, the council confirmed his deprivation in the Star Chamber and condemned him to perpetual imprisonment.

## Restoration and Persecutions under Mary I
Upon the accession of Mary I, Bonner was released and restored to his see, as his previous deprivation was viewed as invalid and Ridley was considered an intruder. Bonner vigorously restored Roman Catholicism in his diocese and submitted to the papal jurisdiction he had previously forsworn.

In 1555, Bonner began the persecutions for which he is famous. While apologists claim his actions were merely "official," Bonner helped ensure that the condemnation of heretics to the fire became part of his ordinary official duties. It is noted that while he had protested the enforcement of the first Book of Common Prayer, he made no such protest against burning heretics, suggesting he found the latter duty more congenial.

Some accounts suggest Bonner did not go out of his way to persecute, noting that many of his victims were forced upon him by a council that occasionally felt he was not severe enough. Even Foxe recorded instances where Bonner failed to persecute. However, he showed no mercy to fallen foes; he is described as being at his worst when delivering brutal jeers to Cranmer while degrading his former chief. Despite his prominence, neither Mary nor Henry VIII ever admitted him to the privy council, as his own party seemed to view him as a useful instrument for disagreeable work rather than a desirable colleague.

## Succession
Following the end of Bonner's tenure, the Bishopric of London was eventually filled by Grindal. Grindal had abandoned his preferments upon Mary's accession and fled to Strassburg and Frankfurt as a Marian exile before returning to England in January 1559.
