# BOILING TO DEATH

Boiling to death was a form of punishment utilized on the European continent and in England. In the English legal system, the only surviving legislative record of this practice is an act passed in 1531 during the reign of Henry VIII. This statute mandated that individuals convicted of poisoning be boiled to death. Prior to this act, boiling was frequently cited as a punishment for the crime of coining.

## English Legal Application and Cases
The 1531 statute passed by Henry VIII categorized poisoning as treason. The preamble of this act references the case of Richard Roose (also known as Coke), a cook who killed a man and a woman by placing poison in food intended for the poor of the parish of Lambeth and the household of the bishop of Rochester. Roose was found guilty of treason and sentenced to be boiled to death at Smithfield without the benefit of clergy.

Other recorded instances of this punishment in England include:
* **1522**: The *Chronicles of the Grey Friars* describe a man being boiled for poisoning at Smithfield. The process involved fastening the man to a chain and lowering him into boiling water repeatedly until he died.
* **1531**: A maid-servant was boiled at King's Lynn for poisoning her mistress.
* **1542**: Margaret Davy, a servant, was boiled at Smithfield for poisoning her employer.

The act authorizing this punishment was subsequently repealed in 1547 during the reign of Edward VI.

## The Boiling-Water Ordeal in Japan
In early Japanese history, before the existence of written law, punishments were prescribed by custom. In this context, the boiling-water ordeal served as a representative of the judicial trial. To prove innocence, a man would take an oath to the deities and demonstrate his innocence by performing one of the following acts:
* Thrusting his arm into boiling water.
* Thrusting his arm into boiling mud.
* Laying a red-hot axe on the palm of his hand.

If the individual was prepared to undergo these actions, he was considered to have complied with all requirements.
