# His Majesty's Settlement in the Bay of Honduras

## Origins and Early Settlement
The territory formerly known in official documents as "His Majesty's Settlement in the Bay of Honduras" originated in 1638. The initial settlers were log-wood cutters who had previously been buccaneers. Local tradition associates the founding of the settlement on St George's Cay—a small island located off the town—with a Scottish buccaneer named Wallis (or Wallace). 

The name "Belize" likely comes from the French word *balise*, meaning "a beacon," suggesting that a light or signal was established there to guide buccaneers who once infested the region. During the 18th century, the names Belize and Wallis were used interchangeably to refer to the country, the river, and the town.

## Expansion and Conflict with Spain
Following the initial settlement, the log-wood cutters were joined by agents of the Chartered Company, which operated pearl fisheries on the Mosquito coast. Despite their industrial activities, the settlers continued their former habits by conducting frequent raids on Spanish log-wood establishments. The Spaniards attempted to expel the British settlers, though these efforts were generally resisted successfully.

One of the most significant Spanish attempts at expulsion occurred in April 1754. Because the position was difficult to approach by sea, the Spaniards organized an inland expedition of 1,500 men at the town of Peten. As this force neared the coast, it was met and completely routed by 250 British men.

## Diplomatic Recognition and Later Hostilities
The stability of the log-wood cutters' position was recognized in the treaty of 1763 with Spain. While Great Britain agreed to demolish all fortifications erected by English subjects in the Bay of Honduras, it insisted on a clause ensuring that the log-wood cutters and their workmen would not be molested or disturbed under any pretext in their places of loading and cutting.

This crown recognition encouraged British settlers to make further encroachments into Spanish territory. The Spaniards accused the settlers of illicit practices and smuggling, leading to a large-scale military response. On September 15, 1779, Spanish forces attacked and destroyed the establishment at Belize. The inhabitants were taken prisoner and transported first to Mérida in Yucatan and then to Havana, where the majority died.

## Recovery and the Treaty of 1783
The survivors of the 1779 attack were liberated in 1782 and permitted to travel to Jamaica. In 1783, they returned to the region accompanied by many new adventurers to resume the cutting of woods. This period of instability was addressed on September 3, 1783, when a new treaty was signed between Spain and Great Britain. This agreement expressly granted the subjects of His Britannic Majesty the right to carry away, load, and cut log-wood within a specific district.

## The Role of Buccaneers in the Region
The history of the settlement is closely linked to the broader presence of buccaneers in the West Indies. These individuals functioned as a mercenary navy, characterized by their extraordinary seamanship, cruelty, and daring. They were often employed by nations seeking to challenge Spanish power in exchange for a share of the plunder. 

Some individuals, such as the English buccaneer, navigator, and hydrographer William Dampier (1652-1715), bridged the gap between these two lifestyles. Dampier spent time with log-wood cutters in the Bay of Campeachy between 1675 and 1676, alternating that work with buccaneering.
