# Francis Bacon and the Experimental Method

## Influence on Natural Science
Francis Bacon's significance in the field of natural science is evidenced by references to his work among the primary scientists of Italy, Germany, France, and England. Leaders of the new scientific movement held him in high regard, as demonstrated by quotations collected from Jean Baptiste du Hamel, S. Sorbiere, Gassendi, and Descartes. 

S. Sorbiere, despite not being partial to English matters, described Bacon as "celuy qui a le plus puissamment solicite les interests de la physique, et excite le monde a faire des experiences" (the one who has most powerfully solicited the interests of physics and excited the world to perform experiments).

## Reception in France
Bacon's fame reached its peak in France through the work of the encyclopaedists and Voltaire. Voltaire hailed him as "le pere de la philosophie experimentale" (the father of experimental philosophy). This sentiment was echoed by Condillac, who stated that no one knew the cause of human errors better than Bacon. 

The *Encyclopedie* dedicated a eulogistic article titled "Baconisme" to him. Furthermore, in the preliminary discourse of the *Encyclopedie*, d'Alembert characterized Bacon as the most eloquent, universal, and greatest of philosophers.

## Critical and International Testimony
Beyond the eulogies, Bacon received valuable critical testimony from other European thinkers. Huygens noted Bacon's "bonnes methodes" (good methods). Leibnitz described him as "divini ingenii vir" (a man of divine genius) and, following the lead of several other German authors, categorized him alongside Campanella.

## Impact on the Royal Society
In England, during the latter half of the 17th century, one of Bacon's most significant achievements was the impetus provided by his work *New Atlantis* toward the establishment of the Royal Society. 

Dr. Thomas Sprat, who served as the first historian of the society and the bishop of Rochester (1635-1713), asserted that Bacon possessed "the true imagination of the whole extent" of the enterprise more than any other person. Sprat further maintained that the best arguments for the experimental method of natural philosophy are found within Bacon's works. Additional references to this influence are found in the remarks of Dr. John Wallis and Cowley's *Ode to the Royal Society*.
