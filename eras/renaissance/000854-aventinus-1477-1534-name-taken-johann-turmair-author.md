# AVENTINUS

Aventinus (1477–1534) was the name adopted by Johann Turmair, a historian and scholar who has been referred to as the "Bavarian Herodotus." He was born on July 4, 1477, in the town of Abensberg, which is known by the Latin name Aventinum.

## Education and Early Career
Turmair pursued his studies across several European cities, including Paris, Cracow, Vienna, and Ingolstadt. He returned to Ingolstadt in 1507. In 1509, he was appointed as the tutor for Ernest and Louis, the two younger sons of the late duke of Bavaria-Munich, Albert the Wise. He held this tutoring position until 1517. During his tenure as a teacher, he authored other manuals and a Latin grammar for his students. In 1515, he traveled to Italy in the company of Ernest.

## The Annales Boiorum
Encouraged by William IV., duke of Bavaria, Aventinus began writing the *Annales Boiorum* (or *Annals of Bavaria*) around 1517. He completed the work in 1521. The *Annales* consist of seven books that cover the history of Bavaria in conjunction with general history, spanning from the earliest times up to the year 1460. In this work, the author expressed a strong sympathy for the Empire during its struggles against the Papacy.

Aventinus applied immense effort to the *Annales*, anticipating the modern scientific method of historical writing to some degree. Following the completion of the Latin version, he undertook a German version titled *Bayersche Chronik*, which he finished several years later.

The *Annales* were first published in 1554, though this edition omitted many important passages because they reflected on Roman Catholics. A more complete edition was later published in 1580 at Basel by Nicholas Cisner.

## Academic Contributions and Religious Views
Aventinus helped found the *Sodalitas litteraria Angilostadensis*, an organization under which several old manuscripts were discovered. In addition to his major historical work, he wrote other books of lesser importance. A complete edition of his works was published in Munich between 1881 and 1886, and a more recent six-volume edition has since appeared.

Regarding his religious beliefs, Aventinus did not definitively adopt the reformed faith, but he did sympathize with the teaching and the reformers. He also harbored a strong dislike for monks. Due to these views, he was imprisoned in 1528, though he was soon released through the efforts of his friends.

## Final Years
The latter part of Aventinus's life was characterized as somewhat unsettled. He died in Regensburg on January 9, 1534.
