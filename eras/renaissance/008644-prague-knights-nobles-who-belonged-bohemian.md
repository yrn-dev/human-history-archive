# Prague and the Bohemian Nobility and Knights

The city of Prague served as the central political and religious hub of Bohemia, acting as the primary site for the meetings of the estates, royal coronations, and the flashpoints of systemic conflict between the Bohemian nobility, the urban citizenry, and the ruling Habsburg monarchs.

## Political Role of the Estates and the Nobility
The Bohemian estates—comprising the nobles, knights, and representatives of the cities—held significant constitutional power. In the 15th century, the nobility was often divided into rival factions. Following the death of King Albert, a "national party" led by George of Podebrad competed with a "Roman or Austrian division" led by Ulrich of Rosenberg. These parties eventually reached an agreement known as the "Letter of Peace" (*list mirny*), in which they pledged to support the Compacts and recognize John of Rokycan as the archbishop of Prague.

By 1446, a general meeting of the estates established that members should be divided into three bodies, or *curiae*. In 1517, during the diet of St Wenceslas, the three estates agreed to live together in "friendly intercourse," with the nobles and knights recognizing the municipal autonomy of the citizens in exchange for the renunciation of certain city privileges.

## Religious Conflict and the Bohemian Brethren
Prague was the center of intense religious strife, beginning with the execution of Huss in 1415. In response, the nobles of Bohemia and Moravia met in Prague on September 2, 1415, to issue the *Protestatio Bohemorum*, a strong protest against the execution and a declaration that they would defend the law of Jesus Christ at the cost of their blood.

In later centuries, the Bohemian Brethren—a community that included several powerful nobles and knights—became an influential force. During the mid-16th century, these nobles and knights strongly sympathized with German Protestants. Their influence was predominant during an unconstitutional assembly of the estates in Prague in March 1547, which took on a revolutionary character.

## Conflict with the Habsburgs
The relationship between the Bohemian nobility and the Habsburg dynasty was marked by struggles over ancient liberties and hereditary rights. Ferdinand I sought to make Bohemia a hereditary kingdom and curtail its ancient constitution. When Ferdinand attempted to raise an army in 1546, many nobles and knights, particularly those belonging to the Bohemian Brethren, refused to send men.

Following the imperial victory at Muhlberg, Ferdinand occupied Prague on July 20 with Spanish and Walloon mercenaries. While Ferdinand treated the nobles and knights with "great forbearance," only confiscating the estates of those most compromised, he dealt severely with the city of Prague. He revised the city's ancient privileges, resulting in a broad confiscation of municipal lands and the imposition of royal officials known as town-captains and town-judges.

The tension culminated in the "bloody diet" (*Krvavy'sneem*) on August 22, where four leaders of a revolt—two knights and two citizens of Prague—were decapitated outside the Hradcany palace.

## Later Dynastic and Military Struggles
Prague remained a site of political transition and warfare into the 18th century. During the Thirty Years' War, the city was a theater of hostilities, including the occupation of part of Prague by the Swedish general Konigsmark in 1648.

In 1720, the estates met in Prague to sanction the Pragmatic Sanction, recognizing female succession to the throne in favor of Maria Theresa. However, in December 1741, Charles, elector of Bavaria, invaded the country and occupied Prague. During this period, a large portion of the Bohemian knights and nobles took an oath of allegiance to Charles. Following Maria Theresa's recovery of the land, subsequent administrative changes under her and her son Joseph II further limited Bohemian autonomy, including the 1749 abolition of separate Bohemian law-courts.

## The 1618 Crisis
The conflict between the Romanist and Protestant estates reached a breaking point in 1618. After the archbishop of Prague and the abbot of Brevnov suppressed Protestant services on their domains, Protestant members of the diet assembled in Prague on May 21, 1618. Led by Count Thurn—one of the few nobles who had refused to recognize Ferdinand as heir to the throne—the group proceeded in full armor to the Hradcany palace to confront the councillors of King Matthias.
