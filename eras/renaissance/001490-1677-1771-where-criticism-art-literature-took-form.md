# Criticism of Art and Literature (1677-1771)

During the period between 1677 and 1771, the practice of criticizing art and literature varied significantly across different European social and intellectual circles, ranging from the informal atmosphere of Parisian salons to the structured application of philosophical methods.

## The Salon of Madame Doublet de Persan
In Paris, a notable center for the critique of art and literature was the *salon* of Madame Doublet de Persan, which existed from 1677 to 1771. In this specific social environment, criticism did not take a formal academic shape but instead manifested as malicious gossip. The *salon* maintained a journal that served as a register of news, focusing heavily on scandals and providing accounts of books that had been suppressed by the censor. 

Louis Petit de Bachaumont (1690-1771), a French litterateur from a noble family raised at the court of Versailles, spent his entire life in Paris and was associated with this *salon*. Bachaumont's name is commonly linked to the first volumes of the *salon's* register, which was eventually published anonymously as *Memoires secrets pour servir a l'histoire de la Republique des Lettres*. This work, which reached 36 volumes between 1774 and 1779 through the contributions of Bachaumont, Pidansat de Mairobert (1707-1779), and others, is considered a historical source of value, particularly regarding prohibited literature.

## The Influence of Cartesianism
In the broader intellectual landscape of France, the Cartesian method influenced the development of literary criticism. Figures such as Jean Terrasson (1670-1750), Charles Perrault (1628-1703), and Fontenelle applied the Cartesian method to literary criticism, which led to a worthier estimate of modern literature.

However, by the early 18th century, the status of Cartesianism shifted. While it was subject to prohibitions in 1705, by 1720 the *Meditations* and *Method* of Descartes were listed as text-books for philosophical study alongside the works of Aristotle. By 1725, Cartesian texts were read in Parisian colleges, but the philosophy had become antiquated. It was subsequently targeted by the wit and raillery of Voltaire in his 1728 work *Lettres sur les Anglais*, which utilized the principles of Newton and Locke against it. Despite this, the Cartesian method remained influential in the way thinkers approached logic, grammar, and jurisprudence.

## Developments in Danish Literature and History
In Denmark, the period saw the formation of the national language, allowing it to be used for philosophy and science. Thomas Kingo (1634-1703), who became bishop in Fünen in 1677, is credited as the first to write Danish with grace and ease. His lyrical writing in the *Winter Psalter* (1689) and *Kingo's Psalter* (1699) helped close the period of semi-articulate Danish.

Later, the intellectual life of Denmark was further stimulated by the reopening of the university of Copenhagen in 1742. Hans Gram (1685-1748), who founded the Danish Royal Academy of Sciences, established the foundation of critical history in Denmark by utilizing a comprehensive knowledge of sources and documents, particularly in his annotated editions of older chroniclers.

## Other Forms of Literary Imitation
Parallel to formal criticism, the period and its precursors saw the use of the burlesque—a comic form of art that imitates a work to excite laughter through exaggeration or distortion. This often involved a ludicrous contrast between style and subject, such as making common men speak like gods. In France, this attained great popularity during part of the reign of Louis XIV, with writers like P. Scarron producing travesties of the *Aeneid*, *Iliad*, and *Odyssey*.
