# Roger Ascham's Early Education and Academic Career

## Early Education and Private Tuition
Roger Ascham did not receive his early education at a school. Instead, he was educated in the house of Sir Humphry Wingfield, a barrister who served as the speaker of the House of Commons in 1533. Sir Humphry was known for his fondness for having many children brought up in his home. In this environment, the children were under the guidance of a tutor named R. Bond.

A significant part of the children's activity in the Wingfield household was archery. During term times, Sir Humphry would bring bows and shafts down from London and personally accompany the students to the field to observe their shooting. This early experience heavily influenced Ascham; it led to the writing of his first English work, *Toxophilus*, and informed his belief in the importance of archery within educational establishments. This influence is likely seen in the statutes of Elizabethan schools such as Harrow and St Albans.

## University Studies at Cambridge
Around 1530, at the age of fifteen, Ascham was sent to St John's College, Cambridge, which was the most learned and largest college in either university at the time. During his tenure there, he was influenced by Sir Thomas Smith and John Cheke, the latter of whom was admitted as a fellow during Ascham's first year. 

Ascham's primary guide and friend was Robert Pember, described as a man of admirable facility in the Greek tongue and great learning. Following Pember's advice, Ascham adopted a specific method of learning: he taught Greek to boys while he was still a boy himself to accelerate his own mastery of the language. He also followed the precept, "I know nothing about the subject, I have not even lectured on it." In his Latin studies, Ascham focused specifically on the works of Caesar and Cicero.

Ascham became a Bachelor of Arts on February 18, 1534/5. At that time, the master of the college was Dr. Nicholas Metcalfe. Although Metcalfe was a "papist," he provided private exhibition and open praise to young men who excelled in the "new learning." Metcalfe helped secure Ascham's election to a fellowship. However, as a new bachelor of arts, Ascham spoke against the Pope among his companions. This resulted in punishment and a "grievous rebuke," and a warning was issued to the fellows that none should vote for Ascham in the election. Despite this, Ascham regarded the day of his election as his "birthday."

## Professional Appointments and Tutelage
Ascham served as a letter-writer for his college and the university starting in 1541 or earlier. In 1546, he was elected the university's public orator following the retirement of Sir John Cheke. One of his notable professional achievements was a 1548 letter written to the protector Somerset on behalf of Sedbergh school, which had been attached to St John's College in 1525 by Dr. Lupton.

During the early reign of Edward VI, Ascham publicly professed Protestant opinions during a disputation on the doctrine of the Mass. Although the vice-chancellor stopped the disputation after it moved to the public schools of the university, Ascham wrote a letter of complaint to Sir William Cecil. This correspondence, along with previous letters to Princess Elizabeth, led to Ascham being selected as the princess's tutor against a candidate supported by Queen Katherine and Admiral Seymour.

Ascham tutored the sixteen-year-old Elizabeth for two years, primarily at Cheshunt. He praised her industry, wisdom, stature, and beauty, noting her proficiency in English, French, and Italian, as well as her ability to speak Latin readily and Greek moderately. Under his instruction, Elizabeth read most of Cicero and a large portion of Titus Livius, as well as the Greek Testament, the tragedies of Sophocles, select orations of Isocrates, Melanchthon's Commonplaces, and St Cyprian.

## Later Career and *The Scholemaster*
In 1550, following a quarrel with Elizabeth's steward, Ascham returned to Cambridge. He later became the secretary to Sir Richard Morrison, an ambassador to Charles V. During this embassy, Ascham traveled to Venice, Innsbruck, and Louvain—finding the university at Louvain inferior to Cambridge—and read Greek with the ambassador several days a week.

Following the accession of Mary, Ascham was appointed Latin secretary to Queen Mary through Bishop Gardiner, receiving a pension of L20 a year. During this period, he quietly suppressed his Protestantism. On June 1, 1554, he married Margaret Howe, the niece of Sir R. Wallop, with whom he had two sons. 

Ascham resumed his studies with Princess Elizabeth in 1555, focusing on the orations of Aeschines and Demosthenes' *De Corona* in Greek. After Elizabeth became queen, Ascham was given the prebend and canonry of Wetwang in York minster on October 5, 1559, despite being a layman.

In 1563, Ascham began writing *The Scholemaster*. The impetus for the work occurred on December 10, 1563, during a dinner with other ministers and Sir William Cecil at Windsor during the "great plague" in London. Cecil mentioned that several scholars from Eaton had run away from school to avoid beatings, expressing a wish for more discretion in the correction of students. Ascham acted as the champion for the "anti-floggers" during the resulting debate. This position was reinforced when Sir Richard Sackville told Ascham that a "fond schoolmaster" had caused him to hate learning through brutality.
