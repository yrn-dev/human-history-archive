# Queen Elizabeth

Queen Elizabeth was the monarch of England during a period marked by significant political administration, the establishment of educational institutions, and the production of influential literary works. Her reign saw the rise of key officials and the creation of poetic forms that would shape English literature.

## Political Administration and Governance
During her reign, Elizabeth relied on a circle of privy councillors and officials to manage the state. Sir Nicholas Bacon, the second son of Robert Bacon of Drinkstone, served as the lord keeper of the great seal of England. Appointed in December 1558, Bacon was a knight and privy councillor who presided over the House of Lords during the opening of Elizabeth's first parliament. While Bacon generally supported a closer union with foreign Protestants and recognized threats from the religious policies of Scotland and France, he occasionally opposed the Queen's policies, such as making war on France due to England's poverty. In 1564, he was temporarily dismissed from court because Elizabeth suspected his involvement in the publication of a pamphlet.

Another prominent figure in her service was Francis Knollys, a member of parliament and privy councillor who was also the vice-chamberlain of the royal household. Knollys was married to Elizabeth's cousin, Catherine, the daughter of William Carey. Knollys served the Queen in various capacities, including as governor of Plymouth and as a mission agent in Ireland in 1566 to report on the conduct of Sir Henry Sidney. He was also tasked with the guardianship of Mary Queen of Scots, holding her at Bolton Castle and Tutbury Castle. Although Knollys was a lifelong Puritan and often expressed frank objections to Elizabeth's "tortuous foreign policy," he remained in her favor and served as one of her commissioners during the trials of Anthony Babington, Philip Howard (earl of Arundel), and Mary Queen of Scots. He also served as treasurer of the royal household from 1572 until his death in 1596.

## Literary Representations and the Spenserian Stanza
Queen Elizabeth is a central figure in the poetry of the Elizabethan age. In Edmund Spenser's *The Faerie Queene*, she is represented as the idealized figure of the Faerie Queene. Spenser's work is noted for its pictorial allegoric trappings, melody, and the use of a specific technical invention: the nine-line stanza (*ababbcbcC*). This form was developed by adding an alexandrine to the eight-line stave used in Chaucer's *Monk's Tale*.

The Spenserian stanza was adopted by other poets of the era. Barnfield wrote a panegyric on Queen Elizabeth titled "Cynthia," which is considered probably the earliest extant example of the Spenserian stanza outside of *The Faerie Queene*. Barnfield's work, published in January 1595 as part of *Cynthia, with certain Sonnets*, exemplifies an early study of Spenser.

## Education and Legacy
The Queen's influence extended to the establishment of local institutions. In the market-town of Horncastle, Lincolnshire, Queen Elizabeth's grammar school was founded in 1562.
