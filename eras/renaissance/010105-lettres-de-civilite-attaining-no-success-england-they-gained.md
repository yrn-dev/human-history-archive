# Lettres de Civilite

The *lettres de civilite* refers to a rival French cursive type that was introduced during the 16th century. This typographic effort was an attempt to establish a specific French alternative to the italic type.

## Typographic Context and Competition
The 16th century saw significant shifts in printing and typography, largely driven by the popularization of the small octavo in 1501 by Aldus at Venice. Along with these handy books, a new type known as italic was introduced. Throughout much of the century, italics grew steadily in favor. By approximately 1570, italic had become the nearly standard vernacular type used in Italy.

The popularity of italics extended to France as well. It was within this environment of italic dominance that the attempt to introduce the *lettres de civilite* occurred. However, this effort to establish a rival French cursive type attained no success.

## Printing Trends in England
While the *lettres de civilite* failed in France, typographic developments in England followed a different trajectory. In England, italic types gained only slight popularity. Instead, the roman type—which had not been utilized at all during the 15th century—made steady progress. The roman type competed against black letter, the latter of which had become little used by the end of the 16th century, except for the printing of proclamations and Bibles.

## Evolution of English Orthography
Following these typographic shifts, the modern practices regarding the use of specific letters emerged. The distinct use of "i" and "j," as well as "u" and "v," dates from approximately 1580. These conventions were not firmly established, however, until the reign of Charles I.
