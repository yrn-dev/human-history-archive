# George Buchanan and the Detectio

George Buchanan (1506–1582) was a Scottish humanist, poet, and educator who held numerous influential positions in Scotland and abroad. His career was marked by a transition from a critical but non-repudiating stance toward the Church of Rome to an open alignment with the Protestant, or Reformed Church.

## Early Life and Education
Born in February 1506 to a father from an old family who possessed the farm of Moss in Stirlingshire and a mother, Agnes Heriot, of the Heriots of Trabroun, Buchanan grew up in poverty following his father's early death. He attended Killearn school before being sent to the university of Paris in 1520 by his uncle, James Heriot. In Paris, he focused on writing verses. After his uncle's death in 1522, he returned to Scotland, where he joined the French auxiliaries under John Stewart, duke of Albany, and participated in an unsuccessful inroad into England.

Buchanan later entered the university of St Andrews, graduating B.A. in 1525. He followed the lecturer John Major back to Paris in 1526, subsequently earning a B.A. in 1527 and an M.A. in 1528. He served as a regent or professor at the college of Sainte-Barbe for over three years starting in 1529 and was elected Procurator of the "German Nation" in the university of Paris.

## Academic and Literary Career in Europe
In 1532, Buchanan became tutor to Gilbert Kennedy, 3rd earl of Cassilis, returning to Scotland in early 1537. During his time in Lord Cassilis's household, he wrote *Somnium*, a satirical poem attacking monastic life and Franciscan friars. This work earned him the favor of James V, who appointed him tutor to his natural son, Lord James Stewart. Buchanan subsequently wrote *Palinodia* and *Franciscanus & Fratres*, which caused him to be hated by the Franciscan order.

Following his arrest during a 1539 persecution of Lutherans, Buchanan escaped to London and then Paris. He eventually moved to Bordeaux upon the invitation of André de Gouvéa, where he was appointed professor of Latin at the college of Guienne. In Bordeaux, he completed his dramas *Jephthes (sive Votum)* and *Baptistes (sive Calumnia)*, as well as translations of *Medea* and *Alcestis*. His pupil during this time was Montaigne.

In 1547, Buchanan joined a group of humanists invited by André de Gouvéa to lecture at the university of Coimbra in Portugal. However, he was later committed for trial by a commission of inquiry. Accused of Judaistic and Lutheran practices, Buchanan defended himself with courage and frankness, admitting some charges were true. In June 1551, he was sentenced to abjure his errors and was imprisoned in the monastery of São Bento in Lisbon. During his seven-month imprisonment, he began translating the Psalms into Latin verse. After his release and the annulment of travel restrictions in February 1552, he returned to Paris. By 1553, while serving as regent in the college of Boncourt and later as tutor to the son of the Maréchal de Brissac, he aligned himself with the Calvinists.

## Return to Scotland and Political Influence
Buchanan returned to Scotland in 1560 or 1561. In April 1562, he was installed as tutor to Queen Mary, with whom he read Livy daily. He openly joined the Reformed Church and was appointed principal of St Leonard's College, St Andrews, in 1566 by the earl of Murray. The queen also granted him the revenues of Crossraguel Abbey.

Despite being a layman, Buchanan's reputation for administrative capacity and learning led to his appointment as moderator of the general assembly in 1567, having sat in the assemblies since 1563.

## The Detectio and Later Offices
Buchanan accompanied the regent Murray into England. During this period, his work titled *Detectio*, which was published in 1572, was produced to the commissioners at Westminster.

Following the assassination of Murray in 1570, Buchanan was appointed as one of the preceptors to the young King James VI. It was through Buchanan's tuition that James VI acquired his scholarship; Buchanan also educated John Erskine, the 2nd or 7th Earl of Mar. In addition to his role as royal tutor, Buchanan held several high offices, including director of chancery and lord privy seal. The latter position granted him a seat in the parliament, an office he held until at least 1579. George Buchanan died on September 28, 1582.
