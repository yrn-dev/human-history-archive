# England and the Figures of Mary

The historical records of England contain various figures named Mary, as well as accounts of individuals who resided within the country or interacted with its monarchy. These accounts range from the political struggles of the Tudor era to the lives of poets and exiled royalty.

## The Tudor Dynasty and the Succession
The English monarchy during the 16th century was marked by complex familial relationships and disputes over the crown. Mary Tudor, the second daughter of Henry VII, married Charles Brandon, the duke of Suffolk, after being widowed by Louis XII of France. From this union, Lady Frances Brandon was born, who in turn was the mother of Lady Jane Grey.

Lady Jane Grey, born around 1537, was a highly educated woman who mastered Latin, Greek, French, and Italian, as well as Hebrew, Chaldee, and Arabic. Despite her academic achievements, she became a victim of political ambition. Following the death of Edward VI on July 6, 1553, a cabal including her father and father-in-law proclaimed her queen on July 10. However, her reign lasted only nine days.

During this period, Queen Mary (the daughter of Henry VIII) gathered support in Norfolk. While the people in the city reacted with coldness to Jane's proclamation—including one vintner's boy who called for Queen Mary and subsequently had his ears nailed to a pillory and cut off—the nobility and council eventually shifted their allegiance. The lord mayor proclaimed Queen Mary, and Lady Jane Grey relinquished the crown, returning to the status of a private person.

## Other Royal and Noble Relations
The archives also detail the life of Anne Boleyn, who became the queen of Henry VIII. Anne, the daughter of Sir Thomas Boleyn and Elizabeth Howard, accompanied Mary Tudor to France in 1514 for the princess's marriage to Louis XII. Unlike her sister Mary, who had fallen victim to the solicitations of King Henry, Anne sought to be his queen rather than his mistress, driven by motives of ambition.

In later years, the English court interacted with Mary, queen of Scots. Henry Stewart (Lord Darnley), a great-grandson of Henry VII, married Mary of Scots in July 1565. This union was a matter of practical politics. Although Elizabeth I and her council expressed dislike for the marriage, Elizabeth had previously permitted Darnley to travel to Scotland.

## Literary and Cultural Figures
England served as a home or place of residence for several notable women named Marie or Mary. Marie de France, a poet and fabulist active between approximately 1175 and 1190, spent most of her life in England at the court of Henry II. Her work, including the *Lais* and the *Ysopet*, was written in a literary dialect of French.

In the 19th century, Agnes Mary F. Duclaux (born Mary F. Robinson in 1856) became a recognized English poet and critic. Educated at University College, London, she specialized in Greek literature and produced works such as *A Handful of Honeysuckle* and a translation of Euripides' *The Crowned Hippolytus*.

## Exiles and Foreign Visitors
England also provided refuge for foreign royalty. Marie Amélie Thérèse, the queen of Louis Philippe of France, took refuge in England with her four children following the revolution of 1830. She lived for two years at Orleans House in Twickenham and later spent her final years at Claremont, where she died on March 24, 1866.

Earlier historical accounts mention the arrival of Olaf in England in 988 A.D. During a "Thing" (an assembly), he met a queen named Gyda, the sister of Olaf Kvaran. Gyda, who had been the wife of an English earl, had assembled the Thing to choose a new husband from the men in her dominions. After questioning Olaf about his identity, she asked if he would have her if she chose him.
