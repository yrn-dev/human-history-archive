# The Roxburghe Club and the 1471 Boccaccio Edition

The formation of the Roxburghe Club was the direct result of a high-profile competition between prominent book collectors during the early 19th century. The catalyst for this event was the 1812 sale of the library belonging to the duke of Roxburghe.

## The Auction of the Duke of Roxburghe
During the auction of the duke of Roxburghe's books in 1812, a significant competition emerged between Lord Spencer and the duke of Marlborough (who was at the time the marquess of Blandford). The object of their contention was a specific edition of Boccaccio that had been printed by Valdarfer in Venice in 1471. The excitement generated by this particular competition led to the establishment of the Roxburghe Club, which was formed at a commemorative dinner.

## Provenance of the Valdarfer Boccaccio
Following the 1812 auction, the Boccaccio volume remained in the possession of the duke of Marlborough. In 1819, when the duke of Marlborough's books were sold, the volume was acquired by Earl Spencer for the sum of L750, despite the fact that the duke of Marlborough had previously paid L2260 for it. 

The book remained with Earl Spencer until his death in 1834. It subsequently passed, along with his other rare books, to Mrs. Rylands in 1892. In 1899, through her gift, the volume entered the John Rylands library at Manchester.

## Context of Book Collecting in the Era
The competition for the Valdarfer Boccaccio occurred within a broader tradition of significant book collecting. During the reign of George III, the English king accumulated a magnificent library consisting of over 440 manuscripts and more than 80,000 pamphlets and books. This collection included English classics, works from Greece and Rome, and early typographical works from Venice, Rome, and Mainz, as well as presses in Oxford, London, and Westminster. This royal library was transferred to the British Museum in 1823.

Other notable collectors of the period contributed to the British Museum, including Sir Joseph Banks, who provided natural history books, and Garrick, who bequeathed a collection of English plays. Other specialized collections were formed during this time, such as those of Malone, which went to the Bodleian library at Oxford, and Capell's Shakespearian treasures, which enriched Trinity College, Cambridge. While some "noble book-buyers" continued the tradition of large general libraries with rare items, others shifted toward the formation of special collections.
