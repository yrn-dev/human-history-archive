# The Spanish-French Conflict in Florida (1565–1568)

The mid-16th century witnessed a violent struggle for territorial control in Florida between the Spanish Empire and French Huguenots. This conflict was driven by the strategic importance of the region, which the Spaniards had already explored and claimed. Because Florida sat upon the trade route used for the return of goods from Mexico, the Spanish viewed any other power's possession of the land as highly dangerous.

## The Spanish Expedition of Pedro Menéndez de Avilés

To eliminate the peril posed by French attempts to plant a colony in Florida, King Philip II of Spain entered into an *asiento*, or contract, with a seaman named Avilés on March 20, 1565. Under the terms of this agreement, Philip II advanced 15,000 ducats to Avilés. In return, Avilés was constituted the proprietor of any colony he could establish in Florida, provided the advanced money was repaid.

Avilés departed for Florida on July 28, 1565, leading a force of 1,500 men supported by ten sloops and one vessel of 600 tons. On August 28, he arrived at and named the Bay of St Augustine, where he began the construction of a fort.

## The Fall of Fort Caroline and the Massacre of 1565

The Spanish military campaign quickly targeted the existing French presence. On September 20, 1565, Avilés captured the French post known as Fort Caroline. Following this victory, a body of Frenchmen led by the Huguenot Jean Ribault arrived on the Florida coast with the intent of relieving their colony. In October, Avilés exterminated this group.

The Spanish commander slew nearly all of the prisoners. Their bodies were subsequently hung from trees with inscriptions stating, "Not as Frenchmen but as Lutherans." Avilés, who described himself in letters as a pious and high-minded officer, viewed the Frenchmen not as fellow nationals but as heretics and pirates; he maintained that no honest man could believe he had gone too far in the massacre.

## French Retaliation and Aftermath

The Spanish renamed the captured Fort Caroline as Fort San Mateo. In 1568, a French sea-captain named Dominique de Gourgues sought revenge for the previous massacre. De Gourgues captured Fort San Mateo and hung the Spanish garrison, placing inscriptions on the bodies that read, "Not as Spaniards but as murderers."

Avilés remained active in Florida until 1567, when he returned to Spain. He made one final voyage to the region before his death on September 17, 1574. He is survived in the record by three daughters from his marriage to Maria de Solis.
