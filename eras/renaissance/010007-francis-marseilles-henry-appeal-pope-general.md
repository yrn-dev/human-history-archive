# Francis I, the Papacy, and Henry VIII's Appeal

## Political Rivalries and the Imperial Election
The early 16th century was defined by the ambitions of young, vigorous monarchs, including Francis I, who ascended the French throne in 1515 at age twenty-one, and Henry VIII, who became King of England in 1509 at age eighteen. A central conflict of this era was the effort by Francis I and Pope Leo X to prevent the election of Charles as Emperor, as they feared the concentration of excessive power in a single individual. Both Henry VIII and Francis I offered themselves as candidates to the imperial electors. However, due to a long-standing tradition of Habsburg Emperors dating back to 1273 and the use of energetic bribery, Charles was successfully elected.

## Military Conflicts in Italy and France
The struggle for dominance primarily unfolded in North Italy, where Charles, allied with Henry VIII, generally found success against the Turk and Francis I. The military campaigns were characterized by dull generalship, with advances and retreats dictated largely by the arrival of reinforcements. During these conflicts, the German army invaded France but failed to capture Marseilles. After falling back into Italy and losing Milan, the German forces were besieged in Pavia. 

Francis I attempted a long siege of Pavia, which proved unsuccessful. He was subsequently defeated, wounded, and taken prisoner by fresh German forces. Following his capture, Francis I sent a message to his queen stating that all was "lost but honour." Although he eventually secured a humiliating peace, he broke the agreement as soon as he was liberated.

## Shifting Alliances and the Sack of Rome
Following the capture of Francis I, the Pope and Henry VIII shifted their allegiance toward France to prevent Charles from becoming too powerful, adhering to the rules of Machiavellian strategy. During this period of instability, unpaid German troops in Milan, led by the Constable of Bourbon, raided Rome in 1527. The city was stormed and pillaged while the Pope sought refuge in the Castle of St. Angelo. The Pope eventually ended the slaughter by paying the German troops four hundred thousand ducats. This era of confused fighting lasted ten years and impoverished Europe. By 1530, the Emperor emerged triumphant in Italy and was crowned by the Pope at Bologna.

## Henry VIII's Appeal and the Mission of Edmund Bonner
By 1530, Henry VIII began a quarrel with the Papacy. In January 1532, the king sent Edmund Bonner—a graduate of civil and canon law from Oxford and former chaplain to Wolsey—to Rome to obstruct judicial proceedings against Henry within the papal curia. 

In October 1533, Bonner was assigned the "unmannerly task" of delivering a message to Pope Clement VII. At the time, the Pope was the guest of Francis I at Marseilles. The message conveyed Henry VIII's appeal from the pope to a general council. While some accounts, such as those by Burnet, suggest that Clement VII threatened to have Bonner burnt alive for this delivery, there is no good authority to support this story.

## Diplomatic Presence at Marseilles
The conference at Marseilles involving Francis I and Pope Clement VII also served as a venue for other diplomatic figures. Pietro Carnesecchi, an Italian humanist and first secretary to the pope, participated in the conference. Through his conduct during these proceedings at Marseilles, Carnesecchi gained the favor of influential figures at the French court, including Catherine de' Medici.
