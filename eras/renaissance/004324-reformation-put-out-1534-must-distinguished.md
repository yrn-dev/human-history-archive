# Reformation

The Reformation was a period of profound religious and intellectual upheaval that significantly affected European politics, including those of Scandinavia. It was characterized by the emergence of reforming parties, the production of new statements of faith, and a revival of intellectual life.

## Confessions and Theological Disputes
During the Reformation, various statements of faith were produced to define the beliefs of reforming parties. One such document was the Confession of Basel, published in 1534. Authored by Oswald Myconius, who based the work on a shorter confession by his predecessor in the Basel church, Oecolampadius, the Confession of Basel was an attempt to align the reforming party with both the anabaptist section and those still inclined toward the old faith. 

The publication of the Confession of Basel provoked significant controversy, particularly regarding its statements on the Eucharist. This led to accusations from the people of Strassburg that those in Basel were celebrating a "Christless supper." The document, also known as the Confession of Muehlhausen due to its adoption by that town, was read publicly from Basel's pulpits every year on the Wednesday of Passion week until 1826. It was practically annulled by a resolution of the city's great council in 1872.

## The Role of Scripture and Translation
The Reformation saw a steady increase in demand for New Testaments. In England, this led to the production of several unauthorized reprints of the Worms edition of the New Testament before 1534. In August 1534, George Joye released a fourth edition of Tyndale's New Testament, which contained new renderings and alterations that caused indignation for Tyndale.

To supersede these unauthorized versions, Tyndale published his own revision of the New Testament in November 1534 at Antwerp, which included translations of the *Epistles of the Old Testament* according to the use of Salisbury. This edition featured new marginal glosses and prologues for most books, excluding the Apocalypse and the Acts. Tyndale later produced another revised edition in 1535, printed at Antwerp, which added headings to the Acts and Gospels but omitted the 1534 marginal notes. This final work is believed to have been revised while Tyndale was imprisoned in the castle of Vilvorde prior to his execution on October 6, 1536.

## Intellectual and Cultural Expressions
The intellectual revival accompanying the Reformation found early expression in the composition of Psalms. In Holland, the *Souter-Liedekens* appeared in Antwerp in 1540, though it was composed before the Reformation had fully set in there. Later, Jan Utenhove printed a volume of Psalms in London in 1566 for Protestant congregations. Petrus Datheen and Lucas de Heere also translated the hymns of Clement Marot; the printing of Datheen's volume in 1567 led to Herman Schinkel of Delft being burned to death in 1568.

The fervor of the period was further captured in the lyrical pieces and hymns of the Mennonite Church martyrs, printed in 1562 and 1569, as well as publications in Leeuwarden, Ghent, Bruges, and Haarlem. Conversely, the victorious Reformers known as the "Geuzen" or "Gueux" produced battle-songs of triumph. Their 1588 song-book, the *Geusen Lieden Boecxken*, contained political ballads ridiculing Spanish leaders and classical Dutch songs, such as the Ballad of Egmond and Horn and the Ballad of Heiligerlee.

## Political and Individual Responses
The Reformation influenced various figures across Europe. In Bavaria, Johann Turmair (known as Aventinus) sympathized with the reformers' teaching and expressed a strong dislike for monks, which led to his imprisonment in 1528. In his *Annales Boiorum*, he showed sympathy for the Empire's struggle against the Papacy.

Within the Catholic Church, Cardinal Cajetan (Tommaso de Vio) acted as a legate to Germany to quiet the commotions raised by Luther. While he helped draw up the bull of excommunication against Luther in 1519, Cajetan later held the position of a moderate reformer. He recommended certain concessions to Lutherans, such as communion in both kinds and the marriage of the clergy.

In Münster, the reforming movement was linked to figures like Bernhardt Rothmann and Bernt Knipperdollinck. By February 14, 1533, Münster was constituted an evangelical city by charter from Philip of Hesse. Knipperdollinck became a burgomaster in February 1534. This environment saw the rise of Anabaptism, proclaimed in Münster on September 8, 1533. By January 1534, Heinrich Roll preached Anabaptist doctrines from a city pulpit, followed by the arrival of Jan Beukelsz (John of Leiden) as an apostle of the Anabaptist prophet Jan Matthysz.

In France, extreme Protestants pasted "placards" in the streets of Paris on October 17, 1534, which vilified the Mass and its celebrants, resulting in renewed religious persecution.
