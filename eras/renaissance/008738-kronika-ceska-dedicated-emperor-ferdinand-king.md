# Kronika ceska

The *Kronika ceska* is a significant historical work written by Wenceslas Hajek of Libocan, a priest who was active in 1524 and died in 1553. Produced during the 16th century—a period of comparative tranquillity in Bohemia that saw the development of native literature—the work was dedicated to the emperor Ferdinand I., who served as the king of Bohemia.

## Nature and Reception of the Work
The *Kronika ceska* appeared under the auspices of government officials, which contributed to its strong Romanist and dynastic tendencies. Because of these affiliations, the book's circulation was permitted during a time when many other Bohemian books were prohibited or totally destroyed. 

The work achieved widespread recognition and was translated into several languages. It was frequently quoted, sometimes even by writers who had likely never encountered Hajek's original text. Despite its popularity, the archive's sources characterize the book as being grossly partial and inaccurate.

## Ferdinand I and the Bohemian Throne
The dedication of the *Kronika ceska* to Ferdinand I. relates to a pivotal era of Habsburg accession to the Bohemian throne. Following the death of King Louis—who was killed by Suleiman the Magnificent at the battle of Mohacs on August 28, 1526—Bohemia entered a state of anarchy and disorder. The previous kings had primarily resided in Hungary, leaving the Bohemians without a strong indigenous government.

Archduke Ferdinand of Austria (later emperor Ferdinand I.) laid claim to the throne as the husband of Anna, the daughter of King Vladislav. On October 23, 1526, a group of twenty-four electors, chosen equally from the three estates, elected Ferdinand of Habsburg as king. This event marked the permanent accession of the Habsburg dynasty to the Bohemian throne.

## Reign and Policies of Ferdinand I.
Ferdinand I. pursued the establishment of his dynasty's hereditary right to the throne with characteristic obstinacy. In 1541, after a great fire in Prague destroyed state documents, he obtained the consent of the estates to replace the original charter—which stated he had been king by election—with a new charter stating he was recognized as king due to the hereditary rights of his wife, Anna. This change caused significant dissatisfaction among the people.

While Ferdinand shared the traditional Habsburg devotion to Rome, he initially showed moderation in religious matters. Over time, he sought to make the nobles and knights amenable to the royal will through the influence of his court and the Jesuits, whom he established in Bohemia.

## Conflict and Succession
The tensions regarding Ferdinand's rule eventually led to an unsuccessful rising of the Bohemians against him. This episode is documented in the *Memorials of the Troubled Years 1546 and 1547* by Sixt of Ottersdorf. Following the failure of the revolt, Ferdinand ordered the decapitation of four principal leaders—two citizens of Prague and two knights—on August 22 on the square outside the Hradcany palace. This event led the diet of that day to be known as the "Krvavy'sneem" (bloody diet).

In 1562, Ferdinand succeeded in securing the coronation of his eldest son, Maximilian, as king of Bohemia to ensure the succession. Ferdinand I. died in 1564.

## Other Contemporary Historians
While Wenceslas Hajek is the best-known historian of this period, the archive notes two other writers:
* **Bartos (Bartholomew):** Surnamed "pisar" (the writer), he served as a secretary for the city of Prague and died in 1539. His work, the *Chronicle of Prague*, is considered to have great historical value, particularly in its description of the troubles in Bohemia and Prague during the reign of the absentee King Louis.
* **Sixt of Ottersdorf (1500-1583):** He wrote the *Memorials of the Troubled Years 1546 and 1547*, a work enhanced by the fact that Sixt personally took part in the Bohemian movement against Ferdinand I.
