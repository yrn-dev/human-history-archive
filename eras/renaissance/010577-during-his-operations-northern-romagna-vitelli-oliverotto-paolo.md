# Operations in Northern Romagna: Vitelli, Oliverotto, and Paolo Orsini

The military and political operations in northern Romagna during the early 16th century were characterized by the expansionist ambitions of Cesare Borgia, the Duke of Romagna, and the subsequent betrayal of his captains. These events culminated in a decisive confrontation at Senigallia involving the *condottieri* Vitellozzo Vitelli, Oliverotto da Fermo, and Paolo Orsini.

## The Conquests of Cesare Borgia
In October 1500, Cesare Borgia entered the Romagna with an army of 10,000 men, supported by the friendship of Venice. His campaign led to the flight of Giovanni Sforza of Pesaro and Pandolfo Malatesta of Rimini, both of whom opened their city gates to him. The city of Faenza resisted due to the devotion of its people to their eighteen-year-old lord, Astorre Manfredi. Although Manfredi surrendered in April 1501 under a promise that his life would be spared, Cesare broke this word; Manfredi was sent to Rome as a prisoner, where he was put to death after being foully outraged.

Following the capture of Castel Bolognese in June, Cesare returned to Rome to engage in Franco-Spanish intrigues regarding the partition of Naples. Having acquired extensive territory, he was created Duke of Romagna by the pope. Cesare became a figure of terror throughout Italy, described as morose, silent, unsympathetic, and possessing an insatiable avidity and a total lack of scruple.

## The Conspiracy of La Magione
Cesare's power was eventually challenged by the conspiracy of La Magione, a castle located near Perugia. The plotters included several princes whom Cesare had deposed, the Orsinis, and some of his own captains. Among these captains were G.P. Baglioni, Oliverotto da Fermo, and Vitellozzo Vitelli. Despite having been granted estates by Cesare, these men feared the loss of their holdings and joined forces against the Borgias.

This conspiracy sparked risings in Romagna and Urbino, leading to the defeat of papal troops. Cesare initially found himself without allies, and it appeared that Italy might turn against the Borgia family. However, the situation shifted when the French king promised assistance, which frightened the confederates into seeking terms. The confederates were noted for their lack of military and political skill, with several members being prone to betraying one another.

## The Betrayal at Senigallia
To demonstrate their repentance to Cesare, a group consisting of Vitelli, Oliverotto, Paolo Orsini, and the duke of Gravina operated in northern Romagna. In Cesare's name, they seized the town of Senigallia, which was still held for the duke of Urbino. 

Upon his arrival at Senigallia, Cesare decoyed the unsuspecting *condottieri* into his house. Once inside, he had them all arrested. This event marked the culmination of the intricate intrigues Cesare used to eliminate his disaffected captains.

## Observations of Niccolò Machiavelli
In October 1502, Niccolò Machiavelli was sent as an envoy to the camp of Cesare Borgia in Romagna. During this assignment, Machiavelli observed the duke's methods, including the murder of his captains. Machiavelli developed a strong admiration for Cesare's character, specifically his combination of diplomatic prudence and audacity, his self-reliance, and his adroit use of fraud and cruelty.

Machiavelli viewed Cesare's ability to cement a new state from scattered elements and his handling of doubtful allies or false friends as worthy of scrupulous imitation. He later modeled a political ideal of the perfect prince—which he called "Valentino"—upon the image of Cesare Borgia. Additionally, Machiavelli noted Cesare's successful method of substituting native troops for mercenaries and aliens, a practice Machiavelli later sought to implement in Florence to create a national militia.
