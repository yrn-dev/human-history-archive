# Germany and the Imperial Settlements of the 16th Century

## The Role of Ferdinand of Habsburg
Ferdinand of Habsburg, the Archduke of Austria and later Emperor Ferdinand I, became a central figure in the politics of Germany during the 16th century. His involvement in the region was bolstered by his election as king of the Romans and heir to the Empire in 1531, an achievement reached primarily through the influence of his brother, Emperor Charles V. Following this election, Ferdinand took a significant role in German political affairs, particularly after the conclusion of a treaty of peace in 1547.

Prior to his focus on Germany, Ferdinand had established himself in Bohemia. He laid claim to the Bohemian throne as the husband of Anna, the daughter of King Vladislav. On October 23, 1526, he was chosen as king by twenty-four electors representing the three estates. This event marked the permanent accession of the Habsburg dynasty to the Bohemian throne. While Ferdinand was devoted to Rome, he initially demonstrated great moderation regarding religious matters. He was characterized by a persistent obstinacy in establishing the hereditary right of his dynasty to the Bohemian throne. This ambition led him to replace a charter stating he was king by election with one stating he was recognized via the hereditary rights of his wife, Anna, after a fire destroyed state documents in Prague in 1541.

## The German Religious War and the 1547 Settlement
The German religious war commenced in 1546, occurring a few months after the death of Martin Luther. During this conflict, the Protestant Saxon army suffered a severe defeat at Lochau. Philip of Hesse, who was the chief remaining antagonist to the Emperor, was captured and imprisoned in an act described as something similar to a breach of faith. Simultaneously, the Turks were managed through the payment of an annual tribute.

The year 1547 brought a significant shift in the geopolitical landscape with the death of Francis I, which provided great relief to Emperor Charles V. Consequently, by 1547, Charles was able to reach a form of settlement and made his final attempts to establish peace in a region where peace did not exist.

## Subsequent Conflict and the Abdication of Charles V
The stability achieved in 1547 proved temporary. By 1552, Germany was once again engulfed in war. Emperor Charles V narrowly avoided capture during this period, escaping from Innsbruck in a precipitate flight. A new, though unstable, equilibrium was reached in 1552 through the treaty of Passau.

By this time, Charles V was exhausted by the splendors and cares of the empire. Suffering from gout and described as naturally indolent with an unsound constitution, he chose to abdicate. He resigned his sovereign rights in Germany to his brother, Ferdinand, while resigning Spain and the Netherlands to his son, Philip. Charles subsequently retired to a monastery at Yuste in the hills north of the Tagus valley, where he died in 1558.

## Historical Context of the Empire
During this thirty-two-year period of imperial politics, the European mind was focused almost exclusively on the struggle for European ascendancy. During this era, Germans, English, French, and Turks had not yet recognized any political interest in the continent of America or the significance of new sea routes to Asia. While Spain benefited from a stimulating influx of silver resulting from Pizarro's subjugation of Peru and Cortez's conquest of the Neolithic empire of Mexico, these events remained secondary to the internal struggles of Europe.
