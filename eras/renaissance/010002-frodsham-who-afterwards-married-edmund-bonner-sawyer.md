# Elizabeth Frodsham and Edmund Bonner

## Family and Early Life
Elizabeth Frodsham is identified in historical accounts as the mother of Edmund Bonner (1500?–1569), who later served as the bishop of London. According to an account printed by Strype, Bonner was the natural son of Elizabeth Frodsham and George Savage, the rector of Davenham in Cheshire. Following this relationship, Elizabeth Frodsham married a man also named Edmund Bonner, who was a sawyer from Hanley in Worcestershire. 

This specific account of Bonner's parentage and Elizabeth's subsequent marriage was disputed by Sir Edmund Lechmere, a contemporary of Strype. Lechmere asserted that Edmund Bonner was of legitimate birth, though the evidence provided for this claim is described as not very satisfactory.

## Career of Edmund Bonner
Edmund Bonner received his education at Broadgates Hall (now Pembroke College), Oxford. He graduated as a bachelor of civil and canon law in June 1519 and was ordained around the same time. He later achieved the degree of D.C.L. in 1525.

By 1529, Bonner served as the chaplain to Wolsey and was present with the cardinal at Cawood during the time of Wolsey's arrest. He subsequently entered the service of the king, possibly through the influence of Cromwell. In January 1532, he was dispatched to Rome to obstruct judicial proceedings against Henry in the papal curia. In October 1533, he was tasked with informing Clement VII—who was then a guest of Francis I at Marseilles—of Henry's appeal from the pope to a general council. While Burnet claimed that Clement threatened to burn Bonner alive for this, there is no good authority for this story.

As a reward for these services, Bonner was granted several livings and was appointed archdeacon of Leicester in 1535.

## Episcopacy and Religious Persecution
Following the accession of Mary, Bonner was restored to his see after a period of deprivation. He worked vigorously to restore Roman Catholicism within his diocese and submitted to papal jurisdiction. In 1555, he began a period of persecution for which he is well known. While his apologists argue that his actions were merely "official," Bonner ensured that the condemnation of heretics to the fire became part of his ordinary official duties. He similarly carried out the enforcement of the first Book of Common Prayer.

Historical perspectives on his severity vary. Some records suggest that Bonner did not always go out of his way to persecute and that many of his victims were forced upon him by the council, which occasionally viewed him as insufficiently severe. Even Foxe recorded instances where Bonner failed to persecute. However, he is noted for having no mercy for fallen foes, specifically demonstrated by his brutal jeering of Cranmer during the latter's degradation.

## Political Standing
Despite his prominence and his role as an "ecclesiastical sheriff" in a refractory district, Bonner was never admitted to the privy council by either Mary or Henry VIII. It appears he was viewed by his own party as a useful instrument for performing disagreeable work rather than as a desirable colleague.
