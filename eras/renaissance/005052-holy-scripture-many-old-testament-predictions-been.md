# Holy Scripture

Holy Scripture, primarily referring to the Bible, consists of the Old and New Testaments. Throughout history, these texts have been the subject of extensive translation, distribution, and critical scholarly analysis.

## Translation and Distribution
The effort to translate Holy Scripture into vernacular languages has been a recurring theme in religious and political history. In 1530, Henry VIII issued a commission to investigate the necessity of having both the Old and New Testaments in the English tongue. While an initial report advised against a vernacular translation until religious opinions were more settled, the king intended for the texts to be translated by "great, learned and Catholic persons" if it seemed convenient. By December 1534, the Convocation of Canterbury petitioned the king to decree that the Scriptures be translated into the "vulgar tongue" and delivered to the people.

Between 1525 and 1539, several translations were produced by private individuals without public authority. One such effort was led by Coverdale, who revised Matthew's Bible by consulting the Hebrew text by Sebastian Muenster and the Latin version of the Old Testament, as well as the Vulgate and Erasmus's Greek editions for the New Testament. Archbishop Cranmer noted in 1537 that these private translations could be read until the bishops produced a better authorized version.

In later centuries, Christian missionaries and societies became primary agents of distribution. The *Congregatio de Propaganda Fide* produced the first complete Arabic Bible in Rome in 1671. The Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge (founded 1698) worked to multiply affordable copies of the Scriptures in English, Welsh, and various foreign languages, including Tamil and Telugu. The Society in Scotland for Propagating Christian Knowledge (founded 1709) published the first Gaelic New Testament in 1767 and Old Testament between 1783 and 1801. By 2005-1906, the S.P.C.K. was publishing versions of the Scriptures in 38 different languages.

## Historical and Textual Development
The physical and structural organization of the texts evolved over time. While divisions into verses already existed in the Hebrew Old Testament, the New Testament was first divided into verses in R. Stephanus' Greek-Latin New Testament (4th edition, 1551). 

Early efforts to standardize the text include the 797 commission by Charlemagne, who tasked Alcuin with preparing an emended text of the Vulgate. The Latin Bible was the first book printed in Europe, with an estimated 124 editions of the Vulgate issued by the end of the 15th century. Other early printed versions included a dozen Italian Bibles and eighteen German Bibles before the appearance of Luther's version.

## Biblical Criticism and Scholarship
Modern Old Testament criticism seeks to determine the actual meaning intended by the writers, the reality of events in Hebrew history, and the nature of the religion described. This differs from dogmatic or homiletic treatments, which look for specific dogmas or incentives for living.

Some scholars have challenged traditional views of authorship and prophecy. Charles Augustus Briggs, an American scholar, faced heresy charges in 1892 for teaching that:
* Moses was not the author of the Pentateuch.
* Isaiah did not author half of the book bearing his name.
* Errors may have existed in the original text of Holy Scripture.
* Many Old Testament predictions were reversed by history, and the majority of Messianic predictions have not been, and cannot be, fulfilled.
* Reason and the Church are fountains of divine authority that can enlighten men independently of Holy Scripture.

Regarding the canon, the book of 1 Maccabees is written in the style of Old Testament historical books but lacks the divine name and Messianic hope. While not accepted as canonical by Protestant churches, Luther noted its close resemblance to other books of Holy Scripture.

## Scriptural Geography and Predictions
Scripture provides specific geographical markers and predictions. The "little river of Egypt" mentioned in Scripture as the boundary of Palestine towards Egypt was a small river running through the desert, rather than the Nile. This marked the extent of the land promised to the posterity of Abraham.

In the Old Testament, Jerusalem is referred to as *Air Hakkodesh* (the city of holiness) or the holy city, a title stemming from the time Solomon established his temple as the common place of worship for all Israel.

Regarding prophecy, some texts mention predictions concerning the fall of Jerusalem and the burning of the sanctuary. While some of these predictions are absent from present copies of the Old Testament, others were noted by Josephus as being fulfilled despite being mocked by contemporaries. Some scholars, such as Grotius, have asserted that many predictions of the prophets were preserved by memory rather than in writing, though this view has been contested by other researchers.
