# Pflug of Rabenstein

The archive's records regarding Pflug of Rabenstein are limited, primarily mentioning him in the context of the political and military instability in Bohemia during the mid-16th century.

## Military Activity in Bohemia
Pflug is identified as the leader of an army during a period of significant tension between the Bohemian estates and their sovereign, Ferdinand. This era was characterized by a shift in the views of Bohemian reformers; while some advanced Utraquists held views similar to German Lutherans, the Bohemian Brethren—who gained influence through the support of powerful nobles—strongly sympathized with German Protestants.

Ferdinand had attempted to raise a Bohemian army under the pretext that the Turks menaced the country, as the consent of the estates was required to raise armed forces. However, many nobles and cities, particularly those associated with the Bohemian Brethren, refused to send men. Those soldiers who did arrive were often Protestants who sympathized with their co-religionists in Germany and refused to cross the Saxon frontier.

Following the imperialist victory at Muhlberg, which temporarily crushed German Protestantism, the Bohemians found themselves in a precarious position, having offended their sovereign without providing aid to the German Protestants. It was during this period of instability that the army of Pflug hastily dispersed.

## Aftermath and Political Consequences
Following the dispersal of Pflug's forces, the estates assembled at Prague attempted to propitiate Ferdinand. They sent envoys to the king's camp while he and his brother Charles were besieging Wittenberg. Although Ferdinand treated the nobles and knights with forbearance, he maintained his objective of making Bohemia a hereditary kingdom under Habsburg rule and curtailing its ancient constitution.

Upon arriving in Prague on July 20 with Spanish and Walloon mercenaries, Ferdinand occupied the city. While he only confiscated the estates of a few compromised individuals, he dealt severely with the towns, specifically Prague. He revised their ancient privileges, which resulted in a broad confiscation of municipal lands, and imposed state officials—known as town-captains in Prague and town-judges elsewhere—to exercise unlimited control over municipal affairs. Additionally, the Bohemian Brethren faced severe persecution, and their bishop, Augusta, was imprisoned for many years.
