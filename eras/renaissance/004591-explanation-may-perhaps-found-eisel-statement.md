# The Evolution of the Bassoon and Related Instruments

The history of the bassoon is characterized by a transition from early, unwieldy wind instruments to the modern form. This evolution involves a series of structural modifications to improve portability and pitch, as well as a distinction between different regional traditions of the instrument.

## The Deutscher Basson and the Bombard
In 1738, J. J. Eisel wrote regarding the *Deutscher Basson*, which he distinguished from the *Basson* (the modern bassoon). Eisel noted that the *Deutsche Bassons*—also referred to by German ancestors as *Fagotte* or *Bombardi*—were no longer in use by his time, as music had since been "clothed in Italian and French style." Eisel concluded that because these instruments were obsolete, it was "unnecessary to waste paper on them."

These instruments refer to the bass pommer or *bombard*, which were characterized by being extraordinarily long. These were produced successfully by Schnitzer. The existence of the *Deutscher Basson* suggests that the modern bassoon did not originate in Germany. Furthermore, J. J. Quantz, who served as the flute-master for Frederick the Great, credited France with the transformation of the pommer (bombard) into the bassoon, and the schalmey into the oboe.

## The Development of the Curtail
To address the unwieldy proportions of the tenor pommer in C and to make it more portable, a new construction method was developed. Instead of a single long pipe, the instrument was made from a block of wood with a diameter more than double that of the pommer. Two bores were cut into this block, which communicated at a flat bottom. At the top, the crook (containing the double reed mouthpiece) and the bell were positioned side by side.

This instrument featured two keys, one hole at the back, and six holes in front. It was known by several names depending on the region:
*   **England:** *curtail*, *curtal*, or *curtoll*.
*   **France:** *courtaud*.
*   **Other regions:** *dulceian*, *dolcian*, or *doucaine*.

Historical records from 1582 mention the "Curtoll" alongside the "Drone" and "Hobius (Hautboy)" as common "bleting musick."

## The Double Curtail and Early Fagotto
The next stage of evolution was the production of the double curtail. This instrument was essentially a converted bass pommer pitched an octave below the single curtail. In terms of both construction and pitch, the double curtail was identical to the early *fagotto* in C.

A drawing of the double curtail is found in the manuscript of *The Academy of Armoury* by Randle Holme, written before 1688. The accompanying description states that the double curtail is "double the bigness of the single" and is played "8 notes deeper." It is described as consisting of two pipes fixed within one thick bass pipe; one pipe is significantly longer than the other, and a crooked brass pipe containing a reed extends from the top of the lower pipe.
