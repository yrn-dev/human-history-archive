# Spanish Settlements in the Americas

## Intellectual and Cultural Impact
The establishment of Spanish settlements had a significant impact on the distribution of talent and enterprise from the "old country." These settlements drew away much of Spain's best talent and enterprise. This shift was evident in the proliferation of printing presses in cities such as Mexico, which produced a high volume of publications. While these works were primarily religious in character, the presses also published a variety of historical and linguistic texts.

## Conquest and Early Governance
The expansion of Spanish influence in the Americas was often driven by adventurers. In Peru, Pizarro sailed from the Isthmus of Panama in 1530 with an expedition of 168 Spaniards. Similar to Cortez in Mexico, Pizarro utilized native dissensions and treachery—specifically seizing the Inca of Peru—to secure possession of the state. Following these initial conquests, the regions were reduced to Spanish provinces through a process involving Spanish reinforcements from Mexico and the suppression of native insurrections.

The early phase of these settlements was characterized by violence, loot, and cruelty. Spanish adventurers frequently ill-treated native populations and quarreled among themselves. Because the law and order of Spain were distant—often months or years away—the transition from a period of conquest to one of formal government and settlement occurred very slowly. Despite this instability, a steady stream of silver and gold flowed from the Americas across the Atlantic to the Spanish people and government.

## Colonial Administration and Trade
Spanish colonial policy was often marked by oppressive practices and strict monopolies. In the West Indies, the island of Santo Domingo was almost depopulated in the early 16th century due to these policies. Spanish monopolies on the produce of the soil deprived local establishments of convenient markets, leading colonists to welcome Dutch, French, and English vessels to trade their cargoes. This environment fostered the rise of the buccaneer—individuals who were alternately hunters, sailors, and planters—who developed an intense detestation of Spain.

In the La Plata region, the foundation of Buenos Aires saw multiple attempts. The first in 1535 by Don Pedro de Mendoza failed due to mismanagement and Indian hostility, though it led to the founding of Asunción. A second attempt in 1542 under Cabeza de Vaca also failed. The city was finally established in 1580 by Don Juan de Garay, who sought to create a port where ships could be provisioned and supplies from Spain received. Although Buenos Aires eventually became the capital of its own province in 1620 and the seat of a bishopric, the Spanish crown and merchants initially showed little interest in the station, as they were dazzled by the gold and silver of Peru. This lack of attention, combined with the proximity of Portuguese Brazil, made smuggling common.

## Territorial Transitions and Diplomacy
Spain's colonial reach extended into North America, notably with the formal transfer of Louisiana to Spain on August 18, 1769. Under Spanish rule, the Spanish tongue and law replaced French ones, though the colony remained essentially French. Spanish governors, such as the popular Bernardo de Galvez (1756-1794), pursued liberal and conciliatory policies, encouraging intermarriages between the Spanish, French, and English. Galvez provided valuable aid to the United States during the American War of Independence and captured important posts in British West Florida between 1779 and 1781. Later, in 1794, a treaty recognized the Mississippi River as the western boundary of the United States, separating it from Louisiana.

In the Bay of Honduras, Spanish authorities struggled to expel British log-wood cutters. Despite a major inland expedition of 1,500 men from the town of Peten in April 1754, the British resisted. While the treaty of 1763 provided some protections for these cutters, tensions remained. In September 1779, the Spaniards attacked and destroyed the establishment at Belize, taking prisoners to Havana and Mérida in Yucatan.
