# Johann Sebastian Bach

Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750) was a German musical composer whose importance in the history of music is described as impossible to exaggerate. Though his art was neglected by his younger contemporaries—who viewed it as crabbed and old-fashioned—his influence eventually became all-pervading.

## Family and Early Influences
The Bach family maintained a significant presence in music history for nearly two hundred years. The family genealogy, compiled by J. Sebastian and completed by his son Philipp Emanuel, identifies Veit Bach, a miller and baker, as the family founder. Veit's son, Hans Bach ("der Spielmann"), was the first professional musician in the family. Sebastian's grandfather was Christoph, another son of Hans. Other influential ancestors included Johann Michael and Johann Christoph (sons of Heinrich of Arnstadt), the latter of whom is believed by some to be the author of the motet *Ich lasse dich nicht*. Sebastian particularly admired Johann Ludwig, from whom he copied twelve church cantatas, occasionally adding his own work to them.

## Career and Personal Life
In 1723, Bach moved to Leipzig to become the cantor of the Thomasschule, as he found his previous position at Coethen uninspiring for choral music. During this time, he also maintained positions as visiting *Kapellmeister* at Coethen and Weissenfels. 

Bach's personal life was marked by two marriages. His first wife died in 1720, leaving him with seven children, including Friedermann and Philipp Emanuel. He married again in December 1721. His second wife, a soprano, inspired many of his most inspired arias and assisted him extensively with his work; her musical handwriting eventually became nearly indistinguishable from his own.

A significant event in Bach's career occurred in 1747 when he was summoned to visit Frederick the Great at Potsdam. This visit was prompted by the success of his son Philipp Emanuel, who had been appointed court-composer to the king of Prussia. During this visit, which Bach regarded as the culmination of his career, he played on Silbermann's newly invented pianofortes and tested the organs of Potsdam's churches. Frederick provided a theme for Bach to extemporize upon, which later became the basis for *Das musikalische Opfer*.

## Musical Style and Works
Bach is characterized not as a reformer or inventor of new forms, but as an "unbiassed seeker of truth" who vitalized every form he touched. He utilized the Da Capo Aria, developing it in various ways without abusing it as a staple for opera, as he did not intend to write dramatic music in the strict sense.

His work is divided into several periods:
* **Arnstadt-Muehlhausen, Weimar, and Coethen:** The Coethen period is noted for its instrumental music, while its choral music is described as easy-going and comparatively uninteresting.
* **The Final Period (1733–1750):** During this time, instrumental music showed a preference for large-scale designs (seen when comparing the first book of *Wohltemperirtes Klavier* from 1722 to the second book from 1744), while choral works became more numerous and terse, such as *Jesu, der du meine Seele*.

His church music includes 190 cantatas, 40 of which are for solo voices and about 60 based on chorales. Others are practically short oratorios. He also composed three wedding cantatas, the Easter oratorio, and the Christmas oratorio, which consists of six connected cantatas. Among his instrumental works, the *Goldberg* variations are noted for their exceptional length.

## Death and Legacy
Bach's sight began to fail two years after his visit to Potsdam, and he eventually became perfectly blind. He died of apoplexy on July 28, 1750. At the time of his death, he was mourned as one of the greatest clavier players and organists of his era, though little of his compositions were known. His manuscript works were divided among his sons, and many were lost.

The "Bach-revival" was later driven by the efforts of Mendelssohn, Schumann, and others, as well as earlier performances by Wesley and Crotch in England. In 1850, the *Bach-Gesellschaft* was formed to publish his remaining works. This effort spanned over fifty years, resulting in the first publication of about four-fifths of his choral works. Wilhelm Rust, the editor of the *Bach-Gesellschaft* for fourteen years, is credited with an unrivaled insight into Bach's style.
