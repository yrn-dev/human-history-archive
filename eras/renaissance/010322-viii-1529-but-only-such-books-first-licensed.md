# Printing and Book Culture in the 16th Century

The early 16th century was marked by a significant increase in the demand for books, driven largely by the Reformation in England under Edward VI and Henry VIII, as well as religious dissensions across the European continent. This period saw the emergence of strict state controls over the press, the development of early book ownership markers, and the publication of influential linguistic and religious texts.

## Press Regulation and Censorship in England
In England, neither the Stuart nor the Tudor dynasties tolerated a free press, leading to various efforts to curb the production and distribution of literature. In 1529, Henry VIII granted the first patent for the office of king's printer to Thomas Berthelet. Under this system, only books that had first been licensed were permitted to be printed. The restrictions were severe; during this era, the possession or purchase of a book that had not been licensed was considered a punishable offence.

Further institutional controls were established in 1556 with the incorporation of the Company of Stationers, which was granted extensive powers to repress "obnoxious books." Censorship was later exercised effectively by the Star Chamber. Despite these precautions, the high demand for polemical books led many to be printed outside of England and smuggled into the country surreptitiously.

The level of interference varied by monarch. Queen Elizabeth generally interfered little with books, except for those that touched upon her royal prerogatives or originated from Roman Catholics. Bookselling flourished toward the end of her reign and during the reign of her successor, James. However, Archbishop Laud later introduced many arbitrary restrictions. These were nearly all removed during the Commonwealth. By the Protectorate, bookselling had increased to such an extent that W. London published *A Catalogue of the most Vendible Books in England* in 1658, which categorized works under headings such as History, Physic, School Books, Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Divinity. Following this period, the Restoration reinstated the office of Licenser of the Press, a position that remained until 1694.

## Notable Publications and Translations
The early 16th century saw the production of several significant works across different languages and regions. In 1529, the Italian translation of Trissino's *De vulgari eloquentia* was first published at Vicenza. This treatise, written in two books (though intended to be four), sought to establish the Italian language as a literary tongue and distinguish "courtly" speech—which could serve as a national bond and a demarcation against external nations—from local dialects. It also aimed to establish rules for poetical composition, specifically the canzone, ballata, and sonetto. The original Latin version of this work was not published until 1577 in Paris.

In the realm of religious translation, Tyndale worked on the English translation of the Bible. While his manuscript translation of Deuteronomy is noted to have perished in a shipwreck off the coast of Holland in 1529, the entire *Pentateuch* was printed in Marburg in 1530 by Hans Luft, featuring marginal annotations and prefaces of a strongly controversial nature. This work was reissued in 1534 and again in London in 1551. Additionally, the *Book of Jonah* appeared in 1531 with a significant prologue.

## Book-Plates and Ownership
The use of the book-plate, or *ex-libris*, emerged nearly as early as the printed book itself. These printed labels served to indicate individual ownership, evolving from the hand-painted armorial devices found in medieval manuscripts. The earliest known examples are German; woodcuts representing a shield of arms supported by an angel were pasted into books given to the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim by Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach around 1480.

Other early examples include a French book-plate from 1529 belonging to Jean Bertaud de la Tour-Blanche. In England, an early example is a gift-plate used by Sir Nicholas Bacon for books presented to the university of Cambridge. Other early national examples include a 1597 plate from Holland belonging to Anna van der Aa, an Italian plate from 1622, and the first known American example, a plain printed label for John Williams in 1679.

## Global Context of 1529
The year 1529 was a period of significant geopolitical activity. While England was establishing its printing patents, Suleiman the Magnificent, the Sultan who ruled from Hungary to Bagdad, besieged Vienna. In other regions, Marcus Fugger (born 1529) would later become an author of a book on horse-breeding and a translator of the *Historia ecclesiastica* of Nicephorus Callistus. In England, the town of Buckingham began returning two members to parliament in 1529.
