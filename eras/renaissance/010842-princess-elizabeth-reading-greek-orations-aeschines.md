# Princess Elizabeth and the Study of Greek Oratory

## Education under Roger Ascham
Princess Elizabeth received extensive classical instruction under the tutelage of Roger Ascham. Ascham was selected as her tutor through the influence of Sir William Cecil and at the princess's own request, prevailing over another candidate supported by Queen Katherine and Admiral Seymour. This appointment followed the death of Elizabeth's previous tutor, Grindal, in January 1548.

Ascham taught the princess for two years, primarily at Cheshunt, beginning when she was sixteen years old. In correspondence with the Strassburg schoolmaster Sturm, Ascham praised Elizabeth's industry, wisdom, stature, and beauty. He noted her proficiency in several languages, stating that she spoke English, French, and Italian well, and was capable of speaking Latin readily and Greek moderately. Ascham further commended her handwriting in both Latin and Greek.

## Curriculum and Literary Focus
The princess's studies were characterized by a rigorous engagement with classical texts. Her knowledge of Latin was derived almost exclusively from two authors: Titus Livius and Cicero, the latter of whom she read nearly in full. Her daily routine included dedicating her mornings to the Greek Testament. Following this, she read the tragedies of Sophocles and select orations by Isocrates. Ascham supplemented these studies with the works of Melanchthon's Commonplaces and St Cyprian.

## Advanced Study of Aeschines and Demosthenes
After a period of separation—during which Ascham served as secretary to Sir Richard Morrison, the ambassador to Charles V—Ascham resumed his studies with Princess Elizabeth in 1555. During this period, their curriculum focused on Greek oratory. Specifically, they read the orations of Aeschines and the work *De Corona* by Demosthenes.

The texts studied by the princess represent a significant period of Athenian rhetorical conflict. The oration *De Corona* (also referred to as "On the Crown") was delivered by Demosthenes as a response to Aeschines. This conflict began in 336 when Aeschines gave notice that he intended to proceed against Ctesiphon for proposing that Demosthenes receive a golden crown from the state. After six years of avoidance, Aeschines delivered the speech "Against Ctesiphon" in 330, which attacked the public life of Demosthenes. Demosthenes responded with "On the Crown," a work described as one of the most pathetic and splendid examples of ancient eloquence, resulting in an overwhelming victory for Demosthenes and the honor of Athens.

## Rhetorical Context of the Texts
The works of the orators studied by Elizabeth reflect different stylistic qualities as noted by Cicero. While Demosthenes was distinguished by force (*vis*), Aeschines was characterized by resonance (*sonitus*). The archival records indicate that the body of work attributed to Aeschines varies in authenticity; while some of his political speeches are valuable, others, such as the six epistles and certain rhetorician works (Or. 60 and Or. 61), are considered forgeries.
