# Policies of the Government of Queen Elizabeth

The government of Queen Elizabeth was characterized by a reliance on a close circle of advisors, a cautious approach to foreign diplomacy, and the strategic use of public perception to maintain authority. Her administration was marked by a tension between the deliberative caution of her chief ministers and the queen's own tendencies toward hesitation and caprice.

## Administrative Leadership and Council
Queen Elizabeth placed an exceptional level of trust in William Cecil, relying on him more than any other individual. Cecil's approach to governance was defined by caution and deliberation, seeking a *via media* (middle way) in matters of state, church, and foreign affairs. His primary goal was to provide England with the time necessary to grow strong enough to withstand external shocks, avoiding open ruptures through a policy of deliberation.

Other key figures in the administration included Sir Nicholas Bacon, who served as lord keeper of the great seal and a privy councillor. Bacon's tenure involved presiding over the House of Lords during the opening of Elizabeth's first parliament and securing the archbishopric of Canterbury for Matthew Parker. Additionally, Francis Knollys served as a privy councillor, vice-chamberlain of the royal household, and treasurer of the royal household from 1572 until 1596.

## Foreign Policy and Diplomacy
The foreign policy of Elizabeth's reign was often described as "tortuous," drawing objections from officials like Francis Knollys. A central theme of her diplomacy was the management of relations with continental powers and the handling of Mary, Queen of Scots. 

While William Cecil generally favored more decided intervention to support continental Protestants, Elizabeth often shrank from such responsibility. The government provided aid to the Dutch and Huguenots, but only in amounts sufficient to keep them active in struggles that warded danger away from English shores. 

Diplomatic relations with France involved various missions, including those by Castelnau, who served as ambassador to Elizabeth for ten years. During this time, Castelnau attempted to promote a marriage between Elizabeth and the duke of Alencon to strengthen the alliance between the two nations; however, Elizabeth frequently made promises only to break them. Internal disagreements also existed within the council; for instance, Sir Nicholas Bacon opposed the policy of making war on France to benefit the enemies of Mary, Queen of Scots, citing England's poverty.

## Internal Security and Legal Measures
The government employed rigorous measures to protect the queen from internal and external threats. In 1584, a bill was introduced into the House of Commons to legalize a national association for Elizabeth's defense. The administration also utilized a network of spies, such as those under Walsingham, to intercept correspondence and uncover conspiracies. This was evident in the case of Anthony Babington, whose plot to murder Elizabeth and her ministers—supported by Philip II of Spain—was uncovered after his letters to Mary, Queen of Scots, were intercepted.

The government also managed the detention of political prisoners. Francis Knollys was tasked with the charge of Mary, Queen of Scots, holding her at Carlisle, Bolton Castle, and Tutbury Castle. Elizabeth's government also conducted high-profile trials for those deemed threats, including the trials of Anthony Babington, Philip Howard (earl of Arundel), and Mary, Queen of Scots.

## Public Image and Governance
Elizabeth utilized specific "arts" to manage the minds of her subjects and prepossess them in favor of extraordinary government measures. This practice was described as a form of "music" used to influence the public. 

The queen also used royal progresses and state symbols to maintain visibility and connection with her subjects. In 1564, a state coach was made for her. During a progress to Warwick, she ordered every part and side of her coach to be opened so that her subjects could behold her.
