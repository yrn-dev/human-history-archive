# Tunisia and Algeria

Tunisia and Algeria are states located in Barbary, a general designation for the region of northern Africa bounded by the Mediterranean to the north, the Sahara to the south, the Atlantic to the west, and Egypt to the east. The name "Barbary" is derived from the Berbers, who are the chief inhabitants of this region.

## Establishment of Turkish Power
The foundation of Turkish rule in northern Africa was closely linked to the activities of the Barbary pirates. Following the 1492 conquest of Granada by the Catholic sovereigns of Spain, many Moors were driven into exile and responded with piratical attacks on the Spanish coast. They were aided by Moslem adventurers from the Levant, most notably Arouj and his brother Khair-ed-Din (known to Christians as "Redbeard" or Barbarossa), both natives of Mitylene.

In response to these attacks, Spain began conquering coastal towns, including Tunis, Algiers, and Oran. After Arouj fell in battle against the Spaniards in 1518, Khair-ed-Din sought assistance from Selim, the sultan of Turkey. The sultan provided troops, which enabled Khair-ed-Din to drive the Spaniards from a fort on a rocky island in front of Algiers in 1529, thereby establishing Turkish power in the region.

## Governance and Administration (1518–1659)
From approximately 1518 until 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli. This period of administration ended with the death of Uluch Ali in 1587.

From 1587 until 1659, the governance of these territories shifted. They were ruled by Turkish pashas who were dispatched from Constantinople. These pashas were appointed to govern for three-year terms. However, this system ended in 1659 when a military revolt occurred in Algiers, which reduced the pashas to nonentities.

## Post-1659 Political Status
From 1659 onward, while the African cities of Tunisia and Algeria nominally remained parts of the Turkish empire, they functioned in reality as anarchical military republics. These republics lived by plunder and chose their own rulers. These pirate communities, which relied entirely on plunder for their resources, persisted until the French conquest of Algiers in 1830.

## Regional Characteristics and Economy
The coasts of Algeria and Tunisia are noted for their important fisheries, specifically for the harvesting of precious red coral.

The region's history is also marked by the presence of "Rumas." This term was used by Algerians, Tunisians, and Moroccan Moors to describe the descendants of the Spanish Moors. In the seventeenth century, these "Romans" are credited in Algeria with conquering the Upper Niger basin for Morocco, with their descendants remaining between Timbuktu and Jenné.

## Later History and French Influence
In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, the region became a site of geopolitical tension. Between 1898 and 1911, Germany attempted to undermine French rule in Tunis and Algeria by providing arms and money to Moroccans, encouraging attacks on French authority in Southern Tunis and Western Algeria.

In Tunisia, the 19th century saw the influence of Khaireddin, a Turkish statesman of Circassian race. Originally sold to Hamuda Pasha, the bey of Tunis, Khaireddin rose to become the minister of finance. He was highly valued by subsequent beys—Ahmet (1837), Mohammed (1855), and Sadok (1859)—and was responsible for a series of liberal measures. Khaireddin also secured the Turkish sultan's confirmation of the reigning family's right to succeed to the beylic. Despite his liberal views, he strongly opposed the French plan to establish a protectorate over Tunisia in the early 1870s.
