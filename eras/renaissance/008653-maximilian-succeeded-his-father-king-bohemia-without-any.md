# Maximilian II of Bohemia

Maximilian succeeded his father, Ferdinand I, as king of Bohemia without any opposition. His accession was ensured by his father, who had obtained Maximilian's coronation as king of Bohemia in 1562, a few years before Ferdinand's death in 1564. In addition to the Bohemian throne, Maximilian also succeeded his father as the king of Hungary and as emperor.

## Religious Policy and Internal Relations
The circumstances surrounding Maximilian's accession were largely in his favor due to his early education. Having been primarily educated by Protestant tutors in his youth, he openly avowed a strong sympathy for the party of church reform for a period of time. This reputation secured him the support of Bohemian church reformers. Conversely, the Romanists and the retrograde Utraquists traditionally supported the house of Habsburg.

Despite these beginnings, Maximilian's reign did not fulfill the expectations of the church reform partisans. While he generally refused to sanction measures against Protestants—despite the advice of the Jesuits, who were gaining significant influence in Bohemia—he did publish new decrees against the Bohemian Brethren. Over time, Maximilian began to attend the functions of the Roman church again after a long absence. His reign was characterized by an indifference toward the many ecclesiastical disputes of the era, a trait perhaps rooted in religious scepticism.

## The Conflict Over the Compacts
In 1567, Maximilian visited the Bohemians for the first time since his accession. During this visit, he faced a common difficulty for Habsburg princes of the period: the need to raise funds for warfare against the Turks. When Maximilian requested supplies for this purpose, the Bohemian estates responded by presenting demands regarding religious matters.

The estates' primary demand was that the "Compacts" cease to be part of the laws of the country. The Compacts had previously granted freedom of worship to moderate Utraquists—those whose faith differed from the Roman church primarily in their claim to receive communion in both kinds. However, Ferdinand I had utilized the Compacts as an instrument to justify the oppression of advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian Brethren.

## Succession and Legacy
The results of the diet of 1575 were generally favorable to the estates. Consequently, the estates recognized Maximilian's eldest son, Rudolph, as his successor and consented to his coronation. Maximilian died in 1576, and Rudolph succeeded him without opposition.

Under the broader rule of Emperor Maximilian II (1564–1576), a liberal policy was maintained to preserve peace. However, he was unable to establish religious liberty on a permanent basis within his dominions. During his tenure, various forms of Christian belief struggled for mastery in Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia, while Catholicism remained largely confined to the mountains of Tirol. Additionally, he was unable to resolve the humiliating position of his government as a tributary to the Turk.
