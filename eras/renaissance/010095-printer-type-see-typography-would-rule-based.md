# Early Printing and Typography

## Development of Printer's Type
In the early stages of printing, the general appearance of a printed copy was very similar to that of a formal book-hand manuscript written in the same district. As a rule, the printer's type was based on handwriting that scribes considered appropriate for works of the same class. To further mimic manuscripts, printers often added illustrations, paragraph marks, initial-letters, headlines, and chapter headings by hand, using styles that closely resembled the decorations found in the original manuscripts.

The character of the type used in books is typically categorized as italic, gothic, or roman. For example, the first printed English Bible (Coverdale's Bible), likely printed by Christopher Froschouer of Zuerich, was produced in German black letter. In the 15th century, types were often identified by their number in the sequence of founts used by a specific printer.

## Identification and Typographical Detection
Many early printed works, known as incunabula, contain no information regarding who printed them, where they were produced, or when they were finished. However, because early types possessed a high degree of individuality compared to modern types, "typographical detectives" have been able to track the origins of most of these works. Robert Proctor, who died in 1903, is regarded as the greatest of these detectives; he enumerated and described all known types used by each printer in his *Index to the Early Printed Books at the British Museum*. These descriptions were later made more precise by Dr. Haebler in the *Typenrepertorium der Wiegendrucke* (1905).

To assist in this identification, various volumes of facsimiles have been published, including works by K. Burger, J. W. Holtrop, O. Thierry-Poux, K. Haebler, and Gordon Duff, as well as publications from the Type Facsimile Society and the *Woolley Facsimiles*. Using these facsimiles and descriptions, it is often possible to assign a newly discovered book to a specific press and sometimes determine its completion date within a few days or weeks.

## Evolution of the Title-Page and Colophon
Early printed books generally lacked title-pages and often contained no statement regarding the printer, date, or location of production. If such information was provided, it was placed in a paragraph at the end of the book called a colophon. Printers frequently attached a device to the colophon, such as a fanciful design or the arms of the printer or the town where the work was produced.

The first known dated title-page is a nine-line paragraph from 1470, printed by Arnold ther Hoernen at Cologne for the work *Sermo ad populum predicabilis in festo presentacionis Beatissime Marie Semper Virginis*. In 1476, Erhard Ratdolt and partners in Venice printed a Latin calendar featuring their names, the date, and verses on the title-page, surrounded by a four-piece border. For the following twenty years, title-pages—when used—usually consisted only of a short title and occasionally a printer's or publisher's device or a woodcut.

## Notable Printers and Typefounders
Several individuals significantly advanced the art of typography and printing:

*   **John Baskerville (1706–1775):** An English printer who began experiments in type-founding around 1750. He produced types of superior elegance and distinctness. His 1762 edition of *Horace* is noted for the beauty of its type and paper.
*   **William Caslon (1692–1766):** A London typefounder whose types were prized for their legibility and distinction, earning him patronage across England and the continent. His "Caslon Old Face" type was revived around 1845 for the printing of the *Diary of Lady Willoughby*.
*   **Daniel Bomberg:** A Christian printer active in Venice between 1516 and 1549 who introduced a new era in Hebrew typography, producing the first complete edition of the Talmud (1520–1523) and the *editio princeps* of the rabbinical Bible (1516–1517).
*   **Laurens Koster (c. 1370–1440):** A Dutch official from Haarlem whose claims as one of the inventors of the art of printing have been recognized by many investigators.

## Technical Measurements
In the study of old books, the "type-page" refers to the printed portion of the page. Because uncut copies of old books are rare, bibliographers measure the type-page in millimetres and record the number of lines per page. These measurements can vary by approximately 3% depending on how much the paper was damped for printing.
