# Henry Balnaves

Henry Balnaves (1512?–1579) was a Scottish reformer and politician born in Kirkcaldy around 1512. Educated at St Andrews and on the continent, he adopted Protestant views during his studies. Upon returning to Scotland, he pursued legal studies and was appointed a lord of session in 1538. Around the same time, he married Christian Scheves, and in 1539 he was granted the estate of Halhill in Fife.

## Early Political Career and Imprisonment
Before 1540, Balnaves was sworn into the privy council of James V. He became known as a member of the party that favored an ecclesiastical reformation and an alliance with England. While he is described as having served as treasurer to James, he was appointed secretary in the new government of the infant Queen Mary in January 1543 by the regent Arran. 

During this period, Balnaves promoted an act that permitted the reading of the Scriptures in the vulgar tongue. He also served as one of the commissioners tasked with arranging a marriage treaty between the young queen and the future Edward VI. During his time in London, Balnaves was not considered as complaisant as his fellow commissioners and was excluded from some of the engagements they made. Due to the hostility of Beton, who "loved him worst of all," and Arran's shift toward the priestly party, Balnaves was deprived of his offices and imprisoned in Blackness Castle in November 1543.

## English Agency and Captivity
Balnaves was released in May of the following year upon the arrival of Hertford's fleet. Following his release, he became a paid agent for the English cause in Scotland. Although he did not participate in the murder of Beton, he was an active defender of the castle of St Andrews. His services were compensated by the English government; he received £100 from Henry VIII in December 1546 and a £125 annuity from Protector Somerset in 1547, while also serving as the English paymaster of the forces in St Andrews.

In July 1547, when the castle surrendered to the French, Balnaves was taken as a prisoner to Rouen. Despite efforts by Somerset to secure his release, he remained in captivity, though his pension continued. While imprisoned, he provided information to the English government, for which Mary Tudor rewarded him with £50 in June 1554. During this time, he wrote a "comfortable treatise of justification," which was later published in Edinburgh in 1584 as *The Confession of Faith* with a preface by Knox.

## Return to Scotland and Later Life
In 1557, Balnaves was permitted to return to Scotland and regain his property. This permission was likely granted under the assumption that the burnings carried out by Mary Tudor had diminished his English affections and that he would serve his own country during the threatening war between two Catholic nations.

The accession of Queen Elizabeth altered his standing, leading Mary of Guise to accuse him of "practices out of England." Balnaves took an active role in the rising of 1559 and was commissioned by the Congregation to solicit help from the English government via Sir Ralph Sadleir at Berwick. In February 1560, he was selected as one of the Scottish representatives to negotiate with the duke of Norfolk.

Balnaves was restored to his office as lord of session in 1563 and was appointed by the General Assembly to revise the *Book of Discipline*. His later years were marked by significant judicial and political involvement; in 1567, he served as one of Bothwell's judges for the murder of Darnley, and in 1568, he accompanied Moray to the York inquiry regarding the guilt of Queen Mary. He resigned his judicial office in 1574 and died in Edinburgh in 1579. Additionally, he has been claimed as a Scots bard based on a single ballad titled "O gallandis all, I cry and call," printed in Allan Ramsay's *Evergreen*.
