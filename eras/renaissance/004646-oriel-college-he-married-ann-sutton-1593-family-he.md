# Oriel College

Oriel College is an institution of higher learning in Oxford that has been attended by a variety of figures, ranging from poets and clergy to members of the British peerage.

## Notable Alumni and Associates

The archive records several individuals associated with Oriel College across different centuries:

**Nicholas Breton**
Nicholas Breton (1545?–1626) was an English poet from an old family based at Layer-Breton, Essex. While official records of his residence at the university are absent, the diary of the Rev. Richard Madox notes that Breton was "once of Oriel College." Breton was a popular writer among his contemporaries, producing satires, miscellaneous prose tracts, and religious and pastoral poems. Although his references to Mary Magdalene and the Virgin suggested Catholicism to some, his prose writings demonstrate that he was an ardent Protestant. He was patronized by Mary, countess of Pembroke, until approximately 1601.

**Edward King**
Edward King (1829–1910) was an English bishop and the second son of the Rev. Walter King. After graduating from Oriel College, Oxford, he was ordained in 1854. He served as a lecturer and chaplain at Cuddesdon Theological College, later becoming its principal from 1863 to 1873. King subsequently served as a canon of Christ Church and the regius professor of pastoral theology at Oxford. Known for his sincerity, goodness, and personal charm, he became the bishop of Lincoln in 1885. His tenure was marked by a prosecution for ritualistic practices between 1888 and 1890 before the judicial committee of the Privy Council and the archbishop of Canterbury, Dr. Benson. Despite these proceedings, he was revered across all classes for his saintliness of character.

**Richard Grenville**
Richard Grenville (1797–1861), the 2nd duke of Buckingham and Chandos, received his education at Eton and Oriel College, Oxford. He served as a member of parliament for Buckinghamshire from 1818 to 1839 and was the individual responsible for the "Chandos clause" in the Reform Bill of 1832. He held the office of lord privy seal from September 1841 to January 1842. Known as the "Farmers' Friend" due to his opposition to the repeal of the corn laws, Grenville later faced financial ruin; his residences were seized by creditors in 1847, leading him to leave England. Upon his return, he focused on literature, publishing several memoirs concerning the courts and cabinets of George III, George IV, William IV, and Victoria.
