# Anne Boleyn

Anne Boleyn (c. 1507–1536) was the Queen of England and the wife of Henry VIII. She was the daughter of Elizabeth, daughter of Thomas Howard (later duke of Norfolk and earl of Surrey), and Sir Thomas Boleyn, who later became the earl of Wiltshire and Ormonde. While some later writers have ascribed her birth to 1501 or 1502, Camden records her birth year as 1507.

## Early Life and Court Experience
In 1514, Anne accompanied Mary Tudor to France for the princess's marriage to Louis XII. Following the death of the king, Anne remained in France, serving as one of the women in waiting to Queen Claude, the wife of Francis I. She returned to England in 1521 or 1522, where she attracted numerous suitors and admirers. Among these were the poet Sir Thomas Wyatt and Henry Percy, the heir of the earl of Northumberland. A proposed marriage between Anne and Percy was stopped by the king, who instead provided a match for her in Sir James Butler. However, Anne remained unmarried.

## Relations with Henry VIII
The exact beginning of Anne's relationship with Henry VIII is unknown. Some have surmised that it originated as early as 1523, though there is no proof that the king's passion existed prior to May 1527, when proceedings for divorce were initiated; the love letters associated with this period are undated. Between 1522 and 1525, Henry bestowed a series of favors and grants upon Anne's father, which some have interpreted as a symptom of the king's affection, though this is considered doubtful.

Unlike her sister Mary, who is believed by contemporaries to have fallen victim to Henry's solicitations—a claim supported by the extraordinary terms of the dispensation granted for Henry to marry Anne—Anne had no intention of becoming the king's mistress. Her conduct appeared to be governed by ambition, as she intended to be his queen. 

Anne's name was first openly linked to the king as a potential wife in a letter dated August 16, 1527, written by Mendoza, the imperial ambassador, to Charles V. At this time, Cardinal Wolsey opposed the alliance and was negotiating a marriage for Henry with Renee, the daughter of Louis XII. Despite this, Henry sent his secretary, William Knight, on a separate mission to Rome to secure facilities for his marriage to Anne.

## Trial and Execution
On May 1, the king abruptly ended a tournament at Greenwich following reports of the queen's ill-conduct. Several of Anne's reputed lovers were arrested, and on May 2, Anne was committed to the Tower. She was charged with adultery with various individuals, including her own brother, Lord Rochford. On May 12, Mark Smeaton, Henry Norris, William Brereton, and Sir Francis Weston were found guilty of high treason. On May 15, an assembly of twenty-six peers unanimously condemned Anne and Lord Rochford. The sentence was delivered by Anne's uncle, the duke of Norfolk, who presided as lord steward.

During these events, Anne informed Cranmer of a supposed impediment to her marriage with the king—some accounts suggest a previous marriage to the earl of Northumberland, though he denied this. On May 17, a committee of ecclesiastical lawyers and the archbishop pronounced this impediment sufficient to invalidate the marriage.

Anne was executed on May 19 on Tower Green, where she was beheaded with a sword by the executioner of Calais. Sir William Kingston, governor of the Tower, noted that unlike most who faced execution in sorrow, Anne appeared to face death with joy and pleasure. On May 20, the day after her death, Henry became betrothed to Jane Seymour.

## Legacy and Legal Findings
The question of Anne's guilt remains a subject of historical debate. While she protested her innocence to Sir William Kingston and made no confession on the scaffold, the archive notes that her guilt is more than probable. This is based on the fact that two grand juries, a petty jury, and a tribunal of lay peers all unanimously passed a sentence of guilt. Furthermore, her own father and uncle supported the sentence.

The discovery of her alleged misdeeds coincided with Henry's disappointment in her failure to provide a male heir. While Anne had given birth to a child every year of their marriage, the king's despotic power and Anne's universal unpopularity may have hindered the administration of pure justice. Among her accused lovers, only Mark Smeaton made a full confession, though it is possible this was obtained through hopes of pardon or threats of torture. Henry Norris also confessed but later claimed he had been betrayed into doing so.
