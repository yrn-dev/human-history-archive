# Early Venetian Bookbindings

## Venetian Binding Techniques
In the history of bookbinding, early Venetian work is noted for specific structural and decorative characteristics. A distinctive feature of many early Venetian bindings is the presence of recessed panels. These panels were created through the use of double boards, where the upper board was pierced. This specific method of construction is described as being finished in a true oriental fashion.

## The Influence of Gold Tooling
The art of gold tooling on leather is a highly regarded skill that was likely brought to Venice from the East. The finest examples of this craft are found in Italian work from the late 15th century. This art form eventually spread beyond Italy; for instance, Thomas Berthelet, who served as the Royal Binder to Henry VIII and worked until approximately 1558, is believed to be the first binder in England to practice gold tooling. Berthelet's early work exhibited a strong Italian influence, suggesting he may have been instructed by an Italian master.

## Notable Venetian Printers and Styles
Aldus Manutius, a prominent Venetian printer, utilized a specific aesthetic for several of his books. These were bound in dark morocco and featured small dolphins and "Aldine" knot leaves, which were applied using both gold and blind tooling. A similar style was employed by the Florentine printer Giunta, although his bindings did not include the dolphin motifs.

## Evolution of European Binding
The introduction of printing to Europe around the middle of the 15th century initially sparked a reaction against the precious covers and large, illuminated manuscripts of the past. This led to the production of cheaper, smaller, and less attractive books. These were generally bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf and were ornamented with blind stamps in large panels. 

Prior to the rise of gold tooling, the earliest leather ornamentation consisted of impressions made by lines or small metal points pressed into the leather. This evolved into the use of decorative stamps, most of which were cut intaglio to create cameo-form impressions. In England, this "blind" stamping (performed without gold leaf) reached a state of perfection during the 12th and 13th centuries in locations such as London, Cambridge, Oxford, and Durham. An example of this work is the 12th-century binding of the Winchester Domesday Book.
