# The Literary Parallel of Paris and Troy in Orlando Inamorato

## Literary Influence and Structure
The *Orlando Inamorato*, a significant poem in Italian literature composed by Boiardo, is recognized as the first example of the romantic epic to serve as a model, subsequently influencing Ariosto's *Orlando Furioso*. Boiardo wrote the work for the amusement of his court and Duke Hercules, often composing while retired at his estates in Scandiano. He frequently incorporated descriptions of the environs of his chateau into the poem. Additionally, Boiardo drew the names of several of his heroes—including Agramant, Sacripant, Gradasse, and Mandricardo—from the names of his own peasants, as he felt their "uncouthness" was appropriate for Saracen warriors.

According to the writers Mazzuchelli and Gravina, Boiardo utilized Homer's *Iliad* as his model. This influence resulted in the *Orlando Inamorato* acting as a "reflex image" of the *Iliad*. Specifically, the poem mirrors the Trojan conflict in that Paris is besieged in a manner similar to the city of Troy, and the character Angelica holds the place of Helen. Despite these structural parallels to Homer, the actual subject matter of Boiardo's poem is derived from the *Fabulous Chronicle* of the pseudo-Turpin. While principal warriors such as Oliver, Roland, and Charlemagne appear in both, there is little resemblance between the detailed plot of the *Fabulous Chronicle* and Boiardo's work.

## The Historical and Mythological Troy
The city of Troy is central to various ancient narratives regarding siege and destruction. In the traditional accounts, the Greeks besieged the city to reclaim Helen and the treasures plundered from her husband. Some accounts, such as those cited by Plutarch, note that Paris is said to have slain Achilles at the gates of Troy.

Alternative versions of these events exist, particularly those recorded by Herodotus from Egyptian priests. This version suggests that Helen never actually entered Troy. According to this narrative, Paris was driven by a storm into the Canopic mouth of the Nile upon his return from Sparta with Helen. He was conducted to Memphis to meet King Proteus, who reproached Paris for his perfidy in stealing the wife and effects of his host. Proteus detained Helen and the riches to restore them to their lawful owner, ordering Paris to leave his dominions within three days or be treated as an enemy.

Consequently, when the Greeks besieged Troy, the Trojans maintained under oath that Helen was not in their city. Herodotus argues that the wise Prince Priam would not have seen his children and country destroyed rather than provide the "just and reasonable satisfaction" the Greeks desired. The destruction of the city is thus presented as a divine judgment, serving as a lesson that great crimes are met with signal punishments from the offended gods. Menelaus eventually recovered Helen and her treasure from King Proteus after his return from Troy.

## Chronology and Aftermath
The timeline of the fall of Troy served as a chronological marker for later historical events. Thucydidês fixed the "Return of the Herakleids" at eighty years after the capture of Troy. This calculation was based on the lineage of the kings of Sparta, which connected the mythical world to the real. Using a generation length of thirty years, the interval accounts for the generation of Hêraklês before the siege, his son Tlepolemus who fought in the army, and the subsequent generations leading to the acquisition of the Peloponnêsus. This timeline suggests the struggle of the siege itself lasted ten years.

## Publication History of Orlando Inamorato
Boiardo did not live to finish the *Orlando Inamorato*. The poem was printed in Scandiano the year following his death under the supervision of his son, Count Camillo. While the book itself is without a date, a Latin letter from Antonia Caraffa di Reggio prefixed to the poem is dated the kalends of June 1495. A second edition appeared in Venice before the year 1500, and the work was reprinted there twice more during the following twenty years.
