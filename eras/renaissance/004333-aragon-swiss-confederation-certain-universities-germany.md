# The Swiss Confederation, Aragon, and Germany

## The Swiss Confederation
The Swiss Confederation originated in the thirteenth century when peasant farmers from three valleys surrounding the Lake of Lucerne decided to manage their own affairs without an overlord. This early movement faced opposition from the Habsburg family, a noble family of the Aar Valley. In 1245, the men of Schwyz burnt the castle of New Habsburg, which had been established near Lucerne to overawe the local population.

Over time, the Confederation expanded. In 1415, the Eight Swiss Confederates conquered the Aargau and the town of Baden. Because the Aargau was the first conquest made by the Confederates, Baden served as the location for their delegates, or federal diet, from 1426 until approximately 1712. During this period, Baden functioned as the capital of Switzerland. The Confederation eventually became frankly republican in 1499 after centuries of nominal adhesion to the Holy Roman Empire.

The Swiss are characterized by a high degree of local autonomy, which is seen as a necessary measure to link populations of dissimilar faiths and languages. While some Swiss populations may gravitate toward natural affinities in Italy, France, and Germany, the common defensive needs of the Swiss mountaineers have historically justified their close linking.

## Basel and the Council of Basel
Basel is the capital of the Swiss half canton of Basel Stadt and is the second most populous town in the Swiss Confederation, ranking after Zuerich. Divided by the Rhine into Gross Basel and Klein Basel, the city is known for its transit trade, aided by the opening of the St Gotthard railway in 1882.

The city is historically significant as the site of the great oecumenical council from 1431 to 1449. The public meetings of this council were held in the choir of the cathedral (Muenster), while committees met in the chapter-house. The council was involved in a seventeen-year struggle regarding the dogma of the superiority of the council over the papacy. This struggle resulted in a schism that lasted ten years. During this time, an antipope found adherents among certain universities, the Swiss confederation, and the hereditary states of Alphonso of Aragon.

The council's presence in the imperial city ended in 1447 when Frederick III, king of the Romans, commanded the burgomaster of Basel to no longer allow the council's presence. The remaining members of the council migrated to Lausanne in June 1448. The antipope abdicated on April 7, 1449, at the instance of France.

## Educational and Academic Institutions
The university in Basel is the oldest in Switzerland. It was founded by Pope Pius II (Æneas Sylvius) in 1460 (though one source cites 1459), who had served as secretary to the council. 

Other academic activities in the region and Germany are exemplified by figures such as Rudolph Albert von Kölliker (1817-1905). Born in Zürich, Kölliker entered the university there in 1836 before studying at the universities of Bonn and Berlin. He graduated in philosophy at Zürich in 1841 and in medicine at Heidelberg in 1842. After a brief return to a chair of physiology and comparative anatomy at the university of Zürich in 1844, he spent the remainder of his academic career at the university of Würzburg in Bavaria.

## Germany and the Holy Roman Empire
In Germany, the status of cities often fluctuated. Many towns sought the position of *Reichsstadt* (imperial city) through purchase, gift, force of arms, or the extinction of dominant families such as the Hohenstaufen. While free towns were more numerous in southern Germany than in the north, many lost their liberties. For example, Mainz was subjected to the archbishop in 1462, and other towns were seized by Louis XIV or deprived of privileges by the emperor.

During the period of the council of Basel, Germany remained neutral. In later years, the German military was influenced by the Swiss; the grandfather of Emperor Charles developed a German infantry based on the Swiss model and the lines expounded in Machiavelli’s *Art of War*. However, maintaining these troops required significant funding, leading to unsecured borrowings from the Fuggers.

## Regional Administration and the Congress of Vienna
The Congress of Vienna created a map of Europe that often lumped together discordant nationalities. In the case of Austria and Hungary, the empire became a mixture of Germans, Hungarians, Roumanians, Jugo-Slavs, Czecho-Slovaks, and Italians. This was further complicated by the confirmation of Austrian acquisitions in Poland from 1772 and 1795. Additionally, the German-speaking Austrians were given control of North Italy as far as Milan, including the former republic of Venice.
