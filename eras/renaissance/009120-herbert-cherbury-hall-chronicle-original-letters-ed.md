# Herbert of Cherbury

Edward, Lord Herbert of Cherbury (1583–1648), was an English writer, diplomat, and poet associated with the "metaphysical" or "fantastic" school of poetry. His work spans several genres, including autobiography, philosophy, and historical writing.

## Literary Contributions and Style
Lord Herbert of Cherbury is recognized as a pioneer of deliberate English biography for his brilliant autobiography, which was first printed in 1764. In the context of the evolution of English prose, his autobiography is categorized alongside the memoirs of Kenelm Digby and the letters of James Howell as belonging to an older, more mannered form of writing, rather than the more modern styles that emerged during the Restoration period.

In the realm of poetry, Herbert is identified as part of the devouter section of the metaphysical poets. His work reflects a "Counter-Renaissance" temper—a revulsion against the claims of the natural man to pleasure—which led to a more mystical and ascetic tone in his verse. While he is noted for this specific temper, his poetic power and felicity are described as fitful.

## Philosophical and Historical Works
Herbert authored a treatise in Latin titled *De Veritate*, which is considered a notable work in the early history of Deism. The use of Latin for such treatises was common among men of genius during the Stuart period, serving to discipline English prose even as it sometimes diverted writers from using their native tongue.

Additionally, Lord Herbert of Cherbury wrote a *History of Henry VIII*, which serves as a source for historical accounts of that period.

## Diplomatic Experience
Lord Herbert of Cherbury served as an ambassador at the court of Louis XIII. During his time in France, he observed the prevalence of duelling among the French nobility. He remarked on the national characteristic of the time, stating, "There is scarce a Frenchman worth looking on who has not killed his man in a duel."
