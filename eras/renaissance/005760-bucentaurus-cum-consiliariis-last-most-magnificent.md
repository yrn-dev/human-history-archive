# Bucentaurus

The Bucentaurus, known in Italian as the *bucintoro*, was the state galley utilized by the doges of Venice. It served as the vessel for a specific annual ceremony performed on Ascension day, during which the doges would put into the Adriatic to "wed the sea." This tradition continued until 1789.

## Etymology and Design
The term *bucintoro* is derived from the Italian phrase *buzino d' oro*, meaning "golden bark." In the middle ages, this was latinized as *bucentaurus*. This latinization occurred based on a supposed Greek word, *boukentauros* (from *bous* and *Kentauros*), meaning "ox-centaur." 

Due to this linguistic connection, a fanciful explanation emerged suggesting that the galley's figurehead was the head of an ox. However, this is not supported by evidence; the name *bucentaurus* is unknown in ancient mythology. Representations that have survived show that the actual figurehead of the bucentaurs was the lion of St Mark.

## Historical Usage
While the term is specifically associated with the state galley of the doges, the name *bucentaur* appears to have been applied more broadly to any Venetian galley that was sumptuous and great. 

Historical records, including the chronicle of the doge Andrea Dandolo (who died in 1354), reference the use of these vessels. One such account mentions a "solemn and artistic" Bucentaurus used to travel to S. Clement, noting that a "more principal and solemn" Bucentaurus, accompanied by *consiliariis* (councilors) and others, had already arrived there.

## The Final Bucentaurus
The last and most magnificent of these vessels was constructed in 1729. This galley was eventually destroyed by the French in 1798, who dismantled it to obtain its golden decorations. 

Despite its destruction, remnants of the 1729 Bucentaurus are preserved in Venice. These remains can be found in the Arsenal and the Museo Civico Correr. Additionally, a fine model of the vessel is preserved at the Arsenal.
