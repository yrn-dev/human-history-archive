# The Diet of 1575 and its Aftermath in Bohemia

The diet of 1575 was a pivotal legislative assembly in Bohemia, convened following the return of King Maximilian. Maximilian had been absent from Bohemia for a long period, and his return was prompted by the estates' refusal to grant subsidies to a monarch who remained absent.

## Proceedings and Religious Conflict
The sittings of the 1575 diet were very prolonged. During the assembly, King Maximilian maintained a vacillating position, caught between the threats of the Bohemians and the advice of the papal nuncio who had accompanied him to Prague. The papal nuncio warned the king that making concessions to the Protestants of Bohemia would create significant difficulties in his other dominions.

The primary demand of the Bohemians was the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg," which served as a summary of the teachings of Luther. Additionally, the estates renewed a demand first expressed at the diet of 1567: the right for the estates to appoint members of the consistory. The consistory was the ecclesiastical body governing the Utraquist church, which had been without an archbishop since the death of John of Rokycan.

## The Confessio Bohemica
After long deliberations, Maximilian ultimately refused to recognize the Confession of Augsburg. In response, the majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drafted a profession of faith known as the *Confessio Bohemica*. 

While the *Confessio Bohemica* was identical to the Augsburg confession in most respects, it differed regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. In this specific point, the Bohemian profession aligned with the views of Calvin rather than Luther, a difference attributed to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren. Although Maximilian expressed his approval of the *Confessio Bohemica* verbally, he refused to allow it to be made public or to have it included among the charters of the kingdom.

## Results and Legal Concessions
Despite his refusal to recognize the *Confessio Bohemica* officially or grant the estates the right to appoint members of the consistory, Maximilian did make certain concessions. He consented to exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the jurisdiction of the consistory. Furthermore, he allowed them to choose fifteen defenders—five from each of the estates—to serve as their supreme representatives.

On the whole, the results of the diet of 1575 were favorable to the estates. This perception is evidenced by the fact that, almost immediately following the diet, the estates recognized Maximilian's eldest son, Rudolph, as his successor and agreed to his coronation as king of Bohemia.

## Succession and the Reign of Rudolph
Maximilian died in the year following the diet, and Rudolph succeeded him without opposition. Rudolph's early reign was characterized by a general apathy and indifference toward matters of state and the ongoing religious controversies, despite early fears that his education in Spain would lead him to treat church reformers with severity. 

Rudolph was a patron of the arts and contributed significantly to the embellishment of Prague, which became the center of the vast Habsburg dominions as his favorite residence. However, Rudolph suffered from fits of melancholia from an early youth and experienced several short periods of actual insanity.

By 1600, Rudolph's mental condition had impaired to the point that the princes of the house of Habsburg became concerned for the state, especially since Rudolph had no legitimate descendants. His eldest brother, Matthias, traveled to Prague to suggest that Rudolph appoint a coadjutor in case of incapacity and make arrangements for the succession. Rudolph indignantly rejected these suggestions. His anger was further provoked by a letter from Pope Clement VIII, which formally but forcibly highlighted the evil results his neglect of royal duties would have on his subjects and urged him to appoint a Habsburg prince as successor to the thrones of Hungary, Bohemia, and the imperial crown.
