# Cesare Borgia and the Crisis of 1503

## The Summer Fever and Political Collapse
In the summer of 1503, Cesare Borgia was in the process of gathering troops for a new expedition in central Italy. During this period, a critical turning point occurred when both Cesare and his father were simultaneously seized with fever. This coincidence proved to be Cesare's ruin, as it was the one contingency for which he had not provided.

While Cesare remained incapacitated, his father, the pope, died on August 18, 1503. This event triggered an immediate uprising of Cesare's enemies. In Rome, the Orsinis—whom Cesare had previously been involved in reducing—rose up once more. Simultaneously, the deposed princes in Romagna began preparing to regain their territories.

## Struggles for Power in Rome
Cesare's political position was severely shaken following the pope's death. In an attempt to maintain control, he tried to intimidate the cardinals using his bravos and Don Michelotto, but the cardinals refused to be browbeaten. Consequently, Cesare was forced to leave Rome in September. He placed his hopes on the Spanish cardinals, trusting they would elect a candidate friendly to his house.

The resulting conclave elected Francesco Todeschini-Piccolomini, who took the name Pius III. Although Pius III appeared respectable and peaceful in his disposition, he was in poor health and of advanced age. During his brief papacy, Cesare's dominion began to disintegrate. The lords of Pesaro, Rimini, and Piombino regained their lands, and Guidobaldo, the duke of Urbino, returned to his duchy with the assistance of Venice. Only Cesena remained loyal to Cesare, held by a faithful governor.

## The Election of Julius II and the End of Borgia Power
Pius III died on October 18, 1503, leading to another conclave. Cesare, still relying on the support of the Spanish cardinals, attempted to block the election of Giuliano della Rovere, a known enemy of his house. However, della Rovere's prospects improved to the point that Cesare was forced to come to terms with him.

On November 1, Giuliano della Rovere was elected and assumed the name Julius II. While Julius II did not initially show ill-will toward Cesare, he declared that the territories Cesare held must be restored to the church, stating a desire to recover what predecessors had wrongfully alienated. 

The situation was further complicated by Venice, which hoped to establish a protectorate over the principalities in Romagna. Julius II was determined to prevent Venetian intervention. After an unsuccessful attempt to use Cesare as a tool to keep the Venetians out, Julius II had Cesare arrested. This event marked the end of Borgia's power, and he was compelled to surrender all of his castles in Romagna.
