# The Introduction of Printing in Europe

The introduction of printing into Europe around the middle of the 15th century fundamentally altered the dissemination and preservation of culture. This technological shift marked a turning point in world history, profoundly affecting literary production and the evolution of language.

## Incunabula and Early Production
Books printed during the 15th century are specialized as "incunabula," a Latin neuter-plural term meaning "swaddling-clothes," "cradle," or "birthplace," signifying the beginning of the art. The term may have originated from Cornelius a Beughem's 1688 publication, *Incunabula typographiae*. While the term is recognized across Europe—appearing as *incunables* in French, *Inkunabeln* (or *Wiegendrucke*) in German, and *incunaboli* in Italian—the anglicized *incunables* has not yet gained full acceptance.

By the end of the 15th century, the primitive character of book production had diminished in major trade centers such as Paris, Cologne, Lyons, and Venice. Although there is no natural halting-place for the classification of these early books between 1490 and 1520, the year 1500 is generally used as the terminus for registration and description. Notable works devoted to this registration include Panzer's *Annales typographici* (1793), which organizes books alphabetically by the town of printing and then by year, and Hain's *Repertorium bibliographicum* (1826-1838), which arranges them by author.

## Impact on Bookbinding and Art
The rise of printing triggered a reaction against the large, valuable illuminated manuscripts and their precious covers. Early printed books were generally smaller, cheaper, and considered "ugly," typically bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf and decorated with blind large panel stamps.

However, this period saw the birth of gold tooling on leather, an art likely brought to Venice from the East. The finest examples of this craft are found in Italian work from the late 15th century. This art spread to England, where Thomas Berthelet, the Royal Binder to Henry VIII, practiced it until approximately 1558. Berthelet's work, which was likely influenced by an Italian master, is strongly Italian in feeling. The high value of these works by great masters has led to the emergence of skilled *faussaires* (forgers), requiring collectors to be wary of forged objects.

## Influence on the English Language and Literature
In England, the printing press played an important role in the transition from Middle to Early Modern English. Through its exclusive patronage of the midland speech, the press raised this dialect above others and secured its victory. As books reached every corner of the land and reading became more common, the "book-English" used in printing became the model for the speech and writing of people from regions such as Somersetshire and Northumberland.

The written form of the language also moved toward uniformity. Unlike scribes, who spelled words based on their own speech and ocular recollections, printing encouraged a direct ocular recognition of words. This led to a continuous tendency to reduce written words to a single form, creating a rigid uniformity in Modern English spelling. The year 1485, marking the establishment of the Tudor dynasty, is conveniently cited as the close of the Middle English transition.

William Caxton, a translator and publisher, operated a press that illustrated this linguistic passage. While Caxton did not introduce new forms or spirits in literary composition, his publications covered the breadth of 15th-century literature. He sought to direct public taste toward the best works of the past, such as the *Canterbury Tales*. The only work of permanent literary significance he introduced was Sir Thomas Malory's *Morte Darthur*, a compilation of older romances that represents the final flower of the old conception of chivalry.

## Dissemination of Science
The printing press also served to perpetuate scientific texts. An example is the *Sphaera Mundi*, a text-book of spherical astronomy written by the Yorkshireman John Holywood (known as Sacro Bosco, d. 1256). The printing press ensured the immense vogue of this work through the production of fifty-nine editions.
