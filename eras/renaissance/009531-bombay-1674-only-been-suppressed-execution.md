# Bombay

## Early English Acquisition and Administration
The English presence in the Bombay presidency began in 1618 with the establishment of a factory at Surat by the East India Company, which operated under a charter from Emperor Jahangir. While an attempt by the English and Dutch to take possession of the island of Bombay failed in 1626, proposals to purchase the island from the Portuguese were suggested in 1653. In 1661, Bombay was ceded to the English crown as part of the dower of the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II.

Due to the low esteem of the acquisition in England and the unsuccessful administration of crown officers, the island was transferred to the East India Company in 1668 for an annual payment of £10. This transfer included powers for the administration of justice and defense. A European regiment was enrolled, and fortifications were built, which successfully deterred a planned Dutch attack in 1673.

## Political Instability and Mutiny (1674–1683)
During the late 17th century, the position of Bombay was precarious. The settlement was isolated between the rival powers of the Mogul empire and the Mahrattas. Additionally, Malabar pirates posed a constant menace to trade, although the city itself was too strong for them to conquer. Maintaining the settlement required the genius of Aungier; following his death on June 30, 1677, the situation deteriorated further. Even during Aungier's tenure, the Siddi admirals of the Moguls asserted their right to use the Bombay harbour as winter quarters for their fleet, though they failed to establish it as a base against the Mahrattas. Under Aungier's weak successor, Rolt (1677–1682), English waters became a battle-ground for rival navies, leaving Bombay at the mercy of both.

Internal instability also plagued the Company's rule, as the board of directors pursued a "niggardly policy" focused on peaceful dividends rather than warlike rule, which frustrated soldiers of fortune. This tension led to a mutiny in Bombay in 1674, which was suppressed only through the execution of the ringleader.

In 1683, a more significant movement occurred under Richard Keigwin, a naval officer and former governor of St Helena. Elected governor of Bombay by popular vote, Keigwin issued a proclamation in the name of the king, citing "exactions, oppressions and intolerable extortions" by the Company and declaring his government to be under the immediate authority of the crown. Keigwin's rule was characterized by moderation, the reform of taxation, the admission of "interlopers" to increase port trade, and the procurement of notable concessions from the Mahrattas. However, the rebellion did not extend beyond Bombay. Keigwin obeyed a royal sign manual ordering him to surrender the fort to Sir John Child, the appointed captain-general and admiral of the Company's forces.

## Later Administrative and Regional History
Bombay was placed at the head of all Company possessions in India in 1687, though it became subordinate to the government of Calcutta in 1753. The English first collided with the Mahratta power in 1774, leading to the 1782 treaty of Salbai, which ceded Salsette to the British and handed Broach to Sindhia. Following the second Mahratta war, which ended in 1803, the East India Company received the districts of Kaira and Broach, having already annexed Surat in 1800.

The government of Bombay is administered by a governor in council. The governor, appointed from England, serves as president alongside two ordinary members appointed by the crown from the Indian civil service. Legislative functions are handled by a legislative council consisting of the governor, the executive council, and between eight and twenty other persons, at least half of whom are non-officials. Justice is administered via a high court at Bombay, featuring a chief justice and seven puisne judges, supported by assistant and district judges. District administration is managed by collectors, assistant collectors, and supernumerary assistants.

## Public Health and Social Unrest (1896–1905)
In recent history, Bombay has been severely affected by famine and plague. Bubonic plague first broke out in Bombay City in September 1896 and spread to the surrounding country. Between September 1896 and October 1902, over 531,000 deaths occurred due to the plague. Further outbreaks in 1903–1904 resulted in 316,523 deaths from 426,387 cases, and 1904–1905 saw 212,948 deaths from 285,897 cases.

Government efforts to fight the disease included inoculation and segregation. Segregation, however, was misunderstood by the population, leading to outbreaks of violence. In March 1898, a riot started by Mahommedan weavers in Bombay was not suppressed until several Europeans were fatally injured.

Simultaneously, the region suffered from famine. The failure of the 1896 monsoon caused distress across the Deccan, affecting 7 million people over 46,000 square miles. In September 1897, 301,056 persons were on relief, with total expenditure reaching Rs. 1,28,000,000. These measures are described as signally successful in mitigating distress and saving lives.

## Industry and Culture
Bombay is known for "Bombay blackwood furniture," manufactured in the city as well as in Ahmedabad and Surat. This furniture uses Shisham or blackwood (*Dalbergia*), a dark-coloured, hard-grained timber. While some pieces are considered clumsy due to original Dutch models, smaller items like ornamental stands and flower stands are highly prized for their graceful contours and lace-like carving. These artistic works are produced by a special class of workmen with inherited traditions. Additionally, "Bombay boxes" are produced, featuring geometrical inlays of ivory, ebony, sapan wood, sandal wood, wire, and stags' horns.
