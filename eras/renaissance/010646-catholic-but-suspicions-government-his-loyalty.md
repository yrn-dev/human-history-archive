# Loyalty and Government Suspicion

The relationship between individual loyalty and government authority has historically been marked by suspicion, often intersecting with religious affiliation, political ambition, and international diplomacy. These tensions have manifested in various forms, from the arrest of nobles for foreign associations to the systemic replacement of state religions during political upheavals.

## Political Suspicion and Individual Loyalty

In several historical instances, governments have viewed the actions of their subjects with distrust, leading to imprisonment or accusations of conspiracy. Thomas Arundell, 1st Baron Arundell of Wardour (c. 1562-1639), experienced repeated cycles of royal favor and suspicion. Despite being recommended by Queen Elizabeth to Emperor Rudolph II and distinguishing himself in Hungary—where he captured an enemy banner at the siege of Gran or Esztergom in 1595—his acceptance of a foreign title as a count of the Holy Roman Empire caused significant friction. Queen Elizabeth expressed her displeasure, stating that faithful subjects should not "gaze upon foreign crowns," and committed him to the Fleet upon his arrival in England. 

Arundell's subsequent years were characterized by instability; he was confined in April 1597, though declared innocent of all charges except for attempting to justify his title through ministers overseas. After being placed under his father's care, he was arrested again the following year on accusations of conspiracy against the government. Although he regained favor in 1599 and was created Baron Arundell of Wardour by James I in 1605, he again fell under temporary suspicion during the Gunpowder Plot.

Similarly, in Corsica, Paoli faced suspicions regarding his loyalty to revolutionary principles. These suspicions were spread in Paris as early as 1791 by Bartolomeo Arena, a Jacobin deputy. Although the French government appointed Paoli as governor and lieutenant-general of the forces in 1792 to secure the island, he remained out of sympathy with the methods of the Terror. By 1793, he was suspected of obstructing an expedition against Sardinia and was summoned before the Convention. Paoli eventually defied the Convention, asserting that his rebellion was not against France itself, but against a dominant faction.

## Religion and State Control

The intersection of religious identity and government loyalty is often evident in state-mandated religious shifts or the persecution of specific missions. In Russian Poland, following a republican revolt in Warsaw that was suppressed by Nicholas I, the government banned the Polish language and substituted the Greek Orthodox church for the Roman Catholic church as the State religion.

In the Loyalty Islands, the introduction of Christianity led to conflict between different missionary groups and the French government. While Protestant missions were established by the London Missionary Society between 1854 and 1865, Roman Catholic missionaries also arrived from New Caledonia. In 1864, the French instituted a commandant who attempted to stop English missions through violence. This conduct caused indignation in England and Australia, leading Emperor Napoleon to appoint a commission of inquiry to secure free liberty of worship for Protestant missions after a protest from Lord Shaftesbury. However, further persecution of Christians in Uea occurred in 1875, prompting another protest from the British government.

## Ambition and Allegiance

Loyalty is sometimes compromised by personal ambition or political expediency. François de Beaumont, Baron des Adrets (c. 1512-1587), served the royal army of France under Henry II before joining the Huguenots in 1562. The records indicate this shift was driven by personal dislike of the house of Guise and motives of ambition rather than religious conviction. After conducting a successful but brutal campaign against Catholics, Des Adrets eventually sought conciliation with them. This shift led to his arrest by Huguenot officers on suspicion in January 1563. He was eventually liberated and died a Catholic in 1587, though he remained distrusted by both the Huguenots and Catholics.

In the context of the Japanese throne, Prince Ohatsuse is noted for simulating suspicion of his elder brothers to remove them from the scene, alongside other relatives, to clear his own path to the throne.

## International and Diplomatic Suspicion

On a broader scale, suspicion often exists between allied governments. During the First World War, Germany harbored doubts regarding the loyalty of its ally, Austria. This suspicion was fueled by internal pressures in the Austrian Empire caused by privations and hunger. A letter from the Austrian Emperor to Prince Sixtus of Bourbon-Parma suggested a desperate need for peace, leading Germany to believe that Austria might prefer a separate peace over no peace at all, regardless of the sacrifices involved.
