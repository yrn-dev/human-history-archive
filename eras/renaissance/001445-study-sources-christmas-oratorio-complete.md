# The Christmas Oratorio

The Christmas oratorio is a major work by Johann Sebastian Bach, consisting of six similar cantatas that are organized into a connected design intended for performance across six separate days.

## Composition and Sources
The study of the sources used in the Christmas oratorio provides essential evidence regarding Bach's range of expression and his compositional methods. A significant portion of the work was derived from occasional cantatas written shortly before the oratorio. Except for those sections set to words from the Bible and the chorale-tune numbers, these materials were adapted from earlier works.

Bach utilized these occasional works as sketches, ensuring that the verses for the oratorio numbers were built upon the same rhythms as those found in the original odes. This method allowed Bach to rescue earlier work from oblivion. While some alterations were made, they were generally confined to details, even in instances where an aria was transposed by a fourth or a fifth. Additionally, some portions of the Christmas oratorio are derived from lost secular works; these sections appear as fair copies in the autograph, though their specific sources are not definitely known.

## Musical Expression and Adaptation
The adaptation of secular material into the sacred context of the oratorio demonstrates Bach's insight into musical expression. For example, an aria originally written for the character Pleasure (Wollust) to allure Hercules from the paths of Virtue was transformed into the Virgin's cradle-song. Similarly, an aria representing Hercules's indignant reply was adapted into an alto aria bidding Zion to "prepare for the Bridegroom."

Bach achieved this transition not by changing the fundamental nature of the music—as he viewed the representation of Pleasure simply as "pleasant music"—but through changes in color and tempo. In the case of the cradle-song, the key was lowered from B flat to G, and the strings were replaced by the "tender reed tone" of a group of oboe d'amore. Furthermore, the soprano part became an alto, with notes doubled in the upper octave by a flute to create a "nimbus" effect. These changes transformed a "boldly executed draft" into a "perfect work" characterized by solemn beauty and richness.

## Place in Bach's Oeuvre
The Christmas oratorio is categorized within Bach's church music with orchestra. It stands alongside other major works such as the Easter oratorio, the Mass in B minor, the Magnificat in D, and the Passions according to St Matthew and St John. 

In the broader context of Bach's output, the Christmas oratorio reflects the characteristics of his final period (1733–1750). During this era, his choral works became more numerous and terse, while his instrumental music showed an increasing preference for large-scale designs. The oratorio also absorbed materials from three of Bach's seven choral cantatas.

## Publications and Scholarship
Various editions of Bach's works exist, though some are criticized for failing to distinguish the original text from the tastes of the editor. The publications of the Neue Bachgesellschaft are noted as disappointing in this regard. Conversely, older vocal scores of cantatas in the Edition Peters are regarded as superior to the complete series issued by Breitkopf and Haertel (which followed the Bach Gesellschaft). 

The Novello publication of the Christmas oratorio is noted for being generally very good and includes a fine analytic preface written by Sir George Macfarren.
