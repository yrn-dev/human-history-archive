# The Dauphin and the Dauphinate

The term "dauphin" historically refers to the eldest son of the king of France, a title and position associated with the province of Dauphine. While the title eventually became a standard designation for the heir to the French throne, its origins and the political status of the dauphin varied across different eras of French history.

## Origins and Evolution of the Title
The title originated in the Viennois, where the chief of the house was known as the Count (and later Dauphin) of the Viennois. In 1349, Humbert II, the last independent Dauphin, sold his dominions to Charles of Valois. Upon ascending the throne as Charles V, he bestowed the Dauphine on his eldest son. From that point forward, the title was borne by all succeeding eldest sons of the kings of France.

A distinction was sometimes made between the "king dauphin" (*le roy daulphin*), referring to the king's eldest son, and the "prince dauphin," who was the dauphin of Auvergne. The dauphinate of Auvergne dates back to 1155 and entered the royal house of France in 1428 through the marriage of Louis de Bourbon, count of Montpensier, to Jeanne, the heiress of dauphin Beraud III. This territory was eventually annexed to the crown in 1693.

## The Province of Dauphine
Dauphine was an old province of pre-Revolutionary France located in the south-east, situated between Savoy and Provence. After 1790, it was divided into the departments of the Hautes Alpes, the Drome, and the Isere. 

The province's history included periods of varying independence. For example, Louis (1440–1461), who later became Louis XI of France, was the last dauphin to occupy a semi-independent position. He governed his principality as if it were an independent state, dismissing the governor, redefining boundaries with the papacy and the duke of Savoy, and curtailing the temporal jurisdiction of bishops. Louis utilized alliances with towns to overthrow the nobility and reorganized administration to maximize revenue. However, this independence ended in 1456 when Dauphine was annexed to the crown.

## Political Conflicts and the Reign of Charles VII
During the reign of Charles VII, the dauphin Louis emerged as a significant political actor and opponent to various court figures. One such figure was Pierre de Brézé (c. 1410–1465), a trusted soldier and statesman of Charles VII. Brézé had served the royal cause against the dauphin Louis and revolted nobles during the Praguerie. 

The rivalry between the two was marked by legal conflict; in 1448, the dauphin Louis brought accusations against Brézé. This led to a formal trial, though Brézé was completely exonerated and restored to favor. Later, in 1452, the dauphin Louis entered a secret treaty and marriage with Charlotte, daughter of Duke Lodovico of Savoy, despite a formal prohibition from Charles VII. This defiance led to a confrontation in which the king marched south, though he eventually withdrew. In 1456, fearing for his life as Charles approached the Bourbonnais, Louis fled to the court of Philip the Good, duke of Burgundy, in Flanders, leaving Dauphine to be definitively annexed to the French crown.

## The Mystery of Louis XVII
The history of the dauphin also includes the contentious accounts surrounding Louis XVII. Following the French Revolution, various pretenders claimed to be the escaped dauphin. One such claimant was Karl Wilhelm Naundorff, who appeared in Berlin in 1810 and later pushed his claims in Paris in 1833. Naundorff's narrative involved a complex substitution of the dauphin with a deaf-mute and a scrofulous child in the Tower. Other pretenders included the comte de Richemont, who claimed he was smuggled out in a basket, and Eleazar Williams, who claimed to have emerged from idiocy at age thirteen in New York State.

The official version of Louis XVII's history, accepted during the Restoration, was documented by authors such as Simien Despréaux, M. Eckard, and A. de Beauchesne. These accounts focused on the captivity of the royal family at the Temple.

## Succession and Later History
The role of the dauphin remained central to the stability of the French succession. During the reign of Louis XIV, the succession was briefly threatened when his eldest son, the dauphin, died in April 1711, followed shortly by his eldest grandson, the duke of Burgundy, and great-grandson, the duke of Brittany, in 1712. This left the heir to the throne as the duke of Anjou, who later became Louis XV.

In the later history of the province of Dauphine, the "States" of the province, which dated from the mid-14th century, were suspended by Louis XIII in 1628. An unauthorized meeting of these States on July 21, 1788, at the castle of Vizille's tennis court (*Salle du Jeu de Paume*), is noted as one of the earliest premonitory signs of the French Revolution of 1789.
