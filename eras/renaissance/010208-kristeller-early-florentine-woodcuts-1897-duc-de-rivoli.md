# Kristeller's Early Florentine Woodcuts and the Duc de Rivoli's Bibliography

The study of early printed illustrations, particularly primitive woodcuts found in incunabula, has seen a significant increase in interest and market value. While certain well-known works, such as the *Hypnerotomachia Poliphili* (Venice, 1499) and the *Nuremburg Chronicle* (1493), maintain more stable prices, rarer illustrated books from these periods are often priced in the hundreds.

## Key Bibliographic Works on Italian Woodcuts

For those researching the history of wood engraving and illustrated books in Italy, several authoritative texts are recognized. Among the most significant are:

*   **Kristeller's *Early Florentine Woodcuts* (1897):** A primary resource focusing on the woodcut traditions of Florence.
*   **The duc de Rivoli's (also known as the Prince d'Essling's) *Bibliographie des livres a figures venitiens 1469-1525*:** First published in 1892 with a new edition appearing in 1906, this work serves as a comprehensive bibliography of Venetian books containing figures.
*   **Lippmann's *Wood Engraving in Italy in the 15th Century* (1888):** Another essential text for the study of Italian woodcuts during the 15th century.

## Context of Early Printed Illustrations

The development of printed illustrations varied by region. In Germany, printed illustrations first appeared around 1461 in small books produced at Bamberg by Albrecht Pfister, which featured rude woodcuts. By the 1480s, such illustrations were common across Germany, France, and the Low Countries. In Italy, however, the full development of printed illustrations did not occur until approximately 1490.

While experiments were conducted with copper engravings in both France and Italy during the 15th century, these efforts did not meet with success during that period.

## Examples of Early Italian Illustrated Editions

The evolution of the illustrated book can be seen in the printing history of the *Commedia*. While early editions were produced in various cities, the first Florentine edition appeared in 1481, printed by Nicolo and Lorenzo della Magna. This volume included the commentary of Cristoforo Landino and featured a series of copper engravings attributed to Baccio Baldini, though the number of engravings varied between two and twenty depending on the copy.

Later, in 1506, the firm of Giunta in Florence printed the poem in a small volume that included cuts. In the broader history of the *Commedia*, the first edition to feature woodcuts was produced by Boninus de Boninis in Brescia in 1487, followed by Bernardino Benali in Venice in 1491.
