# Clerely brought us out in to the lande of mylke and honye

The phrase "Clerely brought us out in to the lande of mylke and honye" appears in the manuscript of the play *Kynge Johan*. This work employs a mixture of real historical figures and allegorical characters to present its narrative.

## The Play Kynge Johan
In the play, King John is depicted as a "noble Kynge" who, likened to a "faythfull Moses," withstood a "proude Pharao" for the sake of "his poore Israel." Despite this, the text notes that the English people remained in the "bondage of Rome." The liberation from this state is attributed to "duke Josue," identified as the late King Henry, who "Clerely brought us out in to the lande of mylke and honye."

The play contains conflicting characterizations of King John; while described as noble in one instance, he is elsewhere called a Lollard and accused of using "heretycall langage." His narrative concludes with him being poisoned by a monk of Swinestead.

## Structure and Allegory
The play utilizes allegorical figures to represent broader concepts. The nation is represented by the character *Ynglonde vidua*. A jocular element is provided by a character named Sedwyson (representing sedition), who functions similarly to a Vice in a traditional morality play. The stage directions suggest that a single actor played multiple roles, as evidenced by instructions such as "Go out Ynglond, and dress for Clargy."

## Discovery and Manuscript History
The manuscript of *Kynge Johan* was discovered between 1831 and 1838 within the corporation papers at Ipswich. It is believed the play was performed in that location, as the text refers to charitable foundations established by King John in the town and its surrounding neighborhood. 

Although the end of the manuscript describes the work as consisting of two plays, there is no obvious division between them, with only the end of the first act being noted. The first part of the work was corrected by Bale, and the second half is written in his handwriting, though his name does not appear within the manuscript itself. However, Bale lists a play titled *De Joanne Anglorum Rege*, written *in idiomate materno* (in the mother tongue), among his works.
