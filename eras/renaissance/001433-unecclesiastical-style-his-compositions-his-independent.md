# Ecclesiastical and Non-Ecclesiastical Styles in Composition

In the history of music and literature, the term "style" often distinguishes between works intended for formal religious or institutional use and those that deviate from such norms or adhere to secular and personal standards. These distinctions are evident in the careers of various composers and writers, ranging from the strict adherence to severe ecclesiastical forms to the "unecclesiastical" approach that often caused friction with authorities.

## Musical Styles: Ecclesiastical and Independent

The contrast between ecclesiastical and non-ecclesiastical musical styles is highlighted by the works of Giuseppe Baini and Johann Sebastian Bach. Giuseppe Baini (1775–1844), an Italian priest and musical director to the choir of the pontifical chapel, composed works that were considered favorable specimens of the "severe ecclesiastical style." A notable example is his ten-part *Miserere*, composed in 1821 by order of Pope Pius VII, which became a permanent part of the Sistine chapel services during Passion Week.

In contrast, Johann Sebastian Bach experienced conflict due to his "unecclesiastical style" of composition and his independent bearing. During his cantorship at Leipzig, Bach was frequently ruffled by town councillors who were shocked by these characteristics. Despite these tensions, Bach produced colossal achievements during his time in Leipzig, including the *B Minor Mass* and the *Passion according to St Matthew*. 

Bach's broader body of work demonstrates a vast range of styles and forms. His organ music includes six sonatas, four groups of six organ preludes and fugues, and *Das Orgelbuechlein*, a collection of short choral-preludes. He also composed 18 larger chorale-preludes, the six "Schuebler" chorales, and three great independent toccatas and a Passacaglia. Additionally, Bach produced unclassifiable instrumental works such as *Die Kunst der Fuge*—a series of fugues written as abstract studies in clavier style—and *Das musikalische Opfer*, which contains two great fugues, puzzle-canons, and a trio for flute, violin, and figured bass.

## Rhetorical and Literary Styles

Beyond music, the concept of style is central to ancient Greek and Roman rhetoric. Critics distinguished between three primary styles of rhetorical composition: the "grand" (or "elaborate"), the "middle," and the "plain." The "plain" style was characterized as being nearest to the language of daily life. Lysias is credited as the model of this "plain style" (*genus tenue* or *subtile*), utilizing a flexible intelligence and an instinct for gracefulness to adapt speeches to the age, station, and circumstances of his clients.

Similarly, Lucian is noted for a style that is fluent, easy, and unaffected, following the best Attic models like the orators and Plato. His *Dialogues of the Dead*, *Dialogues of the Gods*, and *of the Marine Deities* are regarded as models of accurate, polished, and witty Greek composition. Lucian's use of the dialogue form—a prose representative of drama—was a revival of a style that had been discredited by the influence of rhetoric, which preferred to declaim and expatiate over the brief question-and-answer format of dialectic.

## Stylistic Variation in Sacred Texts

The Gospel of Luke exhibits significant stylistic variation, which scholars attribute to the author's independence from or dependence upon various sources. The style varies from Hellenistic (as seen in i. 1-4) to strongly Hebraic (as seen from i. 5 to the end of ii.). This Hebraic style in the section covering the birth and infancy of John and Jesus may have been adopted because it was suitable for the subject matter, or it may have originated from a Hebrew or Aramaic document. Despite these shifts, the narrative is described as simple and direct, characterized by clear character-sketches and the skillful use of parallelisms, such as the interweaving of the accounts of Jesus and John the Baptist.

## Other Applications of Style

The term "style" also appears in the context of art and history. In early painted books, such as the Joshua Roll of the Vatican and the *Natural History* of Dioscorides, styles often included classical survivals like personified rivers. Later miniatures showed a Persian influence in ornamentation, featuring intricate arabesques similar to the style of eastern rugs.

In historical writing, the pursuit of style was often a matter of rigorous refinement. For instance, the author of the *History of England* (published in 1848) engaged in endless corrections of both matter and style to ensure the accuracy of his work.

Finally, the term "unecclesiastical" has been applied to individuals outside of music. Emperor Frederick II is described as an "unecclesiastical person" who attempted to suppress sects in Italy, such as the Albigenses, whose Manichaean tenets—including the belief in two gods and the inherent evil of matter—were viewed as destructive to Catholicism and civil society.
