# Johann Sebastian Bach: The 1717 Dresden Visit and Career Transitions

## The Dresden Tournament
In 1717, during a concert tour, Johann Sebastian Bach visited Dresden. During this visit, he was induced to challenge J. Louis Marchand, an arrogant French organist. Marchand was widely disliked by German musicians, although his musical powers were undeniable. 

The challenge resulted in a proposed organ competition. A specific day was set for the tournament, which was attended by the court and the town's musical celebrities. The event was viewed as a confrontation between French and German music. Before the competition, Bach was given the opportunity to listen to Marchand's playing in secret. It appears that Marchand was also permitted to listen secretly to Bach's playing. Consequently, on the day of the scheduled tournament, Marchand was not present; the only news of him was that he had departed Dresden by the earliest coach.

## Career in Weimar and Coethen
Prior to the events in Dresden, Bach had moved to Weimar in 1708. His success there led to his appointment in 1714, at the age of twenty-nine, as *Hofkonzertmeister* to the duke of Weimar. In this role, he found the composition of sacred music to be one of his most congenial duties; the great cantata *Ich hatte viel Bekuemmerniss* was likely the first work produced under this office.

Following his triumph in Dresden, Bach was appointed *Kapellmeister* to the duke of Coethen, a position he held from 1717 to 1723. During this period, he served as the conductor of the prince of Anhalt-Cothen's orchestra. The Coethen years are characterized as the period of Bach's central instrumental works. These include the Brandenburg concertos, the French and English suites, the solo violin and violoncello sonatas, and the first book of the *Wohltemperirtes Klavier*.

## Later Career and Family
In 1723, Bach moved to Leipzig to become the cantor of the Thomasschule, as he found his position at Coethen uninspiring for choral music. Despite this move, he maintained his post as visiting *Kapellmeister* at Coethen and held a similar position at Weissenfels. 

Bach's personal life during this time included the death of his first wife in 1720, leaving him with seven children. In December 1721, he married a second time. His second wife possessed a beautiful soprano voice, for which he wrote many of his most inspired arias. She provided significant assistance with his work, and her musical handwriting eventually became nearly indistinguishable from his own autographs.

Among his children, Friedermann and Philipp Emanuel were noted for their promising futures. Wilhelm Friedermann Bach (1710-1784), the eldest, was regarded as the most gifted, known as a complete master of counterpoint and a famous improvisor and organist. Friedermann was educated at Leipzig and served as the organist of the Sophienkirche at Dresden starting in 1733, and later as the musical director of the Liebfrauenkirche at Halle from 1747 until 1764.

## Musical Legacy and Compositions
Bach's keyboard mastery ensured he was never unemployed. His voluminous compositions include several distinct collections of organ music:
* Six sonatas.
* Four groups of six organ preludes and fugues.
* *Das Orgelbuechlein*, a planned collection of short choral-preludes that remained less than half executed.
* Eighteen larger chorale-preludes, including his final composition.
* The six "Schuebler" chorales, arranged from cantata movements.
* Three great independent toccatas and the Passacaglia.

Additionally, Bach produced two important unclassifiable instrumental works: *Die Kunst der Fuge*, a series of progressive fugues, and *Das musikalische Opfer*, which consists of two great fugues, a trio for flute, violin, and figured bass, and various puzzle-canons based on a theme provided by Frederick the Great.

In his choral work, Bach utilized the poem of Brockes as the basis for the non-scriptural numbers in his *Passion according to St John*. He also held a deep admiration for Handel, making a manuscript copy of Handel's *Passion nach Brockes*. Despite efforts to meet Handel—including sending his son to Halle to invite Handel to Leipzig in 1729—Bach never met his peer due to illness.
