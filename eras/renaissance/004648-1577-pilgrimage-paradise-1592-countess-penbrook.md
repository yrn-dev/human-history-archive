# Literary and Historical Records of the Late 16th Century

## The Poetical Works of Breton
The late 16th and early 17th centuries saw a variety of poetical and prose contributions from Breton. His early works include *A Floorish upon Fancie* and *The Workes of a Young Wit*, both published in 1577. In 1591, an unauthorized publication titled *Bryton's Bowre of Delights* was edited by Dr Grosart; however, this volume contained some poems that Breton himself disclaimed. Other notable works from this period include *The Pilgrimage to Paradise* (1592) and *The Arbor of Amorous Devises*, which was entered at Stationers' Hall in 1594 and was only partially written by Breton.

Breton's later output included *Marie Magdalen's Love: a Solemne Passion of the Soules Love* (1595), which consists of a prose treatise (likely by another author) and a poem in six-lined stanza written by Breton. In 1600, he produced several works: *Pasquil's Fooles cappe*, *Pasquil's Mistresse*, *Pasquil's Passe and Passeth Not*, and *Melancholike Humours*. His 1601 publications include *A Divine Poem* (containing "The Blessed Weeper" and "The Ravisht Soul"), *The Soules Heavenly Exercise*, and *An Excellent Poem, upon the Longing of a Blessed Heart*. 

Between 1602 and 1614, Breton published *The Mother's Blessing*, *The Soules Harmony*, *A True Description of Unthankfulnesse*, and *Olde Madcappe newe Gaily mawfrey* (all in 1602), followed by *The Passionate Shepheard* (1604), *The Honour of Valour* and *The Soules Immortall Crowne* (both 1605), and finally *An Invective against Treason; I would and I would not* (1614). His prose tracts include *Wit's Trenchmour* (1597), *The Wil of Wit* (1599), and *A Poste with a Packet of Mad Letters* (1603). Certain works, such as *The Passion of a Discontented Mind* (1601), *Mary Magdalen's Lamentations* (1604), and *Sir Philip Sidney's Ourania by N.B.* (1606), are erroneously attributed to him. Additionally, *The Countess of Penbrook's Passion* remained in manuscript (MS.) until it was first printed by J.O. Halliwell Phillipps in 1853.

## English Poets and Figures of the Era
Samuel Daniel (1562–1619), a poet and historian born near Taunton, was encouraged and taught in verse by the countess of Pembroke, whose household he entered as a tutor to her son, William Herbert. Daniel's first known work was a translation of Paulus Jovius in 1585, followed by his first volume of verse in 1592, which featured the romance *The Complaint of Rosamond* and a cycle of sonnets to *Delia*. In 1594, he added the tragedy *Cleopatra* to these works. He later published *The First Four Books of the Civil Wars* (1595) and a volume of *Poetical Essays* (1599).

Fulke Greville, 1st Baron Brooke (1554–1628), was a poet and a lifelong friend of Philip Sidney. Greville resigned a post at the court of the Marches in 1577 to join Sidney at court. He was part of the "Areopagus," a literary clique led by Gabriel Harvey that advocated for classical metres in English verse. Greville served in Normandy under Henry of Navarre around 1591 and represented Warwickshire in parliament during several terms, including 1592–1593.

## Religious and Political Movements
John Greenwood (d. 1593) was an English Separatist and Puritan who entered Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, as a sizar in 1577-1578. After serving as chaplain to Lord Rich and being ordained as a deacon and priest—ordinations he later renounced as "wholly unlawful"—he became the leader of the London Separatists by 1586. Following periods of imprisonment, he was elected "teacher" of the Separatist church in September 1592. He was arrested in December of that year and hanged on April 6, 1593, for "devising and circulating seditious books."

## Other Historical Records
In the realm of science, the Observatory of Cassel was established in 1561 by landgrave William IV of Hesse-Cassel. In 1577, Christoph Rothmann became an assistant there, followed by Joost Burgi in 1579. The observatory is noted for the first European appearance of the altitude and azimuth instrument and its revolving dome.

In French nobility, Honoré d'Albert (d. 1592), seigneur de Luynes, served Henry IV and three Valois kings. He was the father of Charles, the first duke of Luynes, and Honoré, seigneur de Cadenet.
