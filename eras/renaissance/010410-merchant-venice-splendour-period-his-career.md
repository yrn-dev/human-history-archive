# The Merchant of Venice and the Career of Edwin Booth

## The Winter Garden Productions
Between 1864 and 1867, Edwin Booth served as the manager of the Winter Garden theatre in New York. During this tenure, he staged a series of Shakespearian productions characterized by a level of magnificence that was unexampled at the time. These productions included *Hamlet*, *Othello*, and *The Merchant of Venice*.

## Career Trajectory of Edwin Booth
Edwin Booth's entry into the theatrical profession began on September 10, 1849, at the Boston Museum, where he appeared as Tressel in Colley Cibber's version of *Richard III*, acting alongside his father. Described as buoyant in spirits with a lithe and graceful figure, soft and expressive eyes, and dark hair in waving curls, Booth initially faced skepticism. His father watched him with evident disappointment, and other members of the theatrical profession felt that the genius of the elder Booth had not been passed to the son.

Booth made his New York debut on September 27, 1850, at the National theatre in Chatham Street, playing the character of Wilford in *The Iron Chest*. Following his father's illness a year later, he assumed the role of Richard III. However, it was only after his father's death that Booth secured an unassailable position on the stage. 

From 1852 to 1856, Booth performed in the Sandwich Islands, Australia, and California. His brilliant acting during this period captivated audiences and surprised those who had known him previously in the east. Subsequently, his dramatic triumphs were warmly acknowledged; his performances as Richelieu, Richard, and Hamlet were considered superior to those of Edwin Forrest, and his portrayal of Sir Giles Overreach in *A New Way to Pay Old Debts* surpassed that of his father.

## The Impact of 1865
The splendour of the period in Booth's career marked by the Winter Garden productions was dashed for many months in 1865. This decline followed the assassination of President Lincoln by Booth's brother, John Wilkes Booth. The three Booth brothers—Edwin, John Wilkes (1839-1865), and Junius Brutus (1821-1853)—had previously performed together in *Julius Caesar* during the autumn of the preceding year. That specific performance was noted for its excellence and the subsequent tragic circumstances involving two of the principal performers.
