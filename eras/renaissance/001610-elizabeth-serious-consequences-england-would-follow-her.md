# Queen Elizabeth I and the State of England

The reign of Queen Elizabeth, which began with her accession in 1558, was marked by significant shifts in England's internal governance, its relationship with neighboring powers, and the management of its royal prerogatives.

## Governance and the Great Seal
Following her accession, Elizabeth appointed Sir Nicholas Bacon as lord keeper of the great seal in December 1558. Bacon, a former solicitor of the court of augmentations and a member of parliament for Dartmouth, was a close friend and brother-in-law of Sir William Cecil (later Lord Burghley). In his official capacity, Bacon presided over the House of Lords during the opening of Elizabeth's first parliament and was instrumental in securing the archbishopric of Canterbury for Matthew Parker.

By 1559, Bacon was authorized to exercise the full jurisdiction of lord chancellor. However, the administration was not without internal disagreement; Bacon opposed Cecil's policy of waging war on France to assist the enemies of Mary, queen of Scots, citing the poverty of England. Despite this, Bacon recognized the dangers posed to England by the aggressive religious policies and alliance between France and Scotland. Bacon eventually fell into royal disfavour in 1564 and was dismissed from court due to Elizabeth's suspicion that he was involved in the publication of a pamphlet titled "A Declaration of the".

## Relations with Scotland and France
The political landscape of the era was heavily influenced by the instability of Scotland. Before Elizabeth took the throne, the Scottish reformer Knox had published a treatise against "The Monstrous Regiment of Women," a gesture Elizabeth never forgave. Nevertheless, Cecil maintained correspondence with Scottish lords, leading to a league between England and Scotland in July 1559.

This alliance was tested by the presence of French forces. By the end of 1559, the French army had driven the forces of the "congregation" from Leith into Edinburgh and then to Stirling. After delays and evasions typical of the Elizabethan administration, English troops eventually joined their Scottish allies. This resulted in a treaty on July 6, 1560, nominally between Elizabeth and the queen of France and Scotland, which granted the government of Scotland to the nation of the land.

## The Matter of Mary, Queen of Scots and Lord Darnley
The succession and stability of the English crown were complicated by the ambitions of Henry Stewart (Lord Darnley). A great-grandson of Henry VII, Darnley was viewed by his mother, Lady Margaret Douglas, as a potential king of England upon Elizabeth's death. In 1560, Lady Margaret sent Darnley to France with the hope of arranging a marriage between him and Mary, queen of Scots.

Elizabeth reacted to these ambitions by imprisoning both Lady Margaret and Darnley in 1561, though they were released shortly thereafter. Elizabeth's later attitude toward the marriage of Mary and Darnley was contradictory; while she and her council declared a dislike for the union and ordered Darnley and his father to return to London, she had previously permitted Darnley to travel to Scotland. The marriage was eventually celebrated at Holyrood on July 29, 1565.

## Press and Public Image
Under Elizabeth, the English state continued to restrict the free press, a trend that had begun under Henry VIII and Edward VI. While the Star Chamber exercised censorship and the Company of Stationers worked to repress "obnoxious books," Elizabeth generally interfered with publications only when they touched upon her royal prerogatives or emanated from Roman Catholics. Consequently, bookselling flourished toward the end of her reign.

Elizabeth also utilized public appearances to maintain her image. In 1564, a state coach was made for her. During one of her progresses at Warwick, she ordered every part and side of the coach to be opened so that her subjects could behold her.
