# Henry VIII and the English Monasteries

The reign of Henry VIII was marked by significant religious and institutional transformations, most notably the Reformation in England and the subsequent Dissolution of the monasteries. These events created a substantial demand for books, though the Tudor monarchy maintained strict control over the press. In 1529, Henry VIII granted the first patent for the office of king's printer to Thomas Berthelet, stipulating that only licensed books could be printed; during this period, the possession or purchase of an unlicensed book was a punishable offense.

## The Benedictine Order and the Dissolution

Prior to the Dissolution, the Benedictines had flourished extensively in England. The order comprised nearly 300 Black Benedictine houses, which included both men and women and encompassed most of the land's chief religious houses. While grave charges were historically brought against the monks, serious historians, including F. A. Gasquet and J. Gairdner, no longer credit these accusations.

Following the Dissolution, the Benedictine line faced near extinction, though a momentary revival occurred during the reign of Mary, when some surviving monks were gathered and Westminster Abbey was restored. Sigebert Buckley, a monk professed during this revival, lived into the reign of James I. As the sole survivor of the English Benedictines, Buckley affiliated two English priests—who were already Benedictines in the Italian congregation—to the English congregation and Westminster Abbey in 1607. This act perpetuated the old English Benedictine line. By 1619, a pontifical act aggregated several English monks professed in Spain to these representatives. In the early 17th century, the revived English Benedictines established three or four monasteries on the continent, where they remained until the French Revolution drove them back to England.

## Historical Documentation and Scholarship

The history of Henry VIII's reign and the English Church in the 16th century has been documented by various historians and through extensive primary records. Key scholarly works include those by F. A. Gasquet, specifically *Henry VIII and the English Monasteries* (1906) and *English Monastic Life*, as well as the works of Lingard, Froude, and James Gairdner.

A primary source of data for this era is the *Letters and Papers of the Reign of Henry VIII*. A significant portion of the research for these records was conducted by John Sherren Brewer (1810–1879). Born in Norwich and educated at Queen's College, Oxford, Brewer was ordained in the Church of England in 1837. In 1856, the master of the rolls commissioned him to prepare a calendar of the state papers of Henry VIII. Brewer continued this work while serving as the crown living of Toppesfield, Essex, eventually publishing the Introductions to the *Letters and Papers of the Reign of King Henry VIII* in 1884 under the title *The Reign of Henry VIII*.

## Legal and Political Context

The political atmosphere of the era is further detailed in the published Act of Attainder 25 Henry VIII. c. 12. Other historical accounts of the period include the *Chronicle of Henry VIII* (translated by M.A.S. Hume) and the *Spanish Chronicle of Henry VIII*. Legal proceedings and interrogations related to rebellions during this time are also recorded in the *English Historical Review*.
