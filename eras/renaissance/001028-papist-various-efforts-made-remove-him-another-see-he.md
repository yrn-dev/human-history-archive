# Papist

In historical records, the term "Papist" has been used to describe individuals or groups associated with the authority of the Pope or the Roman Catholic faith, often appearing in contexts of religious and political conflict.

## Historical Presence in England and France
According to records from the decline and fall of the Roman Empire, at the beginning of the last century, Papists in England constituted only a thirtieth part of the nation. Similarly, Protestants in France made up only a fifteenth part of their population. Despite these small numbers, the spirit and power of these respective groups were objects of constant apprehension to the larger populations of their nations. These observations are supported by the relations transmitted to the court of Rome by Bentivoglio, who served as nuncio at Brussels and later became a cardinal.

## Ecclesiastical Conflict and Labeling
The term "papist" was frequently used as a label during periods of religious transition and conflict in England. For example, Bonner, while on a mission in Paris, expressed bitterness toward Wyatt and Mason, whom he denounced as "papists." This period was marked by assertions regarding supremacy; in 1536, a preface to Gardiner's *De vera Obedientia* asserted royal supremacy while denying papal supremacy, a position that was received with delight by Lutherans.

## Treatment Under Bishop John Aylmer
During the tenure of John Aylmer as the bishop of London (consecrated in 1576), the term appeared in the context of ecclesiastical discipline. Aylmer became notorious for his harsh treatment of any individuals who differed from him on ecclesiastical questions. This harshness was applied indiscriminately to those he identified as either Puritan or Papist. Due to his conduct in this position, various efforts were made to remove Aylmer to another see.
