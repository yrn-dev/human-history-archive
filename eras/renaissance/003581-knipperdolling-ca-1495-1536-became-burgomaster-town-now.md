# Bernt Knipperdolling

Bernt Knipperdolling (also referred to as Knipperdollinck, and known as Berend or Bernhardt) was a German divine and revolutionary cloth-merchant active in Münster during the early 16th century. Born approximately between 1490 and 1495, he was described as a man of glib tongue and fine presence who had been noted for eccentricity from his youth.

## Early Career and Political Activity
Knipperdolling was a prosperous merchant in Münster. In 1524, he accompanied Melchior Hofman and Melchior Rinck on a business trip to Stockholm, which evolved into an unsuccessful religious mission. By 1527, he had gained influence among the populace of Münster, assisting in the breaking of the prison of Tonies Kruse despite the opposition of civic authorities and the bishop. Although he eventually made peace with the authorities, a subsequent business journey led to his arrest and a year of imprisonment. He was released only after paying a high fine, a matter for which he initiated an action before the Imperial Chamber.

Knipperdolling's primary motivations were political rather than religious. He aligned himself with the reforming movement led by Bernhardt Rothmann, who served as the pastor of the city church of St Lamberti starting in 1532. Following a mandate issued by a new bishop against Rothmann on April 17, 1532, which alienated democrats from moderates in the city, Knipperdolling emerged as a leader of the democrats. On December 26, 1532, he led a surprise action that resulted in the capture of negotiating nobles at Telgte.

## The Anabaptist Period in Münster
By February 14, 1533, Münster was constituted as an evangelical city via a charter from Philip of Hesse. During this period, Anabaptism gained a foothold; a journeyman smith proclaimed it on September 8, 1533, and Heinrich Roll, a refugee, led Rothmann to reject infant baptism in May 1533. Roll began preaching Anabaptist doctrines from a city pulpit on January 1, 1534.

Shortly thereafter, two emissaries of Jan Matthys (also known as Matthisson or Matthyszoon), an Anabaptist prophet and master-baker from Haarlem, arrived in Münster. They were followed on January 13 by Jan Beukelsz (also known as Bockelson or Buckholdt), better known as John of Leiden. John of Leiden, a twenty-five-year-old with a winning personality and organizational skills, won the confidence of Knipperdolling, who gave his daughter to John in marriage.

In February 1534, Knipperdolling was appointed burgomaster. Under the influence of Matthys's ideals, those who did not embrace Anabaptism were driven from the town in 1533. The city soon became besieged.

## Theocracy and Fall of the City
Following the death of Matthys in early 1534, John of Leiden succeeded him as prophet. John established a new constitution for Münster, creating a theocracy with other officers and twelve elders, a structure suggested by the tribes of Israel. However, John eventually superseded these officers to declare himself king of the "new Zion," ruling with severity and pomp. During this time, the city became a scene of cruelty and "grossest licence."

The Anabaptist movement in Münster was characterized by a rejection of the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith as one-sided, the baptism of believers only, and the practice of the breaking of bread. This latter practice was intended as a remembrance of the death of Christ to renew the bond of brotherly love as the foundation of neighbourly life.

The period of Anabaptist rule ended in 1535 when the besieging prince-bishop, Franz von Waldeck, retook the city on June 25. John of Leiden was executed on January 22, 1536, and his body, along with those of his companions, was hung in cages from the tower of the Lamberti church. Bernt Knipperdolling died circa 1536.
