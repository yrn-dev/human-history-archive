# A Declaration of Faith of English People Remaining at Amsterdam in Holland

The "declaration of faith of English people remaining at Amsterdam in Holland" is a confession of faith published in 1611 by the first English Baptist Church. This document is recognized as the first known expression of absolute liberty of conscience in any confession of faith.

## Origins of the English Baptist Movement
The movement that produced the 1611 declaration emerged from a break with Brownism. John Smith (also known as Smyth, d. 1612) was an ordained clergyman appointed as the preacher of the city of Lincoln in 1600. By 1605 or 1606, Smyth became a separatist and subsequently emigrated to Amsterdam with the Gainsborough Independents due to persecution. 

In Amsterdam, Smyth joined the "Ancient" church along with Morton and Thomas Helwys (ca. 1560–ca. 1616). In 1609, after coming under Mennonite teaching, Smyth separated from the Independents. He baptized himself—earning the label "Se-baptist"—while Helwys and others were likely baptized through the pouring method used by Mennonites or Anabaptists. This group subsequently formed the first English Baptist Church.

## Teachings on Baptism
The 1611 declaration outlines specific requirements and views regarding baptism. The text teaches that every church must receive its members through baptism based on the confession of their sins and faith, a process wrought by the preaching of the gospel. The document asserts that this follows primitive practice and institution; consequently, any churches constituted in a different manner or composed of different persons are not according to the testament of Christ.

Furthermore, the declaration defines baptism, or washing with water, as an outward manifestation of walking in newness of life and dying unto sin. Because of this definition, the text explicitly states that baptism in no wise appertaineth to infants.

## Governance and Liberty of Conscience
Beyond the rites of baptism, the declaration establishes principles regarding ecclesiastical authority and the role of the state. The confession holds that no church should claim any prerogative over any other church. 

Regarding the relationship between government and faith, the document teaches that the magistrate is not to compel individuals to any specific form of religion, nor is the magistrate to meddle with matters of conscience or religion.

## Historical Context of 1611
The publication of this declaration occurred during a period of significant literary and religious activity in the English-speaking world. In the same year, 1611, the first edition of the Authorized Version of the Holy Bible was printed in London by Robert Barker. This Bible, translated by special command of the King to be read in churches, was based on the "Received Text" and the critical resources available at the time.

Other notable publications of 1611 include *Coryate's Crudities*, a popular account of travels in Italy and France by Thomas Coryate, and the *Odcombian Banquet*, a collection of commendatory verses. In the realm of theology and law, John Buckeridge was appointed bishop of Rochester in 1611, and the work *Dispute contre l'adoration des reliques des Saincts trespassés* was published. Linguistically, 1611 marks the transition from Early Modern English (Tudor English) to Transitional Modern or 17th Century English.
