# French Printing in the Second Quarter of the 16th Century

## Innovations in Book Size and Format
During the second quarter of the 16th century, printers operating in Paris and Lyons introduced significant changes to the physical dimensions of books. They produced small sextodecimos, which effectively halved the size of the Aldine octavos. While these smaller formats found a ready market, their success was not lasting. Printers from Leiden and Antwerp eventually ousted the French printers by producing even smaller volumes in small twelves or 24mo.

## Materials and Production
The shift toward smaller book sizes was accompanied by changes in the materials used for production. To facilitate these miniature formats, printers utilized paper that was much thinner than the paper used in previous eras. 

Beyond the paper, the overall weight and size of books were further reduced through changes in binding. Printers began substituting wooden sides with pasteboards. This period also saw the introduction of gold tooling on bindings. By the second half of the century, elaborate decoration became fashionable in France, although this trend was eventually checked by a sumptuary law.

## Decline in Quality and Artistic Shifts
Despite the innovations in size and binding, the era experienced a steady decline in the quality of paper. This decline, coupled with a transition in artistic styles, created challenges for the printing trade. Printers abandoned the old, simple outline woodcuts in favor of much more ambitious designs. 

Because of the poor paper quality, it became increasingly difficult for printers to properly execute the detailed work of the artists. Consequently, woodcuts were gradually superseded by copper-plates, which were printed separately from the text. This transition began in the Low Countries before spreading to England and other regions.
