# New Atlantis and the Foundation of the Royal Society

The relationship between the literary work *New Atlantis* and the establishment of the Royal Society represents a pivotal moment in the development of natural science during the seventeenth century. The vision presented in this Utopian story served as a conceptual blueprint for the organized pursuit of scientific research and the experimental method.

## Sir Francis Bacon and New Atlantis
Sir Francis Bacon (1561–1626), who served as the Lord Chancellor of England and was known as Lord Verulam, was a primary leader in the advancement of systematic scientific inquiry. Bacon was a pupil and potentially the mouthpiece of Dr. Gilbert, an experimental philosopher from Colchester (1540–1603). Both men advocated for the importance of experiment and observation.

To express his vision of a great service dedicated to scientific research, Bacon utilized the form of a Utopian story titled *New Atlantis*. This work sought to articulate a dream of organized research that would later influence the structure of scientific institutions.

## Influence on the Royal Society
In the latter half of the 17th century, English writers recognized *New Atlantis* as a primary impetus for the foundation of the Royal Society. The society was formed in 1662, and its subsequent work was a realization of the dream Bacon had detailed in his text.

Dr. Thomas Sprat (1635–1713), the first historian of the Royal Society and Bishop of Rochester, asserted that Bacon possessed the "true imagination of the whole extent" of the scientific enterprise. Sprat further noted that Bacon's works provided the most effective arguments for the experimental method of natural philosophy. Other references to this influence are found in Dr. John Wallis's remarks and Cowley's *Ode to the Royal Society*.

## Impact and Legacy of Bacon's Method
Bacon's influence extended beyond England, with his work cited by principal scientists in Italy, Germany, and France. In France, the encyclopaedists and Voltaire hailed him as "le pere de la philosophie experimentale" (the father of experimental philosophy). D'Alembert described him in the *Encyclopedie* as the most eloquent, universal, and greatest of philosophers, while Condillac noted that no one understood the cause of human errors better than Bacon. Other critical testimonies include those of Huygens, who referred to Bacon's "bonnes methodes," and Leibnitz, who called him "divini ingenii vir."

The emergence of the Royal Society, alongside the Florentine Society and other national bodies, fostered the publication and exchange of knowledge. These societies became sources of numerous inventions and introduced a destructive criticism of the "grotesque theological history of the world" that had previously hindered human thought for centuries.

## Evolution of the Scientific Process
The scientific progress initiated by the dream of *New Atlantis* and the work of the Royal Society continued through the eighteenth century. This process was largely driven by "private gentlemen"—independent-spirited and prosperous individuals—rather than universities, which were often viewed as timid, conservative, and resistant to innovation.

Throughout the eighteenth century, this movement led to:
* A systematic development of optical glass for telescopes and microscopes.
* Mathematical advances and a clarification of ideas regarding motion and matter.
* A revival of anatomical science and renewed energy in classificatory natural history.
* The beginning of geology's task in interpreting the "Record of the Rocks," a field previously anticipated by Leonardo da Vinci and foreshadowed by Aristotle.

## Later Administration of the Royal Society
By the mid-eighteenth century, the Royal Society continued to be led by prominent figures of science. George, the 2nd Earl of Macclesfield (c. 1697–1764), a celebrated astronomer and Fellow of the society since 1722, served as its president from 1752 until his death in 1764. During his tenure, Macclesfield was instrumental in the transition from the old style of dates to the new style in 1752, although this move was unpopular with a public that felt eleven days had been stolen.
