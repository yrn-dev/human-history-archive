# The Barbarossa Family and the Turkish Power in North Africa

The Barbarossa family consisted of a lineage of 16th-century Turkish sea rovers and admirals. The family was founded by Yakub, a Roumeliot likely of Albanian descent, who settled in Mitylene following its conquest by the Turks. Yakub was a skipper and coasting trader who had four sons born after 1482: Elias, Isaak, Arouj, and Khizr. While Khizr became a potter and Isaak a trader, Elias and Arouj turned to sea roving.

## Arouj and the Rise of the Barbarossas
Arouj's early career was marked by capture and service. During an engagement with a galley of the Knights of Saint John based at Rhodes, Elias was killed and Arouj was taken prisoner. After being ransomed by a Turkish pasha, Arouj returned to the sea and served the Mamelukes of Egypt for a period. However, during the conflict between the Mamelukes and Sultan Selim I, Arouj moved to Tunis. In North Africa, he found employment as a mercenary amidst the conflicts of Berber princes, supplementing this work with piratical raids against Christian trade.

In Tunis, Arouj was joined by his brother Khizr, who adopted the name Khair-ed-Din, and later by their brother Isaak. Arouj and Khair-ed-Din collaborated with Moors exiled from Granada—who had been driven out by the Catholic sovereigns of Spain in 1492—to conduct raids on the Spanish coast. The brothers established their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes and sought to expand their influence by supplanting or fighting native African princes. In 1512, they attempted to capture Bougie from the Spaniards but were unsuccessful.

## Establishment of Turkish Power
The Spanish responded to these piratical attacks by conquering the coastal towns of Tunis, Algiers, and Oran. Arouj continued to fight the Spaniards until he fell in battle in 1518. Following this loss, Khair-ed-Din appealed to the Turkish sultan, Selim, who provided him with troops. In 1529, Khair-ed-Din successfully drove the Spaniards from a fort located on a rocky island in front of Algiers, an action that established Turkish power in the region.

## Governance of Northern Africa
From 1518 until 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli. Following the death of Uluch Ali in 1587, the region was governed by Turkish pashas dispatched from Constantinople for three-year terms. This system lasted until 1659, when a military revolt in Algiers stripped the pashas of their power. From 1659 onward, these African cities became anarchical military republics that chose their own rulers and relied on plunder, although they remained nominally part of the Turkish empire.

## Etymology of "Barbarossa"
The name "Barbarossa" (meaning "Redbeard") was a nickname applied by Christians to the family, likely due to their tawny or red beards. While some historians, such as Captain Walsin Esterhazy, suggested the name was a mispronunciation of "Baba Arouj," contemporary evidence contradicts this; the prefix "Baba" was not used for Arouj by his contemporaries. Spanish and Italian sources refer to Arouj as "Orux," "Harrach," or "Ordiche." An Arab chronicle published in 1837 explicitly states that the name Barbarossa was applied by Christians to Khair-ed-Din. The family's naval legacy continued through Khair-ed-Din's son, Hassan.
