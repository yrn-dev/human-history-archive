# The Lands of the Bohemian Crown and the Reign of Matthias

The "lands of the Bohemian crown" historically comprised Bohemia along with the territories of Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia. The administration and sovereignty of these lands were frequently contested during the 15th and 17th centuries, particularly involving the claims of various kings and the internal religious struggles of the Bohemian estates.

## The Conflict Between George of Podebrad and Matthias Corvinus
During the reign of George of Podebrad, the only Hussite king of Bohemia, a period of prosperity initially existed based on a firm alliance with Matthias Corvinus, the king of Hungary. However, this relationship deteriorated as George became involved in a struggle with the papal see. Pope Paul II eventually excommunicated George and released Bohemians from their oath of allegiance to him.

Taking advantage of this, Matthias of Hungary supported a confederacy of Romanist nobles against George. After invading Moravia and seizing Brunn, Matthias was proclaimed king of Bohemia on May 3, 1469. Following the death of George of Podebrad in 1471, the Bohemian nobles were divided; while many supported Matthias, others sought a national dynasty. This led to the election of Prince Vladislav of Poland at Kutna Hora on May 27, 1471.

The struggle between Vladislav and Matthias continued until 1478, when a treaty was concluded at Olmutz. Under the terms of this treaty, Bohemia was secured for Vladislav. Matthias was permitted to retain the "lands of the Bohemian crown"—specifically Moravia, Silesia, and Lusatia—for the remainder of his lifetime, after which they were to be restored to Bohemia. In Lusatia, the people had recognized Matthias Corvinus as their sovereign in 1469, though they returned to the rule of the Bohemian king in 1490.

## The Struggle Between Rudolph and Matthias
In the early 17th century, strife erupted between Emperor Rudolph and his younger brother, Matthias. The rupture was formally caused by Rudolph's refusal to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey that Matthias had concluded as his representative in Hungary. The Hungarians accepted Matthias as their ruler, and after his forces entered Moravia, the estates of that country renounced their allegiance to Rudolph.

Matthias subsequently invaded Bohemia. Although he invited the estates to join him at Caslav, the Bohemians declined. In June 1608, Matthias reached an understanding with Rudolph. Rudolph formally ceded the government of Hungary, Moravia, and Upper and Lower Austria to Matthias, while Rudolph himself retained his rights as king of Bohemia.

## Matthias's Accession and Religious Policy
The peace of Bohemia was disturbed again in 1611 when Archduke Leopold of Austria invaded. While Leopold's army was eventually defeated by troops raised by the Bohemian estates, the event prompted Matthias to occupy Bohemia, viewing his hereditary rights as menaced. At his instigation, the estates formally deposed Rudolph, who died on January 20, 1612. Matthias assumed the Bohemian crown in Prague on May 23, 1611.

Although Matthias had allied with Bohemian Protestants during his conflict with Rudolph, once in power he adopted the traditional Habsburg policy favorable to the Church of Rome. This strained his relations with the Protestants. In 1615, Matthias convoked a general diet that included representatives from the "lands of the Bohemian crown." During this meeting, the issue of nationality became predominant, and it was decreed that Bohemian would be the official authorized language of the country.

## Succession and the Outbreak of Conflict
Because Matthias was childless, he sought to secure the succession for his cousin, Ferdinand, duke of Styria, a known fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome. Despite Ferdinand's reputation as a cruel persecutor of Protestants in Styria, state officials persuaded the estates to accept him as heir; he was crowned at Prague on June 17, 1617.

Under Ferdinand's influence, Matthias's policy became more ultramontane. His councillors, all adherents of the Church of Rome, expressed hope that the Catholic Church would regain its hold over Bohemia. Tensions peaked in December 1617 when the abbot of Brevnov and the archbishop of Prague suppressed Protestant services in churches on their domains, violating an agreement signed by Rudolph in the Letter of Majesty.

Protestant members of the diet, led by Count Thurn, met in Prague on May 21, 1618. On May 22, a secret meeting was held where it was decided to put Matthias's most influential councillors to death. On May 23, the Protestant representatives proceeded to the Hradcany palace.

## Death of Matthias and Aftermath
Matthias died in March 1619, leaving the Bohemian crown vacant. Following his death, the Bohemian estates formally deposed Ferdinand on July 31 and elected Frederick, elector palatine, as their king on August 26. This triggered further warfare with Austria. While Thurn initially occupied Moravia, the Bohemians were eventually defeated by an imperialist army under the duke of Bavaria, Tilly, and Bouquoi at the Battle of the White Hill (Bila Hora) on November 9, 1620.
