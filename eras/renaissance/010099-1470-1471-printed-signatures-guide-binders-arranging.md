# Early Printing and Bookbinding Innovations (1470–1472)

The late 15th century marked a period of rapid technical evolution in the production of books, transitioning from the labor-intensive handwork of manuscripts to standardized printing processes. Key developments during this era included the introduction of pagination, the use of printed signatures to assist binders, and the expansion of the bookselling trade.

## Pagination and Leaf Numeration
The transition to organized page numbering occurred in stages. Printed leaf numeration was present as early as 1470 and became a common practice approximately ten years later. However, printed pagination—the numbering of individual pages rather than leaves—did not become common until nearly the middle of the 16th century. 

Early examples of pagination and headlines were first utilized by ther Hoernen in Cologne during 1470 and 1471. In the study of old books, these numerations are essential for collation, though errors in printed enumeration are common.

## Printed Signatures and Quire Arrangement
Before the adoption of printed signatures, the order of quires or gatherings in manuscripts and early printed books was indicated by signing them with letters of the alphabet. This typically followed the Latin alphabet, where "I" served for both I and J, and "V" served for both U and V (with no "W"). To accommodate books requiring more than twenty-three letters, printers used contractions for *rum*, *con*, and *et*, and occasionally *us*. For larger volumes, letters were doubled or minuscules were used alongside majuscules.

In 1472, Johann Koelhoff in Cologne introduced printed signatures. These served as a guide for binders to ensure that quires were arranged correctly. For modern bibliographers, these printed signatures provide the most efficient means of referencing specific pages in old books. A reference such as "X4 recto" indicates the first page of the fourth leaf in the gathering signed "X."

In the absence of printed signatures, the "make-up" of a book must be determined through other means. While original bindings may show the make-up via strings in the middle of each quire, rebound books may be identified by the smoothness of outer pages (if pressed separately between 1750 and 1850). The most reliable method for books without printed signatures is collation via watermarks—devices used by papermakers to mark sheets.

## Parallel Developments in Printing and Trade
The period around 1470 saw other significant milestones in the printing industry:
* **Illustrations:** While rude woodcuts appeared around 1461 via Albrecht Pfister in Bamberg, illustrations became abundant in Augsburg in the early 1470s. They became common in France, Germany, and the Low Countries in the 1480s, though full development in Italy occurred around 1490.
* **Bookselling:** The modern bookselling system emerged shortly after printing's introduction. Printers often acted as editors and booksellers. For example, Antony Koburger introduced printing to Nuremberg in 1470; he operated sixteen shops and employed agents in every city of Christendom.
* **Literature:** Around 1470, Ulrich Zel printed a Latin letter by Petrarch regarding the heroine Griselda.

## Evolution of Binding
The introduction of printing led to a reaction against expensive illuminated manuscripts. Early printed books were often small, cheap, and "ugly," typically bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf, and decorated with blind panel stamps. This era also saw the birth of gold tooling on leather, an art likely brought to Venice from the East. This technique reached its finest expression in late 15th-century Italian work before spreading to England, where it was practiced by Thomas Berthelet, the Royal Binder to Henry VIII.
