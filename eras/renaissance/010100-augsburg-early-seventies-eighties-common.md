# Augsburg and Early Printing

## Development of Printed Illustrations
In the history of book production, the completion of books independently of handwork progressed fairly rapidly, although the development of the title-page was a slower process. Early printed illustrations first appeared around 1461 in Bamberg, where Albrecht Pfister produced some small books featuring rude woodcuts. 

By the early 1470s, books printed at Augsburg were characterized by an abundance of illustrations. This trend expanded during the 1480s, as such illustrations became common across the Low Countries, France, and Germany. In contrast, the full development of printed illustrations in Italy did not occur until approximately 1490. While experiments were conducted in France and Italy using copper engravings for illustrations during the 15th century, these efforts did not achieve success.

## Printing and Book Production in the Late 15th Century
Other technical advancements in book production occurred during the 1470s. In Cologne, the Hoernen first utilized headlines and pagination in 1470 and 1471. Subsequently, in 1472, Johann Koelhoff introduced printed signatures at Cologne to assist binders in the correct arrangement of quires.

Augsburg also served as a site for the translation and printing of literary works during this era. An example of this is the prose romance *Pontus et Sidoine*, which was founded on the French *Horn*. While the original French version was printed in Lyons in 1480, a German translation was printed in Augsburg in 1483.

## The Augsburg Confession
Separate from the history of printing, the name Augsburg is associated with the dogmatic symbols of those Christians known as Lutherans. Lutherans are those who adopted the principles of Martin Luther in his opposition to the Roman Church, as well as followers of Calvin and sectaries from the Reformation period. Their distinctive name is the *Evangelical*, distinguishing them from the *Reformed* church.

The *Book of Concord* (*Liber Concordiae*) contains the dogmatic symbols of the Lutherans, consisting of nine separate creeds. These include three from the Early Christian church—the *Nicene Creed* (in its Western form with the *filioque*), the *Apostles' Creed*, and the *Athanasian Creed*—and six from the 16th century. The 16th-century documents include:
* The *Augsburg Confession*
* The *Apology for the Augsburg Confession*
* The *Schmalkald Articles*
* Luther's two *Catechisms*
* The *Form of Concord*

Among these, only the *Augsburg Confession* and the three early creeds are recognized by all Lutherans. The *Augsburg Confession*, along with Luther's Short Catechism, contains the distinctive principles that all Lutherans are bound to maintain. However, because the principal controversies of the Lutheran church occurred after its publication and among those who had already accepted it, the *Augsburg Confession* does not encompass everything that is distinctively Lutheran.

The universal acceptance of the *Augsburg Confession* may be attributed to its existence in two forms: the *invariata* and the *variata*. These two versions differ slightly in their statement of the doctrine of the sacrament of the Supper. The *variata* edition was signed by Calvin, who stated that he did so in the meaning of the author, Melanchthon.
