# ARRANGEMENTS OF WORKS BY OTHER COMPOSERS

The practice of arranging musical works by other composers involves the adaptation of existing compositions into different media or the addition of new material to existing schemes. This process can range from faithful reproductions to transformative works that introduce significant original elements.

## The Arrangements of J.S. Bach
Bach demonstrated a unique power in reproducing already perfect works in different media, revealing identities in difference that might otherwise remain unsuspected. His work in this area includes the arrangement of 16 concertos for harpsichord alone; while these are generally described as being by Vivaldi, they include several by other composers. Additionally, Bach arranged four Vivaldi concertos for the organ. 

Many of these arrangements are not mere copies but contain substantial original matter, including large cadenzas and entirely new slow movements. One specific example is the Concerto in A minor for orchestra and four claviers, which Bach adapted from Vivaldi's B minor concerto for four violins. This particular work is noted as the richest and most Bach-like of these arrangements, as well as the most faithful to the original. 

Bach's arrangements extended beyond concertos. He arranged two sonatas for clavier from Reinken's *Hortus Musicus*, adding his own compositions to the ends of the slow movements. He also provided finishing touches to cantatas written by his uncle, Johann Ludwig Bach, and inserted a characteristic, complete "Christe eleison" into one of Johann Ludwig's Kyries. Unlike some of his contemporaries, Bach did not arrange old work simply to please a court where the music was already admired, nor did he leave his arrangements in a state of "mere make-shift."

## Comparative Approaches to Arrangement
The approach to arranging existing material varies significantly between composers. For example, Handel collected several of his favorite choruses into a large instrumental concerto. However, the result of this specific arrangement—particularly in the case of the chorus "Lift up your Heads"—was described as ridiculous. This contrasts with Bach's method of adding parts to complete contrapuntal and harmonic schemes or adapting the same music to different words to expand his range of expression.

In other musical contexts, such as the composition of "peals" or "touches" for bells, arrangement is used to achieve a specific musical effect. Composers of bell music select sequences or contrasts of notes that strike the ear as more musical. On larger numbers of bells, where the possible changes are immense, the composer is free to select the most musical arrangements to improve the effect, provided the bells are in perfect tune and harmony.

## Non-Musical Arrangements of Works
The concept of arrangement also applies to the organization of literary and historical works. In the Epic Cycle, poems were arranged in series based on the principle of the continuity of narrated events (*ἀκολουθία πραγμάτων*). While this continuity was only approximate, it served as the organizing principle for the series. A similar principle of sequence in subjects could have been applied by librarians to tragedies to form a "Tragic Cycle."

In Babylonian literature, the *Epic of Gilgamesh*—composed by Sin-liqi-unninni in twelve books—was arranged according to an astronomical principle, with each division covering a single adventure. Similarly, in the creation epic glorifying Bel-Merodach, the deity arranged the sun, moon, and stars in order and provided them with laws.

In the realm of scholarly and poetic works, arrangement can refer to the structure of the text. Ephraim the Syrian utilized various metrical arrangements for his Syriac works. His simplest arrangements consisted of lines of uniform length, typically pentasyllabic or heptasyllabic. More complex arrangements, such as those in the *Carmina Nisibena*, used strophes with lines of different lengths according to a settled scheme and a recurring refrain. These strophic poems were set to music and sung by alternating choirs of girls to counteract the popularity of heretical hymns by Harmonius and Bardaisan.

In legal treatises, the arrangement of ideas can evolve over time. Grotius's 1604 treatise *De jure praedae* shared the same arrangement and main principles as his later 1625 work, *De jure belli*. However, the later work's arrangement became somewhat confused due to the insertion of the *Jus pacis*.
