# Whole Binding

In the history of bookbinding, "whole" binding refers to a specific stage in the evolution of the protective covering of the codex. This method developed from earlier medieval practices designed to protect the structural integrity of the book's construction.

## Evolution from Half-Binding
The development of whole binding began with the method of sewing quires together. Once this process was understood, binders recognized that the projecting bands at the back of the book required protection. To address this, strips of leather were fastened across the back of the joined quires. 

Additionally, because vellum leaves had a tendency to curl strongly, binders introduced strong wooden boards on each side of the book to counteract this effect. The boards were hinged to the book by fastening the loose ends of the sewing bands to them. The protective leather strip on the back was then drawn over the boards far enough to cover the hinge. This configuration is known as the medieval "half-binding," characterized by a leather strip over the back that projected only a short distance over the boards, leaving the remainder of the boards uncovered. These books were typically held closed by clasps located in the front.

The "whole" binding emerged as the leather strip was expanded to cover the entirety of the boards. This transition provided binders with fine, flat pieces of leather, which served as an ideal field for artistic decoration.

## Materials and Techniques
The materials used for bindings varied by region and era. Following the introduction of printing in Europe around the middle of the 15th century, there was a period of reaction against the precious covers of illuminated manuscripts. This led to the production of cheaper, smaller books generally bound in sheepskin, goatskin, or calf. These were often ornamented using large panel stamps in blind.

A significant advancement in the art of binding was the introduction of gold tooling on leather, an art likely brought to Venice from the East. The finest examples of this technique are found in Italian work from the late 15th century. In England, Thomas Berthelet, who served as the Royal Binder to Henry VIII and worked until approximately 1558, is credited as the first binder to practice gold tooling. His early work reflects a strong Italian influence, suggesting he may have been trained by an Italian master.

## Regional and Royal Styles
Different European traditions developed distinct binding characteristics:
*   **France:** Highly decorative work was produced for Henry II of France, featuring rich coloring and gilding. These bindings included the king's initials, the initials of Queen Catherine de' Medici, and emblems of bows and crescents. Similar styles were used for Diane de Poitiers, duchesse de Valentinois. Later, 18th-century French binders like Jean Charles le Monnier utilized delicate inlays of colored leathers, while Nicholas Denis ("Le Jeune") used "dentelles a l'oiseau," which were dentelle borders with arabesques and interspersed little birds.
*   **Germany:** German bindings were primarily made of pigskin and finely stamped in blind, though some were made of calf. Gilding in these examples was generally of poor quality.
*   **England:** During the 16th century, some fine printed books were bound in satin and velvet, occasionally embroidered or set with enamels. These often utilized strong metal threads. Royal books in England were frequently ornamented with heraldic designs, a fashion that began during the reign of Edward IV. In the mid-18th century, the "Harleian style" emerged through the work of Eliot and Chapman, featuring morocco leather with diamond-shaped center-pieces and broad, gold-tooled borders.

## Specialized Bindings and Forms
Beyond standard leather bindings, other specialized forms existed. The Venetian printer Aldus Manutius used dark morocco with small dolphins and "Aldine" knot leaves in gold and blind tooling. The Florentine printer Giunta used a similar style but omitted the dolphins. Some early Venetian bindings utilized double boards to create recessed panels, a technique described as being in the true oriental fashion.

Additionally, small gold bindings ornamented with enamels were produced during the Tudor period. These were often designed as "jewels" and included a ring at the top, intended for attachment to a girdle.
