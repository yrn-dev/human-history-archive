# Restoration and Release under Mary's Accession

The accession of Queen Mary (Mary I) resulted in the restoration of various individuals and institutions to their previous positions, particularly those associated with Roman Catholicism or those who had been deprived of their offices during preceding reigns.

## Restoration of the Clergy and Religious Houses
Following her accession, Mary's reign saw the restoration of specific religious entities and officials. The Bridgittine convent of Syon at Isleworth, Middlesex—originally founded by Henry V in 1415—was among the few religious houses restored during this period, with nearly twenty members of the old community being re-established there.

In the ecclesiastical sphere, certain officials who had been removed were returned to their positions. One such individual was restored to his see upon Mary's accession, as his previous deprivation was deemed invalid and his successor, Ridley, was regarded as an intruder. Upon his restoration, he vigorously worked to restore Roman Catholicism within his diocese. Although he had previously forsworn papal jurisdiction, he submitted to it without difficulty. By 1555, he began a period of persecution for which he is known. While his apologists claimed his actions were merely "official," he was instrumental in ensuring that the condemnation of heretics to the fire became a part of his ordinary official duties. This contrasted with his previous official duties regarding the enforcement of the first Book of Common Prayer, which he had protested.

## Political and Legal Transitions
The political climate under Mary Tudor also influenced the movements and fortunes of various figures in Scotland and England. In 1557, Balnaves was permitted to return to Scotland to regain his property. This permission was likely granted under the assumption that the burnings carried out by Mary Tudor would diminish his English affections, and that he would serve his own country during the threatened war between the two Catholic nations.

Other figures sought immunity or favor during Mary's rise. Cecil, who had served as the protector's master of requests and private secretary, and had later been a secretary to the king, found himself in a precarious position following the fall of the protector in 1549 and his subsequent time in the Tower. Cecil had been involved in Edward's illegal "devise" of the crown, a responsibility he later attempted to minimize. In his apology to Queen Mary, he did not use the excuse that he had merely signed the devise as a witness; instead, he emphasized his efforts to shift responsibility onto others, such as Sir John Cheke, and highlighted his intrigues to frustrate the queen to whom he had sworn allegiance. Cecil's immunity was largely attributed to the full account of these intrigues which he laid before Queen Mary, as well as the fact that he had not been involved in the humiliation of Mary during the reign of Henry or in the divorce of Catherine.

## Other Instances of Release
The archive records various other forms of release across different eras and contexts:

*   **Ancient Rome:** Caesar was released from his religious obligations in 81 B.C., allowing him to leave Rome for the East to serve his first campaign under Minucius Thermus. Later, after being captured by pirates, Caesar was released and subsequently carried out his threat to crucify them.
*   **Scottish Conflicts:** Sir James Balfour was condemned to the French galleys in June 1547 but was released in 1549, after which he abjured the reformers and entered the service of Mary of Guise. Similarly, Balnaves was released in May following the arrival of Hertford's fleet, subsequently becoming a paid agent for the English cause in Scotland.
*   **Court Figures:** Jeffery Hudson, a dwarf who served as a captain of horse during the Civil War, was imprisoned in the Gate House after being accused of participating in the "Popish Plot." He was eventually released shortly before his death in his sixty-third year.
*   **Political Stratagems:** Sir William Kirkcaldy of Grange, a Scottish politician, used a stratagem in September 1569 to release Maitland of Lethington from confinement in Edinburgh.
