# Francis Bacon

Francis Bacon (1561–1626), also known as Baron Verulam and Viscount St Albans, was an English essayist, statesman, and philosopher. Born on January 22, 1560/1, at York House in the Strand, London, he was the youngest son of Sir Nicholas Bacon, who served as lord keeper of the great seal of England during the reign of Queen Elizabeth. His mother was a daughter of Sir Anthony Cooke, a former tutor to Edward VI. Described as a woman of considerable culture and a warm adherent of the Puritan or Reformed Church, she was well-skilled in the classical studies of her era.

## Early Life and Education
Due to a consistently delicate state of health, Bacon likely received much of his early instruction at home. In April 1573, he entered Trinity College, Cambridge, where he resided for three years with his brother, Anthony. During his time at the university, Bacon diligently studied the sciences as they were taught at the time. This study led him to the conclusion that both the results attained and the methods employed were erroneous. While he maintained a reverence for Aristotle—despite appearing to have limited knowledge of him—Bacon grew to despise the prevailing Aristotelian philosophy, viewing it as fruitless and useful only for disputation. He believed that philosophy required a new method and a true purpose, and he left the university with the initial germs of this conception.

## Philosophical Contributions and Methodology
Bacon is characterized not as a lonely pioneer of modern thought or a great constructive philosopher, but as a figure who summed up the faults revealed in medieval thought by the widening of knowledge. He is positioned among those in 16th-century Europe who strove toward a new intellectual activity.

His methods and results have been the subject of significant scholarly debate. For instance, the critic Liebig attacked Baconian methods and results, specifically those found in the *Sylva Sylvarum*. Liebig claimed that Bacon made induction start from the undetermined perceptions of the senses, used imagination, and applied arbitrary interpretations to phenomena. However, other scholars, such as Fischer and Sigwart, have commented on the unjustifiable tone of these attacks. Fischer noted that the "true scientific method" proposed by Liebig was, in fact, simply the Baconian doctrine that Liebig was attempting to attack.

## Bibliographic and Editorial History
The works of Francis Bacon have been preserved through several major editions. The classical edition was produced by R. L. Ellis, J. Spedding, and D. D. Heath, with the first edition appearing in 1857 and a second in 1870. This edition is divided into philosophical writings (vols. i–iii), translations (iv–v), and professional and literary works (vi–vii). Other editions include a 17-volume set by B. Montagu (1825–1834), which is described as full but unscholarly, and a 1905 reprint of the philosophical works by J. M. Robertson. More recent accessible versions have been published in the "New Universal Library" (1905) and "World's Classics" (1901) series, as well as the *English Works of Francis Bacon* (1905) by Sidney Lee.

## Scholarly Reception and Biographies
Numerous biographies and studies have explored Bacon's life and philosophy. Key biographical works include:
* J. Spedding's *The Life and Letters of Lord Bacon* (1861) and *Life and Times of Francis Bacon* (1878).
* Dr. Rawley's *Life*, found in the Ellis-Spedding editions.
* W. Hepworth Dixon's *Personal History of Lord Bacon* (1861) and *Story of Lord Bacon's Life* (1862).
* P. Woodward's *Early Life of Lord Bacon* (1902).
* John Campbell's *Lives of the Chancellors* (1845).

Philosophical analyses have been conducted by Kuno Fischer (*Fr. Bacon*, 1856), Ch. de Remusat (*Bacon, sa vie ... et son influence*, 1857), and G. L. Craik (*Lord Bacon, his Writings and his Philosophy*, 1846–1847). Other contributors to the study of his work include A. Dorner, E. H. Boehmer, and Ch. Adam. Additionally, the archive notes discussions regarding Bacon's poetical gifts in the *Fortnightly Review* (1905) and his relationship with Ben Jonson in *The Tale of the Shakespeare Epitaphs by Francis Bacon* (1888).
