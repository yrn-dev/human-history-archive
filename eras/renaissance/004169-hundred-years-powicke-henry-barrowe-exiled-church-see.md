# Henry Barrowe and the Exiled Church

## Life and Early Career
Henry Barrowe was an English Puritan and Separatist born around 1550 in Shipdam, Norfolk. He belonged to a family related by marriage to the lord keeper Bacon and likely to Aylmer, the bishop of London. Barrowe matriculated at Clare Hall, Cambridge, in November 1566 and earned his B.A. in 1569-1570. Following his graduation, he spent time "following the court," a period described as frivolous and potentially licentious. Between 1576 and a few years thereafter, he was a member of Gray's Inn, though he was never called to the bar.

A turning point occurred around 1580 or 1581 when Barrowe was deeply impressed by a sermon. This led him to retire to the country, where study and meditation guided him toward the strictest form of Puritanism.

## Separatism and Beliefs
Barrowe eventually formed an intimate relationship with John Greenwood, a leader of the Separatists. Barrowe adopted Greenwood's views without reserve—views that were likely influenced in part by Robert Browne. Although he did not reside strictly in London, Barrowe became associated with the "brethren of the Separation" there, where he became conspicuous due to his eloquence and natural earnestness during secret meetings.

Theologically, Barrowe and Robert Browne shared several core convictions, though they were not identical. Both advocated for congregational independency and maintained that the Church possessed the right and duty to implement necessary reforms without waiting for permission from civil authorities. However, while Browne viewed separation as a means toward a "spiritual democracy," Barrowe viewed the entire established church order as being polluted by relics of Roman Catholicism. Consequently, Barrowe insisted that separation was essential for maintaining pure discipline and worship.

## Imprisonment and Trial
Barrowe's activities led to his arrest on November 19, 1586, while visiting John Greenwood in the Clink. He was brought before Archbishop Whitgift, where he refused to give bail for future appearance or take the *ex officio* oath, arguing that his arrest was illegal. He was subsequently committed to the Gatehouse.

In May 1587, Barrowe and Greenwood were formally indicted for recusancy under an act originally intended for Papists. They were ordered to provide heavy bail for conformity and were held in the Fleet Prison until such bail could be found. Barrowe remained a prisoner for nearly six years, the remainder of his life, alternating between close confinement and "the liberty of the prison."

During his incarceration, Barrowe wrote controversial tracts in conjunction with John Greenwood. He also appeared before the privy council at Whitehall on March 18, 1588, following a petition to the queen, where he vigorously defended the principle of separatism and denounced the prescribed rituals of the Church. While H. M. Dexter and others have credited Barrowe as the author of the "Marprelate Tracts," this is considered improbable.

## Death
In March 1593, Henry Barrowe was tried alongside John Greenwood. Both were condemned to death on charges of "devising and circulating seditious books." After receiving two respites, including one at the foot of the gallows, Barrowe was hanged on April 6, 1593.
