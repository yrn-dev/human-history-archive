# Collections of Quotations and Citations

The compilation of quotations, aphorisms, and verbal indices has served various scholarly purposes across history, ranging from the preservation of classical wisdom and the study of dramatic works to the documentation of historical events and the analysis of ancient texts.

## Literary and Dramatic Compilations
In the field of American publishing, John Bartlett (1820–1905) produced one of the most significant collections of its kind. Born in Plymouth, Massachusetts, Bartlett was a bookseller and publisher who was associated with the firm Little, Brown & Co. in Boston from 1865 until his retirement in 1889. In 1855, he published the first edition of *Familiar Quotations*, a work that was subsequently greatly expanded and remained for a long time the best-known collection of its type. 

Later in his career, after many years of labor, Bartlett published the *New and Complete Concordance or Verbal Index to Words, Phrases and Passages in the Dramatic Works of Shakespeare; with a Supplementary Concordance to the Poems* in 1894, although the work had been copyrighted five years prior. This concordance surpassed previous versions in the fullness and number of its citations from Shakespeare's writings. Bartlett was assisted in all his work by his wife, who was the daughter of Sidney Willard, a professor of Hebrew at Harvard between 1807 and 1831.

## Classical and Aphoristic Collections
The use of quotations to illustrate moral or philosophical points is evident in the work of James Duport (1606–1679), an English classical scholar. Duport, who served as the regius professor of Greek in 1639 and later as the master of Magdalene College, is best known for his 1660 work *Homeri gnomologia*. This publication is a collection of remarkable opinions, maxims, and aphorisms found within the *Odyssey* and the *Iliad*. To illustrate these classical points, Duport utilized quotations from classical literature and the Bible.

## Historical and Regional Citations
Quotations have also played a role in the dissemination of historical narratives, sometimes independently of the original author's recognition. In 16th-century Bohemia, the historian Wenceslas Hajek of Libocan (died 1553) wrote the *Kronika ceska*, which was dedicated to King Ferdinand I. Because the work was produced under the auspices of government officials, it possessed a strong Romanist and dynastic tendency. This allowed the book to circulate even during periods when most Bohemian books were prohibited or destroyed. Consequently, the work was frequently quoted and translated into several languages; some writers utilized second-hand quotations from Hajek's work despite likely having never heard of the author himself.

In other historical contexts, official quotations serve as records of service. For example, a Minute from 1862 by Lord Canning, the Governor-General, is used to record the value of the services provided by Lieut.-Colonel Yule in 1858 and 1859. In this quotation, Canning notes that the Queen's army in India was greatly indebted to Yule's ability, earnestness, and judgment during a time when the staff of Engineer officers was overtaxed and crippled.

## Textual and Linguistic Analysis
In the study of ancient manuscripts, quotations are used as primary evidence to localize and date texts. In the analysis of the Old Latin versions of the Bible, scholars distinguish between African and European types. The African version is identified through the quotations of Primasius (bishop of Hadrumetum, died c. 560), Augustine (excluding the gospels), and Cyprian. Additionally, a collection of quotations known as the *speculum* (m), once attributed to Augustine but likely connected to Spain, provides evidence for this version. There is ongoing scholarly dispute regarding whether the quotations of Tertullian serve as evidence for a specific Latin version or are independent translations from the Greek.
