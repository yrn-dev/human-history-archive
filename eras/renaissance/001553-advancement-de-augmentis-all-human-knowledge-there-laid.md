# Advancement or De Augmentis

The *Advancement of Learning* (1605) and its expanded Latin translation, *De Augmentis Scientiarum* (1623), are central works by Francis Bacon (1561–1626). These texts serve as a comprehensive survey and inventory of the possessions of the human mind, drawing a "map" of all human knowledge. The *De Augmentis* represents the first division of Bacon's larger project, the *Instauratio*, titled *Partitiones Scientiarum*. This project aimed to reorganize the sciences and restore man's command over nature, which Bacon believed had been lost through the fall.

## Classification of Human Knowledge

In the *Advancement* and *De Augmentis*, Bacon posits that all human knowledge can be referred to three primary faculties: memory, imagination, or reason.

*   **Memory:** This faculty collects and stores bare facts presented to the senses. The exposition of these facts is termed *history*, which is further categorized as either natural or civil.
*   **Imagination:** In this faculty, materials from the senses are divided or separated according to the mind's own pleasure rather than according to nature. The result of this process is *poesy* or feigned history.
*   **Reason:** This faculty works materials according to the model or pattern of nature, although Bacon notes a human tendency to err during the progress from sense to reason. The result is *philosophy*.

## The Structure of Philosophy

Bacon divides philosophy into three areas of concern: God, man, and nature. He identifies the study of nature—natural philosophy—as the most important of these and the "great mother of all the sciences." Natural philosophy is further subdivided based on its objective:

*   **Operative or Practical:** This branch is concerned with works.
*   **Speculative or Theoretical:** This branch is concerned with contemplation and is subdivided into physics and metaphysics.
    *   **Physics:** This field inquires into the material and efficient causes of things. Its primary objects are concrete substances or abstract physical qualities.
    *   **Metaphysics:** This field inquires into the final and formal causes. The study of *forms* is the result of metaphysical research and is more general than the study of abstract qualities in physics.

## Relation to the Instauratio and Other Works

The *De Augmentis* is the first part of the *Instauratio* and provides the survey of sciences that precedes the introduction of a new method. This subsequent method, intended to train the mind toward the renovation of science, is represented by the *Novum Organum*. While the *De Augmentis* draws the map of knowledge, the *Novum Organum* addresses the methods of inductive logic and the errors of scholasticism.

Other minor philosophical writings by Bacon relate to these themes. *The Advancement of Learning* was completely worked up into the *De Augmentis*. The *Descriptio Globi Intellectualis* serves as an intermediate work that details the general classification of the sciences. Additionally, *Valerius Terminus* provides a brief outline of the first two parts of the *Instauratio*.

## Influence and Legacy

Bacon's work in the *De Augmentis* and the inductive method described in the *Novum Organum* are considered by some to be the root of theories that construct moral codes through the inductive examination of human consciousness and the results of actions. Utilitarianism is cited as a natural result of applying the Baconian experimental method to human conduct. 

Furthermore, the spirit of the *De Augmentis* (specifically book vii) and the *Novum Organum* influenced English psychological speculation. For instance, the principle in Locke's *Essay* that reflection and sensation produce all ideas is briefly stated in the first aphorism of the *Novum Organum*.
