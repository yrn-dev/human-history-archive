# The Fall of Cesare Borgia

## Rise and Methods of Power
Cesare Borgia (1476–1507), also known as the Duke of Valentino, was the son of Pope Alexander VI (Rodrigo Borgia). His ascent to power was heavily dependent on the support of the papacy, as his father utilized the resources of Christendom to advance the family's interests. Early in his life, Cesare demonstrated a ruthless nature, arranging the murders of his elder brother and his sister Lucrezia's husband. 

Originally a priest, Cesare sought to renounce his priesthood in August 1498 with the pope's permission. Following a mission to France to secure a papal bull annulling the marriage of Louis XII, Cesare was granted the duchy of Valentinois and military assistance. In May 1499, he married Charlotte d'Albret, the sister of the king of Navarre.

With French military support—including 300 French lances and 4,000 Gascons and Swiss—Cesare began carving a principality in Central Italy. He captured Imola and Forli, the latter after a gallant defense by Caterina Sforza. By October 1500, with an army of 10,000 men and Venetian friendship, he entered Romagna. He secured the cities of Rimini and Pesaro after the flight of Giovanni Sforza and Pandolfo Malatesta. In Faenza, Cesare broke a promise to spare the life of the youth Astorre Manfredi, who was sent to Rome to be outraged and killed. For these successes, the pope created him duke of Romagna.

## Administration and Conflict
Cesare was characterized by a combination of audacity, diplomatic prudence, and an utter lack of scruple. While his methods of conquest were treacherous and ferocious, he governed his conquered subjects with firmness and justice. This administrative stability was often preferred by the populace over the anarchy of local despots and factions. He also attempted to modernize his military by gradually substituting native troops from his own duchy for mercenaries and aliens.

Despite his successes, Cesare faced significant opposition. The conspiracy of La Magione saw several deposed princes, the Orsinis, and his own captains—including Oliverotto da Fermo, G.P. Baglioni, and Vitellozzo Vitelli—unite against the Borgias. Although the promise of French aid frightened the confederates into terms, Cesare remained distrustful. In a decisive move at Senigallia, he decoyed the *condottieri* Vitelli, Oliverotto, and Paolo Orsini, along with the duke of Gravina, into his house and had them arrested.

## The Collapse of Power
The ruin of Cesare Borgia was precipitated by a sudden and unfortunate coincidence in 1503. While gathering troops for a new expedition in central Italy, both Cesare and his father were seized with fever. Pope Alexander VI died on August 18, 1503, while Cesare was still incapacitated. This event removed the primary foundation of Cesare's power.

His enemies immediately rose against him. In Rome, the Orsinis rebelled, and in Romagna, deposed princes sought to reclaim their territories. When Cesare attempted to intimidate the cardinals using his bravos and Don Michelotto, the cardinals refused to be intimidated. Cesare was forced to leave Rome in September.

The subsequent election of Pope Pius III (Francesco Todeschini-Piccolomini) saw Cesare's dominion rapidly disintegrate. With Venetian assistance, Guidobaldo, duke of Urbino, returned to his duchy, and the lords of Pesaro, Rimini, and Piombino regained their lands. Only Cesena, held by a faithful governor, remained under Cesare's control.

## Final Downfall
Following the death of Pius III on October 18, 1503, a new conclave was held. Cesare attempted to prevent the election of Giuliano della Rovere, an enemy of his house, but della Rovere was elected on November 1, assuming the name of Julius II. 

While Julius II initially showed no ill-will, he insisted that Cesare's territories be restored to the church, stating a desire to recover what predecessors had wrongfully alienated. Julius II briefly considered using Cesare to prevent Venice from establishing a protectorate over the Romagna principalities, but after this failed, he had Cesare arrested. This marked the absolute end of Borgia's power; he was forced to surrender all his castles in Romagna.

## Influence on Political Thought
Cesare Borgia served as a primary inspiration for Niccolò Machiavelli. Having served as an envoy to Borgia's camp in October 1502, Machiavelli observed the duke's use of fraud, cruelty, and self-reliance. In his work *The Prince*, Machiavelli idealized Borgia as a representative figure of the successful prince, admiring his ability to cement a new state from scattered elements. Machiavelli also adopted Borgia's model of employing national militias over mercenaries when attempting to organize a standing militia for Florence.
