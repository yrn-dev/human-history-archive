# Book-plates

The book-plate, also known as the *ex-libris*, is a printed label used to indicate the ownership of individual volumes. This practice is nearly as old as the printed book itself, serving a similar purpose to the symbolical personal devices or hand-painted armorials found in medieval manuscripts.

## Origins and Early Development in Germany
The earliest known examples of book-plates are German. According to the authority Friedrich Warnecke of Berlin, the oldest movable *ex-libris* are hand-painted woodcuts depicting a shield of arms supported by an angel. These were pasted into books gifted to the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim by Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach around the year 1480.

Germany is considered the starting point for the history of the book-plate, not only due to these earliest examples but because the practice was adopted there in great numbers long before it spread to other nations. German plates are often noted for their high artistic interest and a heavy, over-elaborated style that did not affect neighboring countries. Between 1503 and 1516, Albrecht Durer engraved at least six plates and provided designs for many others. Other notable German artists associated with book-plates include Lucas Cranach, Hans Holbein, and the "Little Masters," such as the Behams, Jost Amman, Virgil Solis, Matthias Zundt, Saldorfer, Georg Hupschmann, and others.

## Spread to Other Countries
The fashion of ornamental book-plates as marks of possession spread from Germany to other regions at different intervals:

*   **France:** The oldest discovered *ex-libris* is from 1529, belonging to Jean Bertaud de la Tour-Blanche. However, movable *ex-libris* did not become common in France until the 17th century, as book owners previously preferred the more luxurious habit of stamping covers with personal devices. The term *ex-libris*, used as a substantive, originated in France.
*   **England:** The earliest known example is a gift-plate from Sir Nicholas Bacon for books presented to the university of Cambridge. Another early example is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585.
*   **Holland:** The archive records a plate from 1597 belonging to Anna van der Aa.
*   **Italy:** An example is attributed to the year 1622.
*   **America:** The earliest known American example is a plain printed label from 1679 belonging to John Williams.

## Evolution of the English Book-plate
English book-plates reflect the decorative tastes of their respective epochs. Early English plates were remarkably simple and primarily armorial, featuring symmetrical mantling and occasionally wreaths or palms, lacking the elaborateness of the German style. Following the Restoration, book-plates became an established accessory for well-ordered libraries.

During this period, plates began to display tincture lines and dots based on a method devised by Petra Sancta in *Tesserae Gentilitiae*. The mantling also became more elaborate, resembling the periwigs of the era. This style was imported from France, and until the French Revolution, English decoration generally followed French taste with a delay of several years.

English compositions eventually diversified into several pictorial genres:
*   **Library interiors and book-piles:** Exemplified by the *ex-libris* of William Hewer (1699), secretary to Samuel Pepys.
*   **Portrait-plates:** Notably those of John Gibbs and Samuel Pepys.
*   **Allegories:** Engraved by artists such as Bartolozzi, Hogarth, George Vertue, and John Pine.
*   **Landscape-plates:** Produced by the Bewick school of wood engravers.

## Artistic Value and Collecting
Book-plates are collected as personal relics of famous people or as specimens of decorative fashion. While some "ex-librists" claim the practice is a science, some bibliophiles view the removal of plates from old books as vandalism. 

Many plates are considered highly valuable works of art. Notable designers across history include Charles Eisen, D.N. Chodowiecki, Hubert Francois Bourguignon (dit Gravelot), Simon Gribelin, and various English artists such as W. Marshall, David Loggan, and Sir Robert Strange. In more modern times, artists such as Aubrey Beardsley, Walter Crane, Kate Greenaway, and Caldecott have contributed to the craft.

## Modern Trends and Production
In recent years, the design of book-tokens has shifted from the routine skill of heraldic stationers to a recognized branch of higher art. Modern designs tend to make the heraldic element subsidiary, allowing symbolic or allegorical elements to dominate. 

The popularization of the book-plate—which was once restricted to important collections or ancestral libraries—has been aided by "process work," which has made the reproduction of elaborate designs cheaper and easier. Despite this, some artists continue to use the graver. Notable modern engravers include G.W. Eve and C.W. Sherborn in England, and E.D. French, K.W.F. Hopson, and J.W. Spenceley in America.
