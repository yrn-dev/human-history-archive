# Prague and the Habsburg Dynasty

Prague served as a primary residence and central hub for the vast Habsburg dominions, particularly during the reign of Rudolph II. As a patron of the arts, Rudolph contributed significantly to the embellishment of the city. Under his patronage, Prague became a center for scientific and astrological study. Following the death of Tycho on October 24, 1601, Rudolph appointed Kepler as imperial mathematician. While in Prague, Kepler produced works such as *De fundamentis astrologiae certioribus* (1602) and *De stella nova in pede Serpentarii* (1606), and he provided astrological forecasts and horoscopes for the emperor and other magnates.

## The Reign of Rudolph II

Rudolph succeeded his father, Maximilian, without opposition after being recognized as successor and crowned king of Bohemia following the diet of 1575. Educated in Spain, Rudolph was initially expected to treat church reformers with severity; however, he instead displayed apathy and indifference toward both matters of state and religious controversy. His reign was marked by periods of insanity and fits of melancholia.

By 1600, Rudolph's mental impairment became so severe that the Habsburg princes grew concerned about the state's future, especially since he had no legitimate descendants. His brother Matthias traveled to Prague to suggest that Rudolph appoint a coadjutor to fulfill royal duties if he became incapacitated and to make arrangements for the succession of the throne. Rudolph indignantly rejected these suggestions. He also received a formally courteous but forceful letter from Pope Clement VIII, who warned of the evil results caused by Rudolph's neglect of his duties and urged him to appoint a Habsburg prince as successor to the imperial crown and the thrones of Hungary and Bohemia.

## Conflict with Matthias

Strife eventually broke out between Rudolph and his younger brother Matthias, who sought to supplant him by utilizing political and religious controversies. The formal rupture occurred when Rudolph refused to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey that Matthias had concluded as a representative in Hungary. This led the Hungarians to accept Matthias as their ruler. Subsequently, the estates of Moravia renounced their allegiance to Rudolph.

Matthias invaded Bohemia and invited the estates to meet him at Caslav, but the Bohemians declined. In March 1608, the estates met at Prague; while they submitted demands regarding ecclesiastical matters to Rudolph, they authorized him to levy troops for Bohemia's defense. Matthias's forces reached Liben, a small town near Prague. On June 25, 1608, the brothers reached an understanding: Rudolph formally ceded the government of Upper and Lower Austria, Moravia, and Hungary to Matthias, though Rudolph retained his rights as king of Bohemia.

## Religious Tensions and the Diet of 1609

Following the settlement with Matthias, the Bohemian estates again presented demands to the king. At the diet of January 20, 1609, religious controversies were heavily influenced by personal enmities. Despite appearing as a champion of Catholicism, Rudolph was secretly negotiating with Christian of Anhalt, the leader of the German Protestants.

The estates sought the recognition of the *Confessio Bohemica* as a fundamental law of the kingdom and demanded complete religious liberty for all classes. They requested that Roman Catholics and Protestants—a term then encompassing Lutherans, Utraquists, and Bohemian Brethren—have equal rights to hold state offices, and they sought to limit the Jesuits' ability to acquire land. They also requested redress for grievances caused by Rudolph's misrule. After protracted discussions, Rudolph stated on March 31 that he could grant no concessions regarding religion.

## Transition to Matthias and Ferdinand

The peace of Bohemia was further disturbed in 1611 when Archduke Leopold of Austria, bishop of Passau, invaded and took possession of part of Prague. After Leopold's army was defeated by troops raised by the Bohemian estates, Matthias occupied Bohemia and instigated the formal deposition of Rudolph. Rudolph died on January 20, 1612.

Although Matthias had previously allied with Bohemian Protestants, he adopted the traditional Habsburg policy favoring the Church of Rome. In 1615, he convoked a general diet where the issue of nationality became predominant, resulting in the reaffirmation of enactments enforcing the national language and the decree that Bohemian be the official language of the country.

Because Matthias was childless, he sought to secure the succession for his cousin, Ferdinand, duke of Styria, who was a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome. Despite Ferdinand's reputation as a cruel persecutor of Protestants in Styria, state officials persuaded the estates to accept him. Ferdinand was crowned at Prague on June 17, 1617.

## The Outbreak of Conflict

Under Ferdinand's influence, Matthias's policy became more ultramontane. In December 1617, the abbot of Brevnov and the archbishop of Prague ordered the suppression of Protestant services in churches on their domains, violating an agreement signed by Rudolph in the Letter of Majesty. In response, Protestant members of the diet met at Prague on May 21, 1618, and proceeded in armor to the Hradcany palace to complain to Matthias's councillors. On May 22, a secret meeting involving Wenceslas of Ruppa, Ulrich of Kinsky, and Count Thurn resulted in a decision to put the most influential of Matthias's councillors to death.
