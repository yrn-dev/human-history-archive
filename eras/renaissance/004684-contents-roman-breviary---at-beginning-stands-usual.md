# Roman Breviary

The Roman Breviary (from the Latin *breviarium*, meaning epitome or abridgment) is the book containing the offices for the canonical hours, which constitute the daily service of the Roman Catholic Church. It includes hymns and lessons for every day of the year. However, it is less comprehensive than some other liturgical books in that it excludes the Eucharistic office (which is found in the Missal) and special offices for ordination, burial, marriage, and baptism (which are found in the Pontifical or the Ritual).

## Structure and Contents
The Roman Breviary begins with introductory matter, including general rubrics, the calendar, and tables used to determine the date of Easter. The main body of the Breviary is divided into four seasonal parts: winter, spring, summer, and autumn. Under each of these seasonal divisions, the book comprises five components:
1. The Psalter
2. *Proprium de Tempore* (the special office of the season)
3. *Proprium Sanctorum* (special offices of saints)
4. *Commune Sanctorum* (general offices for saints)
5. Extra Services

These five parts are frequently published as separate volumes.

## The Psalter
The Psalter serves as the groundwork and backbone of the Breviary, providing the basis for versicles, responsories, and antiphons. The psalms are arranged according to an 8th-century disposition:
* **Matins:** Psalms 1–108 (with some omissions) are recited; twelve are said each day from Monday to Saturday, and eighteen on Sunday.
* **Lauds, Prime, and Compline:** These hours include the psalms omitted from Matins.
* **Vespers:** Psalms 109–147 (excluding 117, 118, and 142) are recited, with five psalms said each day.
* **Lauds:** Psalms 148–150 are always used, giving the hour its name.

The text used is the Gallican Psalter, which is the 392 A.D. second revision of the old *Itala* version by Jerome. While Jerome's first revision (the Roman version) is still used at St Peter's in Rome, the Gallican version was introduced into Gaul in the 6th century by St Gregory of Tours and subsequently adopted by the English Church in the 10th century and Ireland in the 7th century.

## Historical Development and Revisions
In the early days of Christian worship, the Bible provided all necessary materials. The Breviary evolved first by separating the Psalter into a choir-book. By the 4th century, psalms began to be grouped, a process encouraged by monks who recited all 150 psalms daily. To manage the time required, monks spread the recitation over a week, dividing the day into hours. St Benedict created such an arrangement in the 6th century, likely based on an older, less skillful Roman division. Over time, collects, responses, and antiphons were added for those unable to improvise metrical compositions.

Until the council of Trent, bishops had the power to regulate the Breviary of their own diocese, and monastic communities maintained their own versions. Pope Pius V (1566–1572) made the Roman Breviary obligatory everywhere, except for those that could prove 200 years of existence. This led to the Roman version becoming nearly universal, though dioceses were allowed additional offices for specially venerated saints.

The Roman Breviary has seen several notable revisions:
* **1536:** Cardinal Francis Quignonez produced a remarkable revision. Although not accepted by Rome, it served as the model for the 1549 reform by the Church of England.
* **1588:** Sixtus V introduced the revised Vulgate text.
* **1602:** Clement VIII revised the rubrics through Bellarmine and Baronius.
* **1623–1644:** Urban VIII altered the text of the hymns, which some consider an injury to their historic worth and literary charm.

In France during the 17th and 18th centuries, a movement led by the Port Royal school and figures such as Louis Sébastien le Nain de Tillemont and Jean de Launoy sought to modify Breviaries. They emphasized communion with God over the invocation of saints and required that responses and antiphons use exact Scriptural words. These reformed French Breviaries, such as those by Archbishop François de Harlay (1680) and Archbishop Charles Gaspard Guillaume de Vintimille (1736), were eventually suppressed by an Ultramontane movement during the pontificate of Pius IX, with the diocese of Orleans being the last to surrender in 1875.

## Usage and Translations
Every member of a religious order and every clerk in orders is required to read the daily allotted services either privately (aloud, using both lips and eyes) or publicly. Reading the services privately takes approximately two hours. In large churches, services are grouped: Matins and Lauds (approx. 7:30 A.M.), Prime, Terce, Sext, and None (approx. 10 A.M.), and Vespers and Compline (4 P.M.). Depending on the number of high masses and music, this can occupy four to eight hours in the choir. Laymen rarely use the Breviary for devotion.

The Roman Breviary has been translated into German, French, and English. The English translation by the marquess of Bute (1879, with a 1908 edition including the Martyrology) is noted for including hymn renderings by J.M. Neale and J.H. Newman.
