# Axioms and the Baconian Method

In the study of logic and philosophy, an axiom is defined as a general proposition or principle that is accepted as self-evident, either absolutely or within a specific sphere of thought. While different sciences possess their own axioms, some may be proven within a wider science. Historically, the term's usage has evolved: Plato likely confined it to mathematical axioms, whereas Aristotle expanded the meaning to include the ultimate principles of thought—such as the principle of contradiction—which underlie all special sciences. Following Aristotle, the Stoics and the school of Ramus used the term for a proposition simply. In the modern era, Rene Descartes (or his followers) reintroduced the term in the search for a self-evident basis for a new philosophy, exemplified by the Cartesian axiom *Cogito ergo sum*. Immanuel Kant further narrowed the definition to include only intuitive synthetic propositions regarding space and time.

## The Baconian Approach to Axioms

Francis Bacon used the term "axiom" to refer to any general proposition. His approach to establishing these axioms was rooted in a new method of induction, which he viewed as the "thread" (*filum labyrinthi*) necessary to navigate the "vast labyrinth" of the world. Bacon argued that the human mind must be a servant and interpreter of nature, and that knowledge is limited to what has been observed in fact or thought.

Bacon identified several faults in the process leading from senses to axioms. He noted that sensory impressions are often faulty or deceptive, and that the notions derived from these impressions are frequently confused and indefinite. He specifically criticized the "induction" that infers scientific principles through simple enumeration without employing "solutions" or "exclusions" of nature. Furthermore, he described the method of establishing general principles first and then trying intermediate axioms by them as the "curse of all science."

## The Inductive Process and Exclusion

The essence of Bacon's induction was not the simple argument from particulars to a general proposition, but rather the process of elimination, exclusion, and rejection. This method was based on his doctrine of forms. To implement this, Bacon required a "natural and experimental history"—a competent collection of facts—to serve as a foundation. He emphasized that the mind must remain receptive and free from preconceived ideas to avoid substituting a "dream of our own imagination" for the actual patterns of the world.

The inductive investigation involves the construction of specific tables of instances:
*   **Table of Essence and Presence:** Instances where the nature being studied is present.
*   **Table of Absence in Proximity:** Instances where the nature is absent, focusing on those most akin to the instances of presence.
*   **Table of Degrees (or Comparison):** Instances where the nature is present in varying degrees, either in the same subject or across different subjects.

Once these tables are formed, the process of exclusion is applied to eliminate the non-essential and detect the cause or form. To assist this process, Bacon devised *adminicula* (helps), which include Prerogative Instances, Supports of Induction, and the Rectification of Induction, among others.

## Testing and Refinement of Axioms

In the establishment of axioms through this inductive method, Bacon taught that it is necessary to examine whether the established axiom is "framed to the measure" of the particulars from which it was derived, or if it is "larger or wider." If the axiom is wider, one must observe if it indicates new particulars that confirm this wideness. This serves as a "collateral security" to ensure the thinker does not "loosely grasp at shadows and abstract forms" or remain stuck only in things already known, but instead grasps things "solid and realized in matter."

While Bacon focused on induction, he did not entirely reject the syllogism. He believed the syllogism was incompetent for "the principal things" and the "generality," but maintained that there was no reason why it should not be employed in mathematics. In physics, he argued that the syllogism could be used only after the first-class notions and their corresponding axioms had been well-defined through induction.

## Critical Perspectives

The value of the Baconian method has been subject to debate. Some critics, such as Macaulay, questioned its efficiency. Others have argued that the method is impractical because it treats the scientific problem as one of co-existence and assumes each phenomenon has only one cause. Logicians have noted that science has not historically followed a gradually ascending scale of inductive axiom formation; instead, the more powerful instrument has been the deductive method and the use of hypotheses followed by verification. This suggests that the mind is not entirely passive or recipient, as Bacon's doctrine implied.
