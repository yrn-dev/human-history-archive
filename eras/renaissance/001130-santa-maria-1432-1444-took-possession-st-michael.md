# The Azores

The Azores (also known as *Acores* or the Western Islands) are an archipelago located in the Atlantic Ocean and belong to the kingdom of Portugal. The islands extend in an oblique line from the northwest to the southeast, situated between 36° 55' and 39° 55' N. and between 25° and 31° 16' W. They are the farthest island group in the Atlantic from any continent; the nearest continental land is Cape da Roca on the Portuguese coast, located 830 m. east of St Michael's. Other nearby points include Cape Cantin on the African mainland (more than 900 m. distant) and Cape Race in Newfoundland (more than 1000 m. distant). Despite this isolation, they are generally regarded as part of Europe due to their European flora and climate.

## Geography and Composition
The archipelago is divided into three widely severed groups that rise from depths exceeding 2-1/2 m.
*   **The South-Eastern Group:** Consists of St Mary (Santa Maria), St Michael's (Sao Miguel), and Formigas.
*   **The Central Group:** Consists of Graciosa, Terceira, St George (Sao Jorge), Pico, and Fayal (Faial).
*   **The North-Western Group:** Consists of Corvo and Flores.

The islands share similar general characteristics, featuring an undulating and elevated outline with little to no tableland. The coastlines are generally high and precipitous, with open bays or inlets serving as harbors for trading towns. The islands vary in elevation, with the lowest peak found on Corvo at 350 ft. and the highest on Pico, reaching 7612 ft. above sea-level.

## Discovery and Early History
While there is no evidence that the ancient Romans or Greeks knew of the Azores, the discovery of Carthaginian coins on Corvo suggests the islands may have been visited by Carthaginians. Arabian geographers from the 12th century (Edrisi) and 14th century (Ibn-al-Wardi) described nine islands in the Western Ocean after the Canaries, which are likely the Azores. These writers claimed the islands were populous and contained cities of some magnitude, though the population had been reduced by intestine warfare.

The islands appeared distinctly on a map in 1351, where the western group was called the Brazil Island (*De Brazi*), the middle group the Wind or Dove Islands (*De Ventura sive de Columbis*), and the southern group the Goat Islands (*Cabreras*). A Catalan map from 1375 identifies St George as *San Zorze*, Flores as *Li Conigi*, and Corvo as *Corvi Marini*.

The Portuguese discovery is associated with several accounts. One suggests that van der Berg was driven to the islands in 1432, which sparked interest at the court of Lisbon. Consequently, the navigator Gonzalo Velho Cabral was sent to pursue the discovery. An alternative version states that Prince Henry the Navigator sent Cabral based on the accuracy of a map in his possession.

## Volcanic Activity
The archipelago is volcanic in nature, a fact confirmed by numerous eruptions and earthquakes since its discovery. The primary erupted materials are scoria and basalt. While Flores, Corvo, and Graciosa have been exempt from such activity, and Fayal has experienced only one eruption (1672), St Michael's has been the center of most activity. In contrast, the neighboring island of St Mary has entirely escaped volcanic eruptions.

Significant events on St Michael's include:
*   **1444-1445:** A great eruption occurred, though preserved accounts are noted to exaggerate its importance.
*   **1522:** A violent convulsion buried the town of Villa Franca, the island's capital at the time, along with its 6000 inhabitants.
*   **Subsequent Activity:** The island was visited by successive earthquakes and eruptions in 1630, 1652, 1656, 1755, and 1852.

Other islands have also experienced activity, including Pico in 1572, St George in 1580, and a town in Terceira in 1614. Subterranean eruptions have occurred on various occasions (1638, 1720, 1811, and 1867), sometimes creating temporary islands. A notable example occurred in June 1811, when an island called Sabrina was thrown up about half a league from the western extremity of St Michael's.
