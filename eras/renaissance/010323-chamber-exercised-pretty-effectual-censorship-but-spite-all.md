# Censorship and the Control of Information

Censorship has been employed across various eras and political systems to regulate the dissemination of information, repress "obnoxious" materials, and maintain state or religious authority. These efforts have ranged from the licensing of printing presses in early modern England to the official censorship commissions of 19th-century France and the wartime restrictions of the 20th century.

## Censorship in Early Modern England

During the reigns of the Tudor and Stuart dynasties, the English government did not tolerate a free press. The demand for books increased significantly due to the Reformation under Edward VI and Henry VIII, as well as religious dissensions across the continent. To curb this, Henry VIII granted the first patent for the office of king's printer to Thomas Berthelet in 1529. Under this system, only books that had been licensed could be printed; the possession or purchase of an unlicensed book was considered a punishable offense.

In 1556, the Company of Stationers was incorporated and granted extensive powers to repress books deemed obnoxious. In subsequent reigns, the Star Chamber exercised a "pretty effectual censorship." Despite these precautions, the high demand for polemical books led many to be printed abroad and smuggled into England. 

The approach to censorship varied by monarch. Queen Elizabeth generally interfered little with books, unless they were written by Roman Catholics or concerned her royal prerogatives. Bookselling flourished toward the end of her reign and during the reign of James. However, Archbishop Laud later introduced many arbitrary restrictions on booksellers. These restrictions were nearly all removed during the Commonwealth. Bookselling grew so extensively during the Protectorate that by 1658, W. London published *A Catalogue of the most Vendible Books in England*, which categorized works under headings such as History, Physic, and Divinity. Following this period, the Restoration reinstated the office of Licenser of the Press, which remained in place until 1694.

## French Political and Literary Censorship

In 19th-century France, censorship was advocated by conservative figures such as Louis Gabriel Ambroise, Vicomte de Bonald (1754–1840). A philosopher and politician who emigrated in 1791 due to his dislike of the Revolution, Bonald wrote the conservative *Theorie du pouvoir politique et religieux* (1796), a work condemned by the Directory. 

After the restoration, Bonald served as a deputy in the chamber from 1815 to 1822, where his speeches were characterized as being on the extreme conservative side. He advocated for literary censorship and, upon being made minister of state in 1822, presided over the censorship commission.

## Wartime Censorship

Censorship has also been utilized during periods of military crisis to manage public perception. During the German advance in the early 20th century, the British government employed censorship to control the flow of information. Despite these measures, reports in newspapers remained alarming, and rumors often exceeded the information that was actually printed. To provide comfort without concealing the truth, a communiqué was drafted on September 4 at the request of the Prime Minister and Lord Kitchener to provide a general survey of the British Army's operations.

## The Roman Censor

In ancient Rome, the role of the censor was a distinct political office. Vespasian and Titus were the last to hold this position, as Trajan refused the honor, setting an example followed by the Antonines. The censorship could be used for moral and civic regulation; for instance, Cato the Censor introduced "wise regulations and virtuous discipline" to restore a state he found corrupt and degenerate. His measures included destroying houses that encroached on public streets, cutting off water-pipes that led from public fountains to private gardens, and lowering the price of public works contracts. Despite the severity of these measures and opposition from the Senate and tribunes, Cato's censorship was considered wonderfully popular with the Roman people.
