# Greece

## Social and Legal Customs
In the nations of antiquity, including Greece, the practice of selling a debtor and their family into slavery was habitually exercised. This severe approach to debt contributed to serious disturbances in political relations between the plebeians, who comprised the majority of debtors, and the patricians, who generally made up the wealthier creditor class.

During the Grecian heroic age, society was characterized by the omnipotence of private force, which was guided and tempered by family sympathies. In this era, the collective sovereign later known as "The City" was a practical nullity. However, as historical Greece developed, The City eventually became the central and paramount source of obligation. An example of the earlier heroic age's customs was the specific obligation of a relative who inherited the property of a murdered man to avenge that person's death.

## Religious Practices and the Bacchanalia
Certain populations in Greece, specifically those reputed to be the wisest of all Greece, practiced the Bacchanalia. These ceremonies were characterized by frantic spirits, dancing, drunkenness, debauchery, and abandoned licentiousness. Plato noted that he had seen the entire city of Athens drunk at once during these events.

These "impure and abominable mysteries" eventually crept into Rome. In the Roman context, the Bacchanalia led to horrid disorders committed under the cover of night. Because initiates were obliged to observe inviolable secrecy under horrid imprecations, the practices continued until the Roman senate, upon becoming aware of the affair, imposed severe penalties. The practitioners were first banished from Rome and subsequently from Italy.

## Travel and Exploration
Greece has been a destination for various scholars and antiquaries over the centuries. Edward Dodwell, an English writer on archaeology and traveller educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, travelled in Greece from 1801 to 1806. His work resulted in several publications, including *Views in Greece* (1821), *A Classical and Topographical Tour through Greece* (1819), and *Views and Descriptions of Cyclopian or Pelasgic Remains in Italy and Greece* (1834).

Jean Jacques Boissard (1528-1602), a French Latin poet and antiquary, also attempted to visit the region. After spending several years in Italy developing a taste for antiquities and collecting monuments from Rome and its vicinity, Boissard visited the islands of the Archipelago with the intent of travelling through Greece. However, he was unable to complete this journey as a severe illness obliged him to return to Rome.
