# Customary Methods and Practices

The term "customary method" appears across various disciplines within the archive, referring to established protocols in accounting, cartography, legal citation, and cultural traditions. These practices range from standardized professional procedures to ingrained social customs.

## Accounting and Book-keeping
In the field of book-keeping, a customary method for managing capital accounts involves deducting withdrawals of capital from the account and adding profits to it. An alternative plan exists where the capital account balance remains constant, and net profits and drawings are instead transferred to a separate "current account." While this second approach is rarely used by private firms or undertakings owned by individuals, it is invariably adopted by joint-stock companies, where it is generally referred to as an "appropriation of profit account."

Regarding the recording of transactions, it is now usual to utilize several books of first-entry. However, for smaller businesses, a single book known as the "journal" is often sufficient. For many years, the journal represented the only book of first-entry used in book-keeping, allowing one person to record all transactions as they occur.

## Cartography and Topographical Surveys
In topographical surveys, field sheets are typically designed on conical projections with rectified meridians, which are considered sensibly errorless for ordinary topographical scales (not less than 1/500,000) and small areas. To reduce labor, it is customary to use either form of polyconic projection, as the errors for these scales are negligible.

In certain surveys, practitioners avoid the difficulty of plotting flat arcs for parallels by replacing them with polygons, where each side corresponds to the length of the arc it replaces. This specific method is particularly suitable for scales of 1:125,000 and larger, though it is occasionally applied to smaller scales. The resulting projection is usually called a graticule.

## Legal Citation and Historical Documentation
The archive notes a conflict between traditional and rational methods of legal citation. Civilians of the "darker ages" established a mode of quotation described as absurd and incomprehensible, which was maintained through custom and authority. In referencing the Institutes, the Pandects, and the Code, these civilians would recite the first words of the title and provide the number of the law, but omit the number of the book. Because there are more than a thousand such titles, this was viewed as a "pendantic yoke." In contrast, a more rational method—adopted by Gibbon and followed by M Hugo—involves numbering the book, the title, and the law.

## Cultural and Religious Customs
Customary practices also extend to social and religious rites across different civilizations. In the northern parts of the Realm during the Sung Dynasty in China, it was customary to perform marriages after death for unmarried youths and girls. This process involved "match-makers for disembodied souls" who acquainted the families with one another and cast lots to determine if the union would be happy. If approved, wedding garments for the next world were created, and wine and fruit were set out at the lad's grave. The movement of small streamers placed near two seats served as a sign of whether the souls approached each other or disapproved of the marriage.

In the context of early Christianity, the religion adopted the practical methods of popular religions of its time almost insensibly. This included the adoption of characteristic garments and head-shaving for priests, mirroring the customs of Egyptian priests. Over time, these customary acquisitions buried the originally revolutionary teaching.
