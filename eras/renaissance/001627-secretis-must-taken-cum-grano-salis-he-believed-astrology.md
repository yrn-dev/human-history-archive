# Astrology

## Origins and Early Development
The history of astrology can be traced back to ancient Babylonia, beginning approximately 3000 B.C. In Babylonia and Assyria, astrology was part of the official cult and served as one of two primary methods used by priests, known as *bare* or "inspectors," to determine the intentions and will of the gods. The second method involved the inspection of a sacrificial animal's liver. While liver divination was based on the theory that the liver was the seat of the animal's soul, astrology was based on a theory of divine government of the world.

This early belief system grew from the observation that human happiness and life were dependent on heavenly phenomena, such as the sun and rain affecting soil fertility, or storms and inundations causing damage in the Euphratean Valley. Consequently, it was concluded that the great gods resided in the heavens. During the "nomadic" stage of this culture, the moon cult was even more prominent than sun worship.

## Expansion to the West
Around the middle of the 4th century B.C., Babylonian astrology began to spread westward into Roman and Greek culture, and particularly into Egypt. This expansion occurred simultaneously with the rise of a genuine scientific phase of astronomy within Babylonia itself, which may have weakened the local hold of astrology. Additionally, the arrival of the Persians and their higher order of religion acted as a disintegrating factor for the old faith in the Euphrates Valley. In the Greek and Roman worlds, "Chaldaean wisdom" became a synonym for divination via stars and planets, though the term "Chaldaean" often carried suspicions of deception or charlatanry.

## Greek and Egyptian Contributions
The Greeks and later Egyptians expanded both astronomy and astrology far beyond Babylonian limits. Until the 16th century, these two fields remained harmoniously combined. A significant Greek contribution was the development of the individual horoscope, which attempted to trace a person's fate based on the position of the stars and planets at the time of birth or conception. This system of genethliology was so perfected that later ages added few essential changes.

Some Greeks applied astrological explanations to the myths of Homer and Hesiod. For instance, the treatise *De Astrologiâ* suggests that figures like Æneas, Minôs, and Askalaphus were not the literal children of Aphroditê, Zeus, and Mars, but were instead men born under the favorable influences of those specific planets.

## Medieval and Early Modern Influence
The Greek system was adopted by Arab astronomers and incorporated into the Kabbalistic lore of Christians and the Bani Israel. This formed the basis of middle-age astrology, which divided the practice into "natural astrology" (the genuine science of heavenly motions) and "judicial astrology" (a pseudo-science).

In various courts and governments, astrology remained influential:
* **France:** The influence of the Medici made astrologers popular. Cardinal Richelieu utilized astrology as an engine of government, employing Jacques Gaffarel. At the birth of Louis XIV, Morin de Villefranche was used to cast a horoscope.
* **Political Conflict:** Charles V and Francis I both employed astrologers to fight their battles. In Italy, prophecies regarding the ruin of France were highly influential, affecting public funds and the Change in Rome. In 1536, the marquis of Saluces betrayed his country due to prophecies predicting the ultimate success of Charles V.
* **The East:** Marco Polo recorded that the Emperor's camp included astrologers among the attendants. He specifically mentioned "crafty enchanters and astrologers" from the nations of Tebet and Kesimur who claimed to prevent storms from passing over the Emperor's Palace through the help of God, though Polo attributed this to the Devil.

## Notable Predictions and Figures
Astrological predictions have historically ranged from the personal to the universal:
* **Universal Cataclysms:** In 1186, astrologers threatened a cataclysm that did not occur. In 1524, Stoffler predicted a universal deluge based on three planets meeting in the sign of Pisces; this year was instead marked by drought, though President Aurial of Toulouse built a Noah's ark in anticipation.
* **Personal Fate:** In the *Essay of Prophecies*, Bacon recounts a story from Dr. Pena regarding a queen mother in France who had her husband's nativity calculated under a false name. The astrologer predicted he would be killed in a duel; the king was later slain during a course at tilt when splinters from Mongomery's staff entered his bever.
* **Scientific Figures:** Johannes Kepler, who made almanacs in his youth and once correctly prophesied a hard winter, applied astrological interpretations to the disappearance of a brilliant star in 1572. Theodore Beza believed this same star predicted the second coming of Christ.
* **Francis Bacon:** Bacon believed in astrology, the philosopher's stone, and the doctrine of signatures. His own predictions in the *De Secretis* are noted as requiring a degree of skepticism (*cum grano salis*).
