# Ship of Fools

The *Ship of Fools* (originally *Narrenschiff*) is a work by Sebastian Brant that served as the starting point for a new genre of satirical literature. First appearing in 1494, the work was essentially German in its treatment and conception. It featured 133 types of fools, though the text primarily focused on the censure of vices and sins rather than mere follies.

## Literary Characteristics and Themes
The work represents a transition between morality and drama, as well as between narrative and allegory. Unlike the general allegorical satires of previous centuries, which relied on abstractions, the *Ship of Fools* utilizes concrete examples of folly. These include:
* The bibliophile who collects books without learning from them.
* The evil judge who accepts bribes to favor the guilty.
* The old fool whose folly is only strengthened by time.
* Individuals eager to follow fashions.
* Priests who spend their time in church telling "gestes" of Robin Hood.

The original 1494 edition included woodcuts, which were said to have been devised and perhaps partly executed by Brant himself. These illustrations introduced a sense of humor absent from the text and provided an impulse for the production of "emblems."

## European and English Adaptations
The work gained widespread popularity across Europe, aided by Jacob Locher's Latin elegiac version, *Stultifera Navis*, published in 1497. In England, the work was adapted by Alexander Barclay (c. 1476–1552), a British poet and doctor of divinity. Barclay's version, titled *The Shyp of Folys of the Worlde*, was first printed in 1509 by Richard Pynson.

Barclay translated the work from Dutch, French, and Latin, though he appeared most familiar with the Latin version. He exercised significant freedom in his translation, adding or removing material as he deemed necessary or superfluous. To ensure the moral lessons were driven home, Barclay added his own *envoi* at the end of nearly every section.

## Barclay's Satirical Contributions
In his translation, Barclay infused the work with local color and used it as a vehicle to satirize the social evils of his own time. While translating the work while serving as chaplain of the college of St Mary Ottery in Devonshire, he introduced his own neighbors into the satire. An example of this local application is found in the lines: "For if one can flatter, and beare a Hauke on his fist, He shall be parson of Honington or Cist."

## Related Works and Editions
The *Ship of Fools* and its various versions have been the subject of several scholarly editions and studies. These include T. H. Jamieson's 1874 edition, Zarncke's edition of Brant (1854), and Dr. Fedor Fraustadt's 1894 study on the relationship between Barclay's version and its Latin, French, and German sources. Additionally, Henry Watson produced a prose version of Locher's *Stultifera Navis*, which was printed by Wynkyn de Worde in 1518.
