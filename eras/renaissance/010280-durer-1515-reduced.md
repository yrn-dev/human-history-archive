# Albrecht Dürer (1515)

Albrecht Dürer (1471–1528) was a prominent German engraver, draughtsman, and painter born in Nuremberg. By the early 16th century, Dürer had established a reputation that extended across Europe, from Rome to Flanders. His career was marked by an ideal elevation and decorum, and he maintained friendships with the leading masters of his age, including Raphael, with whom he exchanged drawings.

## Works of 1515
In 1515, Dürer produced a book-plate for Lazarus Spengler. This work is an example of an *ex-libris*, a term that originated in France as a substantive for a book-plate. During the period preceding the 17th century, many book-owners preferred the more luxurious habit of stamping a personal device onto a book cover, which often rendered the use of labels superfluous.

## Artistic Mastery and Copper-Engraving
Dürer is recognized as one of the greatest minds to express itself through the medium of copper-engraving. Between 1513 and 1514, he produced three of his most famous masterpieces: "The Knight and Death" (also referred to by the artist simply as "The Knight"), "Melancolia," and "St Jerome in his Study." 

"The Knight and Death" combines the image of a pilgrim with the concept of the Christian knight from Erasmus's *Enchiridion militis Christiani*. In this work, Dürer solved the problem of accurately depicting the proportions and action of a horse in motion, utilizing an Italian example. His design closely repeats a drawing by Leonardo da Vinci of an equestrian statue of Francesco Sforza. Additionally, the rider's face recalls the statue of Bartolommeo Colleoni in Venice, while the armor was based on a drawing Dürer had signed and dated in 1498.

"Melancolia," which is numbered "1" as if part of a series, depicts a brooding winged genius surrounded by symbols and scientific instruments. It is generally recognized as an embodiment of the spirit of intellectual research.

## Woodcuts and Series
Dürer demonstrated an inexhaustible variety of graphic invention by repeating similar incidents across different media. He completed two woodcut series—the Life of the Virgin and the Great Passion (the latter begun around 1498–1499)—which were first published in 1511. In that same year, he released a second edition of the Apocalypse series and the "Little Passion" on wood, a popularly conceived series consisting of thirty-seven subjects. He followed this in 1512 with a set of fifteen small copper-engravings on the same "Little Passion" theme. Other notable single woodcuts from 1511 include "St Christopher," "St Jerome," and the "Mass of St Gregory."

## Scientific Contributions and Influence
Beyond the fine arts, Dürer engaged with the scientific principles of the era. In 1525, he became one of the first individuals to deal scientifically with the principles underlying the construction of printed gores used to cover globes. 

His influence on the art world was significant; artists across the Alps frequently borrowed his expressive types and striking combinations. He also engaged in a friendly rivalry with the Dutch engraver Lucas van Leyden. While Vasari asserted that Dürer was the superior designer, both were considered unsurpassed in their use of the graver.

## Personal Life and Legacy
Dürer was the second of eighteen children born to Albrecht Dürer the elder, a goldsmith, and Barbara. He maintained a deep affection for his parents, painting two portraits of his father (in 1490 and 1497). 

Dürer resided in his native Nuremberg from the spring of 1507 until the summer of 1520. He was highly honored in Germany by all social classes, including Emperor Maximilian. However, historical accounts provided by his intimate friend Willibald Pirkheimer suggest a difficult domestic life. Pirkheimer's letters describe Dürer's wife, Agnes Dürer, as an "incorrigible shrew and skinflint" who plagued her husband with meanness and pressured him to overwork for money.
