# Ballet

Ballet is a theatrical performance involving music, dancing, and pantomime. The term is derived from the Greek *ballizein* (to dance), passing through the Medieval Latin *ballare* and Italian *ballare* and *ballata* before becoming the French *ballet* and the Old English *ballette* (and subsequently *ballad*). According to Rousseau, in Old French, *ballet* signified "to dance, to sing, to rejoice." While the term *ballad* is now confined to singing, the modern acceptation of *ballet* refers to a theatrical representation where a story is told through gestures accompanied by music.

## Historical Development
The modern ballet is believed to have first been produced on a considerable scale in 1489 at Tortona, given by Bergonzio di Botta to celebrate the marriage of the Duke of Milan (Duke Galeazzo). These early performances were common amusements at European courts, often featuring five acts, each containing several *entrees*, which in turn contained several quadrilles. These productions frequently utilized movable scenery, sculpture, and painting, and were often performed at night.

The art form was developed and perfected in France, where it became closely associated with the history of the opera. Catherine de' Medici introduced allegorical, moral, and ludicrous ballets to France, spending large sums on performances to distract her son from state affairs. A significant milestone occurred in 1581 with the "Ballet Comique de la Reyne," composed by Baltasarini (also known as Beaujoyeulx), which was the first time music and dance were arranged to display coherent dramatic ideas.

The development of ballet is categorized into three distinct stages:
1. **The Court Period:** Ballet was found only at court for princely entertainments. High-ranking ladies performed, and monarchs such as Henry IV, Louis XIII, and Louis XIV performed various characters. Richelieu utilized the ballet for political expression. Lully, the first to make an art of ballet music composition, insisted on admitting women as dancers; previously, men dressed as women played feminine roles. This stage ended when Louis XIV became too fat to dance and court ballet lost popularity.
2. **The Academic Period:** Ballet was adopted in colleges for prize distributions and other occasions, frequently performing the works of Quinault and Lully.
3. **The Stage Period:** The final stage is marked by ballet's appearance on the theatrical stage, where it has remained.

## The Ballet d'Action and Jean Georges Noverre
The *ballet d'action*, or pantomime ballet, is the form in which a story is told exclusively by gesture. This is distinguished from a *divertissement*, which is a single scene, such as a village festival. 

Jean Georges Noverre is considered the father of ballet dancing as it is known today. He is credited with the invention of the *ballet d'action*. Noverre's central theory was that mere display was insufficient to ensure the life and interest of a ballet. He perfected the art of using pantomime to present a story and insisted that dancing must be a direct expression of a specific attitude of mind. It has been noted that the ballet has never advanced beyond the stage Noverre reached, and in some respects, has gone back.

## Notable Figures and Schools
Beyond Noverre, Gaetano Apolline Balthazare Vestris—who called himself *le dieu de la danse*—is regarded as the greatest ballet-master and the finest male dancer Europe ever produced. Vestris collaborated with Gluck on *Iphigenie en Aulide*. In 1750, Vestris performed in Paris in the ballet-opera *Leandre et Hero* alongside Madame Camargo, who is credited with introducing short skirts to the ballet.

In England, the word "balette" was first used by Dryden in 1667. The first descriptive ballet seen in London was *The Tavern Bilkers* in 1702 at Drury Lane. English ballet largely followed French developments and reached its "palmy days" in the first half of the 19th century. Famous dancers of this era included Carlotta Grisi, Fanny Elssler, Mdlle Taglioni, Mdlle Cerito, Mdlle Carolina Rosati, Miss Lucile Grahn, and Miss P. Horton. 

Later, Kate Vaughan introduced a new type of grace to England and popularized skirt-dancing. While a beautiful exhibition, skirt-dancing is viewed by some as a departure from true dancing because the steps are of little importance compared to the whirling of draperies. Vaughan's imitators included Miss Letty Lind and Miss Sylvia Grey, while Adeline Genee became the finest exponent of the older classical school in London.

## Modern Trends and Decadence
In later years, ballet has been affected by a trend in entertainment that prioritizes visual spectacle over intellectual appreciation. This has led to a form of ballet where the chief interest is independent of the dancing itself, often involving large numbers of women in elaborate costumes (such as military uniforms) who primarily march and group themselves by color and mass on stage. Sir Augustus Harris identified this shift toward mere display as a reason for the decadence of the modern ballet.
