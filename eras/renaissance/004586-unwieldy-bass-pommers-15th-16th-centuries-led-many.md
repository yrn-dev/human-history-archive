# The Evolution of the Bass Pommer and the Bassoon

The bass pommer was a straight woodwind instrument measuring 6 feet in length. During the 15th and 16th centuries, these instruments were considered unwieldy, which prompted various attempts to create a more practical bass for the orchestra. This led to the development of instruments that doubled back the long tube to reduce their proportions and increase portability.

## The Development of the Fagotto
The process of doubling the tube transformed the pommer into the fagotto (also known as the bassoon). The German and Italian names for the instrument derived from a perceived resemblance to a bundle of sticks, as the bassoon was the first instrument of its kind to be doubled back upon itself. In contrast, the English and French names refer to the instrument's pitch as the bass of the wood-wind.

There are conflicting accounts regarding the invention of the fagotto. One source ascribes the invention to Afranio, a canon of Ferrara, in a work by his nephew, Theseus Ambrosius Albonesius, published in Pavia in 1539. However, detailed descriptions and illustrations of Afranio's "phagotus" suggest it was not related to the bassoon. Afranio's instrument consisted of two twin columns with keys and finger-holes, played by a bellows or bag-pipe. Marin Mersenne noted that Afranio's design resembled two "bassons" interconnected by tubes and classified it with the Neapolitan *sourdeline*, a complex type of musette.

## The Curtail and Double Curtail
Another path in the evolution of the pommer involved the curtail (also known as the *dulceian*, *dolcian*, *doucaine*, *curtoll*, or in France, the *courtaud*). To make the tenor pommer in C more portable, it was constructed from a block of wood with a diameter more than double that of the original pommer. Two bores were cut into the wood, communicating at a flat bottom, with the bell and double reed mouthpiece located side by side at the top. This instrument featured two keys and seven holes (six in front and one in the back).

The subsequent evolution of this design was the double curtail. This was a converted bass pommer pitched an octave below the single curtail, making it identical in construction and pitch to the early fagotto in C. A drawing from before 1688 in the manuscript *The Academy of Armoury* by Randle Holme describes the double curtail as being double the size of the single version and played eight notes deeper.

## Related Instruments and Terminology
The pommer family included several variations:
* **The Bombard:** A medieval musical instrument (also called *bumhart*, *pumhart*, or *pommer*) that served as the forerunner to the schalmey or bass oboe. The Italian term for the contrabass pommer, *bombardone*, is the origin of the name "bombardon" (bass tuba).
* **The Cor Anglais:** This instrument developed from the alto pommer (or *haute-contre de hautbois*), which was improved with key-work, likely during the 17th century.
* **The Pedalle:** A 17th-century French manuscript refers to the *pedalle* as the bass pommer or bass oboe.

## Regional Variations and Makers
In Germany, the *Deutscher Basson* (also called *Fagotte* or *Bombardi*) was distinguished from the modern bassoon. According to Eisel in 1738, these instruments—which referred to the bombard or bass pommer—were no longer in use. This suggests that the modern bassoon may not have been of German origin.

Sigmund Schnitzer, a craftsman in Nuremberg, was renowned for making *fagotte* of extraordinary size and pure intonation. While some retrospectives suggested he might have been the first to double the tube of the bass pommer, contemporary records from 1549 by Johann Neudorfer describe him simply as a *Pfeifenmacher und Stadtpfeifer* (pipe maker and town piper), providing no evidence that he was an inventor of a new instrument form.
