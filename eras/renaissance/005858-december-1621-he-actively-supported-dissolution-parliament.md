# The Political Activities of Buckingham (1621–1623)

## Parliamentary Conflict and Dissolution
In 1621, the English parliament convened in a state of anger upon discovering that no assistance was being provided to the Palatinate. This tension manifested in a loud outcry against the system of monopolies, a system from which Buckingham's dependants and brothers had profited. While it was believed these profits were greater than they actually were, Buckingham initially pleaded for the dissolution of the parliament.

However, following the advice of Bishop Williams, Buckingham shifted his strategy to place himself at the head of the movement. During a conference between the Lords and the Commons, he acknowledged the implication of two of his brothers in the monopoly system, while stating that his father had begotten a third brother who would assist in their punishment.

During the subsequent impeachment of Bacon, Buckingham provided the assistance possible without risking his own influence or position, as he owed much to Bacon's counsels. Although he initially demanded the immediate dissolution of parliament, he eventually joined the attack on the chancellor as the public outcry intensified, though he attempted to mitigate the severity of the charges during the hearing before the House of Lords. Despite this, Buckingham utilized Bacon's need for help to acquire possession of York House. By December 1621, Buckingham actively supported the dissolution of parliament.

## Relations with Spain and the Prince of Wales
During the winter of 1621 and the following year, Buckingham was entirely under the influence of Gondomar. This influence was so significant that in May 1622, Laud had to argue with difficulty to prevent Buckingham from declaring himself a Roman Catholic. 

By May 1622, when the Spanish ambassador departed England, an understanding had been reached with Buckingham regarding a visit to Madrid by the prince of Wales the following year. The Spanish court hoped to secure the prince's conversion to the Roman Catholic Church before allowing him to marry the infanta Maria.

On February 17, 1623, Charles and Buckingham embarked on an expedition, passing through Paris and arriving in Madrid on March 7. The mission was characterized by mutual deception: Charles and Buckingham hoped for the restitution of the Palatinate to the son-in-law of James as a marriage gift for Charles, while the Spaniards sought extreme concessions and the conversion of Charles to Roman Catholicism.

## Break with Spain
The mission to Madrid eventually failed as personal disputes arose between Buckingham and the grandees, specifically Olivares. These personal conflicts accentuated existing political differences. When Buckingham and Charles sailed from Santander in September, they did so with a final resolution to break entirely with Spain.

## Associated Diplomatic Figures
During this era, other diplomats operated in the sphere of English-Spanish relations. John Digby, the 1st Earl of Bristol, had previously been sent to Spain to negotiate a marriage between the infanta Anne and Prince Henry, and later to effect a union between Charles and the infanta Maria. Digby had favored a political treaty over a matrimonial one and preferred a Protestant marriage. After the departure of Charles and Buckingham in March 1623, Digby's influence was wrecked. He later incurred Buckingham's "implacable jealousy" by writing to James to inform him that Buckingham was unpopular among Spanish ministers.

Additionally, Dudley Carleton, Viscount Dorchester, served as an ambassador to Holland and later returned to England in 1625 with the duke of Buckingham. Carleton acted as a defender of Buckingham and supported the king's violent exercise of his prerogative when parliament attacked Buckingham in 1626.
