# The Curtoll and Related Wind Instruments

The curtoll (also referred to as the *curtail*, *curtoll*, *dulceian*, *dolcian*, *doucaine*, or in France as the *courtaud*) is a wind instrument that appeared in historical records as early as 1582, where it was described as part of "the common bleting musick of ye Drone, Hobius (Hautboy) and Curtoll."

## Design and Evolution
The curtoll was developed to increase the portability of the tenor pommer in C by shortening its unwieldy proportions. To achieve this, the instrument was constructed from a block of wood with a diameter more than double that of a pommer. Two bores were cut into this block, which communicated at a flat bottom. At the top of the instrument, the bell and the crook—which contained the double reed mouthpiece—were positioned side by side. The curtoll featured two keys, one hole at the back, and six holes in the front.

The evolution of the instrument led to the creation of the double curtail. This version was a converted bass pommer pitched an octave below the single curtail, making it identical in construction and pitch to the early fagotto in C. A description from a manuscript of *The Academy of Armoury* by Randle Holme, written before 1688, describes the double curtail as being "double the bigness of the single" and played eight notes deeper. It is described as consisting of two pipes fixed within one thick bass pipe, with one pipe being significantly longer than the other; a crooked brass pipe containing a reed extends from the top of the lower pipe.

## Related Instruments and the Pommer Family
The curtoll is part of a broader history of wind instruments including the pommer and the bassoon. Eisel, writing in 1738, distinguished the *Basson* (modern bassoon) from the *Deutscher Basson* (also called *Fagotte* or *Bombardi*), which were the instruments used by German ancestors before the adoption of French and Italian styles. These earlier instruments, such as the bombard or bass pommer, were extraordinarily long and are no longer in use.

Other members of the hautbois family include the *haute-contre de hautbois* (the cor anglais), which is a gradually improved alto Pommer provided with key-work. In a 17th-century French manuscript, an "accord de hautbois" is listed, comprising a *pedalle* (bass oboe or Pommer), a *taille* (tenor), and a *haute-contre*. By the end of the 17th century, the *Grande Ecurie du Roi* employed two players of the *haute-contre de hautbois*.

## Comparison with Bag-pipes and Drones
The 1582 mention of the curtoll alongside the "Drone" and "Hobius" links it to the tradition of instruments utilizing a drone ground bass. The bag-pipe is characterized by this continuous sound, produced by beating-reeds fixed in sockets. These instruments are generally divided into two classes based on inflation: those using a blow-pipe and those using a small bellows.

Various regional drones and bag-pipes existed during the periods associated with the curtoll:
*   **France:** The *cornemuse* and *chalemie* were used before the *musette*. The shepherd's *cornemuse* featured a large drone (*gros bourdon*) and a small drone (*petit bourdon*), both tuned to C. Another variety was played with the *Hautbois de Poitou*.
*   **Germany:** The *Sackpfeife* or *Dudelsack* existed in five sizes, including the *Grosser Bock* (double-bass), the *Bock* (pitched in C), the *Schaeferpfeife*, the *Huemmelchen*, and the *Dudey* (treble).
*   **Ireland:** The union pipes of the 18th century were blown by bellows and featured drones tuned to A.
