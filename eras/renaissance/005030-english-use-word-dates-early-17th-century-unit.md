# The 17th Century in English Language, Culture, and Organization

The 17th century represents a period of significant transition in the English language, the evolution of military structures, and the development of material culture and social roles.

## Linguistic Evolution and the English Language
The 17th century is characterized as a transitional period for the English language. According to chronological nomenclature, the era between 1611 and 1689 is defined as "Transitional Modern" or "17th Century English." This period followed "Early Modern English" (Tudor English), which lasted from 1450 to 1611. 

The year 1611 serves as a critical marker, coinciding with the appearance of the Authorized Version of the Bible and the end of Shakespeare's literary work. At this point, the language became modern in its structure, style, and expression. However, literary spelling did not settle into present usage until approximately the revolution of 1688. The period between 1611 and 1689 is described as a "Seventeenth-Century Transition," during which the literary vocabulary was sifted and spelling was gradually fixed. 

The year 1688 also marked the disappearance of many words from literature, particularly those derived from Latin during the 16th and 17th centuries. While those that survived 1688 remain in use, many others vanished. During this broader era of modernization, the English vocabulary became more composite, adopting terminology from Greek and Latin to support the development of physical sciences, as well as words from modern European languages such as French, Italian, Portuguese, and Dutch.

## Military Organization and Terminology
The 17th century saw the introduction of specific military terminology into the English language. The use of the word "brigade" in English dates from the early 17th century. A brigade is a unit of military organization composed of two or more regiments of artillery, cavalry, or infantry. It is commanded by a colonel, brigadier-general, or major-general. 

In the British system, an infantry brigade typically consists of four battalions (roughly 4000 bayonets) with attached medical, transport, and supply units. A cavalry brigade consists of two or three regiments. In contrast, an artillery brigade in Great Britain is a smaller unit commanded by a lieutenant-colonel and consisting of two or three batteries. The staff of a cavalry or infantry brigade generally includes the brigade-major, the brigadier commanding, and his aide-de-camp.

Outside of England, in France, the 17th and 18th centuries saw the use of the "ban" (vassals called out by the king) and the "arriere-ban" (sub-vassals called upon by vassals). These units were noted for a lack of discipline when called out and were last summoned in 1758.

## Social Roles and Legal Terms
Various social and legal terms evolved or were applied during this century. The word "groom," which in 16th and 17th century literature frequently appeared in pastorals to describe a shepherd lover, later evolved into the modern meaning of a male servant attached to stables. During the 16th and 17th centuries, the "groom-porter" was a royal household official who prepared the sovereign's apartment and acted as the authority on gaming and the provider of dice and cards within the court.

In the legal sphere, the term "ignoramus" (Latin for "we do not know") was an English law term used by a grand jury to "throw out" a bill of indictment. While the term is now obsolete, its application to describe an ignorant person dates from the early 17th century. This connection to the law is illustrated by a 1615 Latin play titled *Ignoramus* by George Ruggle, which satirized the pettifogging and ignorance of common lawyers.

Additionally, the use of the word "bull" to describe a verbal blunder involving a contradiction in terms began in the 17th century. Early quotations indicate that during this time, no specific country was credited with these blunders, though modern associations sometimes link them to the Irish.
