# English Book-plates and the Example of Sir Thomas Tresham

## Overview of English Book-plates
The study of the English book-plate across various styles, spanning from the late Elizabethan era to the late Victorian period, provides a faithful reflection of the prevailing tastes in decorative art throughout different epochs. While a hand-painted armorial device attached to a folio once belonging to Henry VIII exists in the British Museum's King's library, it is not considered a book-plate in the modern sense. The earliest discovered example of a gift-plate is attributed to Sir Nicholas Bacon.

## Early Examples and Characteristics
Following the example of Sir Nicholas Bacon, the next known book-plate is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, which is dated 1585. During this early period, and continuing until the last quarter of the 17th century, the number of authentic English plates remained very limited. 

The composition of these early plates was characterized by a remarkable simplicity, lacking the elaborateness found in German examples. These plates were generally plain and armorial in nature. Decorative elements were typically restricted to a symmetrical arrangement of mantling, though wreaths or palms were occasionally displayed.

## Post-Restoration Evolution
Following the Restoration, book-plates became an established accessory for most well-ordered libraries. While they maintained the heraldic simplicity of the previous age, their overall "physiognomy" changed significantly. Two primary characteristics defined this new era:

*   **Tincture Markings:** Plates began to invariably display dots and tincture lines. This method was originally devised in the middle of the century by Petra Sancta, the author of *Tesserae Gentilitiae*, and eventually became adopted across Europe.
*   **Elaborate Mantling:** The mantling surrounding the face of the shield became much more elaborate, bearing a strong resemblance to the periwigs of the period.

## French Influence on Decoration
The elaborate style of the post-Restoration period was undoubtedly imported from France. Although it developed its own specific character within England, English modes of decoration for book-plates—and most other chattels—continued to follow the ruling French taste with a delay of some years. This trend persisted from the Restoration until the dawn of the French Revolution.
