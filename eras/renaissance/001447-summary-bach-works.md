# Summary of Bach's Works

Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750) was a German musical composer whose importance in the history of music is described as impossible to exaggerate. While his art was neglected by his younger contemporaries as "crabbed" and "old-fashioned," his work was later rediscovered, profoundly influencing the history of music and the "Romantic" movement.

## Compositional Periods and Style
Bach's career is divided into several distinct periods: Arnstadt-Muehlhausen, Weimar, Coethen, and a final period spanning 1733 to 1750. The Coethen period (1717–1723) is specifically noted for its focus on instrumental music, while its choral music from this time is characterized as "easy-going." 

In his final period, Bach's choral works became more numerous and terse, as seen in *Jesu, der du meine Seele*. During this same time, his instrumental music showed an increasing preference for large-scale designs; this shift is evident when comparing the first book of the *Wohltemperirtes Klavier* (1722) with the second book (1744). Bach was also known for constantly rearranging his own compositions, demonstrating a unique power to reproduce perfect works in different media and adapt music to different words.

## Church Music
Bach's output of church music is extensive, particularly those works written with orchestra. This includes:
* **Church Cantatas:** 190 cantatas, some of which are known only from fragmentary sets of parts. Of these, 40 were written for solo voices and approximately 60 were based more or less on chorales. The remainder are practically short oratorios, a title Bach frequently used himself.
* **Other Choral Works:** Three wedding cantatas, the Easter oratorio, and the Christmas oratorio, the latter consisting of six similar cantatas designed for performance over six separate days.

Regarding his *Passion according to St John*, Bach utilized the poem of Brockes as the groundwork for the non-scriptural numbers, transforming it with literary skill.

## Instrumental Works
The Coethen period served as the era for Bach's central instrumental compositions. Key works from this time include:
* The first book of the *Wohltemperirtes Klavier*.
* The Brandenburg concertos.
* The French and English suites.
* Solo sonatas for the violin and violoncello.

Later in his career, following a visit to Frederick the Great at Potsdam in 1747, Bach wrote *Das musikalische Opfer* based on a theme provided by the king.

## Publication and Rediscovery
During his lifetime, Bach engraved most of his instrumental collections himself, which were almost the only works of his published. The only church cantata published during his life was *Gott ist mein Koenig*, written when he was nineteen.

Following his death from apoplexy on July 28, 1750, his manuscript works were divided among his sons, and many were lost. For nearly a century, his work was largely unknown. His rediscovery is closely linked to Mendelssohn, as well as the efforts of Schumann, Wesley, and Crotch. 

In 1850, a society was established for the correct publication of his remaining works. The *Bach-Gesellschaft* spent over fifty years publishing approximately four-fifths of his choral works. Wilhelm Rust, the editor of the *Bach-Gesellschaft* for fourteen years, is noted for his unrivaled insight into Bach's style and his ability to restore priceless harmonies from incomplete texts.

## Family and Influences
Bach came from a family of musical importance spanning nearly two hundred years. The family founder, Veit Bach, was a miller and baker. The first professional musician in the family was Hans Bach ("der Spielmann"). Sebastian Bach particularly admired his ancestor Johann Ludwig, copying twelve of his church cantatas. 

Bach also held a deep admiration for Handel, though the two never met. Bach made a manuscript copy of Handel's *Passion nach Brockes*. Additionally, Bach's second wife, a soprano, inspired many of his most inspired arias and assisted him significantly with his work, producing copies of his music that are often difficult to distinguish from his own autographs.
