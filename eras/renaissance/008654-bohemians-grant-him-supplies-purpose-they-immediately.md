# Bohemia: Political and Religious History

The history of Bohemia is characterized by long-standing tensions between the monarchy—particularly the house of Habsburg—and the Bohemian estates over matters of religious freedom, national autonomy, and the administration of the state.

## Early Monarchy and Constitutional Development
In the early medieval period, Bohemian rulers sought formal recognition from the Empire. Vratislav obtained the title of king in 1088 from Emperor Henry IV. Later, in 1156, Emperor Frederick I. Barbarossa conferred the title of king upon Prince Vladislav II in exchange for his participation in Italian campaigns.

By the early 16th century, the Bohemian estates played a significant role in governance. Following the death of King Vladislav in 1516, his son Louis succeeded him. Although Sigismund of Poland and Emperor Maximilian I acted as guardians for the young king, the Bohemian estates refused to allow them any right of interference in internal affairs. During this period, the country was ruled almost without control by great nobles, specifically the supreme burgrave, Zdenek Leo, lord of Rozmital. A pivotal constitutional moment occurred at the diet of St Wenceslas in 1517, where the three estates agreed to live together in friendly intercourse, with the other estates recognizing the municipal autonomy of the citizens.

## The Hussite Legacy and the Compacts
The religious landscape of Bohemia was deeply shaped by the Hussites. After the defeat of the Germans at Domazlice on August 1, 1431, negotiations began at Basel. Internal conflicts eventually split the Hussites into the "army of the nobles" (aristocratic Hussites) and the "army of the towns" (Taborites). The Taborites were defeated at Lipan on May 30, 1434, leading to a reconciliation with the Church of Rome and Sigismund. 

This reconciliation resulted in the "Compacts," which granted Bohemians the right to preach the gospel freely and receive communion in both kinds, while limiting the clergy's ability to acquire worldly goods. While these enactments provided freedom of worship to moderate Utraquists, they were later used by Ferdinand I as a tool to oppress advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian Brethren.

## The Reign of Maximilian and Rudolph
Maximilian succeeded his father as king of Bohemia without opposition. Initially, his education under Protestant tutors and his sympathy for church reform secured him support from Bohemian reformers. However, his reign was marked by indifference and religious scepticism. He published decrees against the Bohemian Brethren but generally refused to sanction measures against Protestants, despite pressure from the Jesuits.

In 1567, Maximilian visited Bohemia for the first time since his accession. When he requested supplies from the Bohemians to fund warfare against the Turks, the estates responded by demanding religious concessions, specifically requesting that the Compacts cease to be part of the country's laws. In 1575, the estates further demanded the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg." When Maximilian refused, the majority of the diet—comprising advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian brotherhood—created the *Confessio Bohemica*. This document aligned with Calvin regarding the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. Maximilian gave verbal approval but refused to make it public or include it in the kingdom's charters.

Maximilian was succeeded by his son Rudolph. Though educated in Spain, Rudolph exhibited apathy toward religious controversies and matters of state, compounded by fits of melancholia and periods of insanity. Despite his mental decline, he was a patron of the arts who embellished Prague. By 1600, his impaired mental state led his brother Matthias and Pope Clement VIII to urge him to appoint a successor.

## Religious Conflict and the 1609 Diet
By 1609, tensions peaked during a diet that met on January 20. The estates had previously drafted twenty-five "Articles" demanding that the *Confessio Bohemica* be recognized as a fundamental law and that complete religious liberty be granted to all classes. They sought equal rights for Roman Catholics and Protestants (a term then encompassing Lutherans, Utraquists, and Bohemian Brethren) to hold state offices, and sought to limit the Jesuits' ability to acquire land. Rudolph demanded the destruction of this document, but it was held by Budova, who refused to surrender it. On March 31, Rudolph ultimately stated he could grant no religious concessions.

## National Identity and the Language Question
Bohemian national identity was also tied to language. Early genuine documents include legends of St Dorothy and St Catherine, and the rhymed chronicle (wrongly called the chronicle of Dalimil), which expresses strong racial antipathy toward Germans.

In the 19th century, the "language question" became a central political conflict. In 1897, Count Badeni issued a decree requiring government officials in Bohemia to know both Bohemian and German. This caused violent opposition from German inhabitants of Austria and led to the fall of Badeni's cabinet. Subsequent governments repealed these equal-rights decrees, leading to troubles in Prague that were severely repressed by Austrian authorities.

In 1871, a ministry led by Count Hohenwart attempted to satisfy Federalist demands. A royal message recognized the separate existence of the Bohemian kingdom and promised the emperor would be crowned king at Prague. However, this sparked resistance from Germans in Upper Austria, Moravia, and Carinthia. Ultimately, the emperor decided the Bohemian demands could not be accepted, insisting that the Czechs consider constitutional revisions in Vienna.
