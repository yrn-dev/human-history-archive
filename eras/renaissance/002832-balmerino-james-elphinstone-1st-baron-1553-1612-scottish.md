# BALMERINO, JAMES ELPHINSTONE, 1st BARON

James Elphinstone, 1st Baron Balmerino (c. 1553–1612), was a Scottish politician who rose to prominence during the reign of James VI. He was the third son of Robert, 3rd Lord Elphinstone, who died in 1602.

## Political Career and Rise to Power
Under James VI, Elphinstone ascended to significant positions of authority, serving as a royal secretary and a judge. In 1603, he accompanied the king to London. His elevation to the peerage occurred in 1604, when he was created Lord Balmerino (also referred to as Balmerinoch). His professional ascent continued into 1605, when he was appointed president of the court of session.

## Religious Conflict and Downfall
Elphinstone's political career was eventually undone by his devotion to the Roman Catholic religion. In 1599, acting on behalf of the king but without his actual knowledge, Elphinstone sent a letter to Pope Clement VIII. This correspondence was written in very cordial terms. When a copy of the letter was seen by Queen Elizabeth of England, she demanded an explanation from James. In response, both the king and Elphinstone claimed the letter was a forgery, and the matter remained dormant for several years.

The issue resurfaced in 1608 during a controversy between James and Cardinal Bellarmine. Under fresh interrogation, Balmerino confessed to writing the compromising letter. He admitted that he had surreptitiously obtained the signature of the king and had subsequently added the full titles of the pope to the document.

## Trial and Death
Following his confession, Balmerino was tried in March 1609. He was attainted and sentenced to death. However, after a period of brief imprisonment, he was released. He died at Balmerino in July 1612.

## Successors and Family Legacy
The title of Lord Balmerino was permitted to pass to James's elder son, John (d. 1649), in 1613. John became a prominent opponent of Charles I in Scotland and a chief among the Covenanters. He served as a trusted counsellor to the marquess of Argyll and presided over the Edinburgh parliament in August 1641. Despite being sentenced to death for "leasing-making" in 1634, he was pardoned due to popular sympathy and the intercession of others, including the poet Drummond of Hawthornden.

The title then passed to John's son, also named John (1623–1704), the 3rd lord, who in 1669 inherited the title of Lord Coupar from his uncle James. He was succeeded by his son, John, 4th Lord Balmerino (1652–1736), a reputable lawyer and representative peer in 1710 and 1713 who opposed the Union.

The lineage concluded with Arthur (1688–1746), who became the 6th Lord Balmerino upon the death of his half-brother John in January 1746. Arthur is noted for his role as a Jacobite; he joined the partisans of James Edward (the Old Pretender) after the battle of Sheriffmuir in November 1713 and lived in exile until receiving a pardon in 1733. In 1745, he joined Charles Edward, marching to Derby and fighting at Falkirk. Following his capture at Culloden, he was tried for treason in Westminster Hall and found guilty. He was beheaded on August 11, 1746. Because he died without issue, the titles of the family became extinct.
