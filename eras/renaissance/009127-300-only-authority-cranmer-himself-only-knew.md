# Thomas Cranmer and the English Reformation

## Role in Authorized Scripture
Archbishop Cranmer was a central figure in the effort to provide the English people with authorized translations of the Holy Scripture. In a letter to Cromwell dated August 4, 1537, Cranmer expressed impatience regarding the progress of an authorized version, remarking that a better translation set forth by the bishops might not occur "till a day after doomsday." This letter referred to a project that had been in preparation for several years.

The result of these efforts was *The Great Bible*, a splendid folio volume. The printing of this Bible began in Paris in 1538, entrusted to the printer Francis Regnault by a licence granted by Francis I at the request of Henry VIII. Despite being interdicted by the Inquisitor-general for France on December 17, 1538, and the seizure of the impression, Coverdale and Grafton managed to rescue many finished sheets and move the operation to London. The work was completed in April 1539. The title-page of *The Great Bible* depicts Henry VIII giving the "Word of God" to Cromwell and Cranmer, who then distribute it to clerics and laymen. 

Following this, a second edition was issued in 1540, known as *Cranmer's Bible* because it included a long Preface written by the Archbishop.

## Theological Positions and Influence
Cranmer is identified as one of the more conservative theologians in England when compared to the extreme reforming party. He, along with Ridley, Bucer, Peter Martyr, and Latimer, opposed the efforts of Hooper to spread a specific form of religion in England. Hooper, whose Zwinglian views placed him further beyond the pale than Cranmer, approved of the *Consensus Tigurinus* (negotiated in 1549 between Swiss Calvinists and Zwinglians) and sought to reduce the limits of the episcopacy.

Cranmer's intellectual legacy extended to the education of others. George Cranmer, the archbishop's grand-nephew, was a favorite pupil of the writer Richard Hooker during Hooker's time as a tutor at Corpus Christi College, Oxford.

## Contributions to the Book of Homilies
Cranmer contributed to the *Books of Homilies*, which were designed to stop errors being spread by "ignorant preachers." The first volume originated from a 1542 convocation and was published in 1547 under the authority of the King's Majesty. Of the twelve homilies in this first book, the first four are probably attributed to Cranmer.

## Other Historical Records
In the *Letters and Papers of Henry VIII* (vi. p. 300), Cranmer is cited as the only authority on a specific matter, noting that he himself only became aware of it a fortnight later. This record is associated with a marriage commonly antedated to November 14, 1532.
