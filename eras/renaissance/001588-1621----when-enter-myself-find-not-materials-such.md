# 1621: Legal and Political Events

The year 1621 is associated with several distinct historical figures and legal developments across Europe, ranging from the political downfall of Spanish officials to the personal defenses of individuals facing royal or parliamentary censure.

## The Case of the Unnamed Petitioner
In a letter addressed to the king on March 25, 1621, an individual facing a "tempest" of accusations defended his character and conduct. He asserted that he had never been the author of "immoderate counsel," but had instead always sought to have matters carried out via *suavibus modis*. In his defense, he denied being a "haughty or intolerable or hateful man" in his carriage or conversation, nor an "avaricious oppressor of the people." 

Regarding charges of gifts and briberies, the writer expressed hope that upon the opening of the "book of hearts," he would not be found to possess a "corrupt heart in a depraved habit of taking rewards to pervert justice," though he admitted to being frail and partaking in the "abuse of the times." Despite these defenses, the individual later acknowledged that, having understood the particulars of the charge—though not formally from the House—he found the matter sufficient to move him to "desert the defence" and to move the lords to "condemn and censure" him.

## Rodrigo Calderón
Rodrigo Calderón, the Count of Oliva and Marques de las Siete Iglesias, died in 1621. Born in Antwerp to Francisco Calderón and Maria Sandelin, Rodrigo was said by some to have been born out of wedlock. He rose to power as the trusted agent of the duke of Lerma, who held unbounded influence over King Philip III. 

Calderón's career was marked by greed, audacity, and insolence, leading him to be viewed as an "insolent upstart" by enemies of Lerma. He attained several titles, including knight of Santiago and *secretario de cámara* to the king. His influence waned in 1611 when he was removed from the secretaryship through the influence of Queen Margarita, who had been worked upon by Mariana de San José (a prioress) and Juan de Santa Mariá (a Franciscan). 

Following the death of Queen Margarita in 1614, Calderón faced accusations of using witchcraft against her, and it became known that he had ordered the murder of Francisco de Juaras. When the duke of Lerma was driven from court in 1618 by the king's confessor, Aliaga, and the duke of Uceda, Calderón became an "expiatory victim" to satisfy public clamour, resulting in his arrest and despoliation.

## Literary Developments
The period around 1621 also saw the publication of works by the Spanish dramatist Guillen de Castro (1569–1631). Between 1618 and 1621, Castro published his *Comedias*, dedicating the first volume to the daughter of Lope de Vega. Castro, a Valencian who had served as a captain of the coast-guard and governor of Scigliano, was known for *Las Mocedades del Cid*, a work that provided materials for a tragedy by Corneille. His work *El Prodigio de los Montes* is also cited as the source for Calderón's *El Magico prodigioso*.
