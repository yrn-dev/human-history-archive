# Barbarossa and the Conflict Between Islam and Christendom

## The Barbarossa Family
The name "Barbarossa," meaning "Redbeard" (from the Latin *barba*), was applied by Christians to a family of 16th-century Turkish sea rovers and admirals. This family was founded by Yakub, a Roumeliot likely of Albanian descent who settled in Mitylene following the Turkish conquest. Yakub had four sons born after 1482: Elias, Isaak, Arouj, and Khizr. While Khizr became a potter and Isaak a trader, Elias and Arouj pursued sea roving.

During an engagement with a galley of the Knights of Saint John based at Rhodes, Elias was killed and Arouj was captured. After being ransomed by a Turkish pasha, Arouj returned to the sea. He initially served the Mamelukes in Egypt but later moved to Tunis during the conflict between the Mamelukes and Sultan Selim I. In northern Africa, Arouj found employment as a mercenary amidst the conflicts of Berber princes, alternating this work with piratical raids against Christian trade.

## Rise of Khair-ed-Din and Arouj
Arouj was eventually joined in Tunis by his brother Khizr, who adopted or was given the name Khair-ed-Din. Isaak also joined his brothers. Together, Arouj and Khair-ed-Din collaborated with exiled Moors from Granada to conduct raids on the coast of Spain. They furthered their ambitions by fighting, murdering, or supplanting native African princes, eventually establishing their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes. In 1512, they attempted to capture Bougie from the Spaniards but were defeated.

Regarding the origin of the name "Barbarossa," some historians, such as Captain Walsin Esterhazy, suggested it was a mispronunciation of "Baba Arouj." However, contemporaries did not apply the prefix "Baba" to Arouj. An Arab chronicle published in 1837 explicitly states that "Barbarossa" was the name Christians used to refer to Khair-ed-Din.

## Historical Context of Religious Conflict
The activities of the Barbarossa family occurred within a long history of tension between Islam and Christendom. While there had been a period of relative truce and "border-bickering" following the decline of the Omayyads, the struggle became a "bitter fanatical struggle" again in the eleventh century. This revival of vigor in Islam was driven by the Seljuks, who poured out of Turkestan to conquer Asia Minor and the empire of Bagdad. These Seljuks, described as Moslems of a primitive type, redirected the Moslem world toward religious war against Christendom.

Earlier, in the ninth and tenth centuries, the Mediterranean was a site of significant instability. Saracen raiders and pirates from North Africa raided the Greek Islands and South Italy almost unchallenged due to the weakness of Western Christendom and the Byzantine Empire. Additionally, the Egyptian Fatimites—Shiite Caliphs claiming descent from Ali and Fatima—were noted as fanatics who embittered relations between Islam and Christianity by taking Jerusalem and interfering with Christian access to the Holy Sepulchre.

## Christianity and Expansion
Parallel to the rise of Islamic power, Christianity was often used as a unifying force by aggressive rulers to cement conquests. In north-western Europe during the ninth century, the region became an arena of conflict between the "old faith and the new." Charlemagne (Charles the Great), who became sole king of the Franks from 771 to 814, conducted wars of aggression that were definitely religious in nature. In this period, whole nations were converted to Christianity by the sword, similar to how Islam had converted nations in Africa, Central Asia, and Arabia a century or so prior.

## Cultural and Religious Interactions
Despite the conflicts, there were periods of intellectual exchange. Nestorian Christians, located east of orthodoxy, were highly educated and preserved Hellenic medical science and the works of Aristotle in Greek and Syrian translations. They were tolerated by Islam until the Turks gained ascendancy in the eleventh century. During the Omayyad era, many physicians in the Caliph's dominions were Nestorians, and some learned Nestorians professed Islam without significant changes to their thoughts or work.

However, a deep-seated difference in religious feelings persisted between Europe and Asia. While Buddhism, Christianity, and Islam all originated in Asia, Islam remained clearly repugnant to Europeans. This is evidenced by the fact that Europeans under Moslem rule, such as those in Turkey, refused to accept the faith in higher proportions than Hindus did under similar circumstances.
