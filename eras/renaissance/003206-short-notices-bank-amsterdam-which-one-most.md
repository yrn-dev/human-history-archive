# Bank of Amsterdam

The Bank of Amsterdam, originally known as the *Amsterdamsche Wisselbank* (exchange bank), was a financial institution established by an ordinance of the city of Amsterdam on January 31, 1609. Its creation was driven by the increased commerce of Holland, which had positioned Amsterdam as a leading city in international dealings.

## Nature and Purpose of Exchange Banks
The Bank of Amsterdam belonged to a class of institutions known as "exchange banks," which differed from "banks of deposits." While banks of deposit received deposits and made loans to associate themselves with general industries and trade, exchange banks were established specifically to facilitate trade with other countries and deal with foreign exchange.

The primary objective of these institutions was to remedy the inconveniences merchants faced due to the uncertain value of foreign currencies relative to the local currency. By providing a common standard of value, exchange banks created a "local manufacture" of international coinage. This allowed merchants to avoid the delays and expenses associated with verifying the weight, purity of metal, and intrinsic value of various coins. The bank turned the values entrusted to it into "bank money" (or "current money"), a currency accepted immediately by merchants without the need for testing the bullion or coin.

## Operations and Regulations
The Bank of Amsterdam operated without its own capital. It was understood that the bank held in its vaults the entire amount of specie for which "bank money" was outstanding. Any person could bring bullion or money for deposit and withdraw the money or the equivalent worth of the bullion at their pleasure.

To ensure the efficiency of this system, the establishing ordinance required that all bills of 600 gulden (£50) or more be paid through the bank via the transfer of credits or deposits. In 1643, this limit was lowered to 300 gulden (£25). These transfers became known as "bank money." The bank's sole source of income was the small charge it levied for making these transfers. Customers found this charge to be a worthwhile expense in exchange for the advantages gained by using the bank's currency.

## Decline and Dissolution
Although the bank was intended to maintain full specie reserves, this regulation was not strictly observed. At various dates, the bank provided loans to the Dutch East India Company. By 1795, a report revealed that the city of Amsterdam was heavily indebted to the bank. As security for this debt, the bank held obligations of the states of West Friesland and Holland.

While the debt was eventually paid, the bank could not be revived. The institution, which had held a leading position in European commerce for generations, ceased to exist in 1820.
