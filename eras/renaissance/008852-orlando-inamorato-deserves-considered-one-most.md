# Orlando Inamorato

The *Orlando Inamorato* is a significant work of Italian literature composed by Boiardo. It is recognized as the first example of the romantic epic that was worthy of serving as a model, and it subsequently influenced the creation of Ariosto's *Orlando Furioso*.

## Composition and Inspiration
Boiardo composed the *Orlando Inamorato* primarily for the amusement of his court and Duke Hercules. Although the work was intended for the court, Boiardo did not write it within the court's precincts; instead, he retired to his estates, such as Scandiano, to focus on his composition. 

The poem incorporates descriptions of the agreeable environs of Boiardo's chateau. Furthermore, the names of several heroes in the poem—including Agramant, Sacripant, Gradasse, and Mandricardo—were derived from the names of Boiardo's own peasants. Boiardo selected these names because their "uncouthness" made them appear appropriate for Saracen warriors.

## Literary Models and Subject Matter
There are varying accounts regarding the models Boiardo used for the poem. Gravina and Mazzuchell, along with subsequent writers, have asserted that Boiardo modeled the work after Homer's *Iliad*. According to this view, the poem acts as a reflex image of the *Iliad*, with Angelica occupying the role of Helen and Paris being besieged in a manner similar to the city of Troy.

However, the actual subject matter of the *Orlando Inamorato* is derived from the *Fabulous Chronicle* of the pseudo-Turpin. Despite this source, the detailed plot of Boiardo's poem bears little resemblance to the *Fabulous Chronicle*, with the exception of the inclusion of principal warriors such as Oliver, Roland, and Charlemagne.

## Publication History
Boiardo did not live to finish the poem. It was printed in Scandiano the year following his death under the supervision of his son, Count Camillo. While the book itself does not feature a date, a Latin letter from Antonia Caraffa di Reggio that is prefixed to the poem is dated the kalends of June 1495. A second edition was printed in Venice before the year 1500, and the work was reprinted in Venice twice more during the following twenty years.
