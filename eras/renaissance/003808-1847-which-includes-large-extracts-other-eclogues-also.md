# Alexander Barclay

Alexander Barclay was a priest and author who wrote during the early 16th century. He is noted for his contributions to English pastoral poetry and his translations of foreign works.

## Certayne Ecloges
Barclay wrote the *Certayne Ecloges of Alexander Barclay, Priest* during his youth. While these were likely printed as early as 1513, the earliest extant edition is found in a 1570 reprint of the *Ship of Fools* by John Cawood. With the exception of Henryson's *Robin and Makyn*, these works represent the earliest examples of the English pastoral.

The collection includes several distinct eclogues:
* **The first three eclogues:** These are dialogues between characters named Cornix and Coridon. They were borrowed from Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini (Pope Pius II) in the *Miseriae Curialium* and include an eulogy for John Alcock, the founder of Jesus College, Cambridge, and bishop of Ely.
* **The fourth eclogue:** Based on Mantuan's *De consuetudine divitum erga poetas* with significant additions, this piece contains an elegy for Sir Edward Howard, the lord high admiral of England, who died in 1513 during an attack on the French fleet in the harbour of Brest. It also features the "Descrypcion of the towre of Virtue and Honour."
* **The fifth eclogue:** Printed without a date by Wynkyn de Worde, this work is titled the "Fyfte Eglog of Alexandre Barclay of the Cytezen and the uplondyshman." Based on Mantuan, it features a dialogue between two shepherds, Faustus and Amintas, regarding the merits of country life versus town life and a fable concerning the origins of different social classes.

## Literary Style and Themes
Barclay's pastorals served as satires on social evils, utilizing "Colin Clout" type rustic shepherds to discuss surrounding corruptions and follies. His work also captured authentic images of rustic life, such as the struggle for food at great feasts, football, and Sunday games in the village.

Barclay's views differed from those of John Skelton; he had no sympathy for Skelton's anti-clerical diatribes and attacked him on more than one occasion. A work titled *Anti-Skeltonum* is mentioned by Bale, though it is now lost.

## Other Works and Translations
In addition to his eclogues, Barclay produced several other texts:
* ***The Castell of Laboure*** (1506): Printed by Wynkyn de Worde, this was translated from the French work of Pierre Gringoire.
* ***Introductory to write and to pronounce Frenche*** (1521): Printed by Robert Copland.
* ***The Myrrour of Good Maners***: A translation of Dominicus Mancinus's *De quatuor virtutibus*, printed by Richard Pynson (undated).
* ***Cronycle compyled in Latyn***: A work attributed to the renowned Sallust, also printed by Richard Pynson (undated).

## Bibliography and Editions
Various editions and scholarly accounts of Barclay's work exist, including T. H. Jamieson's 1874 edition of the *Ship of Fools*, which provides a bibliography of his works and an account of the author. J. W. Fairholt's 1847 edition of *The Cytezen and Uplondyshman* includes large extracts from Barclay's other eclogues. Other relevant scholarly works include Zarncke's edition of Brant (1854) and Dr. Fedor Fraustadt's 1894 study, *Ueber das Verhaeltnis von Barclays Ship of Fools zu den lateinischen, franzoesischen und deutschen Quellen*.
