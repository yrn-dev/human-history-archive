# Romanists and Utraquists in Bohemia

The religious landscape of Bohemia from the 16th to the early 17th century was characterized by a complex struggle between various ecclesiastical factions, primarily the Romanists (Roman Catholics) and the Utraquists. These groups, along with the Bohemian Brethren and Lutherans, frequently clashed over the nature of faith, the administration of the church, and the legal rights of worship within the kingdom.

## The Utraquist Factions
The Utraquists, also known as Calixtines, emerged as a more moderate branch of the Hussite movement. Their primary distinguishing claim was the right to receive communion in both kinds. Because of this specific focus, the most moderate Utraquists differed very little in their faith from the Roman church. 

Over time, the Utraquists split into two distinct groups: the moderate and the advanced. The moderate Utraquists were often aligned with the Romanists and the house of Habsburg. In contrast, the advanced Utraquists moved further away from Roman Catholicism, eventually differing very little from German Lutherans. By the late 16th century, the advanced Utraquists, alongside the Bohemian Brethren, became a driving force for church reform.

## Political Alliances and the Habsburgs
The house of Habsburg generally maintained a traditional alliance with the Romanists and the retrograde (moderate) Utraquists. However, the individual monarchs of the dynasty exhibited varying degrees of commitment to these alliances.

King Maximilian succeeded his father as king of Bohemia with the support of the Romanists and retrograde Utraquists, though he had previously been educated by Protestant tutors and expressed sympathy for church reform. Despite this, Maximilian's reign was marked by indifference and religious skepticism. While he refused to sanction measures against Protestants despite the influence of the Jesuits, he also published new decrees against the Bohemian Brethren and eventually returned to attending the functions of the Roman church.

His successor, Rudolph, was similarly characterized by apathy and indifference toward religious controversy, partly due to fits of melancholia and periods of insanity. Although he was educated in Spain and initially feared to be severe toward reformers, he often vacillated in his policies. Later in his reign, however, Rudolph assumed a more truculent attitude toward the estates, demanding the destruction of documents that sought to establish religious liberty.

## Legal and Ecclesiastical Disputes
A central point of contention was the "Compacts," enactments that had granted freedom of worship to moderate Utraquists. While these served the moderates, Ferdinand I used the Compacts as an instrument to oppress the advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian Brethren. Consequently, by 1567, the estates expressed a desire that the Compacts cease to be part of the laws of the country.

Another major conflict concerned the consistory, the ecclesiastical body that ruled the Utraquist church. Since the death of Archbishop John of Rokycan, the church had lacked an archbishop. The Bohemian estates demanded the right to appoint the members of the consistory themselves rather than leaving the appointment to the king. While Maximilian rejected this demand, he eventually exempted the Lutherans and advanced Utraquists from the consistory's jurisdiction.

## The Confessio Bohemica and the Protestant Coalition
As the influence of the Bohemian Brethren grew and the advanced Utraquists aligned more closely with German Protestants, a broader coalition emerged. By the early 17th century, the term "Protestants" was used to jointly describe the Utraquists, Lutherans, and Bohemian Brethren.

In 1575, after Maximilian refused to recognize the "Confession of Augsburg" (a summary of Luther's teaching), the advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian brotherhood drafted the *Confessio Bohemica*. This profession of faith was largely identical to the Augsburg confession, though it followed the views of Calvin rather than Luther regarding the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. Although Maximilian verbally approved the *Confessio Bohemica*, he refused to make the approval public or include the document among the kingdom's charters.

## Escalation and Conflict
The tension between the Romanists and the Protestant coalition reached a breaking point under King Matthias and his successor, Ferdinand, duke of Styria. Ferdinand was known as a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome and a persecutor of Protestants.

A direct infringement of the agreement between the Romanist and Utraquist estates occurred in December 1617, when the abbot of Brevnov and the archbishop of Prague ordered the suppression of Protestant religious services in churches located on their domains. This incident led to a mobilization of Protestant members of the diet, including Count Thurn, and culminated in a violent confrontation at the Hradcany palace on May 23, 1618.
