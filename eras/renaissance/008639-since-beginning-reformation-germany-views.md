# The Reformation in Germany and Its Influence

The Reformation in Germany was a period of profound spiritual and political upheaval that fundamentally altered the religious landscape of Western Europe. It was characterized by a struggle between reformers and conservatives, eventually transforming into a broader conflict between Protestants and Romanists.

## The Role of the Bible and Theological Shifts
During the beginning of the 16th century, early champions of Church reform utilized the Bible as their most trustworthy weapon. The New Testament's depiction of Apostolical Christianity provided a stark contrast to the papal system of the later middle ages. Consequently, the authority previously ascribed to the Church was transferred by the Reformed Churches to the Bible, leading to the watchword: "The Bible, the Bible alone, is the religion of Protestants."

Martin Luther, a primary leader of German Protestantism, exercised critical judgment over the New Testament's contents. In his German translation—which holds a status in Germany similar to the Authorized Version of 1611 in English-speaking lands—Luther placed the books of Jude, James, Hebrews, and the Apocalypse in an appendix with explanatory prefaces. While these discriminations were largely ignored in practice by the 17th century, they established a link to later German critical study.

## The Princely Reformation vs. The Popular Reformation
The Reformation manifested differently depending on whether it was led by the nobility or the common people.

**The Princely Reformation:** For the princes, reformation often meant replacing the Pope with the prince as the head of religion and the controller of the people's consciences. Seeking to establish national churches dependent on the throne, these leaders were wary of granting full freedom of judgment to their subjects, citing the Anabaptists and Hussites as cautionary examples. They permitted only enough reformation to sever ties with Rome, resisting any "dangerous break" toward the crude direct interpretation of the Bible or primitive teachings of Jesus.

**The Popular Reformation:** In contrast, the Reformation of the common man was more honest and enduring, though more confused and less immediately successful. These individuals relied on their own minds and consciences, using the newly translated and printed Bible as a counter-authority to the church. This led to a variety of interpretations, as students of the Bible often ignored contradictions and riddles to accept what their consciences approved. This movement produced the Nonconformists—a medley of sects united by their resistance to authoritative religion, whether from the State or the Pope.

## Impact on Bohemia
The views of Bohemian reformers underwent considerable change following the start of the Reformation in Germany. The Bohemian Brethren, who gained influence through the support of powerful nobles, felt a strong sympathy for German Protestants. Similarly, some advanced Utraquists differed little from German Lutherans.

These religious sympathies created significant political obstacles for Ferdinand, who attempted to raise a Bohemian army to support his brother. Despite using the pretext that Bohemia was menaced by the Turks to secure a general levy from the estates, Ferdinand found that many nobles and cities—particularly those associated with the Bohemian Brethren—sent no men. Many of the soldiers who did arrive were Protestants who sympathized with their German co-religionists and refused to cross the Saxon frontier. By the end of 1546, Ferdinand was forced to disband these forces. In 1547, the majority of Bohemians viewed the situation as an opportunity to recover ancient liberties that Ferdinand had encroached upon by curtailing old privileges and claiming a hereditary right to the crown.

## Institutional and Cultural Developments
The Reformation period also saw efforts toward Protestant unity, such as the Colloquy of Marburg in 1529, a conference of divines held to promote the unity of Protestant Germany.

The divide between religious factions also extended to the adoption of the calendar. While Catholic states of Germany adopted the Gregorian calendar the year after Rome, the Protestant states of Germany adhered to the Julian calendar until 1700. At that time, the diet of Regensburg decreed the adoption of the new style and Gregorian correction. However, instead of using epacts and golden numbers for movable feasts, they used astronomical computation from the Rudolphine tables—a method eventually abandoned in 1774 at the instance of King Frederick II of Prussia.
