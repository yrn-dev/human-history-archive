# Edward Hyde, Earl of Clarendon

Edward Hyde, the Earl of Clarendon, was a prominent political figure and historian whose life and work are closely tied to the events of the English Civil War and the subsequent Restoration. He is recognized as a significant prose stylist and a chronicler of contemporary history.

## Literary Contributions and Style
Clarendon is the author of the *History of the Rebellion*, a work printed between 1702 and 1704, thirty years after his death. This text is regarded as one of the most vivid "portrait-galleries" and greatest memoirs of its kind. It is noted for its subtle discrimination of political motives and character, though it is described as being spiritually unappreciative of the opposing side. 

In terms of literary style, Clarendon is characterized as the last "old master of prose," distinguished by his use of long, sinuous sentences. His work is compared to that of Retz and Saint-Simon in its role as a record of contemporary history. Beyond his *History*, Clarendon's *Life* provides a personal record, specifically through its depiction of Falkland and his associates.

## Political Conflicts and Opponents
Clarendon's tenure in power was marked by significant friction with other ambitious figures of the era. One such opponent was the 2nd Earl of Bristol (Digby). Bristol adopted an attitude of violent hostility toward Clarendon and, in June 1663, attempted to upset Clarendon's management of the House of Commons. This intrigue was exposed by King Charles, leading Bristol to confess in parliament that he had "taken the liberty of enlarging" his speech. On July 10, Bristol impeached Clarendon in the Lords on charges of high treason, though the charge was dismissed.

Clarendon also had a contentious relationship with Buckingham. Clarendon's influence served to debar Buckingham from high office, which led Buckingham to organize parties in both houses of parliament to effect the chancellor's ruin. Buckingham later took an active part in the prosecution of Clarendon. Following Clarendon's fall, Buckingham became the chief minister, despite holding no high office other than master of the horse.

## Observations on Contemporary Figures
Through his writings, Clarendon provided assessments of various historical figures. He praised the courage, devotion to duty, and unselfishness of William Feilding, the 1st Earl of Denbigh, who died in 1643. Conversely, his view of the 2nd Earl of Bristol was more critical; Clarendon described Bristol as the only man he ever knew who possessed "incomparable parts" yet remained "none the wiser for any experience or misfortune that befell him."

Clarendon also recorded the conduct of the House of Lords, noting a specific instance where Buckingham and the marquess of Dorchester came to blows during a conference on December 19. Clarendon remarked that no misdemeanour so flagrant had previously offended the dignity of the House of Lords.

## Historical Records and Responses
Clarendon's historical accounts were not without challenge. He drew up an answer to a partisan account of affairs in the west of England written by Grenville and printed in T. Carte's *Original Letters* (1739). Clarendon later incorporated the bulk of this response into his *History*. Additionally, the *Works* of George Granville, Lord Lansdowne, contains a *Vindication* of Sir Richard Grenville against charges brought by Clarendon.
