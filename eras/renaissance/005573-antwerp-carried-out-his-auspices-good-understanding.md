# Antwerp

## Economic Rise and Development
During the fifteenth century, Antwerp emerged as a primary economic center, eventually replacing Bruges as a leading seaport. This transition was accelerated by the departure of German Hansa merchants from Bruges and the decline of the Flemish cloth industry. Between 1442 and 1444, the English Merchant Adventurers sent large numbers of settlers to Antwerp, who were subsequently joined by Portuguese, Spanish, and Italian merchants, as well as bankers.

Antwerp's growth was aided by natural geographical changes; inundations in Zeeland at the start of the fifteenth century enlarged the western Scheldt, providing a direct route to the sea. The city adopted a modern and liberal spirit, reducing taxes on foreign merchants in contrast to the protectionist and restrictive legislation maintained by Bruges. Consequently, Antwerp became the center of the cloth-weaving industry, while Bruges declined to the point of being known as "Bruges la morte," with 4,000 to 5,000 empty houses by the end of the fifteenth century. Unlike some Flemish communes, Antwerp did not engage in bloody struggles against the dukes and maintained a good understanding with the central power.

## Religious and Symbolic History
The city maintains a tradition involving an image of the Holy Virgin, richly arrayed in a long robe with a golden crown, located in the center of a northern side-aisle on a portable framework. Traditionally, she is carried in a procession around the town on the Sunday following August 15th. A stained-glass portrait in the third window from the left on the northern side depicts Emperor Charles V, who severed the connection between the Netherlands and the Germanic Holy Roman Empire. According to local tradition, the Emperor left the keys of Antwerp in the charge of this image of the Virgin, referred to as "Black Maria."

## Fortifications and Engineering
The defenses of Antwerp were significantly shaped by the engineer Brialmont, who served as inspector-general and drafted the overall scheme for Belgian defenses. His early plans for Antwerp followed the ideas of Vauban, utilizing a bastioned trace for both forts and the enceinte. However, by 1859, when the great entrenched camp at Antwerp was established, Brialmont had transitioned to the school of polygonal fortification and the ideas of Montalembert.

Following the events of 1870-71 and the introduction of long-range guns, Brialmont developed final principles for the extreme detached forts of the Antwerp region. These principles included the multiplication of intermediate batteries, the suppression of artillery positions open to overhead fire, and the lavish use of armor to protect internal artillery.

## Military Conflict and the Siege of Antwerp
During a period of conflict involving German forces, Antwerp became a focal point of strategic concern. Reports from a French Attaché indicated that while the military situation was not good, it was not regarded as "really bad," noting that Germans had suffered severe losses in attacks on outer works and appeared to have a limited siege train and no more than two army corps before the city.

Efforts to relieve the fortress included plans by the French Government to send two Territorial Divisions, complete with cavalry and artillery, to Ostend. Additionally, a contemplated combined movement of Belgian, British, and French forces on the extreme left of General Joffre’s armies was intended to force a German retreat from the neighborhood of Antwerp.

British contributions to the relief effort included:
*   The deployment of Marines and armored trains with naval guns and bluejackets.
*   Two Naval Brigades, which were scheduled to enter Antwerp on the evening of the 5th to be interspersed with Belgian divisions for encouragement.
*   The disembarkation of the British 3rd Cavalry Division and 7th Division at Zeebrugge and Ostend starting on the 6th, having been carried across the water under orders from Prince Louis.

The strategic importance of Antwerp was highlighted by the fact that German forces detained there provided cover for Sir John French to secretly move the British Army from the Aisne to the neighborhood of St. Omer to strike at Lille. Despite these efforts, the fall of Antwerp was described by some as a "bitter blow."
