# Confessio Bohemica

The *Confessio Bohemica* was a profession of faith drawn up by a majority of the Bohemian diet in the late 16th century. It emerged from a period of religious and political tension between the Bohemian estates and the monarchy, specifically during the reign of Maximilian.

## Origins and Development
In 1575, King Maximilian returned to Bohemia after a long absence, prompted by the refusal of the estates to grant subsidies to a monarch who was not present in the country. During the subsequent prolonged sittings of the diet, the Bohemians presented several principal demands. Chief among these was the request that the "Confession of Augsburg"—a summary of the teachings of Luther—be officially recognized in Bohemia. Additionally, the estates renewed a demand from the diet of 1567, seeking the right to appoint members of the consistory, the ecclesiastical body responsible for ruling the Utraquist church. This demand was particularly pressing as the Utraquist church had lacked an archbishop since the death of John of Rokycan.

Maximilian's response was vacillating, as he was caught between the threats of the Bohemians and the advice of the papal nuncio in Prague. The nuncio warned the king that granting concessions to the Protestants of Bohemia would create significant difficulties in his other dominions. Ultimately, Maximilian refused to recognize the Confession of Augsburg.

In response to this refusal, a majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drafted their own profession of faith, which became known as the *Confessio Bohemica*.

## Theological Content
The *Confessio Bohemica* was designed to be largely identical to the Augsburg Confession in most points. However, it differed significantly regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. In this specific area, the Bohemian profession aligned with the views of Calvin rather than those of Luther. This divergence is attributed to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren.

## Royal Reception and Legal Status
Upon its presentation to Maximilian, the king expressed his approval of the *Confessio Bohemica* verbally. Despite this, he refused to allow the approval to be made public and denied the request to include the *Confessio* among the charters of the kingdom. Furthermore, Maximilian rejected the demand that the estates, rather than the king, should appoint the members of the consistory.

A temporary settlement was eventually reached in which Maximilian consented to exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the jurisdiction of the consistory. He also permitted them to choose fifteen defenders—with five members representing each of the estates—to hold supreme authority.

## Later Political Demands
Following this temporary settlement, the estates of Bohemia continued to seek further concessions. By the early 17th century, the religious landscape involved a group referred to jointly as "Protestants," which included the Lutherans, the Bohemian Brethren, and the Utraquists.

In March of the year preceding 1609, the estates drafted a document consisting of twenty-five "Articles" to formulate their religious demands. These articles expressed the desire for the *Confessio Bohemica* to be recognized as one of the fundamental laws of the kingdom. The document also called for:
* The granting of complete religious liberty to all classes.
* An equal right for Roman Catholics and Protestants to hold all offices of state.
* Limitations on the ability of the Jesuits to acquire land.
* Redress for grievances resulting from the misrule of King Rudolph.

King Rudolph considered these articles illegal and demanded the document be delivered to him for destruction. The document remained in the possession of Budova, who refused to surrender it. Despite negotiations conducted by twelve chosen members of the estates—including Count Henry Matthias Thurn—Rudolph stated on March 31, 1609, that he could grant no concessions regarding matters of religion.
