# Wood

The archive contains records regarding various individuals and locations associated with the name Wood, as well as references to wooded environments and the collection of wood.

## William Wood
William Wood (1671–1730) was an iron merchant based in Wolverhampton. He became involved in the coinage of Ireland after purchasing a patent right from Ehrengarde Melusina, the Duchess of Kendal. The Duchess, who was a mistress of King George I and known for her avarice, sold public offices, titles, and patent rights to obtain large sums of money. 

Upon acquiring the privilege to supply Ireland with a new copper coinage, Wood flooded the country with coins that became known as "Wood's halfpence." This action led to the publication of the *Drapier's Letters*, a famous series of writings by Swift.

## Anthony Wood
Anthony Wood is recorded as having been greatly impressed by Thomas Baltzar, a German violinist born at Luebeck. Baltzar visited England in 1656, showcasing a style of violin-playing that had not been heard in England previously. This influence likely impacted the instrumental music of Henry Purcell.

## Wooded Environments and Forestry
The archive notes several locations characterized by their woods and forests:

### Serra de Bussaco
In Portugal, the Serra de Bussaco mountain range features convent woods that are currently a royal domain. These woods are renowned for their forest trees, including plane, cork, evergreen oak, and cypress, many of which have reached an immense size over several centuries. The preservation of these trees was historically enforced by papal decrees; a bull from Pope Urban VIII in 1643 threatened excommunication for anyone who harmed the trees, while a 1623 bull from Pope Gregory XV forbade women from approaching and anathematized trespassers.

### Lynmouth
In Devonshire, England, the seaside village of Lynmouth is situated where the West Lyn and East Lyn streams flow toward the sea through valleys described as being well-wooded.

## Collection of Wood
In historical accounts of Greece, the collection of wood is mentioned as a pretext for movement during a military conflict. Following a burial ceremony, Menedæus and the Peloponnesians used the pretense of collecting vegetables and wood to slyly steal away in small bands. This maneuver was part of a secret convention, though it caused dismay among the Ambrakiots who attempted to overtake them.
