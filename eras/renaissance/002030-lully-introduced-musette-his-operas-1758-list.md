# The Musette and Jean-Baptiste Lully

## The Musette: Origins and Development
The musette is a bellows-blown bag-pipe whose origins are situated in France. It emerged among the elegant world and courtiers who adopted an idealized, artificial admiration for Nature to harmonize with the luxuries of court life, such as satins and silks. The instrument evolved from the *cornemuse* used by rustic swains and shepherds. To avoid the facial distortion caused by inflating a bag with the breath, bellows were substituted, and the instrument's tone-quality and appearance were refined to suit its exalted social position.

The musette is said to have evolved during the 15th century, and by the end of that century, players were present at the French court. By the beginning of the 17th century, the instrument was fully developed, as evidenced by a detailed description of its parts provided by Mersenne.

## Technical Characteristics
The musette is characterized by a "rustic Watteau-like grace." Its design replaced long, cumbersome drones with a compact short barrel droner. This barrel contained parallel channels pierced longitudinally through the wood to house four or five drones. The reeds used were double "hautbois" reeds set within a wooden box in the bag. The length of the drone pipes could be adjusted using slides or regulators called *layettes*, which moved in longitudinal grooves around the barrel's circumference to produce a simple harmonic bass.

The chaunter of the musette featured a narrow cylindrical bore and a double reed. While the number of holes varied, it typically had eleven holes—four of which were equipped with keys—providing a compass of twelve notes from F to C.

## Innovations and Master Makers
The Hotteterre and Chedeville families were renowned masters of playing and constructing the musette, teaching the highest and noblest individuals in the land. Jean Hotteterre, a court musician in the *Musique de la Grande Ecurie* who played the *dessus de hautbois*, introduced improvements to the musette's drones before his death in 1678. His son, Martin Hotteterre (d. 1712), further developed the instrument by adding a second, shorter chaunter known as the *petit chalumeau*, which featured six keys. This was attached to the *grand chalumeau*, which possessed seven keys, eight finger-holes, and a vent-hole in the bell.

## Role in Opera and Court Life
The cult of the musette persisted through the 17th and 18th centuries, though its popularity began to wane in the 1770s. Jean-Baptiste Lully, the Italian-born composer who became the director of music to Louis XIV and director of the Paris opera, introduced the musette into his operas. Lully is credited as the founder of French opera, having replaced the Italian system of separate aria and recitative with a dramatic consolidation of the two. He also made significant improvements to the orchestra by introducing several new instruments. The influence of the musette in the operatic orchestra remained evident into the mid-18th century; in 1758, the list of instruments for the orchestra at the Opera included one musette.
