# Spain and the Recovery of the Palatinate

The efforts to recover the Palatinate and the shifting diplomatic relations between England and Spain in the early 17th century were characterized by a transition from attempted matrimonial alliances to open conflict. These events were driven largely by the ambitions of King James, his son Charles, and the influential favorite, Buckingham.

## Diplomatic Maneuvers and the Spanish Match
In the years leading up to 1623, English policy toward Spain fluctuated. In 1611, John Digby was sent as ambassador to Spain to negotiate a marriage between Prince Henry and the infanta Anne. Later, in 1614, Digby returned to Spain to attempt a union between Charles and the infanta Maria, though Digby himself preferred a political treaty over a matrimonial one.

By the winter of 1621, Buckingham had fallen under the influence of Gondomar, the Spanish ambassador. This influence was so significant that in May 1622, Laud had to argue Buckingham out of a resolution to declare himself a Roman Catholic. During this period, an understanding was reached that the prince of Wales would visit Madrid. The Spanish court hoped to secure Charles's conversion to Roman Catholicism before granting the hand of the infanta Maria.

On February 17, 1623, Charles and Buckingham departed for Madrid, arriving on March 7. The mission was marked by mutual deception: Charles and Buckingham hoped the restitution of the Palatinate to James's son-in-law would be granted as a marriage gift to Charles, while the Spaniards sought Charles's conversion and other extreme concessions. Personal disputes between Buckingham, Olivares, and the grandees further strained relations. By September, when the two young men sailed from Santander, they were resolved to break entirely with Spain.

## The Shift Toward War
Following the failure of the Spanish negotiations, Buckingham positioned himself at the head of a popular movement against Spain. In February 1624, James summoned parliament, acknowledging that the Palatinate could only be recovered by force. With the support of the heir apparent, Buckingham took an independent political position, and James was persuaded and half-driven to end all negotiations with Spain.

Upon the death of James on March 27, 1625, the new king, Charles, and Buckingham remained aligned in their desire to recover the Palatinate and chastise Spain. Both men were young and inexperienced; however, Charles was described as sluggish in action and obstinate, often submitting his mind to the "versatile and brilliant" Buckingham.

## The French Alliance and Domestic Conflict
To achieve the recovery of the Palatinate, James believed a continental alliance involving France was necessary. This led to negotiations for a marriage between Charles and Henrietta Maria, the sister of Louis XIII. A conflict arose because James and Charles had promised the House of Commons that no concessions would be made to English Roman Catholics, yet Louis XIII demanded large concessions as a condition for the marriage. Buckingham and Charles eventually persuaded James to ignore the promises made to the Commons and accept the French terms, calculating that parliament could not be summoned for war supplies until the marriage was finalized.

Buckingham was sent to Paris to urge the French court to convert its alliance into active cooperation. This was complicated by the rebellion of the Huguenots of La Rochelle. While James had promised English ships to help suppress the rebellion, Buckingham sought to mediate peace between the French king and his subjects to avoid compromising Charles with parliament by attacking Protestants. During his time in France, Buckingham also insolently made love to the queen of France.

## Military Failures and Alternative Schemes
As the virtual ruler of England, Buckingham attempted to commence war despite a lack of money. He organized an expedition of 12,000 Englishmen under the command of a German adventurer, Count Mansfeld, to march through France into the Palatinate. The French insisted the troops march through Holland instead. Regardless of the route, the enterprise ended in disastrous failure, as the troops, lacking provisions and money, died in the winter frosts.

Following this failure, Buckingham proposed several other schemes, including:
* Sending aid to Christian IV, the king of Denmark, for a war in Germany.
* Dispatching an English fleet to attack Spain itself.
* Dispatching an English fleet to attack Genoa, an ally of Spain.
