# Events and Figures of the Early 17th Century

The early 17th century was marked by significant colonial expansions, diplomatic tensions between European powers, and the lives of various scholars and statesmen.

## Colonial Enterprises and Territorial Grants
During this period, the British crown and private individuals expanded their reach into the Americas and the Caribbean. In 1627, Charles I granted various islands in the Caribbean Sea to the earl of Carlisle, a grant that included Dominica. Although the island was granted in 1627, the first European settlers to arrive there were the French in 1632, who brought negro slaves and maintained friendly relations with the native Caribs.

Other colonial activities included the efforts of George Calvert, 1st Baron Baltimore. In 1621, he established a small settlement in Newfoundland, securing a charter for it under the name of Avalon in 1623. He personally visited the settlement in 1627, but due to the unsuitable climate and various disputes, he departed for Virginia. However, he was prohibited from settling in Virginia unless he took the oaths of supremacy and allegiance.

Other British settlements and territorial changes occurred during this era, including the settlement of Novia Scotia in 1628 (which was ceded to France in 1632) and the settlement of Montserrat and Antigua in 1632.

## Political and Military Conflicts
The English government faced significant financial difficulties, leading to the implementation of a forced loan. Individuals who refused to pay this contribution were thrown into prison. 

Military tensions were high between England, France, and Spain. In 1627, the French Richelieu laid siege to La Rochelle, a stronghold of the French Huguenots. On June 27, 1627, Buckingham led a large land force and a numerous fleet from Portsmouth to relieve the city. Simultaneously, the English fleet under Lord Willoughby (earl of Lindsey), sent against the Spaniards, returned home shattered by a storm.

## Notable Figures and Intellectual Pursuits
The era saw the rise of several influential figures in law, science, and diplomacy.

### Isaac Dorislaus
Isaac Dorislaus (1595-1649) was an Anglo-Dutch lawyer and diplomat born in Alkmaar, Holland. He moved to England around 1627 and took a lectureship in history at Cambridge. His tenure there ended in early resignation after he attempted to justify the Dutch revolt against Spain. After making his peace at court in 1632, he served as judge advocate during the army command of Essex in 1642 and the bishops' war of 1640. He later assisted in preparing the high treason charge against Charles I and was murdered by royalist refugees in the Hague on May 10, 1649.

### Jeremiah Horrocks and David Gregory
In the field of astronomy, Jeremiah Horrocks (1619-1641) entered Emmanuel College, Cambridge, as a sizar on May 18, 1632. He later revised the Rudolphine Tables, which had been published by Kepler in 1627. Through this work, Horrocks predicted and observed a transit of Venus on November 24, 1639.

In Scotland, David Gregory (1627-1720) pursued scientific interests after returning from a mercantile house in Holland. He is noted as the first possessor of a barometer in the north of Scotland. Because he used the device to predict weather changes, he was accused of witchcraft before the presbytery of Aberdeen, though he eventually convinced them of his innocence.

## Legal and Diplomatic Disputes
Intellectual conflicts often mirrored national rivalries. Grotius maintained that the ocean was free to all nations, a doctrine presented in the tract *Mare liberum*. This was later refuted in 1632 by Selden in the work *Mare clausum*, as jealousies between Holland and England grew.

Additionally, the 1st Baron Arundell of Wardour experienced a volatile relationship with the English crown. After serving with imperial troops against the Turks in Hungary and being created a count of the Holy Roman Empire by Rudolph II in 1595, he faced the resentment of English peers and his own father. Queen Elizabeth expressed displeasure at his pursuit of foreign titles, stating that faithful subjects should keep their eyes at home rather than gaze upon foreign crowns. Arundell faced multiple arrests and accusations of conspiracy against the government before being created Baron Arundell of Wardour by James I on May 4, 1605.
