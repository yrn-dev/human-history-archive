# Bohemia

## Political and Religious Conflict
During the early 17th century, Bohemia was characterized by significant religious controversies and political instability. These disputes were often influenced by personal enmities, particularly those involving King Rudolph. Rudolph maintained a contentious relationship with his younger brother, Matthias, who sought to supplant him by utilizing the political and religious tensions of the era. The rupture between the brothers was formally caused by Rudolph's refusal to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey that Matthias had concluded while acting as a representative in Hungary.

While the Hungarians accepted Matthias as their ruler and the estates of Moravia renounced their allegiance to Rudolph, the Bohemians initially declined Matthias's overtures. In March 1608, the estates met at Prague; they authorized Rudolph to levy troops for the defense of Bohemia, though they simultaneously submitted demands regarding ecclesiastical matters. Following the entry of Matthias's forces into Liben, a town near Prague, an agreement was reached on June 25, 1608. Rudolph retained his rights as king of Bohemia but formally ceded the government of Upper and Lower Austria, Moravia, and Hungary to Matthias.

## The Diet of 1609 and Religious Demands
The diet that convened on January 20, 1609, is regarded as one of the most important in Bohemian history. Prior to this, the estates had drawn up a document in March of the previous year consisting of twenty-five Articles formulating their religious demands. King Rudolph considered this document illegal and demanded its destruction, but it remained in the possession of Budova, who refused to surrender it.

The estates appointed twelve negotiators, including Count Henry Matthias Thurn, to discuss these matters with the king and his councillors. The "articles" requested the following:
* The recognition of the *Confessio Bohemica* as one of the kingdom's fundamental laws.
* The granting of complete religious liberty to all classes.
* Equal rights for Roman Catholics and Protestants—a term then used to collectively describe the Bohemian Brethren, Lutherans, and Utraquists—to hold all offices of state.
* Limitations on the ability of the Jesuits to acquire land.
* Redress for grievances resulting from Rudolph's misrule.

Despite protracted discussions, Rudolph stated on March 31 that he could grant no concessions regarding religious matters.

## Mineral Resources and Industry
Bohemia possesses a vast array of useful metals and minerals, with the sole exception of salt, which is entirely absent from the region. Lignite and coal are the most important minerals in terms of value and quantity. Some of Europe's richest lignite fields are located in the north-east corner of Bohemia, specifically around Teplitz, Ossegg, Falkenau, Dux, and Brux. Coal is mined in several areas, including Radnitz—which contains the oldest coal mines in Bohemia, dating to the 17th century—as well as Nurschan, Rakonitz, Schlan, Pilsen, Buschtehrad, and Kladno.

The iron industry has become a major center due to refining improvements, with principal foundries located around Konigshof and Kladno, and iron ores found at Nucic and Krusnahora. Other extracted minerals include:
* **Silver:** Extracted at Joachimsthal and Pribram (the middle-ages mines at Kuttenberg are now abandoned).
* **Lead:** Extracted at Pribram.
* **Tin:** Found at Graupen in the Erzgebirge, the only location in Austria where it is found.
* **Other Elements:** Uranium and radium (near Joachimsthal), antimony (near Tabor at Milleschau), graphite (near Budweis and Krumau), and porcelain-earth (near Carlsbad).

Additionally, the region produces copper, cobalt, nickel, alum, sulphur, arsenic, building stone, and precious stones such as the Bohemian garnet (pyrope). Large quantities of asphalt and peat—particularly in the south-west—are also collected.

## Notable Locations
Bilin (Czech *Bilina*) is an old town located 90 miles north of Prague on the Biela. It is primarily inhabited by Germans and features a 17th-century castle belonging to Prince Lobkowitz. The area is known for extensive lignite mines and the *Biliner Sauerbrunnen* mineral springs. These springs, which contain a high proportion of bicarbonate of soda and have a temperature of 45.6 deg. F., export approximately 4,000,000 bottles of water annually, as well as salt recovered through evaporation. The geography of the area includes basaltic rocks, such as the Radelstein (2460 ft.), and the Boren or Biliner Stein (1763 ft.), a mass of clinkstone or phonolite.
