# The Northern Lasse and Richard Brome

## Overview of The Northern Lasse
*The Northern Lasse* is a play published in 1632. It is recognized as the work that established the reputation of its author, Richard Brome. The play is notable for containing lines written to Brome by Ben Jonson, which serve as a prefix to the work; these lines provide the primary basis for historical knowledge regarding Brome's personal character.

## Richard Brome
Richard Brome (d. 1652) was an English dramatist whose talent was centered entirely in comedy. He began his career as a servant to Ben Jonson, a relationship that eventually evolved into a friendship. Brome's work reflects a significant influence from Jonson, particularly in the development of his plots, the creation of strongly marked characters, and the inclusion of curious information.

Throughout his career, Brome produced fifteen pieces. His works were published in various formats:

*   **Five New Playes (ed. by Alex. Brome, 1652?):** This collection included *Novella* (acted 1632), *Court Begger* (acted 1632), *Madd Couple Well Matcht* (acted 1639?), *City Witt*, and *The Damoiselle or the New Ordinary*.
*   **Five New Playes (1659):** This volume contained *The Queen and Concubine*, *The New Academy, or The New Exchange*, *Covent Garden Weeded*, *The Love-Sick Court, or The Ambitious Politique*, and *The English Moor, or The Mock Marriage*.
*   **Separate Publications:** Brome published several plays individually, including *The Sparagus Garden* (acted 1635, printed 1640), *The Antipodes* (acted 1638, printed 1640), *The Queenes Exchange* (printed 1657), and *A Joviall Crew, or the Merry Beggars* (acted 1641, printed 1652, and later revised as an "opera" in 1731).

Additionally, Brome collaborated with Thomas Heywood on *The late Lancashire Witches*, which was printed in 1634. His collected dramatic works were published in 1873.

## Related Figures and Connections
The circle of dramatists associated with Ben Jonson included others such as William Cartwright (1611–1643). Cartwright, a divine and dramatist, was considered a "son" of Jonson. Like Brome, Cartwright produced comedy in imitation of Jonson; his play *The Ordinary* (1635?) represented pot-house society.

Another figure associated with the Brome name was Alexander Brome (1620–1666). A poet and attorney by profession, Alexander Brome wrote satirical verses and drinking songs, earning him the title of the "English Anacreon" in Edward Phillips's *Theatrum Poetarum*. He authored a comedy titled *The Cunning Lovers* (1654) and edited two volumes of Richard Brome's plays. Alexander Brome also contributed commendatory verses to the 1651 collected poems of William Cartwright.
