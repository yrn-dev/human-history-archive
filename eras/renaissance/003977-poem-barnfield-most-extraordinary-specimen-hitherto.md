# Richard Barnfield

Richard Barnfield (1574–1627) was an English poet born in Norbury, Staffordshire. He was baptized on June 13, 1574, to parents Richard Barnfield (or Barnefield) and Maria Skrymsher, who had married in April 1572. Following the death of his mother in early 1581 during the birth of a daughter, Barnfield and his siblings were cared for by his mother's unmarried sister, Elizabeth Skrymsher.

## Education and Early Influences
Barnfield matriculated at Brasenose College, Oxford, in November 1589. He earned his degree in February 1592 and completed the exercise required for his master's gown, though he left the university abruptly without proceeding to the M.A. It is conjectured that he arrived in London in 1593, where he may have become acquainted with Spenser, Drayton, and Watson. Although Sir Philip Sidney had died while Barnfield was still a school-boy, Sidney's death strongly affected the poet's imagination and served as an inspiration for some of his earliest verses.

## The Affectionate Shepherd
In November 1594, at the age of twenty-one, Barnfield published his first work, *The Affectionate Shepherd*. Published anonymously and dedicated with familiar devotion to Penelope, Lady Rich, the work is a florid romance written in two books of six-line stanza. Barnfield later admitted that the poem was a paraphrase or expansion of Virgil's second eclogue, focusing on the complaint of Daphnis for the love of Ganymede.

The poem is described as the most extraordinary specimen produced in England of the licence introduced from Italy during the Renaissance. While successful, it faced censure from a moral perspective. Barnfield utilized the conventional outlines of the work to incorporate his fancy, coloured touches of nature, and various epithets. The work is noted for its picturesqueness—specifically the "long-bill'd woodcock," "speckled flower call'd sops-in-wine," and "fine ruff-footed doves"—and is considered to have equalled the immediate imitators of *Venus and Adonis*.

## Cynthia, with certain Sonnets
In January 1595, Barnfield published a second volume titled *Cynthia, with certain Sonnets*. Unlike his first work, he signed the preface, dedicating it to William Stanley, the new earl of Derby, in terms that suggest a close personal relationship. 

The volume contains two distinct parts:
*   **Cynthia**: A panegyric dedicated to Queen Elizabeth. It is written in the Spenserian stanza and is likely the earliest extant example of this form outside of *The Faerie Queene*.
*   **Sonnets**: A sequence of twenty sonnets celebrating the charms of a young man with the initials J. U. or J. V. (of whom nothing else is known) with extravagant ardour. 

These sonnets are of unusual poetic merit and were published fourteen years before Shakespeare's sonnets, though they are closer to Shakespeare's manner than any other Elizabethan works. They also show the influence of Drayton's *Idea*, which had appeared a few months prior and also contained amatory sonnets addressed to a young man.

## Legacy and Scholarship
For a long period, the facts of Barnfield's career were unknown, and his very existence was doubted. His life was later clarified through discoveries made by Dr. A. B. Grosart. Barnfield remains of interest to students due to his obscure but close relationship with William Shakespeare, and his work serves as an early example of the study of both Shakespeare and Spenser.
