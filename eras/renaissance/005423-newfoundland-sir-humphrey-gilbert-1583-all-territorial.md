# Newfoundland and Sir Humphrey Gilbert (1583)

## Acquisition of Newfoundland
In 1583, Sir Humphrey Gilbert took possession of Newfoundland for the crown. This act is identified as the first definite step toward the incorporation of the territory into the empire. Within the broader context of British territorial expansion, this event is characterized as a nominal possession. Aside from this action and the acquisitions of the Channel Islands and the Isle of Man, all other territorial acquisitions of the empire occurred in the 17th century and subsequent centuries.

## Status within the British Empire
Newfoundland is recognized as the oldest British possession, dating back to the 1583 acquisition. In the political administration of the empire, it is categorized as part of the colonial empire, which comprises all colonies and dependencies outside of the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland and the Indian empire. 

By the period described in the archive's records regarding the 1914 constituents of the British Empire, Newfoundland had become a "crowned republic." In this capacity, it functioned as a practically independent and self-governing state in alliance with Great Britain. Despite this independence, the government in office appointed a representative of the Crown to the territory.

## Historical Context and Geography
Historical geographical records from as early as 1533 refer to the territory of Newfoundland by the name *Bachalaos*, meaning "Codfish." During the early 16th century, such territories were often grouped with other discoveries in the Western Ocean, including Florida and Mexico, in descriptions of lands beyond the limits of Ptolemy’s Tables.

## Natural History
The region is the original source of the Newfoundland dog, a very large breed of spaniel. These dogs were brought to Great Britain from Newfoundland, though they continued to be bred in their native country. The breed is noted for a developed instinct to save human beings from drowning, which is believed to be an evolution of the retrieving instinct of original spaniels. Varieties associated with the region include the Labrador Newfoundland, a smaller black variety with a less massive head that occurs in both England and Newfoundland.
