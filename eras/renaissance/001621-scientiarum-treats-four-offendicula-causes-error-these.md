# Scientiarum and the Causes of Error

The study of *Scientiarum* and the causes of error is a central theme in the works of the 13th-century scholar Bacon. His approach to the unity of science and the necessity of an encyclopedic treatment of knowledge is most clearly evidenced in his *Opus Majus*.

## The Four Offendicula
In the first part of the *Opus Majus*, sometimes designated as *De Utililate Scientiarum*, Bacon examines the four *offendicula*, which are defined as the causes of error. These four causes are:
*   Authority
*   Custom
*   The opinion of the unskilled many
*   The concealment of real ignorance with a pretence of knowledge

Of these, the pretence of knowledge to hide real ignorance is identified as the most dangerous cause of error, as it is considered the cause of all the other *offendicula*. While some have viewed these classifications as an anticipation of the *Idola* described by Francis Bacon, the archive notes that the two classifications have little in common.

## Relation to Other Works
The *Opus Majus* marks the beginning of Bacon's real activity, and it has been described by Whewell as both the *Organum* and the Encyclopaedia of the 13th century. Bacon's perception of the unity of science is further detailed in the summary of the first part of the *Opus Majus*, which is contained within the *Opus Tertium*.

Bacon viewed several of his large works as introductions or preliminaries to a more comprehensive great work intended to embrace the principles of all sciences. This project, possibly the *Liber Sex Scientiarum*, exists now only in fragments. One treatise associated with this effort, the *Compendium Studii Philosophiae*, contains an account of the causes of error before moving into a detailed discussion of grammar. The intended outline for this comprehensive work was to follow grammar with logic, then potentially physics and mathematics, followed by experimental science and speculative alchemy.

## Experimental Science
In Part VI of his work, Bacon discusses experimental science, which he terms *domina omnium scientiarum*. He posits that there are two methods of obtaining knowledge: argument and experience. Bacon asserts that mere argument is never sufficient; while it may decide a question, it cannot provide the certainty or satisfaction that the mind achieves through intuition or immediate inspection, which is provided by experience.

Bacon further divides experience into two categories:
1.  **External Experience:** Commonly referred to as experiment, this method cannot provide complete knowledge of spiritual things, nor even of corporeal things.
2.  **Inner Experience:** A process where the mind is illuminated by divine truth. This supernatural enlightenment is said to consist of seven grades.

In the *Opus Tertium*, Bacon distinguishes experimental science from operative arts and speculative sciences. He attributes three great prerogatives to experimental science: the ability to verify the conclusions of other sciences through direct experiment, the discovery of truths that other sciences could never reach, and the investigation of nature's secrets to gain knowledge of the future and the past. As a practical example of this inductive research method, Bacon provides an investigation into the cause and nature of the rainbow.
