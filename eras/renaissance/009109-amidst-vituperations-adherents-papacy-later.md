# Adherents of the Papacy and Related Historical Conflicts

The term "adherents of the papacy" appears across various historical contexts in the archive, ranging from the theological and political struggles of the Middle Ages to the legal disputes of the Tudor period and the civil unrest of the 18th century. These adherents were often central to conflicts involving territorial control, ecclesiastical legitimacy, and political loyalty.

## The Great Schism and Papal Loyalty
Between 1378 and 1417, Christendom experienced a period of profound division known as the Great Schism. This split began following the death of Pope Gregory XI, who had returned the papal seat to Rome from Avignon in 1377. When an Italian, Urban VI, was elected as his successor, a group of dissentient cardinals—many of whom were of French origin with deep associations in Avignon—declared the election invalid. These cardinals elected an anti-Pope, Clement VII.

This division created two competing centers of papal authority. The Popes in Rome were supported by the Emperor and the kings of Poland, Hungary, England, and the North of Europe. Conversely, the anti-Popes in Avignon were supported by the King of France, the King of Scotland, as well as Portugal, Spain, and various German princes. During this era, each Pope excommunicated and cursed the adherents of his rival, effectively damning all of Christendom by one standard or another. This spiritual and political instability led some, such as Wycliffe, to teach that individuals should think on their own account. The schism was eventually healed at the Council of Constance in 1417, where Martin V became the sole Pope.

## Territorial and Political Struggles
The papacy's influence extended to the governance of various cities and regions, often leading to friction with local populations or rival political factions.

### Avignon
The town of Avignon served as the papal seat from 1309, when it was chosen by Clement V, until 1377. Even after the papacy returned to Rome, the town remained in papal possession, governed by legates. This arrangement persisted until 1791, when the National Assembly annexed the town. This annexation triggered violent conflicts between Republicans and the adherents of the Papacy, resulting in significant bloodshed.

### Bologna
Bologna's history was marked by frequent shifts in sovereignty and struggles between the Guelphs and Ghibellines. The city was given to the papacy in 756 by Pippin. Although it became a free and independent commune by approximately 1123, it was given to the papacy again in 1347. After various revolts and periods of rule by the Bentivoglio family, Pope Julius II drove the Bentivoglio out in 1506, bringing Bologna back under papal sway. It remained under this control, except during the revolutions of 1831 and 1821 and the Napoleonic period (1796–1815), until it joined the kingdom of Italy in 1860.

## The Case of Anne Boleyn
In the context of the English Reformation, the "vituperations of the adherents of the papacy" contributed to the historical record regarding Anne Boleyn. Amidst these attacks and later Elizabethan eulogies, the archive notes that Boleyn's guilt remains unproved due to the absence of the records used for her sentencing. 

While she protested her innocence to Sir William Kingston and made no confession on the scaffold—instead praising King Henry as a "gentle and sovereign lord"—she was sentenced by two grand juries, a petty jury, and a tribunal of lay peers. This sentence was supported by her own uncle and father. The evidence against her included a confession by Smeaton (possibly obtained through threats of torture) and testimony from the wife of Rochford, a woman described as having an infamous character.

## Other Forms of Vituperation
The archive records "vituperation" as a broader rhetorical or literary device beyond religious conflict:
* **In Literature:** Thomas Sibilet defined the *blason* as either a continuous vituperation or perpetual praise of its subject.
* **In Ancient Greece:** In Athens, the orators Demosthenes and Æschines engaged in "mutual eloquence of vituperation," allowing the dikastery or assembly to decide between them. This contrasted with the "Skytalism" (or Cudgelling) at Argos, which was characterized by brutal political passion and executions rather than the Athenian habit of guided speech and discussion.
