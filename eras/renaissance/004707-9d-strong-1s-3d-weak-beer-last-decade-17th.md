# Beer Taxation in Late 17th-Century England

## Overview of Brewing Taxation
The development of the brewing industry in England is closely linked to the history of its taxation and the regulations established to protect government revenue. The first duty on beer in the United Kingdom was introduced during the reign of Charles II in 1660. This initial tax was set at 6d. per barrel for weak beer and 2s. 6d. per barrel for strong beer.

## Tax Rates in the Late 17th Century
Over time, these duties were increased. By the last decade of the 17th century, the taxation rates had risen to 1s. 3d. per barrel for weak beer and 4s. 9d. per barrel for strong beer.

## Subsequent Taxation and Evolution
Following the 17th century, beer taxation continued to evolve. By the year 1800, the duty had increased to a rate of 8s. to 10s., a level that remained until the beer duty was repealed in 1830. 

In addition to the beer duty, a tax on malt was introduced during the reign of William III in 1697. From that point until 1830, both the malt tax and the beer duty were charged simultaneously. The malt tax began at a rate of under 7d. per bushel, eventually increasing to 2s. 7d. before the first repeal of the beer duty in 1830, and further rising to 4s. 6d. after that repeal.

By 1829, the combined cost of the malt and beer taxes reached 13s. 8d. per barrel, which is equivalent to 4½d. per gallon.

## Other Related Duties
Beyond beer and malt, the industry was subject to a hop duty between 1711 and 1862, which varied from 1d. to 2½d. per pound. This specific duty was eventually abolished because the uncertainty of hop crops caused the revenue generated to fluctuate widely. For example, the revenue from the hop duty was £728,183 in 1855, but dropped to only £149,700 by 1861.
