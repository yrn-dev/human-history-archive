# Geological and Historical Developments of Asia and Europe

## Geological Formation of the Himalayas
The Himalayan range is geologically the best-known of the Asiatic ranges. Evidence indicates that its elevation resulted from an overthrust originating from the north. This process occurred as the high land of central Asia was pushed southward against the rigid mass of the old rocks of the Indian peninsula, causing the edges of the over-riding strata to fold and crumple.

Geologically, Asia can be viewed as consisting of a northern mass and a southern mass, both of which were too rigid to crumple but capable of fracturing. Between these two masses lay an intermediate belt of softer rock. Due to the contraction of the earth's interior, the outer crust was forced to accommodate a smaller nucleus, leading the central softer belt to yield through crumpling while the rigid northern and southern masses yielded by faulting. During the Mesozoic era, a sea existed between a northern and southern land mass in Asia, where ordinary marine sediments were deposited. While the current belt of folding does not precisely coincide with this central sea, there is a close correspondence. Additionally, the outline of the eastern coast and the nearly enclosed seas between the mainland and the islands are attributed by Richthofen primarily to simple faulting.

## The Shift in Global Influence: China and the West
For a significant period, China remained ahead of other states. During the Tang rule, which was her greatest period, the artistic but decadent Sung dynasty (960-1279), and the cultured Mings (1358-1644), China exhibited levels of artistic activity, happiness, and prosperity that surpassed any contemporary state. The Chinese demonstrated considerable ingenuity and invention; they were aware of gunpowder by the sixth century and utilized gas heating and coal centuries before Europe. Their hydraulic engineering, bridge-building, and knowledge of materials used in lacquer ware and enamel were also admirable. Furthermore, Chinese shipping engaged in considerable overseas trade.

Despite these achievements, China did not organize a system of cooperation in inquiry and record that produced modern science, nor did they discover Australia or America. Additionally, intellectual education did not reach the general population, leaving the masses illiterate despite a high level of natural intelligence. By the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, the Western world began to pull ahead of China, driven by the discovery of America, the dawn of modern scientific discovery, and the spread of education and printed books.

## European Political and Intellectual Transition
During the fourteenth and fifteenth centuries, Europe experienced a vast expansion of ideas, the development of science, the exploration of the world, and the dissemination of knowledge via printing and paper. This era saw a growing craving for equality and freedom. The Roman Empire lost its prestige of universality due to the expansion of the known world and Turkish conquests. Simultaneously, the influence of the Catholic church over the consciences of men weakened, with the exception of the Spaniards, who maintained enthusiasm for the church following a successful religious war against Islam.

In England, subtle tendencies emerged during this period leading toward a new method of government: the parliament. Although this method would eventually spread across much of the world, the world at large remained practically unconscious of these tendencies during the sixteenth century.
