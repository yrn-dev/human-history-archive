# Francis Bacon

Francis Bacon (1561–1626), also known as Lord Verulam, was an English figure who served as Lord Chancellor to King James I. He has been described as the "apostle" of the scientific method and the "Father of Experimental Philosophy," though some historians argue that the extent of his contribution to the development of scientific work has been overstated. His most significant service to science was the writing of *The New Atlantis*, a fantastic work in which he envisioned a "palace of invention" and a "great temple of science" designed to organize the pursuit of knowledge across all branches with maximum efficiency.

## Early Life and Education
Francis Bacon was the son of Sir Nicholas and Anne Cooke. On June 27, 1576, Bacon and his brother Anthony were entered *de societate magistrorum* at Gray's Inn. Shortly thereafter, Bacon was sent to Paris as part of the entourage of Sir Amyas Paulet, the English ambassador. During his time in France, the unstable state of society and government provided him with valuable political instruction. While it was once believed that his *Notes on the State of Christendom* were the result of these observations, the scholar Spedding has argued that these notes are more likely the work of one of Anthony Bacon's correspondents.

## Financial Hardship and Legal Career
The course of Bacon's life was significantly altered in February 1578/9 by the sudden death of his father. Sir Nicholas had intended to set aside a considerable sum of money to purchase an estate for Francis, his youngest son, who was the only child otherwise unprovided for. Because of the sudden nature of Sir Nicholas's death, this plan was not executed, and Francis received only one-fifth of the intended funds. 

This event was one of the gravest misfortunes of Bacon's life. Starting with insufficient means, he developed a habit of borrowing and remained in debt for the rest of his life. The lack of financial security necessitated that he adopt a profession; he chose the law and returned to Gray's Inn in 1579.

## Political Career and Rise to Power
By approximately 1588, Bacon became acquainted with the earl of Essex, a favorite of Queen Elizabeth. By the end of 1591, Bacon, along with his brother Anthony, was serving as a confidential adviser to the earl. In February 1593, Bacon took a seat in parliament representing Middlesex during a session summoned to address various popish plots.

During the reign of King James I, Bacon remained active in politics. In 1610, during the fourth parliament of James, Bacon participated in debates where he consistently upheld the royal prerogative, even as it clashed with liberty. Following the dissolution of the House in February 1611 and the subsequent death of Salisbury, Bacon hoped to utilize his sagacity and political skill in the king's service. However, his initial ambitions were thwarted; the king undertook the duties of secretary himself, and Bacon's two applications for the mastership of the wards—the first upon Salisbury's death and the second upon the death of Sir George Carey—were both unsuccessful.

Bacon's promotion eventually occurred in 1613. Following the death of Sir Thomas Fleming, a vacancy opened in the chief justiceship of the king's bench. Bacon proposed that Coke be transferred from the court of common pleas to the king's bench. While Bacon provided several reasons for this, it is probable that his primary motive was to move Coke to a position where there was less risk of collision with the king regarding questions of prerogative, a subject Bacon handled with tenderness and circumspection. This maneuver resulted in Hobart filling Coke's vacancy, and Bacon finally attaining the position of attorney-general.

## Intellectual Influence
Bacon's ideas were influenced in part by the work of Dr. Gilbert of Colchester (1540–1603), whose research on magnetism helped shape Bacon's thinking. In the realm of philosophy, Bacon expressed disdain for the pretensions of Lull, the *doctor illuminatus* of Catalonia, whose incoherent formulae and fantastic doctrines had been the subject of various condemnations and renewals of controversy in 1503 and 1578.
