# Banking

## The Bank of Amsterdam
The Amsterdamsche Wisselbank, which later became known as the Bank of Amsterdam, was established on January 31, 1609, by an ordinance of the city of Amsterdam. The bank's creation was driven by the growth of commerce in Holland, which had positioned Amsterdam as a primary city for international dealings.

The bank operated as an exchange bank where any person could deposit bullion or money and withdraw the money or the equivalent value of the bullion at their pleasure. Under the establishing ordinance, all bills of 600 gulden (L50) or more were required to be paid through the bank via the transfer of credits or deposits. In 1643, this requirement was lowered to include bills of 300 gulden (L25) or upwards. These transfers were subsequently referred to as "bank money."

## Operations and Financial Structure
The Bank of Amsterdam was established without its own capital. It was understood that the bank's vaults contained the full amount of specie for all outstanding "bank money." The bank's only source of income was the charge it levied for making transfers.

However, the regulation regarding the holding of full specie was not strictly observed. The bank provided loans to the Dutch East India Company at various times. By 1795, a report revealed that the city of Amsterdam owed a large debt to the bank. As security for this debt, the bank held obligations from the states of West Friesland and Holland. Although this debt was eventually paid, the bank could not be revived and ceased to exist in 1820, ending its generations-long role as a leader in European commerce.

## Banking Literature and Authorities
The study of banking and currency is documented through various historical treatises and authors. In the United States, notable authorities include William M. Gouge, who wrote *A History of Paper Money and Banking in the United States* (1833), and Condy Raguet, author of *A Treatise on Currency and Banking* (1840). Other significant American works include the writings of Albert S. Bolles, Alexander D. Noyes, Davis Rich Dewey, and John Jay Knox. Charles A. Conant contributed *A History of Modern Banks of Issue* (1896) and *The Principles of Money and Banking* (1905).

In Britain, early and influential texts include *The Theory of Money with considerations on the Bank of England* (1811) and Walter Bagehot's *Lombard Street* (1873), which provides a detailed account of the city at that time. Other contributors to British banking literature include J. W. Gilbart, Thomson Hankey, and R. H. Inglis Palgrave, the latter of whom wrote on the money markets of Belgium, Germany, France, England, and Holland. Charles F. Dunbar also authored *Chapters on the Theory and History of Banking* (1891).
