# The Phagotus of Afranio

The *Phagotus d'Afranio* was a musical instrument of short and "fabulous" existence, created during an era characterized by the application of ingenious mechanical devices to musical instruments. Many such inventions of the period were considered mere freaks and did not survive their inventors; the instrument designed by Afranio was among them.

## Origin and Design
The invention of the phagotus is attributed to Afranio, a canon of Ferrara. The instrument was first constructed by Ravilius of Ferrara, who worked from Afranio's specific designs. A detailed description and illustration of the device were later published in 1539 in a work by Afranio's nephew, Theseus Ambrosius Albonesius, titled *Introductio in Chaldaicam Linguam ... et descriptio ac Simulacrum Phagoti Afranii*.

The construction of the phagotus was highly complex and bore little resemblance to the standard fagotto or bassoon. It consisted of two twin columns, each featuring a base and a capital containing keys and finger-holes. These columns were supplemented by two additional columns located in the front and back: a shorter ornamental column in the front, and another even shorter column at the rear. The capital of the rear column could be lifted to allow the insertion of a bag-pipe or a sort of bellows, which was used to sound the instrument.

## Classification and Comparison
The phagotus is often compared to a musical curiosity known as the *flute-a-bec a colonne*, a 16th-century instrument consisting of a stopped column with a whistle and vent concealed in perforated brass boxes. However, Afranio's version was essentially double the size and played via bellows.

Mersenne, who understood the construction of the instrument, did not classify Afranio as the inventor of the bassoon. Instead, he categorized the phagotus as a different kind of fagotto, grouping it with the Neapolitan *sourdeline*, which is a complicated type of musette. According to Mersenne, the instrument functioned as two *bassons* interconnected by tubes and blown by bellows, sounding only when the springs (keys) were open.

## Etymology and the Term "Fagotto"
The name *Phagoto d'Afranio* included a pretended Greek etymology, a claim that Mersenne dismissed as fanciful. The use of this specific term suggests that another instrument known as a *fagotto* already existed in Italy and was known to Afranio.

The term *fagotto* (and the French *basson*) likely originated as a satirical name. Evidence from the archives published by Edm. van der Straeten indicates that in 1426, Louis Willay, an instrument maker from Bruges, sold a set of wood-wind instruments to Philippe le Bon as a gift for Nicolas III, the marquis of Ferrara. This set included "4 bombardes, 4 doucaines and 4 flutes." It is suggested that the unusual appearance of the doucaine provoked the satirical wit of a courtier, leading to the instrument being called a *fagotto*.

## Evolution of the Bassoon
The development of the practical bassoon was a response to the unwieldy nature of bass pommers during the 15th and 16th centuries. To create a more practical bass for the orchestra, inventors attempted to double back the long tube of the pommer. Once transformed in this manner, the pommer became the fagotto. This evolution is distinct from the mechanical "freak" designed by Afranio, as the detailed descriptions and illustrations provided by Albonesius demonstrate that Afranio's phagotus and the orchestral fagotto were not related.
