# The Inductive Method of Francis Bacon

Francis Bacon (1561–1626) is regarded as a leader in the reformation of modern science and a co-inaugurator of modern philosophy alongside Descartes. His work sought to provide a "clue or thread" (*filum labyrinthi*) to navigate the world, which he viewed as a vast labyrinth of phenomena. This thread was his new method of induction, designed to lead the human mind from the observation of nature to knowledge and, subsequently, to power.

## The Nature of Baconian Induction

Bacon's inductive method is distinct from the traditional argument of moving from particulars to a general proposition. Instead, the essence of his induction lies in the process of exclusion, rejection, or "elimination." This approach is a necessary consequence of his doctrine of forms and his view of science. By sifting out the cause from among phenomena, Bacon aimed to avoid rash generalizations and instead employ a critical analysis of experience.

This method bears a strong resemblance to the Socratic search for essence through the analysis of instances, which ends in a definition. Similarly, Bacon's view that the first notions of the intellect are often confused, vicious, and badly abstracted from things—requiring complete revision—mirrors the spirit of Socratic questioning.

## The Process of Inductive Investigation

The application of the Baconian method begins with the indispensable preliminary of observation and the collection of facts, which Bacon termed a "natural and experimental history." He asserted that man can only understand what he has observed in fact or thought. To prevent the mind from being distracted by preconceived ideas or "dreams of our own imagination," the senses and memory must be assisted through a "ministration" that arranges instances into tables.

To investigate the "form" of any nature, Bacon proposed three specific tables:
1. **Table of Essence and Presence**: A list of instances in which the nature is present.
2. **Table of Absence in Proximity**: A list of instances where the nature is absent, focusing on those most akin to the instances of presence.
3. **Table of Degrees or Comparison**: Instances where the nature is present in varying degrees, either within the same subject or across different subjects.

Following the creation of these tables, the practitioner applies the process of exclusion to eliminate the non-essential. While Bacon recognized that this process would be a mechanical "counting out" if the tables were complete and notions clear, he acknowledged that these conditions are never fully met. To address these difficulties, he devised various "helps" (*adminicula*), including the Rectification of Induction, Supports of Induction, and Prerogative Instances.

## Relationship to Other Logical Methods

Bacon was not ignorant of the deductive method or the use of hypotheses. He admitted the syllogism into physical reasoning, though he argued it was incompetent for "principal things" and more suited for mathematics. He maintained that the "inconstancy of the physical body" and the "flux of matter" necessitate induction to allow for the formation of well-defined notions. Once these notions and their corresponding axioms are established via induction, the syllogism may then be employed.

Regarding axioms, Bacon stated that one must examine whether an established axiom is framed to the measure of the particulars from which it is derived. If the axiom is "larger and wider," it must be observed whether new particulars confirm this wideness, ensuring the thinker does not "loosely grasp at shadows" but remains attached to things "realized in matter."

## Influence and Legacy

Bacon's influence is seen in both natural science and philosophy. In English psychological speculation, he is viewed by many as a father figure; the fundamental principle of Locke's *Essay*—that ideas result from reflection and sensation—is briefly stated in the first aphorism of the *Novum Organum*. 

In the realm of ethics, the inductive method discussed in the *Novum Organum* and the spirit of *De Augmentis* (bk. vii.) serve as the root for theories that construct moral codes through the inductive examination of human consciousness and the results of actions. Utilitarianism is noted as a natural result of applying the Baconian experimental method to human conduct.

Despite these influences, some historians note that his impact was not immediate. For example, Thomas Hobbes, who served as Bacon's secretary, made no critical reference to Bacon or Baconian induction in his works. Furthermore, while the specific logical rules Bacon devised have not been found to be of practical service in modern scientific discovery—which relies more on the "scientific imagination" and hypothesis—his fundamental insistence on rejecting rash generalization and analyzing experience remains valuable.
