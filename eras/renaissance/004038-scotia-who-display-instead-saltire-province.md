# Baronetage of Nova Scotia

The baronetage of Nova Scotia was a hereditary honor devised in 1624 to promote the "plantation" of the province of Nova Scotia. The scheme was initiated by King James, who intended to create one hundred baronets. Under the original terms, each baronet was required to support six colonists for a period of two years, or alternatively pay 2,000 marks. Additionally, they were to pay 1,000 marks to Sir William Alexander, who had been granted the province by charter in 1621. In exchange for these arrangements, Sir William Alexander was to receive a "free barony" of 16,000 acres in Nova Scotia and be named a baronet of "his Hienes Kingdom of Scotland."

## Establishment and Expansion
Following the death of King James, Charles I implemented the scheme. On May 28, 1625, the first Scottish baronet was created. The creation charter stipulated that the total number of baronets "of Scotland or of Nova Scotia" was not to exceed one hundred and fifty. Furthermore, the charter required that heirs apparent be knighted upon reaching adulthood and that the honor only be granted to those who fulfilled the financial conditions, specifically the payment of 3,000 marks (L166, 13s. 4d.) toward the colony's plantation.

By November 17, 1629, King Charles I recognized that contractors for the baronets had advanced significant sums to Sir William Alexander based on the security of future payments. To encourage more applicants, the king empowered these contractors to offer further inducements. On that same date, all Nova Scotia baronets were granted the right to wear a badge suspended by an orange tawny ribbon. This badge featured an azure saltire with a crowned inescutcheon of the arms of Scotland and the motto "Fax mentis honestae gloria."

Despite these efforts, the required number of baronets could not be reached. In 1633, Charles I announced that the honor could be extended to Irish and English gentlemen, who began receiving it in 1634. Ultimately, only slightly more than one hundred and twenty baronets were created. By 1638, the grant of lands in Nova Scotia no longer accompanied the creation of the honor. Following the union with England in 1707, Scottish creations ceased, and both Scotsmen and Englishmen subsequently received baronetcies of Great Britain.

## Heraldry and Privileges
In terms of heraldic display, all baronets are entitled to include the red hand of Ulster in their coat of arms, placed either on an inescutcheon or a canton. However, baronets of Nova Scotia display the saltire of that province instead of the red hand.

The precedence of Nova Scotia and Irish baronets relative to English baronets remained undetermined by the Acts of Union and has been considered a moot point among heralds. In the 19th century, efforts were made to secure additional privileges for the order. In 1834, Richard Broun—whose father had assumed a Nova Scotia baronetcy—initiated a movement for certain privileges, but the Heralds' College advised that the request be refused. A similar 1836 petition requesting that all baronets be allowed to wear a badge, similar to those of Nova Scotia, was also denied.

## Legal and Social Disputes
The right of a baronet's eldest son to claim knighthood was revoked by George IV on December 19, 1827, for all future creations. Richard Broun attempted to claim this honor as an heir apparent in 1836; after being refused, he publicly assumed the honor in 1842, an act described as futile and foolish. While some sons of baronets, such as Sir J. Kingston James in 1854 and Sir Ludlow Cotter in 1874, were knighted upon coming of age, a 1895 application by the son of Sir Claude de Crespigny was refused because the lord chancellor did not view the 1805 patent clause as valid.

In 1897, an order granting sons of life peers precedence over baronets led to the formation of "the Honourable Society of the Baronetage" in 1898 to maintain the order's privileges. Although a royal warrant issued on August 15, 1898, confirmed the precedence of the sons of life peers, the organization continued as the "Standing Council of the Baronetage." This council eventually secured invitations for some representatives to the coronation of King Edward VII.

The baronetage of Nova Scotia has been noted as being peculiarly exposed to the "evil" of wrongful assumptions of the dignity. This is attributed to the fact that the honor is descendible to collateral heirs male of the grantee as well as to the heirs of his body. To address this, a departmental committee at the home office was appointed in 1906 to determine how to stop such assumptions.
