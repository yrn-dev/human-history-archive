# Smithfield and the Punishment of Boiling

## Overview of Boiling as Punishment
Boiling to death was a method of execution once practiced on the European continent and in England. In England, the only remaining legislative record of this practice is an act passed in 1531 during the reign of Henry VIII. This specific statute mandated that individuals convicted of poisoning be boiled to death. Prior to this act, the punishment had been frequently mentioned as a penalty for the crime of coining.

## Executions at Smithfield
Smithfield served as a site for public executions by boiling during the early 16th century. According to the *Chronicles of the Grey Friars*, an account from the year 1522 describes a man being executed for poisoning at Smithfield; the process involved fastening the man to a chain and lowering him into boiling water repeatedly until he died.

Later, in 1531, a cook named Richard Roose (also referred to as Coke) was publicly boiled at Smithfield. Roose had been found guilty of treason after putting poison into food intended for the poor of the parish of Lambeth and for the household of the bishop of Rochester, resulting in the deaths of a man and a woman. He was sentenced to be boiled to death without the benefit of clergy.

In 1542, another execution took place at Smithfield involving a servant named Margaret Davy, who was boiled for poisoning her employer.

## Other Instances and Legal Repeal
While Smithfield was a primary site for these executions, other locations were also used. In 1531, the same year as the execution of Richard Roose, a maid-servant was boiled at King's Lynn for the poisoning of her mistress.

The legal authorization for this form of punishment in England ended during the reign of Edward VI, when the act was repealed in 1547.
