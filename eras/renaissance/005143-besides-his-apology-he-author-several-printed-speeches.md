# The Earl of Bristol

The Earl of Bristol was a prominent and conspicuous figure of his era, recognized for his brilliant abilities and skill as a great orator. He was described as a man who could distinguish himself without effort in any field of activity he chose to enter. However, these natural gifts were often undermined by an instability of character and a restless ambition that hindered his achievement of real greatness.

## Character and Reputation
The accounts of Bristol's personality are marked by contradiction. Clarendon described him as a man of "grave aspect" with a presence that commanded respect, possessing great ability and parts. Despite these strengths, Clarendon also noted that he was supercilious, passionate, and tended to be too voluminous when discoursing in council. Furthermore, Clarendon characterized him as the only man he had ever known of such "incomparable parts" who failed to gain wisdom from experience or misfortune.

Horace Walpole further emphasized these contradictions, describing Bristol as a "singular person whose life was one contradiction." Walpole noted that Bristol wrote against popery but eventually embraced it, and that he was a zealous opposer of the court yet became a sacrifice for it. He was described as having been conscientiously converted while prosecuting Lord Strafford, but later acting as an unconscientious persecutor of Lord Clarendon. While possessing romantic bravery, he was consistently an unsuccessful commander, and despite being a Roman Catholic, he spoke in favor of the Test Act. Additionally, he practiced astrology on the birthday of true philosophy.

## Political Career and Ambitions
Bristol served two Stuart sovereigns. He was characterized as a man of high character who refused to compromise with injustice or falsehood. His firm resistance to the tyranny of the king helped pave the way for the movement that eventually destroyed that tyranny.

Politically, Bristol sought to establish a political union between Spain and England. He intended for this union to be separate from marriage questions or religious issues, believing such a policy would have benefited both European and English interests. However, this objective proved impracticable as it was understood by neither the English nor the Spanish.

## Literary Works and Publications
Bristol was a prolific writer and translator. His published works include:

*   ***Apology*** (or ***Apologie***): Written in 1643 (found in the Thomason Tracts), this work served to justify his support of the king's cause.
*   ***Elvira***: A comedy published in 1667 and included in R. Dodsley's *Select Collect. of Old English Plays*.
*   ***Worse and Worse***: An adaptation from the Spanish that was acted but not printed.
*   ***The Adventures of Five Hours*** (1663): A work attributed to him and Sir Samuel Tuke.

In addition to these, he authored several poems and printed speeches. His eloquent and pointed speeches are preserved in the *Biog. Brit.* and the *Thomason Tracts*, and are listed in the general catalogue of the British Museum. He also translated Peter du Moulin's *A Defence of the Catholic Faith* in 1610. A catalogue of his personal library was published in 1680.

## Personal Life
Bristol's family history involves two marriages. He married Beatrix, the daughter of Charles Walcot and widow of Sir John Dyve; this union produced two daughters and two sons. One son, George, succeeded him as the 2nd Earl of Bristol, while the other son, John, died unmarried.

He also married Lady Anne Russell, the daughter of Francis, 4th Earl of Bedford. From this marriage, he had two daughters and two sons. One son, Francis, died unmarried before his father. The other son, John, succeeded him as the 3rd Earl of Bristol.
