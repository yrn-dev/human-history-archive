# The Overbury and Somerset Case

The Overbury and Somerset case was a celebrated legal and political scandal during the early years of the reign of King James I. Characterized by the archive as one of the "Somerset and Overbury horrors," the case involved charges of murder and led to significant shifts in the royal court's power dynamics.

## The Murder of Sir Thomas Overbury
The case centered on the death of Sir Thomas Overbury. Charges of murdering Overbury were brought against Robert Carr, the earl of Somerset, in September. The proceedings also involved other figures; Frances, countess of Somerset, was tried for the murder.

The investigation into the death included an examination of Sir Robert Killigrew (c. 1579–1633), a man of scientific knowledge who had been knighted by James I in 1603. Killigrew had a habit of supplying powders to the earl of Somerset, and he came into prominence due to his alleged connection with Overbury's death. However, the archive notes that it is not certain if the fatal powder actually came from Killigrew.

## Political Consequences and the Rise of Villiers
The downfall of the earl of Somerset provided an opportunity for the ascent of Sir George Villiers. While Somerset had previously maintained a position of pre-eminence, Villiers began to rise in the king's favor. On April 23, 1615, Villiers was promoted to gentleman of the bedchamber despite Somerset's opposition, and he was knighted the following day.

The formal charge of murdering Overbury brought against Somerset in September completed the earl's downfall. Villiers immediately stepped into the vacancy left by Somerset. His subsequent rise was rapid:
* **January 3, 1616:** Appointed master of the horse.
* **April 24, 1616:** Received the order of the Garter.
* **August 27, 1616:** Created Baron Waddon and Viscount Villiers, receiving a land grant valued at £80,000.
* **January 5, 1617:** Made an earl.
* **January 1, 1618:** Created marquess of Buckingham.

By this time, Villiers had become the richest nobleman in England, with the exception of the earl of Pembroke.

## Impact on Other Court Figures
The scandal extended its influence to other members of the court through familial and social associations. William, Viscount Wallingford (c. 1547–1632), a privy councillor and treasurer of the royal household, suffered a temporary reverse in his fortunes in 1616. Because his second wife, Elizabeth, was the daughter of the earl of Suffolk, Knollys was related to Frances, countess of Somerset. When the countess was tried for the murder of Sir Thomas Overbury, her relatives were viewed with suspicion. Consequently, Lord Wallingford resigned the treasurership of the household in 1616 and later resigned the mastership of the court of wards in 1618. He eventually regained royal favor and was created earl of Banbury in 1626.

Additionally, the archive mentions that Francis Bacon had a share in the trial, though the nature of his involvement was not such that it required detailed entry. During this period, Bacon's letters indicate he had become acquainted with Sir George Villiers and remained in the king's good graces, eventually taking his seat as a privy councillor on June 9, 1616.

## Cultural Context
The "horrors" of the Somerset and Overbury case are situated within a broader cultural atmosphere of the early Jacobean era. This period was marked by conspiracies and a revulsion from the buoyancy of the Elizabethan age, featuring enigmatic and sombre figures such as Raleigh. This environment of "troublous thoughts on man and conduct" is reflected in the tragedies of the era, including those of Jonson, Chapman, Dekker, Marston, and Tourneur, as well as the major tragedies of Shakespeare.
