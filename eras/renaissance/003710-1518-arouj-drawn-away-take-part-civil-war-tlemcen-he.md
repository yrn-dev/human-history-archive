# Arouj and the Barbarossa Family

The Barbarossa family consisted of Turkish admirals and sea rovers active during the 16th century. The family included Arouj, his brother Khizr (also known as Khair-ed-Din), and Hassan, the son of Khair-ed-Din. While some historians have suggested that "Barbarossa" was a mispronunciation of "Baba Arouj," contemporary Arab chronicles explicitly state that the name was applied by Christians to Khair-ed-Din. The nickname, meaning "Redbeard" in Latin (*barba*), was likely given to the family due to their tawny or red beards.

## Origins and Early Life
The family was founded by Yakub, a Roumeliot likely of Albanian descent who settled in Mitylene following the Turkish conquest of the area. Yakub worked as a skipper and coasting trader. He had four sons born after 1482: Elias, Isaak, Arouj, and Khizr. While Khizr became a potter and Isaak a trader, Elias and Arouj entered the life of sea roving.

Arouj's early career was marked by capture and service to various powers. During an engagement with a galley of the Knights of Saint John based at Rhodes, Elias was killed and Arouj was taken prisoner. After being ransomed by a Turkish pasha, Arouj returned to the sea and served the Mamelukes in Egypt for a period. However, during the conflict between the Mamelukes and Sultan Selim I, Arouj moved to Tunis.

## Activities in North Africa
In northern Africa, Arouj found employment as a mercenary due to the constant conflicts between Berber princes. He supplemented this work with piratical raids targeting Christian trade. He was eventually joined in Tunis by his brother Khizr, who adopted the name Khair-ed-Din, and later by their brother Isaak.

Arouj and Khair-ed-Din collaborated with exiled Moors from Granada, who had been driven into exile by the Catholic sovereigns of Spain in 1492. Together, they conducted raids on the Spanish coast. They furthered their ambitions by supplanting or fighting native African princes, establishing their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes. In 1512, they attempted to capture Bougie from the Spaniards but were unsuccessful.

## Conflict with Spain and Death
The Spanish responded to the activities of these Moslem adventurers by conquering coastal towns, including Tunis, Algiers, and Oran. Arouj continued his military efforts against the Spanish until 1518, the year he fell in battle.

Following Arouj's death, Khair-ed-Din appealed to the Turkish Sultan Selim for assistance. The Sultan provided troops, which enabled Khair-ed-Din to drive the Spaniards from a fort located on a rocky island in front of Algiers in 1529. This victory established the foundation of Turkish power in the region.

## The Beylerbeys of Algiers
From 1518 until 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli. During this first period, the beylerbeys acted as admirals for the sultan, commanding large fleets and conducting war for political objectives. Though they were ferocious slave-hunters, the archive notes that their Christian enemies were neither more chivalrous nor more humane.

After 1587, the nature of the administration changed. The region was ruled by Turkish pashas sent from Constantinople for three-year terms. However, a military revolt in 1659 reduced these pashas to nonentities. From that point forward, the African cities became anarchical military republics that chose their own rulers and survived through plunder.

## Maritime Trade and Piracy
The maritime activities of the region were managed by a class or corporation of captains known as *reises*. These cruisers were funded by capitalists, and the *reises* commanded them. The treasury of the pasha (or successors titled Bey, Dey, or Agha) received ten percent of the value of all prizes. Bougie served as the primary shipbuilding port, utilizing timber from the surrounding countryside.

The technology of the pirates evolved over time. Until the 17th century, they relied on galleys, which were limited to the coast and the Mediterranean. A Flemish renegade named Simon Danser introduced the use of sailing ships, which allowed the pirates to venture into the Atlantic as far as Iceland or the Canaries. This era saw a significant influx of renegades from Christendom, including an English gentleman from the Buckinghamshire Verney family. The port of Algiers became so dominant in this trade that the term "Algerine" became synonymous with "Barbary pirate." One notable event occurred in 1631, when a Flemish renegade known as Murad Reis sacked Baltimore in Ireland, taking captives to the slave markets of Algiers.
