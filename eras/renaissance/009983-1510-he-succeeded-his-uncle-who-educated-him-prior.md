# Francois Bonivard

## Early Life and Appointment
Francois Bonivard (1493–1570) was born into an old Savoyard family at Seyssel. He was educated by his uncle, whom he succeeded in 1510 as the prior of the Cluniac priory of St Victor, located near Geneva.

## Political Conflict and Imprisonment
Following his appointment as prior, Bonivard opposed the efforts of the duke of Savoy to maintain his rights as the lord of Geneva. These attempts by the duke were supported by the bishop of the city, who was a relative of the duke. Due to this opposition, the duke imprisoned Bonivard at Gex from 1519 to 1521. During this period, Bonivard lost his priory and developed an increasingly anti-Savoyard stance.

In 1530, the duke seized Bonivard once again. He was imprisoned for four years in an underground cell within the castle of Chillon. He remained there until 1536, when he was released by the Bernese, who subsequently wrested Vaud from the duke.

## Religious Transition and Later Years
Although he is the subject of Byron's poem *The Prisoner of Chillon* and has been described as a Renaissance man who strayed into the age of the Reformation, Bonivard's actual history differs from the legendary account. His imprisonments were for political reasons; he did not become a Protestant until after his release in 1536. Upon his release, he discovered that his priory had been destroyed in 1534.

In his later years, Bonivard obtained a pension from Geneva. He married four times, though he remained constantly in debt due to his extravagances.

## Historical Work
In 1542, Bonivard was officially tasked with compiling a history of Geneva from its earliest times. He produced a manuscript titled *Chroniques de Geneve*, which covers history ending in 1530. In 1551, he submitted this manuscript to Calvin for correction. While the work is described as partial and uncritical, it is considered his best title to fame. The manuscript was not published until 1831, and the best edition of the work was released in 1867.
