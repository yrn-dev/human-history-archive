# Maximilian II and the Diet of 1575

Maximilian II, who succeeded his father as king of Bohemia, Hungary, and emperor, presided over a period characterized by a liberal policy that maintained peace, though he was unable to establish permanent religious liberty or free his government from its status as a tributary to the Turk. His reign was marked by a general indifference and perhaps religious scepticism during the frequent ecclesiastical disputes of the era.

## Early Influence and Accession
Maximilian's succession to the Bohemian throne occurred without opposition. In his youth, he had been primarily educated by Protestant tutors and briefly expressed strong sympathy for church reform. This history earned him the support of Bohemian church reformers, while the Romanists and retrograde Utraquists remained traditionally aligned with the house of Habsburg. 

Despite these early sympathies, Maximilian's reign did not fully meet the expectations of the reformers. While he generally refused to sanction measures against Protestants—despite the growing influence and advice of the Jesuits in Bohemia—he also published new decrees against the Bohemian Brethren. Over time, he returned to attending the functions of the Roman church after a long absence.

## The Visit of 1567 and Financial Tensions
In 1567, Maximilian visited Bohemia for the first time since his accession. During this period, like other Habsburg princes, he faced the difficulty of raising funds for warfare against the Turks. When he requested subsidies from the Bohemians, the estates responded by presenting demands regarding religious matters. Their primary request at the time was that the "celebrated Compacts" cease to be part of the country's laws. These Compacts had provided freedom of worship to moderate Utraquists (those who differed from the Roman church primarily by claiming the right to receive communion in both kinds), but Ferdinand I had used them as a tool to oppress advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian Brethren.

## The Return of 1575 and the Diet
By 1575, Maximilian had been absent from Bohemia for a long period. He returned because the estates refused to grant subsidies to a monarch who was absent. This led to the sittings of a diet in 1575 that were very prolonged. 

Throughout these proceedings, the king maintained a vacillating attitude. He was caught between the threats of the Bohemians and the advice of the papal nuncio who had accompanied him to Prague. The nuncio warned Maximilian that making concessions to the Protestants in Bohemia would create significant difficulties in his other dominions.

## Religious Demands and the Confessio Bohemica
The Bohemians presented two principal demands during the 1575 diet:
1. The recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg," which served as a summary of the teachings of Luther.
2. The right of the estates to appoint members of the consistory, the ecclesiastical body ruling the Utraquist church, which had lacked an archbishop since the death of John of Rokycan.

After long deliberations, Maximilian ultimately refused to recognize the Confession of Augsburg. In response, the majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drafted their own profession of faith known as the *Confessio Bohemica*. While largely identical to the Augsburg confession, the *Confessio Bohemica* differed regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper, aligning with the views of Calvin rather than Luther, a shift attributed to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren.

Maximilian gave verbal approval to the *Confessio Bohemica*, but he refused to allow this approval to be made public or to include the document among the charters of the kingdom. He also rejected the demand that the estates, rather than the king, appoint members of the consistory. However, he did consent to exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the consistory's jurisdiction and permitted them to choose fifteen defenders (five from each estate).

## Succession and Legacy
The results of the 1575 diet were viewed as generally favorable by the estates. Consequently, they recognized Maximilian's eldest son, Rudolph, as his successor and agreed to his coronation. Maximilian died in 1576, and Rudolph succeeded him without opposition.
