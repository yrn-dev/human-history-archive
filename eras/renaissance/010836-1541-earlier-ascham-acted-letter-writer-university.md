# Roger Ascham

Roger Ascham (c. 1515–1568) was an English writer and scholar born in the village of Kirby Wiske, North Riding of Yorkshire, near Northallerton. He was the third son of Margaret and John Ascham, the latter of whom served as steward to Lord Scrope of Bolton. Much of the information regarding his early life was collected and edited by Edward Grant, the headmaster of Westminster, who delivered a panegyrical oration on Ascham's life in 1576.

## Education and Early Academic Career
Ascham did not receive a traditional school education; instead, he was educated in the home of Sir Humphry Wingfield, a barrister and 1533 speaker of the House of Commons. Under the guidance of a tutor named R. Bond, Ascham and other children in the house engaged in archery, a sport Sir Humphry encouraged by bringing bows and shafts from London during term times. This early experience influenced Ascham's later work, *Toxophilus*, and his belief in the importance of archery within educational establishments, which likely informed the statutes of Elizabethan schools such as Harrow and St Albans.

Around 1530, at approximately fifteen years of age, Ascham entered St John's College, Cambridge. He was influenced by Sir Thomas Smith and John Cheke, the latter of whom was admitted as a fellow during Ascham's first year. Ascham also studied under Robert Pember, whom he regarded as a friend and guide of great learning and facility in Greek. To accelerate his mastery of the Greek tongue, Ascham followed Pember's advice to teach Greek to boys while he was still a boy himself. In his Latin studies, he focused specifically on Caesar and Cicero. He earned his B.A. on February 18, 1534/5. Despite speaking against the Pope—which resulted in punishment and a rebuke from the college master, Dr. Nicholas Metcalfe—Ascham was eventually elected to a fellowship.

## University Roles and Tutorship
By 1541, or possibly earlier, Ascham served as a letter-writer for both his college and the university. A notable example of his skill in this role was a 1548 letter written to the protector Somerset on behalf of Sedbergh school. This school had been attached to St John's College in 1525 by its founder, Dr. Lupton, but had seen its endowment confiscated under the Chantries Act. In 1546, following the retirement of Sir John Cheke, the university elected Ascham as its public orator.

During the early reign of Edward VI, Ascham publicly professed Protestant opinions during a disputation on the doctrine of the Mass. The debate began at his college and moved to the university's public schools for greater visibility before being stopped by the vice-chancellor. Ascham wrote a letter of complaint regarding this event to Sir William Cecil.

Through the influence of Cecil and the request of Princess Elizabeth, Ascham was selected as the princess's tutor in January 1548, following the death of her previous tutor, Grindal. For two years, primarily at Cheshunt, Ascham taught the sixteen-year-old Elizabeth. He praised her industry, wisdom, beauty, and stature, noting her proficiency in English, French, and Italian, as well as her ability to speak Latin and Greek. Her Latin knowledge was drawn largely from Titus Livius and Cicero. Ascham's curriculum for her included the Greek Testament, the tragedies of Sophocles, select orations of Isocrates, Melanchthon's Commonplaces, and St Cyprian.

## Diplomatic and Court Service
In 1550, after a quarrel with Elizabeth's steward, Ascham returned to Cambridge. Through John Cheke, he secured a position as secretary to Sir Richard Morrison, an ambassador to Charles V. While traveling to join Morrison, Ascham visited Lady Jane Grey at Bradgate, finding her reading Plato's *Phaedo*. During the embassy, which visited Venice, Innsbruck, and Louvain, Ascham read Greek with the ambassador several days a week. His letters from this period were published in English in 1553 as a "Report" on Germany.

Following the accession of Mary, Ascham was appointed Latin secretary to Queen Mary via Bishop Gardiner, receiving a pension of £20 per year. During this time, he quietly suppressed his Protestantism. On June 1, 1554, he married Margaret Howe, the niece of Sir R. Wallop, with whom he had two sons. Despite obtaining various lucrative crown and ecclesiastical leases, Ascham frequently complained of poverty.

Ascham resumed his studies with Princess Elizabeth in 1555, focusing on the orations of Aeschines and Demosthenes' *De Corona*. After Elizabeth's accession, Ascham was granted the prebend and canonry of Wetwang in York minster on October 5, 1559, despite being a layman.

## Literary Contributions
Ascham is recognized as a contributor to the development of early modern English prose, utilizing a style that was less studied than the rhetorical Latin elements of the time. His letters and his work *The Schoolmaster* (1570) presented the classics as the foundation of humane education and a model for a lettered, simple English.

The impetus for *The Schoolmaster* occurred during the "great plague" of 1563. While dining with Sir William Cecil and other ministers at Windsor on December 10, Cecil mentioned that scholars at Eaton had fled their school to avoid beatings. This sparked a debate between "floggers" and "anti-floggers," in which Ascham championed the anti-flogging position. This stance was reinforced when Sir Richard Sackville shared that a brutal schoolmaster had caused him to hate learning.
