# The Utraquist Church and the Confessio Bohemica

The Utraquists were a religious group in Bohemia, emerging from the Hussite movement. They are described as the more moderate wing of the Hussites, also known as the Calixtines. Their primary distinguishing belief was the claim to the right of receiving communion in both kinds.

## The Utraquist Church and the Compacts
Historically, the Utraquist church was governed by an ecclesiastical body known as the consistory. For a period following the death of Archbishop John of Rokycan, the church lacked an archbishop. 

The legal status of the Utraquists was long tied to the "celebrated Compacts," enactments that granted freedom of worship to moderate Utraquists. These individuals differed very little in their faith from the Roman church, with the exception of their insistence on communion in both kinds. However, the Compacts were viewed with ambivalence by different factions; while they protected moderates, they were used by Ferdinand I. as a means to oppress advanced Utraquists and the Bohemian Brethren. By 1567, the Bohemian estates expressed a desire for these Compacts to cease being part of the laws of the country.

## Political and Religious Tensions under Maximilian
During the reign of Maximilian, the Utraquist party was divided. "Retrograde Utraquists" traditionally supported the house of Habsburg, while "advanced Utraquists" aligned with church reformers. 

Maximilian's relationship with the Bohemian estates was often strained by financial needs. When the king sought subsidies for warfare against the Turks, the estates responded with religious demands. In 1567, they requested that the "Confession of Augsburg"—a summary of Luther's teachings—be recognized in Bohemia. Additionally, the estates demanded the right to appoint the members of the consistory.

## The Confessio Bohemica
In 1575, after Maximilian refused to recognize the Confession of Augsburg, a majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drafted their own profession of faith. This document became known as the *Confessio Bohemica*.

The *Confessio Bohemica* was identical to the Augsburg confession in most respects. However, it differed regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. In this specific point, the Bohemian profession aligned with the views of Calvin rather than those of Luther, a divergence attributed to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren.

Maximilian gave verbal approval to the *Confessio Bohemica*, but he refused to allow the approval to be made public or to include the document among the charters of the kingdom. He also rejected the demand that the estates, rather than the king, appoint the members of the consistory. Eventually, Maximilian consented to exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the consistory's jurisdiction, allowing them to choose fifteen defenders (five from each of the estates) to serve as supreme representatives.

## Historical Legacy and Literature
The Utraquist movement was part of a broader period of theological strife in Bohemia following the death of Huss. Because the movement was largely democratic, much of its writing was produced in the national language rather than Latin. 

While the writings of the extreme Hussites (Taborites) were entirely destroyed by the Jesuits after the battle of the White Hill in 1620, some Utraquist literature survived. Preserved works include *Of the Great Torment of the Holy Church* and the *Lives of the Priests of Tabor*, both of which were written with hostility toward the Taborite community. Additionally, the *Postilla* by Archbishop John of Rokycan has been preserved.
