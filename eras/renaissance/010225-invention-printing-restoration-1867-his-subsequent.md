# Invention of Printing to the Restoration (1867)

## Origins and Development of Printing
The art of printing was developed over time rather than invented at a single moment. In China, the practice likely began with the engraving of seals and the use of stone tablets for rubbings, such as the forty-six tablets featuring five sacred books of Confucianism engraved around A.D. 170. By the sixth century A.D., the method of taking impressions on paper from wooden blocks emerged, primarily for religious tracts and pictures. This technique was applied to general book production by A.D. 932 with the first printing of the Confucian Canon.

In Europe, early precursors to the printing press included glass stamps or types created by Pietro di Natali, the Bishop of Aquilœa, at Murano. These were used to print the outlines of large initial letters in public documents, which were then finished by hand. Pamphilo Castaldi (born 1398, died 1490) improved this by using wood or metal types. After seeing Chinese books brought from China by the traveler Marco Polo—which utilized wooden blocks for the entire text—Castaldi created moveable wooden types, each containing a single letter. In 1426, he used these to print single leaves and broadsides in Venice. Tradition suggests that John Faust of Mayence spent time at Castaldi's scriptorium in Feltre and developed the great invention of printing based on the knowledge acquired there.

## Early Printed Works and Bibliophilia
Early printing milestones include a bull of Pope Pius II, attributed to 1463 and printed in types used by Schoeffer and Fust at Mainz, which is claimed to be the first book featuring a printed title-page. Other significant early works include the Great Bible of 1539 and the first folio of Shakespeare, published in 1623.

The value of these works to collectors often depends on their association with famous persons, such as kings, queens, poets, or statesmen. First editions are particularly prized, partly for their superiority in study and partly for the associations they create. While the high prices paid during the era of George III for first printed editions of Latin and Greek classics are difficult to justify—as they lack textual value or links to the authors' personalities—the prestige of first editions of French and English literary masterpieces has increased.

## Scientific and Technical Advancements (1800–1867)
The mid-19th century saw significant advancements in microscopy and industrial chemistry. The first binocular microscope, invented by Father Cherubin, produced a "pseudoscopic impression" where depths were reversed (depressions appeared as elevations). This instrument was neglected for nearly two centuries until 1852, when Charles Wheatstone revived it following the publication of his paper "On Binocular Vision." Subsequently, in 1853, the American J.L. Riddell developed a binocular microscope incorporating Wheatstone's pseudoscope. F.H. Wenham later achieved orthoscopic representations using refracting dividing prisms and developed reflecting dividing prisms, which stimulated further interest in the field. Alfred Nachet also introduced two forms of binocular microscopes in 1853 and 1863.

In the field of textile chemistry, various authorities published works on dyeing and calico printing between 1789 and 1867. Notable texts include Macquer, Hellot and le Pileur d'Apligny's *The Art of Dyeing Wool, Silk and Cotton* (1789) and Schutzenberger's *Traite des matieres colorantes* (1867).

## Political Restoration and the "Peace of President Monroe"
By the mid-1860s, geopolitical shifts occurred in the Americas. The American civil war ended in April 1865 with General Lee's surrender at Appomattox Court House. This victory left the United States federal government in a position to demand that French imperialists leave Mexico. This entanglement prevented Napoleon III from intervening in the 1866 conflict between Austria and Prussia, a situation Bismarck used to precipitate his struggle with Austria.

Napoleon III withdrew French troops from Mexico after inventing a financial quarrel with Maximilian. Maximilian refused to abdicate, fought for his empire, was defeated by his subjects, and was shot in 1867. This event restored the "peace of President Monroe" to the New World, leaving the empire of Brazil as the sole remaining monarchy in America until 1889.
