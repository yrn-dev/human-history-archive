# The 17th Century: Printing, Bookbinding, and Scholarship

The 17th century was a period of significant fluctuation in the quality of printing and the evolution of book production, particularly in England and the Low Countries. While the era saw some of the lowest depths of printing quality, it also witnessed the rise of influential publishing houses and the development of specialized scholarly texts.

## Printing and Production in England
Printing in 17th-century England reached its lowest point during the second quarter of the century. However, a steady improvement followed, driven in part by the adoption of slight modifications to old printing presses. These modifications were first implemented in Holland and subsequently copied by English printers.

Despite poor print quality in the first half of the century, many English books featured attractive copper-engraved portraits or frontispieces. Religious texts, including small Bibles, New Testaments, and prayer-books, were frequently adorned with gay embroideries using gold or silver thread and coloured silks.

By the second half of the century, leather bindings became more refined. Samuel Mearne produced some of the daintiest bindings in England, imitating the style of the great French binder Le Gascon. For trade purposes, sheepskin and rough calf were the most common materials. During this time, the practice of lettering books on the back gradually became more popular than lettering on the fore-edges or sides.

## The Book Trade and Economics
The Low Countries served as the chief center of the bookselling world during the 16th and 17th centuries. Major imprints from Antwerp, Leiden, Utrecht, and Amsterdam—including those of Plantin, Blauw, and Jansen—are found in many libraries. In the southern towns of St Omer and Douai, polemical works in English were produced.

Book prices generally rose during this period due to the increase of money, the influence of monopolists, and higher payments to authors. For example, church Bibles that cost 10s. in 1541 rose to 25s., then 30s., and reached 40s. by 1641. Single plays in quarto, which cost 6d. during the time of Shakespeare, rose to 1s. after the Restoration. Other notable prices included:
*   The Shakespeare folio of 1623: £1
*   Izaak Walton's *Compleat Angler*: 1s. 6d. (in sheepskin)
*   *The Pilgrim's Progress*: 1s. 6d.
*   *Paradise Lost*: 3s.
*   Dryden's *Virgil*: £5 5s. (published by subscription)
*   Bishop Walton's polyglot Bible: £10 to subscribers (though this resulted in a heavy loss)

High prices for subscription books, such as Dryden's *Virgil*, were often driven by a desire to befriend or honour the author.

## The Elzevirs and the Textus Receptus
The Elzevir brothers, Abraham and Bonaventura, operated in Leiden and are well-known for their "charming little pocket classics" and miniature editions, particularly those published around 1635. These editions continue to attract collectors.

In 1624 and 1633, the Elzevirs published two editions based primarily on the text of Beza. In the preface to the 1633 edition, the first edition was described as "textum... nunc ab omnibus receptum," which became the origin of the term "Textus Receptus" (or T.R.) for the ordinary Greek Text. While the Elzevir text became the basis for all non-critical editions on the continent, English scholars more generally followed the 1550 edition of Stephanus. These editions provided a definite text for comparison, enabling later scholars to investigate and collate other manuscripts.

## Scholarly Works and Scientific Memoirs
The late 17th century saw important contributions to biblical scholarship and natural science. In 1657, Brian Walton, the Bishop of Chester, published the 5th and 6th volumes of his "polyglot" Bible, which included the 1550 text of Stephanus and readings from fifteen new manuscripts collated by Archbishop Ussher. Later, in 1675, John Fell, dean of Christ Church, published the Elzevir text with an enlarged apparatus.

In the field of entomology, the 17th century laid the foundations for the science through memoirs on development and anatomy. In 1669, M. Malpighi published a treatise on the silkworm. Around the same time, the Italian naturalist F. Redi used experiments to disprove the spontaneous generation of maggots from putrid flesh, demonstrating that they develop only from fly eggs. Additionally, Harvey published *De generatione animalium* in 1651, though he and Aristotle viewed the pupa as a second egg and the larva as a prematurely hatched embryo.

## Notable Figures
The 17th century also saw the birth of Jean Dumont, a French publicist. After leaving a career in arms, Dumont travelled Europe and stayed in Holland. At his bookseller's request, he published several pamphlets that treated the ministry of France in an unceremonious manner. This led him to establish himself in Holland, where he gave successful lectures on public law and published useful compilations. He eventually became the historiographer for the emperor and was granted the title of baron de Carlscroon before dying in Vienna in 1726.
