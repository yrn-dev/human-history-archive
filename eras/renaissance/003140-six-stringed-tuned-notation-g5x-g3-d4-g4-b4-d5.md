# The Six-Stringed Banjo and Related Stringed Instruments

The six-stringed banjo is a stringed instrument characterized by a specific tuning and construction. According to the archive's records, the six-stringed banjo is tuned to the following notation: 6:G5X 5:G3 4:D4 3:G4 2:B4 1:D5.

## Construction and Design of the Banjo
The banjo consists of a body made from a single piece of vellum stretched over a wooden or metal hoop, similar to a drum-head, to provide resonance. This parchment is adjusted for tension using screws located around the circumference of the hoop. The body has no back and is attached to a long neck. This neck ends in a flat head that serves as a peg-box and is bent back at an obtuse angle.

The instrument may have five, six, or nine strings. These strings are fastened to a tail-piece, pass over a low bridge on the body, and are strained over a nut or ridge at the end of the neck. The strings are then threaded through holes and wound around tuning pegs fixed in the back of the head in an "Oriental fashion," similar to the lute.

The strings are stopped by pressing the fingers against a finger-board located over the front of the neck. Some banjos feature frets made of wooden or metal bands inlaid in the finger-board to indicate the correct positions for scale intervals.

## Stringing and Tuning
While most strings have a vibrating length from bridge to nut of 24 inches, the highest pitch string—referred to as the "melody," "thumb string," or "chanterelle"—is only 16 inches long. The tuning peg for the chanterelle is inserted halfway up the neck. For convenience in playing with the thumb, the chanterelle is placed next to the lowest string rather than in the position of the highest pitch. In accordance tables, the chanterelle is indicated by an X.

## Performance and Technique
The banjo is typically held with the body resting on the right thigh and the neck even with the left shoulder. The front of the instrument is inclined at an angle so the performer can see all the strings. The chanterelle does not lie over the finger-board and is always played open by the thumb.

The banjo is generally a transposing instrument; when played with other instruments, its A corresponds to the C of the violin or piano. Consequently, the key of A major is the first key to be mastered. The G clef is used for notation, although the notes sound an octave lower than written.

The tone of the banjo is harder and louder than that of the guitar, and it is capable of playing chords of two, three, and four notes. For solo performances, a plectrum may be used to produce sustained notes, tremolo, or rapid arpeggio and scale passages. The most effective plectrums are made of tortoise-shell, roughly the size of a shilling, with bevelled, highly polished contact-edges that terminate in a point.

## Comparison with Other Stringed Instruments
The archive contains records of various other stringed instruments with distinct tuning and structural properties:

*   **The Lute:** Praetorius identified seven varieties of lutes distinguished by size and tuning. These included the octave lute (chanterelle C or D), two discant lutes (B and A), the alto (G), the tenor (E), the bass (D), and the great octave bass (G). The bass lutes were double-necked instruments known as theorbos.
*   **The Lyre:** In archaic Greece, the lyre was depicted on vases as having six strings, though Plutarch noted that Terpander and Olympus used only three. The lyre's frame consisted of a hollow sound-chest (echeion), two arms (pecheis), and a crossbar or yoke (zygon).
*   **The Double Bass:** In England and Italy, the double bass sometimes featured three strings tuned in fourths, while in Germany and France, they were tuned to fifths. Modern scoring led to the adoption of four strings, though some German instruments used five.
*   **The Balalaika:** A Russian instrument with a triangular soundboard and a fretted neck strung with two, three, or four strings, usually tuned to produce a minor chord.
