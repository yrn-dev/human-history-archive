# Gresham College and the Gresham Professors

Gresham College, located in London, was founded by Sir Thomas Gresham (1519–1579), a London merchant and descendant of an old Norfolk family. Sir Thomas Gresham was the son of Sir Richard Gresham, a former lord mayor and agent for Henry VIII. After serving an eight-year apprenticeship and spending significant time in the Low Countries—where he acted as a merchant and an agent for Henry VIII—Gresham became a key financial advisor to the English government. His ingenious methods for raising the value of the pound sterling on the Antwerp bourse helped discharge nearly all the debts of King Edward. In addition to founding Gresham College, Sir Thomas Gresham founded the Royal Exchange.

## History and Documentation
Detailed accounts of Sir Thomas Gresham, the Royal Exchange, and Gresham College are found in the two-volume work *Life and Times of Sir Thomas Gresham* (1839) by J. M. Burgon. Other records include *A Brief Memoir of Sir Thomas Gresham* (1833), *The Life of Sir Thomas Gresham, Founder of the Royal Exchange* (1845), and notices in Fuller's *Worthies* and Ward's *Gresham Professors*.

## Notable Professors of Geometry
Several prominent mathematicians held the professorship of geometry at Gresham College:

**Henry Briggs (1556–1630)**
Born at Warley Wood, Yorkshire, and a graduate of St John's College, Cambridge, Briggs became the first professor of geometry at Gresham House (later College) in 1596. During his tenure, he proposed changing the scale of logarithms from the hyperbolic form used by John Napier to a system where unity is the logarithm of the ratio of ten to one. After visiting Napier in Edinburgh in 1616 and 1617 to agree upon this change, Briggs published the first chiliad of his logarithms in 1617. He later published *Arithmetica Logarithmica* (1624), containing logarithms of thirty thousand natural numbers to fourteen places, and *Trigonometria Britannica* (1633), which included tables of logarithmic sines, tangents, and natural sines. Briggs resigned his Gresham professorship on July 25, 1620, to become the Savilian professor of geometry at Oxford. Dr. Smith, in *Lives of the Gresham Professors*, describes Briggs as a man of great probity who was contented with his station and preferred a studious retirement.

**Isaac Barrow**
Barrow was elected professor of geometry at Gresham College in July 1662 upon the recommendation of Dr. John Wilkins. In 1663, he was chosen as a fellow of the Royal Society. That same year, he was appointed as the first professor of a mathematical chair at Cambridge founded by Henry Lucas. Although the two positions were not inconsistent, Barrow resigned his post at Gresham College on May 20, 1664. He eventually resigned his Cambridge chair in 1669 to his pupil, Isaac Newton, to pursue the study of divinity. Barrow later served as the master of Trinity College and vice-chancellor of the university before his death on May 4, 1677.

## Other Professorships and Academic Regulations
The college also hosted professors in other disciplines, such as music. John Bull (c. 1562–1628), an English composer and organist, was appointed music professor at Gresham College in 1596. Because the foundation rules of the college required lectures to be delivered in Latin—a requirement Bull could not meet—the executors of Sir Thomas Gresham granted him a dispensation to lecture in English. Bull delivered his first lecture on October 6, 1597, and remained in the post until 1607.

## Related Economic Concepts
The name of the founder is associated with "Gresham's Law" in economics, a term suggested in 1857 by H. D. Macleod. The principle is summarized as "bad money drives out good," meaning the worst form of currency in circulation regulates the value of the whole currency and drives other forms out of circulation. This occurs when debased or under-weight coins circulate alongside full-weight coins of the same metal, or when inconvertible paper money is used alongside metallic currency. While Macleod believed Sir Thomas Gresham first explained this in 1558, the principle had been previously set forth by writers such as Copernicus and Oresme.
