# Matthew and the B Minor Mass

The *Passion according to St Matthew* and the *B Minor Mass* are identified as two of the most colossal achievements of the composer Johann Sebastian Bach. Both of these works date from the period of his cantorship at Leipzig.

## The B Minor Mass and Related Works
The Mass in B minor is categorized among Bach's ecclesiastical Latin choruses. In the construction of this work, as well as in the Christmas oratorio and four short masses, Bach utilized various adaptations from earlier compositions. Specifically, three of his choral cantatas were almost entirely absorbed into the B minor mass and the Christmas oratorio.

The B minor mass is distinguished from Bach's other masses, such as the four short masses (which consist only of the Kyrie and Gloria). While the short masses were obviously compiled for use in churches with small orchestras and were largely derived from church cantatas, the B minor mass stands as a more substantial achievement.

## The Passion according to St Matthew
The *Passion according to St Matthew* is one of the great Passions composed by Bach. It is cited as a work of such grandeur of conception that it serves as a benchmark for comparing other compositions. For instance, the four-part chorus in the church cantata *Wir muessen durch viel Truebsal in das Reich Gottes eingehen* is described as possessing a grandeur that is not dwarfed even by the *Passion according to St Matthew*.

Bach's work with Passions included the *Passion according to St John* and a lost *Passion according to St Mark*. The latter is known to have been larger than the *Passion according to St John*, based on movements preserved in a funeral ode for the Duchess Eberhardine. While some sources, such as J. N. Forkel, claimed Bach wrote five Passions, there is an autograph of a *Passion according to St Luke* that critics consider worthless, suggesting it may have been a copy made for personal interest.

## Context of Composition in Leipzig
Bach's time in Leipzig was marked by both professional success and personal hardship. While he produced his most massive works there, he frequently clashed with the town councillors of Leipzig. These officials, similar to his previous employers in Arnstadt, were shocked by Bach's independent bearing and a compositional style they characterized as "unecclesiastical."

His personal life was marked by significant loss. Of the seven children he had with his first wife, only three survived him. With his second wife, he had thirteen children, though four of his six sons were lost. Despite the dignity of his post, it was not lucrative for a family of such size, and records of his possessions after his death in 1750 indicate a history of uncomplaining thrift.

## Evolution of Style and Legacy
Bach viewed the evolution of music with equanimity. He acknowledged that the "old style" of polyphonic art—his own specialty—was being eclipsed by a new style, stating, "The art has advanced to great heights: the old style of music no longer pleases our modern ears." This new style was championed by his son, Philipp Emanuel Bach.

While Bach's son Friedermann showed wonderful promise, he eventually lived a dissolute and unproductive life, attaining a disreputable old age. K. Philipp Emanuel Bach noted that Friedermann was more capable of replacing their father than the rest of the family combined.
