# Posthumous and Private Publication of Writings

The history of literary production includes various instances where works were not published during the author's lifetime, were circulated privately, or were managed by associates after the author's death. These practices often stemmed from a desire for secrecy, fear of institutional condemnation, or the efforts of admirers to preserve a legacy.

## Posthumous Publication and Admirers
In some cases, authors intentionally withheld their work from the public to avoid conflict with established authorities. René Descartes, for example, developed a physical doctrine of the universe titled *The World*. Although he initially intended to finish it by 1633, he became concerned about the theological verdict regarding his views on the rotation of the earth around the sun and the infinitude of the universe. After learning that Galileo's cosmical system had been printed in Italy only for every copy to be burnt at Rome and for Galileo himself to be condemned, Descartes abandoned his plans to publish. He consigned *The World* to his desk, as he had a sincere respect for the church and did not wish to engage in open conflict with established doctrine. It was not until after Descartes' death that fragments of his work—including *Le Monde* (a treatise on light) and physiological tracts such as *L'Homme* and *La Formation du foetus*—were given to the world in 1664 by his admirer, Claude Clerselier.

## Private Circulation and Secrecy
Some writers avoided public recognition during their lives, preferring to share their work within a limited circle. The poet Adrianus Bogaers (1795-1870) maintained a great objection to publicity. Consequently, his reputation was delayed because he circulated his writings in secrecy among a few intimate friends. While he did print his poem *Jochebed* in 1835 and privately printed *Romances and Ballads* in 1846, his general preference for privacy limited his early visibility.

## Collected Works and Editorial Efforts
The process of organizing an author's legacy often falls to editors or friends. The works of John Home were collected and published in 1822 by Henry Mackenzie, who also provided an account of Home's life and writings. However, some of Home's smaller poems, such as "The Fate of Caesar" and "Verses upon Inveraray," escaped Mackenzie's observation and were instead printed in 1762 in *Original Poems by Scottish Gentlemen*.

Similarly, the writings of Thomas De Quincey saw various editions. De Quincey began preparing *Selections Grave and Gay* in 1853, followed by *Writings Published and Unpublished* (14 vols., 1853-1860). A first comprehensive edition of 20 volumes was printed in America between 1850 and 1855. Later, David Masson edited the standard English edition, *The Collected Writings of Thomas De Quincey* (1889-1890), and A. H. Japp edited *The Posthumous Writings of Thomas De Quincey* (1891-1893).

## Spurious Imprints and Piracies
The archive also records instances where the origin of printed works was intentionally obscured. In the 16th century, many controversial books printed in England used fictitious imprints, claiming to be issued in German towns or, humorously, "at Rome before the castle of S. Angel at the sign of S. Peter." Other examples of "glaring piracies" include Lyonnese counterfeits of classics printed by Aldus at Venice and unauthorized editions of works by Luther that falsely claimed to be printed at Wittenberg. In 1499, a printer in Brescia used the spurious imprint "Florentiae" to evade a privilege granted to Aldus.
