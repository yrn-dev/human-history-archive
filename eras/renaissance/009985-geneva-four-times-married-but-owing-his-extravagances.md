# Geneva

Geneva is a city that has served as a significant center for education, diplomacy, and religious activity. It is the seat of the League, which maintains a permanent secretariat there. The League's Assembly, consisting of member representatives, and its Council, composed of representatives from the Four Great Allied Powers and four members selected by the Assembly, both operate within this framework.

## Academic and Religious History
The city has a long history as a center of learning and theology. It is described as the metropolis of Calvinism, attracting a constant succession of visitors, including young English gentlemen of birth as part of their continental tours. 

The Academy of Geneva provided advanced education in classical languages. Isaac Casaubon attended the Academy at age nineteen, studying Greek under Francis Portus. Following Portus's death in 1581, Casaubon remained in Geneva as a professor of Greek until 1596. During his tenure, Casaubon worked as a master of ancient learning despite a lack of books and learned associates. He spent his small salary on books and copies of classics. He was acquainted with Theodore de Beza, the professor of theology and university rector, as well as the lawyer and diplomatist Jacques Lect. Casaubon married twice while in the city; his second wife was Florence, the daughter of scholar-printer Henri Estienne.

Other scholars, such as Johannes Buxtorf, also visited Geneva to further their theological and Semitic studies, contributing to the city's reputation in these fields.

## Political Conflicts and Figures
Geneva's history includes struggles for autonomy against the dukes of Savoy. Francois Bonivard, born to an old Savoyard family, became the prior of the Cluniac priory of St Victor near Geneva in 1510. Bonivard opposed the efforts of the duke of Savoy and the city's bishop to maintain lordship over Geneva. These political oppositions led to Bonivard's imprisonment at Gex from 1519 to 1521 and later a four-year underground imprisonment in the castle of Chillon starting in 1530. He was released in 1536 by the Bernese. 

Though later a Protestant, Bonivard's initial imprisonments were for political reasons. After his release, he found his priory had been destroyed (in 1534) and received a pension from Geneva. He was married four times and remained in debt due to extravagances. In 1542, he was officially tasked with compiling a history of Geneva from the earliest times. His manuscript, the *Chroniques de Geneve*, was submitted to Calvin for correction in 1551, though it was not published until 1831.

## International Humanitarianism
Geneva is central to the history of the Red Cross. The International Committee of the Red Cross is based there, and the society was founded on the basis of the treaty of Geneva. Clara Barton, an American philanthropist, visited Geneva in 1869 while seeking health treatments in Switzerland. There, she met members of the International Committee of the Red Cross and began cooperating with their society. Barton later worked to bring the United States into the treaty of Geneva, a goal achieved in 1881-1882. She represented the United States at an International conference held in Geneva in 1884.

## Notable Visitors and Residents
The city has been a destination for various international figures. Louise Florence Petronille Tardieu d'Esclavelle d'Epinay, a French writer, visited Geneva between 1757 and 1759, where she was a frequent guest of Voltaire. Her relationship with J.J. Rousseau soured after her visit to Geneva, leading Rousseau to become her bitter enemy.

Additionally, Paul Kruger, the former president of the South African Republic, spent his final days for the sake of his health at Clarens, located near Vevey on the shores of Lake Geneva. He died there on July 14, 1904.
