# Material Culture and History of the 15th and 16th Centuries

The 15th and 16th centuries were marked by significant developments in book production, military weaponry, and architecture across Europe and the Near East, alongside shifts in linguistic standards and regional political control.

## Book Production and Binding
During the 15th century, European presses produced an estimated total of forty thousand editions in book form, with a possible total of twenty million books entering the market. Early printers in Venice typically favored editions of 300 copies, while those in Rome favored 225. By the end of the century, these numbers increased, though the use of soft metal for types likely limited edition sizes to an average of 500 copies.

The survival of 15th-century literature varies by type. Weightier works often survived in libraries, whereas "lighter" literature, such as cheap romances and catchpenny literature, suffered great destruction. Some broadsides and single sheets survived only because they were used as waste to stiffen bindings during the 16th century. Books of devotion were frequently worn to pieces by use.

In England, during the late 15th and 16th centuries, fine printed books were often bound in satin and velvet, sometimes embroidered or set with enamels. Some examples made for Henry VIII remain in excellent condition due to the use of strong metal threads. A fashion for ornamenting royal books with heraldic designs began during the reign of Edward IV and continued without break; this practice was adopted by private owners during the later Tudor period. Additionally, small gold bindings ornamented with enamels—often featuring a ring at the top for attachment to a girdle—were produced throughout the Tudor period.

In Venice, the printer Aldus Manutius bound books in dark morocco featuring small dolphins and "Aldine" knot leaves in gold and blind tooling. The Florentine printer Giunta used a similar style but omitted the dolphins. Some early Venetian bindings utilized double boards to create recessed panels, finished in an oriental fashion.

## Typography and Printing Evolution
The year 1501 marked a turning point with Aldus of Venice popularizing the small octavo and introducing the italic type. Italics became the standard vernacular type in Italy by approximately 1570 and were also very popular in France, where they successfully resisted a rival French cursive type known as *lettres de civilite*. In England, italics gained only slight popularity. Instead, roman type—which was not used in the 15th century—steadily replaced black letter, which by the end of the 16th century was primarily reserved for proclamations and Bibles. The modern usage of the letters u, v, i, and j dates from around 1580, though it was not firmly established until the reign of Charles I.

## Military Weaponry: The Bill
The bill was a popular English weapon consisting of a cutting head on a tall stave, evolving from primitive forms like hedgers' bill-hooks or short scythe-blades. Before the 15th century, the bill was reinforced with a pike head above the curved blade and another jutting at a right angle from the back. This version became known as the "brown bill" in many ballads.

While not found in the king's host at Calais and Crecy, the bill was often a private man's weapon. In the middle of the 15th century, records show that a Westmorland squire, Walter Strickland, contracted for armed men where more than half of his horsemen used the bill as their chief arm, and seventy-six billmen marched alongside seventy-one bowmen. By the 16th century, the bill and the halbert fell out of use among regular troops, superseded by the pike, which offered better defense against cavalry due to its longer staff. The bill persisted into the 17th century as a weapon for constables or watchmen.

## Architecture and Regional History
In France, the 15th and 16th centuries saw various architectural developments. In Dordogne, the chateau of Jumilhac-le-Grand dates to the 15th century, while the chateau of St Jean-de-Cole spans the 15th, 16th, and 18th centuries. Bourdeilles contains a 16th-century chateau in the Renaissance style. The chateau of Biron features a chapel of early Renaissance and late Gothic workmanship. In Ressons, the nave and side-aisle of the church were rebuilt in the middle of the 16th century, and fragments of stained-glass windows are dated 1561.

In the Near East, the city of Mosul experienced shifting control. After being pillaged by Timur at the end of the 14th century, it fell to the Turkomans during the 15th century and subsequently to Ismail, Shah of Persia, during the 16th century.

## Arts and Culture
The 15th and early 16th centuries in Venice were influenced by the Bellini family of craftsmen, who held a significant place in the history of the Venetian school of painting. In music, T. Bloomer of Birmingham published carols as broad-sides in the 15th century, and the first printed collection of carols was issued by Wynkyn de Worde in 1521, featuring the "Boar's Head carol."
