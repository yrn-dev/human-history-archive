# The Spanish Friar and Related Historical Contexts

## The Spanish Friar in Theatre
Dryden's *The Spanish Friar* is a dramatic work that was performed at the theatre in Smock Alley, Dublin. In 1761, the English actor Robert Baddeley appeared in this production in the role of Gomez. Baddeley, who is said to have worked as a valet and a cook to Samuel Foote (known as "the English Aristophanes") before entering the stage, later became a regular member of the Drury Lane company in London two years after his appearance in *The Spanish Friar*. At Drury Lane, Baddeley achieved significant success in servants' parts and low comedy, and he was the original actor to play Moses in the *School for Scandal*.

## The Dominican Order
The term "friar" is associated with various religious orders, most notably the Dominicans, who are also referred to as the Friars Preachers or, in England, the Black Friars. This name derives from the black mantle they wore over a white habit. The order was founded by St. Dominic (1170–1221), who was born in Calaroga in Old Castile and studied theology at Palencia. 

St. Dominic was originally a canon regular and the prior of the cathedral community at Osma, where he followed the Rule of St. Augustine. After a mission to the Albigensian heretics in Languedoc between 1205 and 1215, Dominic established his first house in Toulouse at the church of St. Romain in 1215. To found a global order of preachers, Dominic sought permission from the papacy. Pope Innocent III required the adoption of an existing rule, leading the community to adopt the Rule of St. Augustine. In 1216, Pope Honorius III provided a series of confirmations for the community as Canons Regular of St. Augustine with a specific mission to preach.

By 1217, Dominic had sent a group of seventeen friars to various locations, including Rome, Paris, and Spain. By the time of his death in 1221, friaries had been established in numerous cities, including Madrid, Barcelona, Seville, Palencia, and several cities in Italy and France. In 1218 and 1221, encyclical bulls were issued to bishops worldwide recommending the Order of Friars Preachers and granting them the faculty to hear confessions and preach.

## Spanish Missions and Foreign Relations
Spanish friars and monks also played roles in international diplomacy and religious expansion, particularly in Japan. Spanish monks, specifically Franciscans, established a mission in Yedo. These Franciscans were noted for declaiming violently against the Jesuits, adopting an abusive and intolerant tone similar to that the Jesuits had previously used toward Buddhism. It has been suggested that the Tokugawa leader Ieyasu encouraged these Spanish monks to serve as a counterpoise to Jesuit influence.

However, Spanish and Portuguese propagandists faced suspicion. In 1620, the admiral of the British and Dutch fleet of defence sent a despatch to the second Tokugawa shogun charging the friars with treacherous machinations and warning against the aggressive designs of Philip of Spain. This suspicion was deepened by the discovery of a letter in a Portuguese ship, captured by the Dutch, which urged Japanese converts to revolt and promised aid from Portuguese men-of-war.

## Other Literary and Translation Works of Dryden
Beyond the theatre, Dryden is associated with the translation of Plutarch's *Lives*. This translation was performed by multiple hands and is considered to be of unequal merit. While some sections are described as racy and idiomatic English, others are viewed as falling short of the standard set by Sir Thomas North. Dryden's version was later re-edited by A.H. Clough of Oriel College, Oxford. In comparison to the later translation by the Langhornes—which is described as dull, commonplace, and characterized by an "inflated monotony"—Dryden's earlier version is noted as having simpler phrases.
