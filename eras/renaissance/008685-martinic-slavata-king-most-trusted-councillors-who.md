# Martinic and Slavata

In the early 17th century, during the reign of King Matthias of Bohemia, the administration of the state was managed by a group of councillors who were all adherents of the Church of Rome. Among these influential officials were Martinic and Slavata, who served as trusted advisors to the king.

## Political and Religious Orientation
Under the influence of Ferdinand, duke of Styria—a cousin of Matthias and a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome known for the cruel persecution of Protestants in Styria—the policy of King Matthias became more pronouncedly ultramontane. The king's councillors, including Martinic and Slavata, openly expressed their hope that the Catholic Church would soon recover its ancient hold over Bohemia.

## The Bohemian Uprising
The tension between the Romanist councillors and the Bohemian Protestants, led by Count Thurn, escalated in December 1617. This conflict was precipitated when the abbot of Brevnov and the archbishop of Prague ordered the suppression of Protestant religious services in churches located on their domains, an act that directly infringed upon the agreement signed by the Romanist and Utraquist estates in the Letter of Majesty.

In response, Protestant members of the diet assembled in Prague on May 21, 1618. They decided to proceed in full armour to the Hradcany palace to present their complaints to the councillors of Matthias. On May 22, a secret meeting was held by a more advanced party—including Ulrich of Kinsky, Wenceslas of Ruppa, and Count Thurn—where it was decided that the most influential of Matthias's councillors should be put to death. On May 23, the representatives of the Bohemian Protestants proceeded to the Hradcany, and William Count Slavata was among the government officials who were thrown from the windows of the palace.

## William Count Slavata
William Count Slavata (1572–1652) was a faithful servant of the house of Habsburg. Following the events of 1618, he remained a political opponent of the nationalist party, specifically opposing figures such as Paul Skala ze Zhore. 

Slavata spent the later years of his long life composing historical works. In 1637, he published his *Pamety* (memoirs), which focused exclusively on the events of 1618 and 1619, periods in which he had played a significant role. He also authored a larger work titled *Historicke Spisovani* (historical works), consisting of fourteen large manuscript volumes, two of which contained his previously written memoirs.
