# Richard Brome and the Five New Playes

## Overview of Richard Brome
Richard Brome (d. 1652) was an English dramatist whose work was heavily influenced by his former master, Ben Jonson. Brome originally served Jonson, and the two eventually developed a friendship. This relationship is evidenced by lines written to Brome by Jonson, which were prefixed to the 1632 play *The Northern Lasse*, the work that established Brome's reputation. Jonson's influence is particularly visible in Brome's strongly marked characters, the development of his plots, and the inclusion of curious information within his writing. Brome's genius was situated entirely in the realm of comedy.

## The Five New Playes and Other Works
Brome left behind fifteen pieces of work. One collection, *Five New Playes* (edited by Alex. Brome, 1652?), included the following:
* *Madd Couple Well Matcht* (acted 1639?)
* *Novella* (acted 1632)
* *Court Begger* (acted 1632)
* *City Witt*
* *The Damoiselle or the New Ordinary*

Another publication from 1659, also titled *Five New Playes*, contained a different set of works: *The English Moor, or The Mock Marriage*; *The Love-Sick Court, or The Ambitious Politique*; *Covent Garden Weeded*; *The New Academy, or The New Exchange*; and *The Queen and Concubine*.

In addition to these collections, Brome published several works separately:
* *The Antipodes* (acted 1638, published 1640)
* *The Sparagus Garden* (acted 1635, published 1640)
* *A Joviall Crew, or the Merry Beggars* (acted 1641, published 1652; later revised as an "opera" in 1731)
* *The Queenes Exchange* (published 1657)

Brome also collaborated with Thomas Heywood on *The late Lancashire Witches*, which was published in 1634. His collected dramatic works were published in 1873.

## Etymology of "Damoiselle"
The term "damoiselle," appearing in Brome's titles, is a later formation of the French *damoiselle* and *demoiselle*. The English literary form "damosel" was imported from France during the 15th century and is derived from the Old French *dameisele* (from *dame*), which parallels the medieval Latin *domicella* or *dominicella* (a diminutive of *domina*).

In the early middle ages, *damoiselle* and *damoiseau* served as titles of honor for the unmarried daughters and sons of lords (*seigneurs*) and royal persons. In England, when used by the court and French-speaking nobility, the title was restricted to the grandson or son of the king. Over time, the use of *damoiselle* expanded to include the wives of *damoiseaus* and young ladies of gentle birth who performed domestic services for the wives of *seigneurs*. Eventually, *demoiselle* became a general title of address for a young unmarried lady.

## Historical Context of 1632
The year 1632, associated with the publication of *The Northern Lasse* and the acting of *Novella* and *Court Begger*, saw the deaths of several prominent English figures. George Calvert, the 1st Baron Baltimore and English statesman who had been secretary of state and was associated with colonial enterprises in Newfoundland and Maryland, died on April 15, 1632. Additionally, Sir William Knollys, who served as a soldier and member of parliament and was created earl of Banbury in 1626, died in London on May 25, 1632.
