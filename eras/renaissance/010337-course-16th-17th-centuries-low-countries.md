# The Low Countries in the 16th and 17th Centuries

During the 16th and 17th centuries, the Low Countries experienced significant developments in commerce, linguistics, architecture, and the arts, establishing themselves as a major center of European intellectual and economic activity.

## The Book Trade and Publishing
For a period during the 16th and 17th centuries, the Low Countries served as the primary center of the bookselling world. This era produced many of the finest quartos and folios found in libraries today, often bearing the imprints of cities such as Antwerp, Leiden, Utrecht, and Amsterdam. Prominent names associated with this flourishing trade include Plantin, Blauw, and Jansen. Additionally, the Elzevirs were known for producing "charming little pocket classics." During this same period, the southern towns of St Omer and Douai were sources of polemical works written in English.

## Linguistic Evolution and Influence
The language of the region was shaped by a history of shifting influences. Prior to Holland becoming an independent kingdom, the area saw alternating predominance of French influences under Burgundian princes and German elements under Bavarian counts. Consequently, elements of these languages remained mixed with the common tongue into the 16th and 17th centuries.

The 16th century, in particular, saw a significant borrowing of words from Latin, which was the language of learned people. In the 17th and 18th centuries, French influence grew due to the impact of poetic clubs. Furthermore, colonial and commercial connections introduced various words from eastern languages into Dutch.

This influx of foreign terminology met with resistance from some figures. During the 16th century, the rhetoricians and Coornhert, as well as later figures such as Vondel, Hooft, and Bredero, opposed these foreign words. Hooft went so far as to create clumsy versions of French and Latin "bastard words" that had been passed down. By 1650, Vondel noted that the language had been gradually cleared of these "bastaard-woorden" and "onduitsch" elements.

## Urban Development and Architecture
The city of Enkhuizen, a seaport in the province of North Holland on the Zuider Zee, serves as a primary example of the region's prosperity during this era. It was an important and flourishing city in the 16th and 17th centuries, and much of its domestic architecture from this time survives. These houses typically feature facades of stone and brick adorned with inscriptions, sculptures, and carvings.

Key structures from this period in Enkhuizen include:
* **The Dromedary (1540):** A tower-gateway overlooking the harbor that once contained a prison.
* **The Westerkerk:** Featuring a pulpit and Renaissance screen from the middle of the 16th century, and a wooden bell-house dating to 1519.
* **The Weigh-house (1559):** The upper story of which served the Surgeons' Gild; some window-panes from approximately 1640 are decorated with the arms of members.
* **The Mint (1611) and Orphanage (1616):** The latter contains leather hangings and portraits from the 17th and 18th centuries.
* **The Town Hall (1688):** Containing archives and paintings by Johan van Neck.

## Cultural and Lexical Contributions
The influence of the Low Countries extended into the English language. The word "luck," which does not appear in English before the 16th century, is considered by the *New English Dictionary* to have been introduced from the Low Countries as a gambling term. It is derived from the Low German *luk*, a shortened form of *geluk*.
