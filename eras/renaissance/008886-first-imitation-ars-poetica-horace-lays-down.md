# Boileau and the Influence of Horace

Nicolas Boileau (referred to in some records as Sieur D....) was a French critic and poet whose work significantly shaped the trajectory of French and English literature. His career was marked by a progression from satirical works to more polished, philosophical poetry, eventually earning him the patronage of Louis XIV.

## Literary Works and Royal Patronage
In 1666, Boileau published *Satires du Sieur D....*, a collection consisting of seven satires and the *Discours au roi*, which he released following the appearance of two unauthorized editions of his work. Starting in 1669, he produced his epistles; these works were characterized by a style more exquisite and polished than his satires, and a tone that was graver and more mature in thought.

The *Epitres* brought Boileau to the attention of Louis XIV, who invited him to court. When the king inquired which of his verses were his best, Boileau diplomatically identified some unprinted lines written in honor of the monarch as his "least bad." Following this recitation, he was granted a pension of 2000 livres.

## L'Art poétique and Le Lutrin
In 1674, Boileau published two of his masterpieces, *L'Art poétique* and *Le Lutrin*, as part of the *Oeuvres diverses du sieur D....*. 

*L'Art poétique* was written in imitation of Horace's *Ars Poetica*. It established a code for future French verse, occupying a position in French literature parallel to that of its Latin prototype. In this work, Boileau does more than establish rules for poetic language; he provides a careful analysis of various types of verse composition and the principles specific to each. The work is divided into four books:
* **First and Last Books:** These contain general precepts, focusing primarily on the rule of *bon sens*.
* **Second Book:** This section addresses the ode, the elegy, the satire, the epigram, and the pastoral.
* **Third Book:** This section focuses on epic and tragic poetry.

While these rules are considered of value, they have been noted for a tendency to make poetic efforts too mechanical or hampered.

Boileau's other 1674 masterpiece, *Le Lutrin*, is a mock heroic poem. Its first four cantos served as a model for Alexander Pope's *The Rape of the Lock*, although the latter is considered superior in its richness of imagination.

## Influence on English Literature
Boileau acted as a chief modernizer of the secondary forms of Latin poetry, particularly the critical and epistolary *pedestris sermo* of Horace. His influence on English literature was substantial, transmitted through a translation revised by Dryden and through Alexander Pope's *Essay on Criticism*, which was a magnificent imitation of Boileau's maxims.

This influence occurred during a period between 1660 and 1700 when the literary empire of France exerted significant control over European letters. During the reign of Charles II, French writers such as Moliere, Bossuet, La Fontaine, and Racine were at their peak. This era saw a vast body of translations from French into English, which heavily impacted social forms of verse and prose, including maxims, criticism, epigrams, comedy, and satire.

## Critical Standing
Boileau is regarded as a great critic in verse, though not an infallible one, and he is not considered a great poet. He is credited with providing the utmost service to his era by destroying the exaggerated reputations of mediocre writers, although his own judgment was occasionally at fault.
