# Printing and Censorship under Francis I

## Influence of Guillaume Budé
The decision of King Francis I to maintain the legality of printing in France was significantly influenced by the French scholar Guillaume Budé (1467–1540). Born in Paris and educated in law at the university of Orleans, Budé was a scholar of the Greek and Latin languages who gained a reputation for his 1514 treatise on ancient coins and measures, *De Asse et Partibus*. 

Budé was held in high esteem by Francis I. Along with Jean du Bellay, the bishop of Narbonne, Budé persuaded the King to establish the library at Fontainebleau—which served as the origin of the Bibliothèque Nationale—and the Collegium Trilingue, later known as the Collège de France. Furthermore, Budé induced Francis I to refrain from prohibiting printing in France, despite advice to do so provided by the Sorbonne in 1533.

## The Printing of the Great Bible
Despite the general allowance of printing, specific works faced severe opposition. In 1538, a large-scale project to print an authorized Bible was entrusted to Francis Regnault, a famous printer in Paris, after the resources of English presses were deemed insufficient for the task. This project was supported by a licence granted to Regnault by Francis I at the request of Henry VIII. 

The work was supervised in Paris by Grafton and Coverdale. However, the process was fraught with danger; the printers were "dayly threatened." On December 17, 1538, the Inquisitor-general for France interdicted the further progress of the printing and ordered the seizure of the entire impression. While Coverdale and Grafton fled Paris, they managed to rescue a significant number of finished sheets that had been sold to a haberdasher rather than being burnt. These materials, along with the workmen, presses, and types, were conveyed to England, where the volume was completed in April 1539 as *The Great Bible*.

## Religious Conflict and the Reforming Party
The political climate of the era was often unfavorable to the Reforming party. In 1535, the poet Marot was implicated in "The Placards" affair and fled France. He sought refuge with Renée, the duchess of Ferrara, who was a steadfast supporter of French reformers. Marot later moved to Venice, but following remonstrances from Pope Paul III to Francis I regarding the severity of the treatment of Protestants, those exiled were permitted to return to Paris on the condition that they recant their errors. Marot returned and abjured his heresy at Lyons, and in 1539, Francis I provided him with grounds and a house in the suburbs.

## Regional Printing and Other Works
Printing activity also existed in sovereign principalities within the region. The principality of the Dombes, which was confiscated by Francis I in 1523 and later held by various monarchs including Henry II and Francis II, maintained printing works at Trevoux that were long considered famous.

Other printed materials of the period included the first printed collection of works by the poet Marot, titled *Adolescence Clémentine*, published in 1532 (though possibly appearing three years earlier). This collection was popular and frequently reprinted, with the 1538 edition by Dolet regarded as the most authoritative.
