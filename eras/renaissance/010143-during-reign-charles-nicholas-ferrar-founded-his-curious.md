# The Establishment at Little Gidding

## Foundation and Purpose
During the reign of Charles I in 17th-century England, Nicholas Ferrar founded a curious establishment located at Little Gidding. Within this community, a bindery was established by Ferrar's niece, Mary Collet, and her sisters.

## Bookbinding and Artistry
The women of Little Gidding produced a variety of works, including large scrap-books and harmonies of the Gospels and other sections of the Bible. These volumes were characterized by their magnificent bindings, which utilized velvet stamped in gold and silver and included illustrations.

The technical skill of the Little Gidding binders was developed under the instruction of a binder who worked for Thomas and John Buck, who served as printers to the university of Cambridge. Due to this connection, the stamps used at Little Gidding are frequently identical to those used by Buck.

## Context of 17th-Century English Binding
The work at Little Gidding occurred during a period when much fine binding was produced in England, primarily using morocco. However, certain royal preferences differed; for example, Henry, prince of Wales, consistently had his books bound in calf. The Jacobean style of the era was heraldic, featuring heavy corners and semis of small stamps. James I also possessed fine bindings in a different style, which may have been produced by John Gibson, a binder who worked for James during his time as king of Scotland.

## The Ferrar Group of Manuscripts
Separate from the 17th-century bindery, the name Ferrar is associated with a group of eight manuscripts known as the Ferrar Group. In Gregory's notation, these are identified as 13, 69, 124, 346, 543, 788, 826, and 828 (or in von Soden's notation as $\epsilon$ 368, $\delta$ 505, $\epsilon$ 1211, $\epsilon$ 226, $\epsilon$ 257, $\epsilon$ 1033, $\epsilon$ 218, and $\epsilon$ 219). 

With the exception of manuscript 69, these were likely written in Calabria during the 12th century. They possess a peculiar text of a mainly "Western" type, showing special affinities to the Diatessaron and the Old Syriac. The group is named in memory of the scholar who first published their text. They are sometimes referred to as *fam.¹ cubed* or quoted using the symbol $\Phi$, although that symbol is properly reserved for the *Codex Beratinus* of the Gospels.

The text of the Ferrar group was published after the scholar's death by T.K. Abbott in 1877 in *A Collation of Four Important MSS. of the Gospels*. Further discussion of the group can be found in the works of Rendel Harris, specifically *The Origin of the Leicester Codex* (1887), *The Origin of the Ferrar Group* (1893), and *The Ferrar Group* (1900).
