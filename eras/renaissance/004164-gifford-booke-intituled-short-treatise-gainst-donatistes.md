## Overview of the Donatist Schism
The Donatist movement was a schism confined to Africa, described as an accidental quarrel resulting from the abuse of freedom. The sect derived its name from Donatus Magnus, who succeeded Majorinus as the Donatist bishop of Carthage. While opponents used the term "Donatists" as a reproach, the members of the sect repudiated this designation, referring to themselves instead as "Pars Donati" or "Pars Majorini."

The movement was characterized by a tension between the sect and the orthodox church. While the church of Rome followed a practice confirmed by the councils of Trent, Nice, and Arles, the Donatists maintained the sentiments of Cyprian and a significant portion of the primitive church.

## Leadership and Key Figures
Three prominent men named Donatus were associated with the movement:
*   **Donatus Magnus:** The bishop of Carthage who succeeded Majorinus. A man of great force of character, his influence helped the schism gain strength in response to the opposition it faced.
*   **Donatus of Casae Nigrae.**
*   **Donatus of Bagoi:** A leader of the Circumcelliones who was captured and executed around the year 350.

## The Circumcelliones and Social Unrest
The Donatist schism acquired a revolutionary aspect through its association with the Circumcelliones. These were bands of vagrant peasants (circum cellas), fugitive slaves, debtors, and political malcontents. Their violence reached levels that threatened civil war. 

In response to these "Donatist terrorists," a synod of Catholic bishops met around 348 to record gratitude for their effective official repression. This synod also adopted twelve canons of clerical discipline and declared against the rebaptism of anyone already baptized in the name of the Trinity.

## Imperial Relations and Persecution
The relationship between the Donatists and the Roman Empire shifted across different reigns:
*   **Constantine:** Initially, attempts to destroy the sect by force only intensified its fanaticism. By 321, Constantine shifted to a policy of toleration, granting the Donatists liberty to act according to their consciences and leaving the disputes to the judgment of God.
*   **Constans:** The successor of Constantine abandoned this policy. After failing to win over the sect with bribes, Constans resorted to persecution. This was driven partly by the need to preserve the empire against the lawlessness of the Circumcelliones.

In response to imperial pressure, the Donatists adopted a world-alien form of Christianity reminiscent of the days of persecution, questioning the emperor's authority over the church. This led to rigorous measures against the party; many bishops were victimized, and Donatus Magnus and others were banished from their sees.

Later imperial laws against the Donatists are recorded in the fifth title of the sixteenth book of the Theodosian Code, spanning from the year 400 to 428. The most severe and effectual of these was the 54th law, promulgated by Honorius in A.D. 414.

## Ecclesiastical Conflicts and the Conference of Carthage
The conflict involved significant theological and administrative disputes. St. Augustin, the bishop of Hippo and doctor of the system of predestination, justified the persecution of the Donatists in his old age, marking a change from his earlier declarations of pity and indulgence toward the Manichæans.

A pivotal event was the "Conference of Carthage" in 411, held by imperial command to terminate the schism. This assembly was one of the most important in the history of the African and Christian churches. During this conference, the Donatist bishops numbered 279, though they asserted their total number was at least 400. The Catholics had 286 present and 120 absent, with sixty-four vacant bishoprics.

Additionally, some Donatists committed suicide, citing the example of Razias from the 14th chapter of the second book of the Maccabees as justification.
