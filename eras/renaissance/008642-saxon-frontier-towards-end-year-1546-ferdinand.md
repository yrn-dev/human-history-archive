# Ferdinand I and the Bohemian Crisis of 1546–1547

## Accession to the Bohemian Throne
Following the death of King Louis, Bohemia entered a period of great disorder and near anarchy, as the previous two kings had primarily resided in Hungary. This lack of a strong indigenous government left the throne open to several candidates, including King Sigismund of Poland, the dukes Louis and William of Bavaria, various German princes, and Bohemian noblemen such as Leo of Rozmital. 

On October 23, 1526, a group of twenty-four electors—chosen in equal numbers from the three estates—elected Archduke Ferdinand of Austria (later Emperor Ferdinand I) as king. Ferdinand laid claim to the throne as the husband of Anna, the daughter of King Vladislav. This event marked the permanent accession of the Habsburg dynasty to the Bohemian throne. While Ferdinand remained devoted to Rome, he initially demonstrated moderation in religious matters.

## Dynastic Ambitions and Internal Conflict
Ferdinand's primary objective was to establish a hereditary right for his dynasty to the Bohemian throne, a goal he pursued with obstinacy. In 1541, after a great fire in Prague destroyed state documents, Ferdinand persuaded the estates to replace the original charter—which stated he had been king by election—with a new one claiming he was recognized as king due to the hereditary rights of his wife, Anna. This substitution caused significant dissatisfaction and contributed to subsequent unrest.

## The Military Crisis of 1546
In 1546, a German religious war began shortly after the death of Martin Luther (who died on February 18, 1546). Ferdinand sought to raise a Bohemian army to support his brother, Emperor Charles V. However, this task was complicated by the shifting views of Bohemian reformers. While some advanced Utraquists were similar to German Lutherans, the Bohemian Brethren—who had gained influence through the support of powerful nobles—strongly sympathized with German Protestants.

To secure the necessary troops, Ferdinand used a "tortuous policy," persuading the estates to vote for a general levy under the pretext that Bohemia was threatened by the Turks, as no armed force could be raised without the consent of the estates. Ferdinand designated the town of Kaaden, located on the Saxon frontier, as the meeting point for these troops. Upon his arrival, Ferdinand discovered that many nobles and cities, particularly those associated with the Bohemian Brethren, had failed to send men. Furthermore, many of the soldiers who did arrive were Protestants who sympathized with their co-religionists in Germany. Consequently, the Bohemian army refused to cross the Saxon frontier, and towards the end of the year 1546, Ferdinand was forced to disband his Bohemian forces.

## The Rising of 1547
Early in 1547, Ferdinand again requested an army from his Bohemian subjects to aid his brother. This request was obeyed only by a small number of Romanists and more retrograde Utraquists. The majority of Bohemians viewed this as an opportunity to recover ancient liberties that Ferdinand had encroached upon by curtailing old privileges and claiming hereditary rights to the crown.

In March 1547, without a royal summons, the estates met at Prague in a proceeding described as unconstitutional. This assembly was dominated by the representatives of the town of Prague and the knights and nobles of the Bohemian Brotherhood, giving the meeting a revolutionary character. This sentiment intensified following news that the elector of Saxony had achieved victory at Rochlitz. These events were later documented by Sixt of Ottersdorf in his work, *Memorials of the Troubled Years 1546 and 1547*, which describes the unsuccessful Bohemian rising against Ferdinand I.

## Later Imperial Transition
Ferdinand's involvement in German politics increased after 1547. Eventually, Emperor Charles V, suffering from gout and weary of the burdens of empire, abdicated. Charles resigned his sovereign rights in Germany to his brother Ferdinand, while leaving Spain and the Netherlands to his son Philip. Ferdinand thus took over the empire and Austria. However, the Austrian line remained obstinately Catholic and focused on its eastern frontiers and Hungarian affairs, including the payment of tribute to the Turks, which limited its influence over the Protestant-leaning north Germans.
