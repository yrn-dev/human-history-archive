# Chronicles of the Grey Friars

The *Chronicles of the Grey Friars* is a historical account published in 1852 by the Camden Society. The text serves as a source for documenting legal punishments and judicial executions in England during the early sixteenth century.

## Accounts of Judicial Punishment
The *Chronicles of the Grey Friars* contains specific records regarding the practice of boiling people to death, a punishment that was common on the continent and in England. While legislative notice of this practice in England is primarily found in an act passed in 1531 during the reign of Henry VIII—which mandated that convicted poisoners be boiled to death—the *Chronicles* provide evidence of the practice occurring prior to this statute.

Specifically, the *Chronicles* record an instance of boiling for poisoning that took place at Smithfield in the year 1522. According to the account, the condemned man was fastened to a chain and lowered into boiling water repeatedly until he died.

## Historical Context of Boiling in England
The records in the *Chronicles* align with a broader pattern of English judicial history where boiling was frequently mentioned as a punishment for coining before it was formally codified for poisoning. Following the 1531 statute, which categorized poisoning as treason, several other high-profile executions occurred:

*   **Richard Roose (or Coke):** A cook who poisoned food intended for the poor of the parish of Lambeth and the household of the bishop of Rochester. He was found guilty of treason and publicly boiled at Smithfield without benefit of clergy.
*   **Unnamed Maid-servant:** Boiled at King's Lynn in 1531 for poisoning her mistress.
*   **Margaret Davy:** A servant who was boiled at Smithfield in 1542 for poisoning her employer.

The legal authority for this specific punishment was eventually repealed in 1547 during the reign of Edward VI.

## The Grey Friars in Religious History
While the *Chronicles* provide a record of legal executions, the Grey Friars themselves were part of a broader religious movement. The appearance of the Grey Friars, along with the Black Friars, began in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries. This emergence represented an early phase of the Reformation process occurring within the church, preceding the sixteenth-century impetus that led to the foundation of the Society of the Jesuits by Saint Ignatius of Loyola (Inigo Lopez de Recalde).
