# ARUNDELL OF WARDOUR AND ASSAMESE

## Thomas Arundell, 1st Baron Arundell of Wardour
Thomas Arundell (c. 1562–1639) was the son of Margaret, daughter of Sir Henry Willoughby, and Sir Mathew Arundell of Wardour Castle in Wiltshire. His father belonged to the ancient Arundells of Lanherne family in Cornwall. Around 1579, Queen Elizabeth personally recommended Thomas to Emperor Rudolph II. While serving with imperial troops in Hungary against the Turks, Arundell distinguished himself; notably, on August 13, 1595, he captured the enemy's banner during the siege of Esztergom (also known as Gran).

In December 1595, Rudolph II created him a count of the Holy Roman Empire. Upon his return to England in January 1596—following a shipwreck in which he barely survived—his foreign title caused significant friction. English peers, who typically gave courtesy precedence to foreign nobles, felt jealousy, and his father disinherited him due to his superior rank. Queen Elizabeth expressed strong displeasure, stating that faithful subjects should keep their eyes at home rather than gaze upon foreign crowns, and committed him to the Fleet. 

Arundell was liberated in April after an examination, but was confined again in April 1597. He was declared innocent of all charges except for "practising to contrive the justification of his vain title with Ministers beyond the seas." He was released in December and placed under his father's care, only to be arrested again the following year on accusations of conspiracy against the government. Despite his petitions for a sea expedition—claiming his goal was "honour which some base minds call ambition"—he was refused. He was restored to favor in 1599 and was created Baron Arundell of Wardour by James I on May 4, 1605. He later faced temporary suspicion during the Gunpowder Plot and encountered further trouble in 1623.

## Henry Arundell, 3rd Baron Arundell of Wardour
Henry Arundell (c. 1607–1694) was the son of Blanche, daughter of Edward, earl of Worcester, and Thomas, 2nd baron. Born on July 21, 1607, he succeeded to the family estates and title in 1643. A Roman Catholic and strong royalist, he supported the king and recaptured Wardour Castle from parliamentarians in 1644, after the site had been taken the previous year despite a brave defense by his mother.

In 1648, he was listed as one of the delinquents exempted from pardon in proposals sent to Charles in the Isle of Wight. Although his estates were confiscated, he was permitted to compound for them around 1653 for £35,000. In 1652, he was arrested after being the second in a duel that resulted in a death. He attempted to plead his peerage during his 1653 trial, but the privilege was disallowed because the House of Lords had been abolished.

Following the Restoration, he regained his estates and became master of the horse to Henrietta Maria in 1663. He was trusted by the king regarding alliances with France and the restoration of the Roman Catholic religion. In 1669, he joined a secret council assembled by Charles II and traveled to France, ostensibly for Henrietta Maria's funeral, but actually to negotiate the treaties of Dover (1670) with Louis XIV. In 1676, he was privy to James's negotiations with Rome via Coleman.

In 1678, Titus Oates accused him of participating in the popish plot. He was one of five Roman Catholic peers imprisoned in the Tower in October, found guilty of high treason by the Middlesex grand jury, and impeached by parliament. While Lord Stafford was executed in December 1680, the proceedings were interrupted, and the three surviving peers, including Arundell, were released on bail on February 12, 1684. Upon the accession of James II on May 22, 1685, the charge was annulled.

## The Assamese Language
Assamese is one of three immediate descendants of Magadhi Prakrit, which was headquartered in south Behar near Patna. The language developed as Magadhi Prakrit spread eastwards through Northern Bengal into Assam. While the language of Northern Bengal is often treated as a dialect of Bengali, it actually serves as a connecting link between Bihari and Assamese. Assamese and Northern Bengali frequently agree in grammar with Oriya, rather than standard Bengali.

The vernacular Assamese possesses a close affinity to Bengali, though it differs by substituting a guttural *h* for the Bengali *h* or *sh*, and *s* for the Bengali *ch*. Due to this resemblance, Bengali served as the official and court language of the province under British rule for a time. However, a government resolution in 1873 re-established Assamese as the language of public business and official life.

In terms of phonetics, Assamese follows Bengali in its accentuation. Unlike literary Bengali, which preserves full word forms, Assamese literature employs colloquial words written as they are pronounced.

## Assamese People and Literature
The population of Assam was 6,126,343 according to the 1901 census, consisting of 3,429,099 Hindus, 1,581,317 Mahommedans, and 1,068,334 Animists. This total included 775,844 foreigners who immigrated as tea-garden coolies. Native-born Assamese experienced a serious decrease in some districts, such as Nowgong, due to diseases including *kalaazar*.

The Assamese are a distinct race from Bengalis, with origins linked to tribes from the Chinese frontier, Burma, or the Himalayan ranges. The Ahoms (or Ahams), an offshoot of the Shan race of northern Burma, were the last conquerors of Assam before the Burmese. While they preserved ancient institutions, they eventually became completely Hinduized, including the nobility and kings who ceased eating beef.

Assamese national literature is noted for its independent growth and its strength in history, featuring chronicles spanning 600 years. In poetry, the Vaishnava reformer Sankar Deb, who flourished approximately 450 years ago, wrote a translation of the *Bhagavata Purana*. Around the same period, Ananta Kandali translated the *Ramayana* and the *Mahabharata*. Additionally, there are forty or fifty known dramatic works in the vernacular, some dating to the time of Sankar Deb, and various translations of Sanskrit works on medicine.
