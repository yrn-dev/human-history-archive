# Boiardo and the Influence of the Iliad

Matteo Boiardo was an Italian poet whose work, most notably the great poem *Orlando Inamorato*, is regarded as one of the most significant contributions to Italian literature. The poem is recognized as the first example of the romantic epic that was worthy of serving as a model, and it is believed to have undoubtedly influenced Ariosto's *Orlando Furioso*.

## Composition and Inspiration
Boiardo composed nearly all of his works, including *Orlando Inamorato*, for the amusement of his court and Duke Hercules. Although written for the court, the poems were not composed within its precincts; Boiardo typically retired to his estates, such as Scandiano, to focus on his writing. 

The poet incorporated elements of his own surroundings into his work, inserting descriptions of the agreeable environs of his chateau. Furthermore, it is stated that he drew the names of several of his heroes—including Agramant, Sacripant, Gradasse, and Mandricardo—from the names of his own peasants. Boiardo felt that the uncouthness of these names made them appropriate for Saracen warriors.

## Relationship to the Iliad
According to the authority of Mazzuchell and Gravina, and subsequent writers, Boiardo intended for the *Iliad* of Homer to serve as the model for his poem. This perspective suggests that *Orlando Inamorato* is a sort of reflex image of the *Iliad*. Specific parallels cited include:
* The city of Paris is besieged in a manner similar to the city of Troy.
* The character of Angelica occupies the position held by Helen in the Homeric epic.

Despite these proposed structural and thematic parallels, the actual subject matter of Boiardo's poem is derived from the *Fabulous Chronicle* of the pseudo-Turpin. While the *Fabulous Chronicle* provides the source material, there is little resemblance between its detailed plot and that of Boiardo's work, with the exception of the inclusion of principal warriors such as Oliver, Roland, and Charlemagne.

## Publication History
Boiardo did not live to see the completion of *Orlando Inamorato*. The poem was printed in Scandiano the year following his death, under the supervision of his son, Count Camillo. While the title of the book does not include a date, a Latin letter from Antonia Caraffa di Reggio that is prefixed to the poem is dated the kalends of June 1495. A second edition was published in Venice before the year 1500, and the work was reprinted in Venice twice more during the following twenty years.
