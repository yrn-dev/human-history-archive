# The War with Sweden (1657-60) and the Reign of Leopold I

The war between Austria and Sweden, which took place from 1657 to 1660, is identified as a conflict of significant importance to Austria itself. This conflict occurred during the early years of the reign of Leopold I, who had succeeded his father, Ferdinand, and was crowned emperor in 1658. Leopold I's reign lasted for 48 years and was instrumental in determining the external policy and internal character of the monarchy.

## Outcomes of the Conflict
The war with Sweden concluded with the peace of Oliva. This diplomatic settlement resulted in two primary achievements for the Austrian monarchy: the independence of Poland was secured, and the frontier of Hungary was safeguarded.

## Context of Leopold I's External Policy
While the struggle with Sweden was of direct importance to Austria, Leopold I's external affairs were characterized by several other major conflicts. He engaged in a long struggle with France, driven by the ambitions of Louis XIV, which eventually culminated in the War of Spanish Succession. However, this French conflict is viewed as belonging more to the history of Europe and Germany than to that of Austria proper.

Of equal or greater importance to the state were the campaigns against the Turks. These occurred in two primary phases: from 1662 to 1664 and from 1683 to 1699. These wars were essential in driving Ottoman power out of Hungary and established the Austrian position regarding the Slav peoples of the Balkans and Turkey for the following century.

## Related Conflicts and the Ottoman Threat
The first of the Turkish wars was triggered by Ottoman aggression in Transylvania. This conflict ended on August 1, 1664, with a victory by Montecuculi over the grand vizier at St Gothard on the Raab. Despite this victory, the general political climate prevented Leopold I from fully capitalizing on the success. The resulting peace of Vasvar, signed on August 10, recognized Transylvania as an independent principality and left the Turks in possession of the fortress of Ersekujvar (Neuhaeusel) and Nagyvarad (Grosswardein).

A subsequent war with the Turks began in 1683. This was the direct result of Leopold's internal policies in Hungary, specifically the suppression of the constitution in 1658 and the persecution of Protestants, which had sparked a widespread conspiracy. Although the constitution and palatinate were restored with some concessions to Protestants after a period of arbitrary government between 1672 and 1679, discontent persisted. Invited by Hungarian malcontents and encouraged by Louis XIV, the Turks invaded Hungary and reached the walls of Vienna. The city was saved on September 12, 1683, by a victory gained over the Turks by John Sobieski, King of Poland.
