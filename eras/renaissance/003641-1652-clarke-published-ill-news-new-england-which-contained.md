# Ill News from New England

*Ill News from New England* is a publication authored by Clarke and published in 1652 while he was in England on public business. The work serves as both a historical record of religious persecution in the American colonies and a philosophical argument for religious freedom.

## Context and Events at Lynn
The publication was prompted by events that occurred in 1651 in Lynn, Massachusetts. Clarke, along with Joseph Crandall and Obadiah Holmes—all members of the Newport church—conducted a religious visit to the area. During this visit, the men held a meeting in a private residence, which led to their arrest.

Following their arrest, the authorities compelled Clarke and his companions to attend the church services of the standing order. Because they held an unlawful meeting and refused to participate quietly in the public service, they were subjected to imprisonment, fines, and other forms of maltreatment.

## Content and Purpose
In *Ill News from New England*, Clarke provided an impressive account of the proceedings taken against himself and his brethren during the events at Lynn. Beyond the narrative of their treatment, the text contains an earnest and well-reasoned plea for liberty of conscience.

## Related Religious Movements in New England
The experiences of Clarke and his associates were part of a broader landscape of religious tension in the region. In 1649, a group of Baptists led by Obadiah Holmes had established themselves at Rehoboth. However, the intolerance of the authorities made the prosecution of their work impracticable, leading these Massachusetts Baptists to become members of the Newport church.

Other shifts in religious conviction occurred during this era. By 1653, Henry Dunster, the first president of the college at Cambridge (Harvard), became convinced that baptism should be reserved for visible believers only. Due to his refusal to hold these views in abeyance, he relinquished his position in 1654 under circumstances of considerable hardship.

Additionally, the Providence church experienced a schism in 1652. A majority of members, including Gregory Dexter, William Wickenden, and Chad Brown, contended for the laying on of hands as indispensable to fellowship and for general redemption. This was opposed by a minority led by Thomas Olney, who maintained particular redemption and rejected the laying on of hands as an ordinance. While Olney's party became extinct after his death in 1682, the surviving church later became involved in Universalism and Socinianism, exerting influence in New York, Connecticut, and Newport. Gregory Dexter eventually became a leading statesman in Providence Plantations and Rhode Island, alongside Williams and Clarke.
