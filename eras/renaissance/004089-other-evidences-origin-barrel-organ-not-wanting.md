# Barrel-Organ

The barrel-organ is a small portable organ that is played mechanically by the turning of a handle. It is known by various names across different languages, including "grinder-organ," "street-organ," "hand-organ," and "Dutch organ" in English; *orgue de Barbarie*, *orgue d'Allemagne*, *orgue mecanique*, *cabinet d'orgue*, and *serinette* in French; *Drehorgel* and *Leierkasten* in German; and *organetto a manovella* and *organo tedesco* in Italian.

## Mechanism and Construction
The instrument derives its name from the cylinder, or barrel, which features pins and staples of various lengths pricked out at definite intervals according to the musical scheme. As the barrel rotates, these pins and staples raise balanced keys. These keys are connected via a simple mechanism to the valves of the pipes, which open to admit a stream of air from the wind-chest.

The rotation of the cylinder is achieved by a handle attached to a shaft, which utilizes a worm working in a fine-toothed gear on the barrel-head. This same motion operates the bellows through connecting rods and cranks on the shaft, forcing air into a reservoir and then into the wind-chest where the pipes are grouped. Typically, one complete revolution of the barrel plays one complete tune. To change the tune, the performer uses a notch-pin in the barrel-head, which contains as many notches as there are tunes.

Standard street barrel-organs generally possess a diatonic scale with a few accidentals (usually C#, G#, and F#), with a compass ranging from 24 to 34 notes. They typically feature two stops: one for closed wooden pipes and one for open metal pipes.

## Origins and Early Development
The origin of the barrel-organ is established in the Netherlands during the middle of the 15th century. This period saw a significant impulse toward automatic contrivances—including clocks, carillons, and speaking animals—driven by the patronage of the dukes of Burgundy. 

Belgian archives preserve letters patent for an organ-builder named Jehan van Steenken (also called Aren), designated as "Master of organs which play of themselves" (*Meester van orgelen spelende bij hen selven*). Unlike portable street organs, Steenken's instruments were imposing; documents detail payments made to him for transporting organs from Bruges to Brussels. For his services to Philip the Good, the duke of Burgundy, Steenken received an annual pension of fifty Rhenish florins, provided he submitted any future instruments he created to his liege.

By 1615, Solomon de Caus published detailed diagrams and descriptions of a large stationary barrel-organ powered by hydraulic power. His instrument, titled "Machine par laquelle l'on fera sonner un jeu d'orgues par le moyen de l'eau," was built into a wall one foot thick. De Caus's design featured a barrel divided into bars, with each bar further divided into eight beats for quavers. The drum was pierced with holes and utilized movable pins, allowing the performer to rearrange them to change the tune.

## Historical Use and Variations
In 1598, an inventory of keyboard instruments belonging to the duke of Modena listed two entries for an *organo Tedesco*. In England, these were referred to as "Dutch organs," a term that persisted even for the small hand-organs used by itinerant musicians. References to these instruments appear in descriptions of the Netherlands' customs and in accounts of the Bartholomew Fair at the start of the 18th century, where a "Dutch organ" was noted as part of a band hired at fair booths.

Various specialized versions of the instrument existed:
* **Church Organs:** More elaborate barrel-organs featuring three or four cylinders set in a circular revolving frame were used in chapels and churches for playing voluntaries, chants, and hymns during the 18th and early 19th centuries. Examples include an instrument built by Wright for Fulham church and a large four-barrel instrument constructed by Bishop for Northallerton church in 1820.
* **Bird-Organs:** A very small variety known as the bird-organ (*serinette*, *turlutaine*, or *merline*) was used in the 18th and 19th centuries. One example in the Brussels Conservatoire is shaped like a book titled "*Le chant des oiseaux, Tome vi.*" It measures approximately 8 x 5-1/2 x 2-3/4 inches, plays eight tunes, and contains ten pewter stopped pipes.

Notable historical mentions include a piece of music for a small barrel-organ written by Mozart. In 1737, Horace Walpole described pursuing a high-quality instrument capable of playing eight tunes, which could be operated by "the most ignorant person" and had its tunes changed. This specific organ eventually fetched £1000 in a lottery.
