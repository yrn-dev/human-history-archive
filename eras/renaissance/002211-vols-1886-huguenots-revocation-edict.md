# The Revocation of the Edict of Nantes

The revocation of the Edict of Nantes was a pivotal event in French history during the reign of Louis XIV. This action, which altogether withdrew the edict in 1685, ended the legal settlement that had previously granted privileges to the French Huguenots.

## Political and Religious Context
The church had consistently opposed the settlement of the Edict of Nantes and had successfully altered it in numerous points over time. The eventual revocation was driven by the autocratic temper of Louis XIV and a new religious zeal that aligned the king's interests with those of the church. Consequently, the privileges of the French Huguenots were gradually decreased before the edict was fully withdrawn in 1685.

## Consequences for France
The results of the revocation were described as ruinous for France. The country suffered the loss of many thousands of its best citizens who emigrated. Furthermore, the blow against Protestantism damaged France's diplomatic standing in Europe by depriving the nation of Protestant alliances that had previously served as significant diplomatic support. This internal shift, combined with the English Revolution of 1688, transformed England from a wavering ally into one of France's most determined enemies.

## Impact on Individuals and Families
The revocation triggered a widespread exodus of Huguenots to various parts of Europe and beyond:

*   **The Low Countries and Holland:** Many French families emigrated to the Low Countries. For example, the family of the Dutch Arabic scholar Reinhart Pieter Anne Dozy emigrated to the Low Countries following the revocation, though some members of the family had settled in Holland as early as 1647.
*   **Switzerland:** Adrien Daille, the son of the French Protestant divine Jean Daille, retired to Zurich upon the revocation of the edict. Similarly, the father of Pierre Desmaiseaux, a minister of the reformed church, was forced to leave France and take refuge in Geneva.
*   **England:** The revocation led to the establishment of the Spitalfields colony in England. Other families, such as the de Blossets from Touraine, left France during this period; this family included the ancestors of Selina Peckwell, the mother of historian George Grote.

## Historiography of the Huguenots
The history of the Huguenots and the impact of the revocation have been the subject of extensive scholarly work. Henry Martyn Baird (1832–1906), an American historian and professor at New York University, produced a comprehensive study of the movement in three parts. His work includes *History of the Rise of the Huguenots of France* (1879), *The Huguenots and Henry of Navarre* (1886), and *The Huguenots and the Revocation of the Edict of Nantes* (1895). Baird's scholarship is noted for its judicial temper and painstaking thoroughness. Additionally, Charles Washington Baird (1828–1887) authored *The History of the Huguenot Emigration to America* (1885), though the work remained unfinished at the time of his death.
