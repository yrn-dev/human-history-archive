# Catholic Faith

The Catholic faith was a central force in the political and religious conflicts of early modern Europe, serving as a primary driver of state policy, international alliances, and internal social struggle. During the period of the Counter-Reformation, the Roman Catholic Church and its defenders sought to maintain supremacy over Protestantism, leading to widespread conflict across various European territories.

## The Counter-Reformation in Austria and Styria
In the Austrian dominions, the religious landscape was deeply divided. During the reign of Emperor Maximilian II (1564–1576), a liberal policy was maintained to preserve peace, though religious liberty was not established on a permanent basis. At that time, nearly the whole of Styria and the entirety of Austria were mainly Lutheran, while Catholicism was almost exclusively confined to the mountains of Tirol.

The situation changed with the accession of Rudolph II (1576–1612), a fanatical Spanish Catholic, who encouraged the Jesuits to advance the Counter-Reformation. This effort continued under subsequent rulers. Ferdinand II (1619–1637), a strong-willed leader, was resolved to return Germany to the Catholic faith. While serving as the archduke of Styria, Ferdinand II crushed out Protestantism within that duchy. Upon being elected king of Bohemia in 1618, he sought to establish the rule of the Jesuits there, an attempt that triggered the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War.

In Styria, the pressure on Protestants was evident in the actions of the government. In 1598, Archduke Ferdinand issued an edict of banishment against Protestant professors and preachers. This forced individuals such as Kepler to flee to the Hungarian frontier, although some were later reinstated through the favor of the Jesuits.

## International Conflicts and the Role of Spain and France
The struggle between Catholicism and Protestantism extended beyond national borders, evolving into a broader European conflict where neutrality was often impossible. Philip II of Spain viewed himself as the absolute defender of Catholicism in Europe. This conviction led to a concentrated struggle in the Netherlands, where Protestants attacked Philip's dominions to prevent the Catholic Church from achieving global victory. Philip's determination to suppress every attempt at revolt eventually resulted in the loss of the northern Netherlands and the creation of the United Provinces.

In France, the Catholic faith was utilized by the monarchy to combat spirits of independence and disunion. Louis XIV, drifting toward the propitiation of the Papacy in his later years, waged war against Protestantism. His government employed "dragonnades," a form of persecution where soldiers were quartered in Protestant homes to disorder their lives and insult their women. Parents were forced to provide Catholic instruction to their children or provide none at all. These persecutions drove many valuable subjects to flee France, taking their arts and industries with them.

## Religious Struggle in Belgium
In Belgium, the conflict between faiths manifested as a war against Catholicism. During a period of anarchy, Calvinists took control of Ghent, Antwerp, and Brussels. In Ghent, a Calvinistic republic was established where leaders used terror and rigorous persecution against Catholics. While some figures, such as William the Silent, initially attempted to introduce a "peace of religion" to allow for the co-ordinate recognition of both faiths, the Protestant ministers rejected this, seeking instead the complete supremacy of their own religion.

## Political and Social Tensions
The influence of the Catholic Church often intersected with ethnic and nationalist tensions. In Austria, the Church's influence was viewed as more favorable to Slav races, partly because they provided more candidates for ordination than Germans. Conversely, Germans in Austria tended to exalt nationality over religion. The Roman Catholic Church's refusal to acquiesce to this nationalist principle, combined with an alliance between the Clerical party and Slav parties, led to the "Los von Rom" movement in 1898. This political demonstration resulted in large numbers of Germans in Styria and other territories leaving the Catholic Church for either Old Catholicism or Protestantism.

Within the church hierarchy, figures like Melchior Klesl, a convert to Roman Catholicism under the influence of the Jesuits, worked to forward the Counter-Reformation in Austria. As Bishop of Vienna and an advisor to Emperor Matthias, Klesl attempted to find a peace between Catholics and Protestants to strengthen the imperial position, though he remained anxious to preserve the dominance of the Roman Catholic Church.
