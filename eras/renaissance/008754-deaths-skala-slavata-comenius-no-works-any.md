# The Decline and Revival of the Bohemian Language

The trajectory of the Bohemian language from the 17th century through the early 19th century was marked by a period of significant decline following the loss of Bohemian independence in 1620, followed by a modest revival driven by the romantic movement.

## The Period of Decline
The year 1620 serves as the starting point for the decline of the national tongue. By the 18th century, the Bohemian language had become almost extinct as a written medium. This decline was further exacerbated by the new Austrian government, which sought to discourage the use of the language in every possible way.

Prior to this era, the language had been greatly influenced and refined by the "Bohemian Brethren." This community produced nearly all of its theological and historical works in the national language, including the *Bible of Kralice*, a joint effort by several divines of the brotherhood first printed in Moravia in 1593. Other significant contributors included Brother Gregory, who wrote on theological matters such as *On good and evil priests*, and various other brothers including Lucas, Blahoslav, Jaffet, and Bishop Augusta. However, many of these historical works perished in the 17th century during the destruction of nearly all anti-Roman books written in Bohemia.

## Late 17th Century Authors
Despite the general decline after 1620, three notable writers produced works in the Bohemian language. The most prominent was John Amos Komensky, known as Comenius (1592-1670), who authored 142 works. Among his native-language writings, the allegorical tale *Labyrinth of the World* is considered perhaps the most famous work written in Bohemian, alongside numerous educational and devotional texts.

Two historians also wrote after 1620, representing opposing political views:

*   **Paul Skala ze Zhore (1582-c. 1640):** An official for Frederick of the Palatinate (the "winter king"), Skala wrote his historical works after his political activity ceased while living in Saxony. His output included a book of chronological tables and a vast ten-volume manuscript titled *Histoire cirkevni* (history of the church), which covered both ecclesiastical and political matters, specifically events from 1602 to 1623.
*   **William Count Slavata (1572-1652):** A servant of the house of Habsburg and a political opponent of Skala, Slavata was one of the officials thrown from the windows of the Hradcany palace during the 1618 uprising. In 1637, he published his *Pamety* (memoirs) regarding the events of 1618 and 1619. He also composed a larger work of fourteen manuscript volumes titled *Historicke Spisovani* (historical works).

## The 19th-Century Revival
Following the deaths of Comenius, Skala, and Slavata, no works of importance were written in the Bohemian language for a considerable period. A shift occurred at the beginning of the 19th century with the onset of the romantic movement.

The initial revival was modest and consisted primarily of translations from foreign languages. Joseph Dobrovsky (1753-1829) was the first writer to draw attention back to the nearly forgotten language. Although Dobrovsky wrote a history of Bohemian literature and a grammar of the language, these were mostly composed in Latin or German. His contributions to the Bohemian language itself were limited to a collection of letters and several essays published in the *Casopis Musea Kralovstvi Ceskeho* (Journal of the Bohemian Museum).
