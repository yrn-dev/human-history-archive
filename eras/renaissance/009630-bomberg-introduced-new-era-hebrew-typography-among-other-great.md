# Daniel Bomberg and Hebrew Typography

## Overview of Daniel Bomberg
Daniel Bomberg was a prominent Christian printer specializing in Hebrew books. His primary professional activities took place in Venice, where he operated between 1516 and 1549, the year of his death. Bomberg is credited with introducing a new era in the field of Hebrew typography through several significant publishing enterprises.

## Major Publications
Among his most notable contributions to the archive of printed texts, Bomberg published the *editio princeps* between 1516 and 1517. This work was a rabbinical Bible, featuring the Hebrew text accompanied by rabbinical commentaries and other related materials. Following this, Bomberg produced the first complete edition of the Talmud, which was published between 1520 and 1523.

## Context of Hebrew Philology and Grammar
While Bomberg advanced the printing of Hebrew texts, other scholars contributed to the linguistic understanding of the language during and before his era. Joseph Kimhi, a native of southern Spain who settled in Provence, was among the first to present the results of Hebraic philology—as expounded by Spanish Jews in Arabic treatises—using the Hebrew language. Influenced by Latin grammar, Joseph Kimhi introduced the innovation of dividing Hebrew vowels into five short and five long vowels, moving away from previous systems that identified seven vowels without distinguishing their quantity. His textbook, *Sefer Ha-Zikkaron* ("Book of Remembrance"), introduced a new classification of verb stems that later scholars retained. He also authored *Sefer Ha-Galuy* ("Book of Demonstration"), which critiqued the philological work of Menahem b. Saruq and the French Talmud scholar R. Jacob Tam.

Additionally, Moses Kimhi authored a Hebrew grammar known as *Mahalak Shebile Ha-daat* (or simply *Mahalak*). This work served as an elementary introduction to Hebrew study, focusing on paradigms and indispensable rules. Moses Kimhi was the first to use the verb *paqadh* as a model for conjugation and established the sequence for enumerating stem-forms that is now common. His handbook became a favorite manual for non-Judaic scholars in the first half of the 16th century after its first edition was published in Pesaro in 1508. It was later translated into Latin by Sebastian Münster and received Hebrew explanations from Elias Levita. Moses Kimhi also wrote commentaries on the biblical books of Ezra, Nehemiah, and Proverbs; these were included in the great rabbinical bibles that were falsely attributed to Abraham ibn Ezra.

## The Massoretic Text
The printed editions of the Hebrew text of the Old Testament, such as those produced in the era of typography, are based on the Massoretic text. The date of the Massoretic text is generally placed between the 6th and 8th centuries of the Christian era, though it is probable that the text became fixed as early as the 2nd century A.D. Since the fixing of the Massoretic text, the transmission and preservation of these sacred books have been conducted with fidelity and care, resulting in a text that has undergone practically no change of real importance.
