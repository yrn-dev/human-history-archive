# St John's College, Cambridge

St John's College, Cambridge, is an academic institution that has served as a center for learning and a recipient of significant historical collections. In the early 16th century, it was described as the largest and most learned college in either of the universities.

## Academic History and Notable Scholars
The college has been associated with numerous scholars, divines, and figures of historical importance. In the 1530s, Roger Ascham entered the college around 1530 at the age of fifteen. During his time there, Ascham was influenced by Sir Thomas Smith and John Cheke, and he was guided by Robert Pember, who was noted for his facility in the Greek tongue. Ascham earned his B.A. on February 18, 1534/5, during the mastership of Dr. Nicholas Metcalfe.

Other notable figures associated with the college include:
* **William Cecil**: The only son of his father, Cecil entered St John's in May 1535 at age fourteen. He studied under John Cheke and Roger Ascham, acquiring an unusual knowledge of Greek. He resided at the college for six years before being removed to Gray's Inn in 1541 without taking a degree.
* **William Fulke**: A Puritan divine born in London, Fulke became a fellow of the college in 1564. He was involved in the "vestiarian" controversy, persuading the college to discard the surplice, which led to a temporary expulsion. He later returned in 1567 as a preacher and Hebrew lecturer.
* **Thomas Cartwright**: An English Puritan divine who studied divinity at St John's. Following the accession of Mary, he was forced to leave the university, but he later returned upon the accession of Elizabeth to be elected a fellow of the college.
* **William Henry Bennett**: A former fellow of St John's College, Cambridge, who served as a Professor of Old Testament Exegesis in New and Hackney Colleges, London, and a lecturer in Hebrew at Firth College, Sheffield.

## Library and Collections
St John's College has been the beneficiary of significant private libraries, reflecting a historical trend toward public ownership of book collections. Specifically, the library of Archbishop Williams was transferred to St John's College, Cambridge. This occurred during a period when many large book-buyers of the 16th, 17th, and 18th centuries sought public resting-places for their treasures.
