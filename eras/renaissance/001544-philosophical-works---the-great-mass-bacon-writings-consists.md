# Philosophical Works of Francis Bacon

The writings of Francis Bacon are categorized into three primary classes: professional, literary, and philosophical. His philosophical output consists largely of fragments or treatises that were either integral parts of his comprehensive scheme, the *Instauratio Magna*, or closely connected to it.

## Classification of Philosophical Writings
Bacon's philosophical works are divided into three specific heads:
1. Writings originally intended for the *Instauratio* but later thrown aside or superseded.
2. Works connected with the *Instauratio* but not directly included in its plan.
3. Writings that actually formed part of the *Instauratio Magna*.

Among the works connected to but not included in the *Instauratio* is the philosophic romance *New Atlantis*. This work describes an ideal state where political machinery and state guidance implement the principles of the new philosophy. While Bacon intended to add a second part regarding the laws of a model commonwealth, it was never written. Other notable tracts in this category include *De Principiis atque Originibus secundum Fabulas Cupidinis et Caeli*, which uses mythological stories to discuss the nature of primitive matter, the origin of things, and the limits of science, providing notices of Telesio and Democritus. Additionally, Bacon wrote the *Cogitationes de Natura Rerum* (likely around 1605) and a treatise on the theory of tides titled *De Fluxu et Refluxu Maris* (likely around 1616).

## Intellectual Contribution and Influence
Bacon is not regarded as a great constructive philosopher or a lonely pioneer of modern thought, but he is credited with summing up the faults of medieval thought revealed by the widening of knowledge. He was part of a broader 16th-century European movement striving toward new intellectual activity.

In the field of natural science, Bacon's influence extended across Europe, with references to his work appearing in the writings of Italian, German, French, and English scientists. In France, the encyclopaedists and Voltaire hailed him as "le pere de la philosophie experimentale," and the *Encyclopedie* described him as the most eloquent, universal, and greatest of philosophers. Other thinkers, such as Huygens and Leibnitz, provided critical testimony; Leibnitz referred to him as "divini ingenii vir."

In England, the *New Atlantis* provided significant impetus for the foundation of the Royal Society. Dr. Thomas Sprat, the society's first historian, asserted that Bacon possessed the "true imagination of the whole extent" of the enterprise and provided the best arguments for the experimental method of natural philosophy.

## Critique of Greek Philosophy
Bacon sought important reforms in physical science, specifically censuring the deductive methods applied to physical subjects by Aristotle, Plato, and other Greek philosophers. He argued that these philosophers were sophists in the sense that they employed many-sided argumentation. While this critique was pertinent to the physical sciences, it was less applicable to the moral sciences. The primary fault Bacon identified in these earlier works was the tendency to admit inaccurate or false axioms too hastily without dispute.

## Other Works and Bibliography
Beyond his philosophical treatises, Bacon produced professional legal works, including the *Maxims of Law*, the *Reading on the Statute of Uses*, and a possibly spurious treatise on the *Use of the Law*. While lacking the technical detail of Sir Edward Coke's reports, Bacon's legal writings are noted for their comprehensive intelligence of abstract principles of jurisprudence.

His literary works include the *Essays*—which grew from ten in the first edition to fifty-eight by 1625—and the *History of Henry VII*. He also wrote *De Sapientia Veterum*, exploring the idea that Greek mythological fables were allegorical and concealed deep philosophical truths.

The classical edition of his works was produced by D. D. Heath, J. Spedding, and R. L. Ellis (1st ed. 1857, 2nd ed. 1870), with philosophical writings contained in volumes i-iii. Other editions include a 17-volume set by B. Montagu (1825-1834) and a 1905 reprint by J. M. Robertson.
