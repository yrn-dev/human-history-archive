# The Early Baptist Movement in England and Holland

## Origins and the Role of John Smyth
The modern Baptist movement in England emerged as a break from Brownism. A central figure in this transition was John Smyth (also referred to as John Smith), who died in 1612. Smyth had been an ordained clergyman and was appointed as the preacher of the city of Lincoln in 1600. However, by 1605 or 1606, he became a separatist. Due to the pressure of persecution, Smyth emigrated to Amsterdam along with the Gainsborough Independents.

In Amsterdam, Smyth joined the "Ancient" church alongside Morton and Thomas Helwys (ca. 1560–ca. 1616). In 1609, Smyth came under the influence of Mennonite teaching and separated from the Independents. He performed a self-baptism, leading to his designation as the "Se-baptist." Helwys and others were also baptized, likely following the Mennonite or Anabaptist practice of pouring.

## The First English Baptist Church
These individuals established the first English Baptist Church. In 1611, the group published a "declaration of faith of English people remaining at Amsterdam in Holland." This confession of faith outlined specific views on baptism and church governance:

*   **Baptism:** The church taught that baptism should be administered only upon a member's confession of faith and sins, as wrought by the preaching of the gospel. They viewed baptism or washing with water as an outward manifestation of walking in newness of life and dying unto sin. Consequently, they taught that baptism does not appertain to infants.
*   **Church Authority:** The group held that no single church should claim any prerogative over another.
*   **Religious Liberty:** The confession stated that the magistrate should not compel individuals to follow any specific form of religion, nor meddle with matters of conscience or religion. This is recognized as the first known expression of absolute liberty of conscience within a confession of faith.

## Expansion to England and the General Baptists
Following the death of Smyth in Holland, Thomas Helwys returned to England in 1612 with his church. He established the first Baptist church to worship on English soil, which met in London at Newgate Street. This congregation became the origin of the "General" Baptist denomination.

The followers of Helwys were Arminians and strongly repudiated the Calvinistic doctrine of predestination, a point of contention that distinguished them from other Independents. Beyond their theological differences, they differed on the power of the magistrate regarding conscience and belief. According to Professor Masson, the absolute doctrine of Religious Liberty first flashed out in England from their "little dingy meeting house." One member of this church was Leonard Busher, who authored "Religious Peace: or a Plea for Liberty of Conscience."
