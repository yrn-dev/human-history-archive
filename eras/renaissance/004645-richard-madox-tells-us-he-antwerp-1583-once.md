# Antwerp and Associated Historical Figures

## The City of Antwerp in Historical Context
Antwerp has appeared as a significant location across various historical eras. In the late 16th century, it was a center of literary and linguistic activity. Between 1555 and 1583, Cornelis van Ghistele of Antwerp translated works by Ovid, Horace, Virgil, and Terence into a "miserable dialect" of Burgundian. During this same period, the city was the site of the publication of *Pegasides Pleyn* (the Palace of Maidens) between 1582 and 1583, a sixteen-book didactic poem by Jean Baptista Houwaert concerning the variety of earthly love.

In more recent military history, Antwerp was a bombarded town where residents and officials faced extreme danger. During such conflicts, the Swedish Consul in Antwerp exposed himself to danger and survived, while an Argentine colleague was killed after taking refuge in a cellar. The city also served as a point of departure for refugees who, after camping in pasture lands and woods, were well received and assisted by the Dutch in Holland.

## Nicholas Breton
Nicholas Breton (1545?–1626) was an English poet from an old family based at Layer-Breton, Essex. He was the son of William Breton, a trader who died in 1559, and Elizabeth Bacon. Following his father's death, his mother married the poet George Gascoigne. While there is no official record of his university attendance, the diary of the Rev. Richard Madox notes that Breton was "once of Oriel College" and was present in Antwerp in 1583.

Breton's professional life was marked by the patronage of Mary, countess of Pembroke, for whom he wrote until approximately 1601. His literary output included satires, miscellaneous prose tracts, and pastoral and religious poems. Although his praise of Mary Magdalene and the Virgin led some to suggest he was a Catholic, his prose writings indicate he was an ardent Protestant. 

In his work *A Poste with a Packet of Mad Letters* (1603, enlarged 1637), Breton provided autobiographical details through letters signed "N.B." In these, he described a life of hardship, claiming to have been imprisoned, long sick, wounded in wars, and plagued by an "unquiet life" and debt. He married Ann Sutton in 1593 and is believed to have died shortly after the 1626 publication of his final work, *Fantastickes*.

## Other Notable Figures of the Late 16th Century
The year 1583 coincides with the activities of several other historical figures across Europe:

*   **Richard Boyle, 1st Earl of Cork:** Born in 1566, Boyle was admitted to Bennet (Corpus Christi) College, Cambridge, in 1583. He later studied law at the Middle Temple and moved to Ireland in 1588 to seek advancement, eventually making a fortune and marrying Joan Appsley in 1595.
*   **Jean Baptista Houwaert:** A leader of rhetoricians in Flanders and Brabant, Houwaert was a "Counsellor and Master in Ordinary of the Exchequer to the Dukedom of Brabant." He was involved in the revolution against Spain and met the prince of Orange in Brussels in 1577. In 1583, he produced the *Commerce of Amorosity*, a collection of dramas including *Mars and Venus*, *Leander and Hero*, *Narcissus and Echo*, and *Aeneas and Dido*.
*   **The Barony of Caher:** In 1583, the barony of Caher was created for Sir Theobald Butler (who died in 1596) after the heirs general surrendered their rights.
*   **Henry, 8th Earl of Kent:** Born circa 1583, he married Elizabeth Talbot, an authoress and daughter of the 7th earl of Shrewsbury.
