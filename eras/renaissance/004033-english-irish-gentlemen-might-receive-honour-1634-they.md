# The Baronetage of Nova Scotia

## Origins and Purpose
The baronetage of Nova Scotia was established in 1624 as a strategic mechanism to promote the "plantation" of the province of Nova Scotia. The scheme was initially devised by James, who intended to create one hundred baronets. Under the terms of this arrangement, each baronet was required to support six colonists for a period of two years. Alternatively, they could pay 2000 marks in place of this support. Additionally, each baronet was required to pay 1000 marks to Sir William Alexander, who had been granted the province by charter in 1621 and was later known as the earl of Stirling. In exchange for these arrangements, Sir William Alexander was to receive a "free barony" consisting of 16,000 acres in Nova Scotia and be named a baronet of "his Hienes Kingdom of Scotland."

## Implementation under Charles I
Following the death of James, Charles I carried the scheme forward. On May 28, 1625, the first Scottish baronet was created. The creation charter established several specific covenants: the total number of baronets "of Scotland or of Nova Scotia" was never to exceed one hundred and fifty, and the heirs apparent of these baronets were to be knighted upon reaching the age of adulthood. To ensure the goals of the colony were met, the honour was restricted to those who fulfilled the financial conditions, specifically the payment of 3000 marks (equivalent to £166, 13s. 4d.) toward the plantation of the colony.

By November 17, 1629, the king addressed the "contractors for baronets," acknowledging that they had advanced significant sums to Sir William Alexander for the plantation, relying on the security of payments from future baronets. To encourage more applicants, the king empowered these contractors to offer further inducements. On that same date, Charles I granted all Nova Scotia baronets the right to wear a specific badge suspended by an orange tawny ribbon. This badge featured an azure saltire with a crowned inescutcheon of the arms of Scotland and the motto "Fax mentis honestae gloria."

## Expansion and Decline
Despite these inducements, the required number of baronets could not be completed. Consequently, in 1633, Charles announced that the honour could be extended to Irish and English gentlemen, who began receiving the title in 1634. Even with this expansion, the king was only able to create a total of slightly more than one hundred and twenty baronets.

The nature of the honour changed in 1638, when the grant of lands in Nova Scotia ceased to be attached to the creation of the title. The system of Scottish creations ended entirely in 1707 upon the union with England. From that point forward, both Scotsmen and Englishmen received baronetcies of Great Britain.
