# Germany

## Roman Relations and Early Tribal Policy
In the early history of the region, the Roman Empire employed a policy of division among the Germanic tribes to ensure its own security. Roman officials used money and negotiations to influence the heart of Germany, utilizing "every art of seduction" to conciliate nations located near the Danube or the Rhine. These tribes were viewed as potentially useful friends or troublesome enemies. Rome fomented quarrels among the Germans and used intrigues to defeat plans for public good or union, favoring private jealousy and interest instead. 

The effectiveness of this strategy is illustrated by the fate of the Bructeri, who were totally exterminated by neighboring tribes. This destruction of over sixty thousand barbarians occurred not through Roman arms, but was witnessed by the Romans for their entertainment. Tacitus noted that such discord among the enemies of Rome was the "utmost verge of prosperity" for the empire.

## Medieval Political Struggles
During the reign of King George of Podebrad, the only Hussite king of Bohemia, there were attempts to obtain supremacy over Germany. However, these far-reaching plans failed following a protracted struggle with the papal see. After negotiations with Rome proved unsuccessful, George assembled the estates at Prague in 1452, declaring his commitment to the communion in both kinds and his willingness to risk his crown and life for his faith.

This conflict led to increased bitterness among the Romanist party in Bohemia. In 1465, nobles of the Roman religion formed a confederacy against the king at Zelena Hora. Pope Paul II supported these confederates by excommunicating George and releasing Bohemians from their oaths of allegiance. This papal influence also led King Matthias of Hungary to desert George and support the league of Zelena Hora. While George initially saw success in the resulting warfare, Matthias eventually invaded Moravia and captured Brunn. On May 3, 1469, Matthias was proclaimed king of Bohemia at a meeting of Catholic nobles in Olmutz.

## Papal and Imperial Conflicts
In later periods, the German policy of Pope Gregory significantly influenced his relations with other European states. Because Germany engrossed the bulk of his powers, Gregory often displayed moderation toward other rulers that he did not show the German king. However, this focus left him vulnerable; when hard pressed by Henry IV, Gregory was abandoned by Robert Guiscard. Guiscard only intervened when threatened by German arms, and after the capture of Rome, he abandoned the city to his warriors, an act that led to Gregory's banishment.

## Diplomatic and Colonial Expansion
In the late 19th and early 20th centuries, German diplomacy was characterized by the efforts of figures such as Bernhard von Bülow. After serving in the Franco-Prussian War and various diplomatic posts in Paris, St Petersburg, and Bucharest, Bülow became the German ambassador at Rome in 1893. In 1897, he was appointed secretary of state for foreign affairs. His appointment was seen by some as a means to strengthen the Triple Alliance due to his popularity in Rome, and by others as a way to maintain the Bismarckian tradition. As foreign secretary, Bülow managed a policy of colonial expansion; his successful negotiations for the acquisition of the Caroline Islands in 1899 resulted in his elevation to the rank of count. He later became chancellor of the empire and president of the Prussian ministry in 1900.

During the lead-up to the World Crisis, negotiations took place regarding the development of the German fleet. British ministers, including Churchill, Grey, and Asquith, debated who should travel to Berlin to finalize an agreement should Germany abandon further fleet development. It was eventually decided that Grey would appear only at the termination of negotiations to sign the document and attend the festivities, while another representative, trusted by Asquith and Grey and known in Berlin, would conduct the preliminary negotiations.

## Post-War Settlements and Treaty Obligations
Following global conflict, Germany entered into various agreements regarding its international rights and properties:

*   **Siam:** Germany recognized that all agreements, including extra-territoriality rights, ceased on July 22, 1917. German public property (excluding diplomatic and consular premises) passed to Siam without compensation. Germany waived all claims regarding the internment of nationals or the seizure of ships.
*   **Liberia:** Germany renounced rights under the 1911 and 1912 international arrangements, specifically the right to nominate a customs receiver, and abrogated all commercial treaties.
*   **Egypt:** Germany recognized the British Protectorate declared on December 18, 1914, and renounced all treaties and capitulations concluded with Egypt as of August 4, 1914. Germany agreed not to intervene in negotiations between Great Britain and other powers regarding Egypt and consented to the transfer of powers for the free navigation of the Suez Canal to Great Britain.
*   **Other Regions:** Germany accepted all arrangements made by the Allied and Associated Powers with Turkey and Bulgaria regarding German interests in those countries. Additionally, commercial treaties with Germany were set to lapse at the end of 1917 alongside the Austro-Hungarian customs treaty.
