# Spanish and Portuguese Territories

The expansion and administration of Spanish and Portuguese territories from the 15th century onward were characterized by global exploration, colonial competition, and the establishment of strategic settlements across the Americas, Asia, and Africa.

## Colonial Division and Exploration
In the late 15th century, the Court of Rome divided the newly discovered lands of the "new world" between Spain and Portugal. The division was established by a line 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands; Spain was granted all territories west of this line, while Portugal was granted everything to the east.

Following this division, the Portuguese sought routes to India via South Africa. In 1497, Vasco da Gama sailed from Lisbon to Zanzibar and then across the Indian Ocean to Calicut. By 1515, Portuguese ships had reached the Moluccas and Java. Meanwhile, Spanish explorers crossed the Isthmus of Panama to sight the Pacific Ocean. In 1519, Magellan, a Portuguese sailor employed by the Spanish King, navigated the "Strait of Magellan" at the southern tip of South America to enter the Pacific.

## Territories in the Americas
The Spanish encountered populations of Mongoloid-type savages, including cannibals, whom they robbed, enslaved, slaughtered, and baptized, while showing little interest in their native customs. The Spanish territories were noted for being abundant in silver and gold. 

In the West Indies, the populations became diverse through the import of African negroes—who make up four-fifths of the population in Jamaica—as well as Chinese and East Indian coolies. The aboriginal populations included Caribs and American Indians of the mainland. Intermixture between these groups and Spanish and Portuguese blood resulted in various mixed types.

In South America, the boundary between Spanish and Portuguese territories required formal delimiting. Don Felix de Azara (1746-1811) served as a commissioner for this purpose for twenty years. His work included observations on natural history and accounts of the history and discovery of Rio de la Plata and Paraguay.

In the Bay of Honduras, Spanish territories were frequently targeted by British log-wood cutters and buccaneers. Despite Spanish attempts to expel them—including a routed expedition of 1,500 men in 1754 and the destruction of the Belize establishment in 1779—the British eventually secured the right to cut and load logwood through treaties in 1763 and 1783.

## Asian and African Settlements
Portuguese influence extended significantly into India. The settlement of Damaun, located on the east side of the entrance to the Gulf of Cambay, became a Portuguese possession. The town of Damaun was sacked and burnt by the Portuguese in 1531, rebuilt, and then permanently taken in 1558, at which time a mosque was converted into a Christian church. The territory of Damaun proper was conquered in 1559, and Nagar Havili was ceded to the Portuguese by the Mahrattas in 1780 as indemnification for piracy. Damaun became known for shipbuilding, deep-sea fishing, and a transit trade in opium between China and Karachi that lasted until 1837.

In Japan, Portuguese and Spanish propagandists faced suspicion. A letter found in a Portuguese ship captured by the Dutch suggested that Portugal would send men-of-war to aid Japanese converts if they revolted. In 1620, a British and Dutch admiral warned the second Tokugawa shogun of the "aggressive designs" of Philip of Spain and the "treacherous machinations" of the friars.

## Frontier Relations and Internal Dynamics
In the Iberian Peninsula, the city of Badajoz served as a Spanish frontier fortress near the Portuguese border on the river Guadiana. Its history was marked by conflict; it was held temporarily by the Portuguese in 1168 and besieged by them again in 1660.

Internally, Spain and Portugal dealt with the "Maranos" (meaning banned or accursed). These were "New Christians"—individuals who converted to Roman Catholicism under compulsion but often continued observing Jewish rites in private. The foundation of the Spanish Inquisition was largely due to the Maranos. While some accumulated wealth and honors, they also faced popular hatred, contributing to the expulsion of the Jews from Spain in 1492. Many Maranos emigrated to Amsterdam, Hamburg, and London, though remnants of Marano families remained in Portugal.
