# Historical and Biographical Memoirs of George Villiers, Duke of Buckingham

The historical and biographical record of George Villiers, 1st Duke of Buckingham, is preserved through a wide array of primary documents, contemporary accounts, and later scholarly biographies. These sources detail his rise from a younger son of the gentry to one of the most powerful figures in the English court.

## Life of the 1st Duke of Buckingham
George Villiers was born in August 1592, the younger son of Sir George Villiers of Brooksby and Mary, the daughter of Anthony Beaumont of Glenfield, Leicestershire. Following her early widowhood, Mary educated her son for a life at court, which included sending him to France with Sir John Eliot. Described as "by nature contemplative," Villiers excelled in fencing, dancing, and basic French.

In August 1614, Villiers was introduced to King James I. At the time, the King had assumed the business of government following the death of Salisbury and sought a private secretary who could provide companionship, amusement, and a buffer against importunate suitors. James I also intended to personally train the youth in the arts of statesmanship. This appointment followed the King's disappointment with Robert Carr, the earl of Somerset, who had become peevish and aligned himself with a party seeking a close alliance with Spain.

The King elevated Villiers through the peerage in rapid succession: he was created earl in 1617, marquess in 1618, and duke of Buckingham in 1623. He served as an English statesman until his death in 1628.

## Successors and the Buckingham Titles
The marquessate and dukedom created for George Villiers became extinct upon the death of the 2nd Duke (of the Villiers line) in 1687. However, the earldom was claimed by a collateral line claiming descent from John Villiers, 1st Viscount Purbeck, based on a special remainder in the 1617 patent. This claim was eventually extinguished in 1774 with the death of George Villiers, a clergyman. The title was not actually borne after 1723, when John Villiers styled himself earl of Buckingham.

Other distinct titles of "Buckingham" appeared in later years:
* In 1703, John Sheffield, marquess of Normanby, was created "duke of the county of Buckingham and of Normanby." This title became extinct in October 1735 upon the death of his son, Edmund.
* In 1784, the 2nd Earl Temple was created marquess of Buckingham "in the county of Buckingham." This title was elevated to the dukedom of Buckingham and Chandos for his son, Richard Grenville (1797-1861), in 1822.

## The 2nd Duke of Buckingham and Chandos
Richard Grenville, the 2nd Duke of Buckingham and Chandos, was educated at Oriel College, Oxford, and Eton. He served as a member of parliament for Buckinghamshire from 1818 to 1839 and was responsible for the "Chandos clause" in the Reform Bill of 1832. Known as the "Farmers' Friend" due to his opposition to the repeal of the corn laws, he also served as lord privy seal from September 1841 to January 1842.

The 2nd Duke's tenure was marked by financial instability; he inherited heavily encumbered estates, and his luxurious tastes led to his residences being seized by creditors in 1847. After leaving England and later returning, he focused on literature. His published works include memoirs regarding the courts and cabinets of George III, George IV, William IV, and Victoria. He died in London on July 29, 1861.
