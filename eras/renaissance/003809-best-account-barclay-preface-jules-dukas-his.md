# Barclay

The name Barclay is associated with several distinct historical figures, including a Latin poet and satirist, a religious leader, a military commander, and a priest, as well as a banking institution.

## John Barclay (1582–1621)
John Barclay was a Scottish Latin poet and satirist born on January 28, 1582, at Pont-a-Mousson. His father, William Barclay, held the chair of civil law there, and his mother was a Frenchwoman of good family. Educated at the Jesuit College, Barclay wrote a commentary on Statius's *Thebaid* at age nineteen.

In 1603, he traveled to London with his father, maintaining his Scottish nationality despite his French surroundings. He is believed to have published the first part of his *Satyricon* in England, though by 1605 he was in Paris, having spent time in Angers and married a French girl, Louise Debonaire. He returned to London with his wife in 1606 and published *Sylvae*, a collection of Latin poems. The second part of the *Satyricon* followed in Paris in 1607.

Barclay remained in London until 1616. During this period, he edited *De Potestate Papae* (1609), an anti-papal treatise by his late father, and issued an *Apologia* (the "third part" of the *Satyricon*) in 1611 to answer attacks from Jesuits and others. In 1614, the *Icon Animorum* appeared, referred to as a "fourth part." This work was translated into English by Thomas May in 1631 as *The Mirrour of Mindes, or Barclay's Icon Animorum*. While James I was said to be attracted by his scholarship, specific details of his London life are unavailable.

In 1616, Barclay moved to Rome, where he lived until August 15, 1621. During this time, he appeared to be on better terms with the Church and Bellarmine, publishing *Paraenesis ad Sectarios* in 1617 from a Cologne press, which attacked the position of Protestantism. His final and best-known work, the romance *Argenis*, was completed approximately two weeks before his death; it has been suggested that his death was hastened by poison.

The best account of this Barclay is found in the preface by Jules Dukas in his 1889 bibliography of the *Satyricon*, which supersedes the account in Bayle's *Dictionary*. Other related scholarly works include dissertations on the *Argenis* by Dupond (1875) and Leon Boucher (1874). Most editions of the *Satyricon* also include a "fifth part" written by Alethophilus (Claude Morisot). Barclay's works have never been collected.

## John Barclay (1734–1798)
John Barclay was a Scottish divine born in Perthshire and died in Edinburgh. A graduate of St Andrews, he served as an assistant to the parish minister of Errol in Perthshire. Following differences with the minister, he left in 1763 to become an assistant to Antony Dow of Fettercairn, Kincardine.

Although popular in his parish, Barclay's opinions were not satisfactory to his presbytery. In 1772, he was rejected as Dow's successor and was denied the testimonials necessary to secure another living. This refusal was upheld by the General Assembly. Consequently, Barclay left the Scottish church and established congregations in London, Edinburgh, and Sauchyburn. His followers, known as Bereans, held a modified form of Calvinism and based their conduct on the diligent study of the Scriptures.

## Alexander Barclay
Alexander Barclay was a priest who, in his youth, wrote *Certayne Ecloges of Alexander Barclay, Priest*. These works, likely printed as early as 1513, are among the earliest examples of the English pastoral. The first three eclogues are dialogues between Cornix and Coridon, borrowed from Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini (Pope Pius II) and containing an eulogy for John Alcock, bishop of Ely. The fourth eclogue is based on Mantuan and includes an elegy on Sir Edward Howard, who died in 1513 during an attack on the French fleet at Brest. The fifth eclogue, featuring shepherds Amintas and Faustus, discusses the merits of country versus town life and is also based on Mantuan.

Barclay's pastorals served as satires on social evils and depicted rustic life, including football and Sunday village games. He disagreed with the anti-clerical views of John Skelton, whom he attacked; a lost work titled *Anti-Skeltonum* is mentioned by Bale. His other works include *The Castell of Laboure* (1506), a translation from Pierre Gringoire; the *Introductory to write and to pronounce Frenche* (1521); *The Myrrour of Good Maners* (a translation of Dominicus Mancinus); and a *Cronycle compyled in Latyn* by Sallust.

## Michael Andreas Barclay de Tolly (1761–1818)
Known to Russians as Prince Bogdanovich, Michael Andreas Barclay de Tolly was a Russian field marshal born in Livonia. A descendant of a Scottish family that settled in Russia in the 17th century, he entered the Russian army young. He served against the Turks (1788–1789), the Poles (1794), and the Swedes (1790). He attained the rank of colonel in 1798 and major-general in 1799.

During the 1806 war against Napoleon, he was promoted to lieutenant-general following his conduct at Eylau and his part in the battle of Pultusk. He commanded forces against the Swedes in Finland in 1808 and seized Umeo in 1809. He served as minister of war from 1810 to 1813. In 1812, he commanded one of the armies against Napoleon, but faced opposition as a foreigner. After a defeat at Smolensk, he resigned his command to serve under Kutusov, though he was present at Borodino.

He returned to field employment in 1813 and was reinstated as commander-in-chief of Russian forces after the battle of Bautzen, serving at Leipzig, Kulm, and Dresden. He was made a count after the battle of Leipzig. Following the 1814 invasion of France, he received the baton of a field marshal in Paris. He commanded the Russian army during the 1815 invasion of France and was named a prince at the war's end. He died in Prussia on May 14 (16), 1818.

## Barclay & Co., Ltd.
Barclay & Co., Ltd., located on Lombard Street, is a banking firm that grew through the amalgamation of several other banks. These include the Gurney & Co. banks of the eastern counties and the Hull Old Bank (Pease & Co.), which dated from 1754.
