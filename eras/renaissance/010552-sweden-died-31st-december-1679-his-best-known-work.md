# Sweden

## Diplomatic Relations and Conflict with Denmark
During the late 17th century, Sweden was involved in complex diplomatic maneuvers involving France and Denmark. Efforts were made to detach Sweden from France, but Sweden showed no inclination for a rapprochement. Consequently, Denmark joined an anti-French league through the treaty of Copenhagen in January 1674. Under this treaty, Denmark agreed to place an army of 20,000 in the field when required, although the official Griffenfeldt stipulated that this provision would not be operative until the allies were attacked by a fresh enemy.

Hostilities escalated in December 1674 when a Swedish army invaded Prussian Pomerania. This action bound Denmark to intervene as a belligerent, though Griffenfeldt attempted to postpone this intervention. This delay was aided by Sweden's own anxiety to avoid hostilities with its southern neighbor, Denmark.

## The Peace of Fontainebleau and Lund
The conflict eventually led to an unprofitable war for Denmark, exacerbated by the impetuosity of King Christian V. The struggle in the Baltic concluded with the peace of Fontainebleau on September 2, 1679. Under the direction of the French king, Denmark was compelled to make full restitution to Sweden. The specific treaty between the two northern powers was signed at Lund on September 26, 1679. Following five years of contest, Denmark emerged exhausted and empty-handed, having spent its treasure and blood.

## Notable Figures and Associations
Sweden's royalty provided refuge to intellectuals of the era. Giovanni Alfonso Borelli (1608–1679), an Italian physicist and physiologist, retired to Rome in 1674. While in Rome, he lived under the protection of Christina, the queen of Sweden, until his death on December 31, 1679. Borelli is recognized as the founder of the iatrophysical school, most notably for his work *De motu animalium* (1680-1681), which applied mechanical principles to the movements of the animal body.
