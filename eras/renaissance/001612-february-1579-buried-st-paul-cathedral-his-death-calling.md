# Sir Nicholas Bacon

Sir Nicholas Bacon (1509–1579) was a prominent English statesman and legal official who served as the lord keeper of the great seal of England during the reign of Queen Elizabeth.

## Early Life and Legal Career
Born at Chislehurst, Bacon was the second son of Robert Bacon of Drinkstone, Suffolk. He received his education at Corpus Christi College, Cambridge, where he graduated with a B.A. in 1527. Following his studies, he spent time in Paris before returning to England to enter Gray's Inn. He was called to the bar in 1533.

Bacon entered public life in 1537 as the solicitor of the court of augmentations. His rise in importance was rapid; following the dissolution of the monasteries, he acquired several estates, primarily located in the eastern counties. In 1545, he became a member of parliament representing Dartmouth. His professional ascent continued in 1546 when he was appointed attorney of the court of wards and liveries, a position noted for both its profit and honor. By 1550, he had become a bencher, and in 1552, he served as the treasurer of Gray's Inn.

## Political Career and the Reign of Elizabeth
During the reign of Queen Mary, Bacon retained his office in the court of wards despite his Protestant sympathies. However, an order was issued during this time to prevent him from leaving England.

The most significant period of Bacon's career began with the accession of Elizabeth in 1558. Due to a close and long-standing friendship with his brother-in-law, Sir William Cecil (later Lord Burghley), Bacon was appointed lord keeper of the great seal in December 1558. Shortly thereafter, he was named a knight and a privy councillor. In his official capacity, he presided over the House of Lords during the opening of Elizabeth's first parliament and helped secure the archbishopric of Canterbury for his friend Matthew Parker. In 1559, he was granted the authority to exercise the full jurisdiction of lord chancellor.

## Policy and Later Years
Bacon occasionally found himself in opposition to Sir William Cecil. Specifically, he objected to the policy of waging war on France to assist the enemies of Mary, queen of Scots, citing the poverty of England. Despite this disagreement, he supported a closer union with foreign Protestants and recognized the threat posed to England by the aggressive religious policies of Scotland and France.

In 1564, Bacon experienced a temporary fall from royal favor and was dismissed from court. This occurred because Queen Elizabeth suspected him of being involved in the publication of a pamphlet titled "A Declaration of the."

## Death and Succession
Sir Nicholas Bacon died in 1579. His sudden death in February 1579 had a significant impact on his youngest son, Francis. Sir Nicholas had set aside a considerable sum of money to purchase an estate for Francis, who was the only son otherwise unprovided for. Because of the suddenness of the death, this intention was not fulfilled, and Francis received only a fifth of the money. This lack of means led Francis to develop a habit of borrowing and a lifelong state of debt, eventually prompting him to take up the study of law at Gray's Inn in 1579.

Following Bacon's death, Sir Thomas Bromley, who had progressed in his profession through the patronage of Bacon, was appointed lord chancellor.
