# George Buchanan and the Monastic Assault

## Early Literary Attacks and Royal Patronage
George Buchanan, during his time in the household of Lord Cassilis in the west country of Scotland, produced a poem titled *Somnium*. This work served as a satirical attack on monastic life in general and specifically targeted the Franciscan friars. At this period, Buchanan did not repudiate the doctrines of the Church of Rome, but instead adopted a position similar to that of Erasmus, believing himself free to criticize the practices of the Church.

This assault on the monks was viewed favorably by James V. As a result of this disposition, James V engaged Buchanan as a tutor for one of his natural sons, Lord James Stewart (distinct from the son who later became the regent Murray). Encouraged by the King to pursue more daring efforts, Buchanan wrote the poems *Franciscanus & Fratres* and *Palinodia*. Although these works remained unpublished for many years, they caused the author to become an object of the bitterest hatred among the friends of the order and the order itself.

## Persecution and Exile
Assailing the church was a dangerous endeavor during this era. In 1539, a bitter persecution of Lutherans took place, during which Buchanan was arrested. He managed to escape and traveled with considerable difficulty first to London and then to Paris. In Paris, he encountered Cardinal David Beaton, who was serving as an ambassador and was an enemy of Buchanan. 

Following an invitation from André de Gouvéa, the principal of the newly founded college of Guienne at Bordeaux, Buchanan moved to Bordeaux. Through the efforts of Gouvéa, Buchanan was appointed as a professor of Latin.

## Academic Achievements in Bordeaux
While residing in Bordeaux, Buchanan completed several of his most significant works, including two dramas, *Baptistes (sive Calumnia)* and *Jephthes (sive Votum)*, as well as translations of *Alcestis* and *Medea*. During this time, he formed a lasting friendship with Julius Caesar Scaliger and later earned the admiration of Joseph Scaliger, who wrote a famous epigram containing a couplet about him.

Buchanan's influence extended to his pupils, including Montaigne, who acted in Buchanan's tragedies. In the essay *Of Presumption*, Montaigne classifies Buchanan as one of the foremost Latin poets of his era, grouping him with other figures such as Turnebus, de L'Hopital, Béza, Aurat, and Montdore.

## Religious Transitions
Although Buchanan listened with interest to the arguments presented by the Reformers during his early career, he did not officially join their ranks until 1553.
