# Geneva and its International Connections

Geneva has served as a significant center for intellectual, religious, and political activity, frequently intersecting with the history of England and other European nations. From the era of the Reformation to the 18th century, the city acted as a refuge for exiled scholars and a hub for the production of influential texts.

## Religious Influence and the English Bible
During the reign of Mary in England, persecutions of the English Bible and its supporters were renewed, leading to the martyrdom of figures such as Cranmer and John Rogers. Consequently, prominent reformers, including Coverdale, sought refuge in Geneva, which was the town of Beza and Calvin. 

While in exile, these reformers worked on a new revision of the Bible. This effort resulted in a New Testament issued in June 1557, which featured an introduction by Calvin and was likely the work of William Whittingham. This volume was printed in clear Roman type, used a convenient quarto size, and included marginal annotations. It was the first Bible to divide the text into sections and verses based on the best editions in other languages. The publication gained immediate popularity in England, filling a long-standing need for a Bible suited for household use.

## Political and Local History
The city's local history includes conflicts over lordship and governance. Francois Bonivard, a member of an old Savoyard family, served as the prior of the Cluniac priory of St Victor near Geneva starting in 1510. Bonivard opposed the bishop of the city and the duke of Savoy, who sought to maintain rights as the lord of Geneva. These political tensions led to Bonivard's imprisonment by the duke at Gex from 1519 to 1521, and later a four-year underground imprisonment in the castle of Chillon from 1530 to 1536. 

After his release by the Bernese, Bonivard became a Protestant and received a pension from Geneva. In 1542, he was officially tasked with compiling a history of Geneva from the earliest times. His manuscript, the *Chroniques de Geneve* (which ends in 1530), was submitted to Calvin for correction in 1551, though it was not published until 1831.

## Intellectual and Academic Contributions
Geneva was a center for legal and ethical study in the 18th century. Jean Jacques Burlamaqui, born in Geneva on June 24, 1694, became an honorary professor of the law of nature and ethics at the university of Geneva at age twenty-five. Burlamaqui's academic approach was characterized by a simple style and precise views, which earned him a wide reputation over fifteen years of lecturing. His fundamental principle is described as rational utilitarianism. After resigning due to ill health, he was elected by his fellow-citizens to the council of state, where he was recognized for his practical sagacity. His major works include *Principes du droit naturel* (1747) and *Principes du droit politique* (1751), both of which were extensively used as text-books.

The city also had a brief encounter with Cartesianism; the system had a short reign in Geneva after 1669, though it did not take permanent root.

## Geneva as a Refuge and Transit Point
The city frequently hosted individuals fleeing political or religious upheaval. Pierre Desmaiseaux, a French writer born probably in 1673, was educated in Geneva after his father, a minister of the reformed church, was forced to leave France following the revocation of the edict of Nantes. Desmaiseaux later moved to England in 1699 via an introduction from Bayle to the 3rd Lord Shaftesbury. While in England, he remained in close contact with religious refugees in Holland and England.

In later centuries, Geneva continued to be a point of residence for international figures. For example, Yule spent part of the winter of 1862 at the Hôtel des Bergues in Geneva, where he was hosted by Colonel Tronchin and encountered John Ruskin.
