# Henry Barrowe

## Early Life and Education
Henry Barrowe was born around 1550 in Shipdam, Norfolk. He belonged to a family that was related by marriage to the lord keeper Bacon and likely to Aylmer, the bishop of London. In November 1566, Barrowe matriculated at Clare Hall, Cambridge, where he subsequently graduated with a B.A. in 1569-1570. Following his studies, he spent some time "following the court," a period described as being licentious or frivolous. Between 1576 and a few years thereafter, he was a member of Gray's Inn, though he was never called to the bar.

## Religious Conversion and Separatism
Around 1580 or 1581, Barrowe was deeply affected by a sermon, which prompted him to retire to the country. Through a process of meditation and study, he adopted the strictest form of Puritanism. He eventually formed an intimate relationship with John Greenwood, a leader of the Separatists. Barrowe adopted Greenwood's views without reserve—views that may have been influenced in part by Robert Browne. 

Although he did not reside strictly in London during this period, Barrowe became associated with the "brethren of the Separation" based there. He became conspicuous in their secret meetings due to his eloquence and natural earnestness.

## Arrest and Imprisonment
On November 19, 1586, Barrowe traveled from the country to visit John Greenwood, who had been imprisoned in the Clink. Upon this visit, Barrowe was detained by the gaoler and brought before Archbishop Whitgift. Barrowe contested the legality of his arrest and refused to give bail for future appearances or take the *ex officio* oath. Consequently, he was committed to the Gatehouse.

After nearly six months of detention and several irregular examinations conducted by high commissioners, Barrowe and Greenwood were formally indicted in May 1587. They were charged with recusancy under an act that had originally been intended for Papists. The authorities ordered them to provide heavy bail for conformity; because this bail was not forthcoming, they were committed to the Fleet Prison.

## Final Years and Execution
Barrowe remained a prisoner for nearly six years, which constituted the remainder of his life. His confinement varied, at times being in close confinement and at other times possessing "the liberty of the prison." During this period, he and his fellow prisoner, John Greenwood, wrote several controversial tracts together.

Barrowe continued to be subjected to examinations, including one on March 18, 1588, before the privy council at Whitehall, which occurred following a petition to the queen. During these proceedings, Barrowe vigorously defended the principle of separatism and denounced the prescribed ritual of the Church.

In March 1593, Barrowe was tried alongside Greenwood. Both were condemned to death on charges of circulating and devising seditious books. Following two respites—one of which occurred at the foot of the gallows—Henry Barrowe was hanged on April 6, 1593.
