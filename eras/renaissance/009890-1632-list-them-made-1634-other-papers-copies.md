# Events and Figures of the Early 17th Century

The early 17th century was marked by significant military conflicts in Central Europe, the development of European drama, and the contributions of various scholars and philosophers.

## Military Conflicts and the Thirty Years' War
During this period, Gustavus Adolphus, the King of Sweden—known as the "Lion of the North" and the champion of the Protestants—sought to transform the Baltic Sea into a "Swedish Lake." In 1632, Adolphus stormed Donauworth and unsuccessfully besieged Furth. His military career ended in 1632 when he was killed during a decisive victory over Wallenstein at Lützen.

Other regional conflicts and shifts in power occurred during this era. In 1634, the town of Furth was burnt and pillaged by the Croats, and Donauworth was captured by King Ferdinand. Wallenstein, a great plunder captain on the Habsburg side, was murdered in 1634. These conflicts eventually led to the Peace of Westphalia in 1648, which reduced the power of the Emperor to a shadow, granted complete independence to Switzerland and Holland, and allowed the Hohenzollern Elector of Brandenburg to acquire enough territory to become the greatest German power after the Emperor.

## European Drama and Literature
In England, the dramatist Richard Brome, a former servant and friend of Ben Jonson, established his reputation with the 1632 play *The Northern Lasse*. Brome's work was characterized by strongly marked characters and curious information, reflecting Jonson's influence. Brome wrote fifteen pieces, including *Novella* and *Court Begger* (both acted in 1632), *The Sparagus Garden* (acted 1635, printed 1640), and *The Antipodes* (acted 1638, printed 1640). He also collaborated with Thomas Heywood on *The late Lancashire Witches*, which was printed in 1634.

In France, Pierre Corneille began his career as an *avocat* after being educated by the Jesuits of Rouen. His first play, *Melite*, was acted in 1629. In 1632, his tragedy *Clitandre* was printed. He followed this with the comedies *La Veuve* and *Galerie du palais* in 1633, and *La Suivante* and *La Place Royale* in 1634. In 1634, Corneille was also enrolled among the "five poets," a group tasked with turning the ideas of Cardinal Richelieu into dramatic forms.

## Scholars and Philosophic Thought
The 17th century saw prominent intellectual movements, particularly in the Netherlands. Baruch Spinoza (1632–1677) and Hugo Grotius (1583–1645) were illustrious figures of this era. Balthasar Bekker (1634–1698), a Reformed preacher in Amsterdam and disciple of Descartes, is remembered for his attacks on the penal code for witchcraft and his masterpiece, *Betoverde Wereld* (The World Bewitched), published between 1691 and 1693.

Jacques Bongars (1554–1612) was a French scholar and diplomatist who served Henry IV of France. Bongars worked to curtail the power of the house of Habsburg and visited northern Europe and England to seek help for Henry. His literary contributions include an abridgment of Justin's history of Trogus Pompeius (1581), a collection of writers describing the crusades titled *Gesta Dei per Francos* (1611), and *Rerum Hungaricarum scriptores varii* (1600). Following his death in 1612, many of his papers were presented to the library at Bern in 1632, with a corresponding list created in 1634.

## Notable Biographies
Lettice Knollys (1540–1634), daughter of Sir Francis Knollys, was the mother of the 2nd earl of Essex and married both Walter Devereux, earl of Essex, and Robert Dudley, earl of Leicester. Her father's sons—Henry, Edward, Robert, Richard, Francis, and Thomas—all served the queen as courtiers in the field or in parliament.
