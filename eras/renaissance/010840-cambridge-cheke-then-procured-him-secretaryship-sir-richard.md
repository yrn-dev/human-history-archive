# Cambridge

Cambridge has served as a center for higher education and intellectual development, characterized by its various colleges and the influence of prominent educationists.

## Academic Life and Educationists
St John's College, Cambridge, was noted as the largest and most learned college in either university during the early 16th century. In this environment, scholars such as Roger Ascham and John Cheke emerged as foremost educationists. Ascham entered St John's College around 1530 at the age of fifteen, having previously received private tuition in the house of Sir Humphry Wingfield, a barrister and speaker of the House of Commons. Under the guidance of Robert Pember, Ascham focused on the Greek tongue, employing a method of teaching Greek to boys to accelerate his own learning. He also specialized in the study of Caesar and Cicero in Latin.

Ascham became a Bachelor of Arts on February 18, 1534/5. Despite the college master, Dr. Nicholas Metcalfe, being a "papist," Metcalfe praised students who embraced the "new learning." However, Ascham's election to a fellowship was complicated by his public speaking against the Pope, which resulted in a rebuke and punishment.

## Influence on Royal Tutelage
The intellectual environment of Cambridge extended to the education of the English royalty. Roger Ascham served as the tutor to the sixteen-year-old Princess Elizabeth for two years, primarily at Cheshunt. He was selected for this role through the influence of Sir William Cecil and the princess's own wish, following the death of her previous tutor, Grindal, in January 1548.

Ascham reported that Elizabeth possessed great industry, wisdom, stature, and beauty. Her linguistic abilities included English, French, and Italian, as well as a readiness in Latin and a moderate proficiency in Greek. Her Latin knowledge was drawn primarily from Titus Livius and Cicero. Her studies included the Greek Testament, the tragedies of Sophocles, select orations of Isocrates, and the works of Melanchthon and St Cyprian.

## Notable Scholars and Figures
The university attracted various individuals who acquired specialized knowledge or entered into significant social connections. William Cecil, the son of a man of the same name, attended St John's College, Cambridge, in May 1535 at age fourteen. There, he studied under John Cheke and Roger Ascham, gaining an unusual knowledge of Greek. Cecil resided at Cambridge for six years without taking a degree before being moved to Gray's Inn in 1541. Shortly thereafter, he married Mary Cheke, the sister of John Cheke.

Other figures associated with Cambridge colleges include:
* **Richard Boyle, 1st Earl of Cork**: Admitted to Bennet (Corpus Christi) College in 1583.
* **Robert Hugh Benson**: Educated at Trinity College, Cambridge, and later returning to the city as an assistant priest for the Roman Catholic church.
* **Ronald Brunlees McKerrow**: Associated with Trinity College, Cambridge.
* **Robert Alexander Stewart Macalister**: Associated with St John's College, Cambridge.
* **Stanley Arthur Cook** and **Shelford Bidwell**: Both associated with Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge.
* **Robert Seymour Conway**: A Fellow of Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge.

## Professional Transitions and Appointments
The connections forged at Cambridge often led to professional opportunities. In 1550, after a quarrel with Elizabeth's steward, Roger Ascham returned to Cambridge. Following this, John Cheke procured for Ascham the position of secretary to Sir Richard Morrison (Moryson), who had been appointed as ambassador to Charles V. While traveling to join Morrison, Ascham visited Lady Jane Grey at Bradgate, where he observed her reading Plato's *Phaedo*.
