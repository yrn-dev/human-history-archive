# Thre Laws

The *Thre Laws* (full title: *Thre Laws of Nature, Moses and Christ, corrupted by the Sodomytes, Pharisees and Papystes most wicked*) is a morality play written by the English author and bishop John Bale. The work was printed in 1538 and again in 1562.

## Author and Context
John Bale (1495–1563) was a bishop of Ossory and a former Carmelite monk who became an ardent partisan of the Reformers while studying at Jesus College, Cambridge. Bale was known as an unscrupulous controversialist who utilized his writing to denounce the monastic system and its supporters. His works, including his miracle and morality plays, were characterized by a strong bias against Roman Catholic writers and were often coarse and violent in nature. Because of these qualities, he is referred to by Wood as "foul-mouthed Bale."

Bale's dramatic productions were designed to impress popular feeling and served as a tool for political and religious aims. During this period, he attracted the notice of Thomas Cromwell, who viewed Bale as an invaluable instrument.

## Content and Characteristics
The *Thre Laws* is categorized as a morality play. It is noted for its use of profane parody, specifically in the "prayer of Infidelitas" which opens the second act of the play. This particular section is cited as an example of the lengths to which Bale went in his attacks.

The play includes specific instructions for the dressing of its characters, which reflect Bale's polemical targets:
* **Sodomy** is to be dressed as a monk of all sects.
* **Ambition** is to be dressed as a bishop.
* **Covetousness** is to be dressed as a spiritual lawyer or a Pharisee.
* **False Doctrine** is to be dressed as a popish doctor.
* **Hypocrisy** is to be dressed as a gray friar.
* **Idolatry** is to be dressed like an old witch.

## Literary Significance
While Bale's plays are described as doggerel, he is considered a figure of dramatic importance. Although *Thre Laws* is a morality play, Bale's later work, *Kynge Johan* (c. 1548), is credited with marking the transition from the old morality play to the English historical drama.

The *Thre Laws* is among the works of Bale quoted by T. Warton in *Hist. Eng. Poetry* (section 41).
