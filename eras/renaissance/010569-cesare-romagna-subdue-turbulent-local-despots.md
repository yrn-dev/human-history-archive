# Cesare Borgia

Cesare Borgia (1476–1507), the son of Pope Alexander VI and Vanozza dei Cattanei, was the Duke of Valentinois and Romagna. Born in Rome while his father was a cardinal, Cesare was the favorite son of Alexander VI, who practiced extensive nepotism on his behalf. Following his father's elevation to the papacy in 1492, Cesare was created archbishop of Valencia and, a year later, a cardinal.

## Early Life and Transition to Secular Power
In the early years of the pontificate, Cesare led a profligate life at the Vatican. In January 1495, he accompanied Charles VIII as a hostage to ensure the pope's good behavior during the conquest of Naples, though he eventually escaped at Velletri. By 1497, Cesare began demonstrating the violence for which he would become notorious; he is attributed with the murder of his brother, Giovanni, Duke of Gandia. While some suggested the motive was rivalry over Donna Sancha or incestuous relations with their sister Lucrezia, it is considered more probable that Cesare envied Giovanni's position and desired the full extent of his father's favors to facilitate a transition from ecclesiastical to secular life.

In July 1497, Cesare served as papal legate in Naples to crown Frederick of Aragon. Seeking a wealthy marriage, he attempted to win Carlotta, the daughter of the king of Naples, but was refused by both her and her father, who rejected an alliance with "a priest, the bastard of a priest." Consequently, in August 1498, Cesare renounced the priesthood with the permission of the pope and the cardinals. In October 1498, he traveled to France as papal legate to Louis XII to deliver a bull annulling Louis's marriage to Jeanne of France. In return, Cesare received military assistance and the duchy of Valentinois. After further failed attempts to marry Carlotta of Naples, he married Charlotte d'Albret, sister of the king of Navarre, in May 1499.

## Conquest of Romagna
Pope Alexander VI planned for Cesare to subdue turbulent local despots in Romagna and, with French assistance, carve out a principality from territories that owed nominal allegiance to the papacy. Establishing headquarters at Cesena, Cesare led an army of Italian troops and 4,300 French lances, Gascons, and Swiss. He quickly captured Imola and besieged Forli, which surrendered on January 22, 1500. The defender, Caterina Sforza, was treated with consideration and later entered a convent.

Cesare returned to Rome in February 1500 after the Sforzas expelled the French from Milan. His father rewarded him by making him *gonfaloniere* of the church. In October 1500, supported by Venetian friendship and an army of 10,000 men, Cesare returned to Romagna. The cities of Pesaro and Rimini opened their gates following the flight of Giovanni Sforza and Pandolfo Malatesta. Faenza surrendered in April 1501; despite a promise to spare the life of its lord, Astorre Manfredi, Cesare sent the eighteen-year-old prisoner to Rome, where he was outraged and killed. Following the capture of Castel Bolognese, the pope created Cesare the duke of Romagna.

## Political Methods and the Conspiracy of La Magione
Cesare's rule was characterized by an insatiable avidity, a lack of scruples, and a morose, unsympathetic personality. While his methods of conquest were treacherous and ferocious, he governed his conquered subjects with justice and firmness, leading some to prefer his rule over the anarchy of local despots. This combination of audacity, diplomatic prudence, and the adroit use of fraud and cruelty earned him the admiration of Machiavelli, who viewed Cesare as a model for the perfect prince.

Cesare's power was later challenged by the conspiracy of La Magione, involving the Orsinis, several deposed princes, and his own captains—including Vitellozzo Vitelli, Oliverotto da Fermo, and G.P. Baglioni. These conspirators feared the loss of their estates and led risings in Romagna and Urbino. Although the papal troops were defeated, the promise of help from the French king frightened the confederates into seeking terms. Cesare eventually outmaneuvered them; he decoyed Vitelli, Oliverotto, Paolo Orsini, and the duke of Gravina to Senigallia, where he had them arrested.

## Decline and Fall
Cesare returned to Rome in early 1503 to help reduce the remaining rebel Orsinis. In the summer of 1503, both Cesare and his father were seized by fever. Pope Alexander VI died on August 18, while Cesare was still incapacitated. This coincidence proved ruinous, as Cesare's system was built entirely upon the support of the papacy.

His enemies rose immediately: the Orsinis rebelled in Rome and deposed princes attempted to reclaim their lands in Romagna. After failing to intimidate the cardinals, Cesare left Rome in September. Following the brief papacy of Pius III, Giuliano della Rovere was elected as Julius II on November 1, 1503. Although Julius II initially showed no ill-will, he insisted that Cesare's territories be restored to the church. To prevent Venice from establishing a protectorate over the principalities, Julius II arrested Cesare. This marked the end of Borgia's power, and he was forced to surrender his castles in Romagna.
