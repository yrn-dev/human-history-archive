# Frankfort, Mainz and Cologne

## Historical and Political Status of Frankfort
Frankfort-on-Main has undergone various changes in its political status over several centuries. In 1803, it was one of only six cities—alongside Hamburg, Lübeck, Bremen, Augsburg, and Nuremberg—permitted by the imperial deputation to maintain their *Reichsfreiheit*, allowing them to hold directly of the Empire. Following the dissolution of the Empire in 1806, Frankfort became the seat of a duchy for Karl Theodor von Dalberg, who served as the elector and archbishop of Mainz and was appointed prince primate of the Confederation of the Rhine. 

By 1815, with the establishment of the German Confederation, Frankfort was recognized as a free city. However, this independence was lost following the war of 1866, after which the city was annexed by Prussia.

## Mainz and the Role of Karl Theodor von Dalberg
Mainz was the seat of the archbishop-elector, a position held by Karl Theodor Anton Maria von Dalberg (1744–1817). Dalberg served as the arch-chancellor of the Holy Roman Empire and later as the prince primate of the Confederation of the Rhine. A student of canon law, Dalberg was known for a "patriotic" stance, leaning toward the Febronian view of a German national church and attempting to create an effective central government for Germany. 

Due to his belief in Napoleon's genius as a force to save Germany from dissolution, Dalberg aligned himself with the French leader. Under the peace of Lunéville, although he surrendered Constance and Worms, he received Wetzlar, Aschaffenburg, and Regensburg. In 1810, the grand-duchy of Frankfort was created for his benefit. By the time of his death on February 10, 1817, he retained only the archbishopric of Regensburg.

## Cologne and Regional History
Cologne has been a site of significant religious, cultural, and military activity. In 1525, P. Quentel printed a Quarto Edition of Tyndale's New Testament in the city. In the early 16th century, the archbishop of Cologne was among the electors who favored the instant outlawry of Luther. Later, in 1801, the peace of Lunéville resulted in Cologne being taken by France, along with Spires, Worms, and Aix-la-Chapelle.

The city is also linked to the town of Königswinter, located 24 miles south-southeast of Cologne. This area features the Drachenfels, where a castle was built in the early 12th century by the archbishop of Cologne. From 1267 onward, trachyte stone from this mountain was supplied for the construction of the Cologne cathedral.

## Printing and Typographic Marks
Mainz and Cologne were centers of early printing. The 42-Line Bible was printed at Mainz between 1452 and 1456. Additionally, an edition of a bull of Pope Pius II, printed in types used by Schoeffer and Fust at Mainz and attributed to 1463, is claimed to be the first book to feature a printed title-page.

The study of early printing includes the analysis of *marques typographiques* (printers' marks). Various works have been devoted to these devices, including those by Berjeau, W. Roberts, and Silvestre. Specifically, the publication *Die Buchermarken oder Buchdrucker und Verlegerzeichen* (Strassburg, 1892–1898) contains the devices used by printers and publishers in Cologne, Mainz, Frankfort, Basel, and Alsace.

## Military and Cultural Intersections
During the 17th century, the region was a theater of strategic military movement. Imperialist forces under Montecucculi advanced through Nuremberg and arrived at Frankfort, forcing a French retreat to the Rhine. Montecucculi subsequently crossed the river at Mainz before moving toward Trier. Later, during the siege of Bonn, the coalition forces gained a line from the Breisgau to Amsterdam, leaving Louis' allies in Munster and Cologne isolated. Montecucculi eventually wintered in Julich and Cologne.

Culturally, the cities attracted various figures. The Irishman Marianus Scotus (1028–1083), a monk and chronicler who wrote a universal history called the *Chronicon*, spent time in the abbeys of Mainz and Cologne (specifically St Martin). In the 19th century, the dramatist Julius Roderich Benedix held engagements in Cologne and served as a teacher at the Rhenish school of music there before becoming the intendant of the municipal theatre in Frankfort-on-Main from 1855 to 1861.
