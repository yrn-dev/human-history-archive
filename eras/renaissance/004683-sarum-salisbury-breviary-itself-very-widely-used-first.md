# The Sarum Breviary

The Sarum Breviary, also known as the Salisbury Breviary, was a widely used liturgical book containing the offices for the canonical hours of the Roman Catholic Church. As a "use" or local variation of the Breviary, it served as a manual for the daily service, providing the lessons and hymns required for every day of the year.

## Printing and Editions
The first edition of the Sarum Breviary was printed in folio at Venice in 1483 by Raynald de Novimagio. The latest known editions were printed in Paris in 1556 and 1557. A distinguishing bibliographical feature of the Sarum editions was their volume structure; while modern Breviaries are typically printed in four volumes (one for each season), the Sarum editions never exceeded two parts.

## Regional Use and the Aberdeen Office
The Sarum Rite was particularly favored in Scotland, where it was utilized as a form of protest against the jurisdiction claimed by the church of York. One specific derivative was the Aberdeen Office, a Scottish form of the Sarum Office revised by William Elphinstone, who served as bishop from 1483 to 1514. This version was printed in Edinburgh by Andrew Myllar and Walter Chapman between 1509 and 1510. 

Although a royal mandate in 1501 enjoined the Aberdeen Office for general use within Scotland, it was likely never widely adopted. Only four copies of this edition survived the convulsions of the 16th century, with only one being complete. This text is considered particularly valuable for its embedded trustworthy notices regarding the early history of Scotland found within the lives of national saints. In 1854, the duke of Buccleuch funded a facsimile reprint for the Bannatyne Club. Elements of the old Aberdeen antiphons and collects were later included in the new Scottish *Proprium* sanctioned for the Roman Catholic province of St Andrews in 1903.

## Liturgical Practices and Rubrics
The use of Sarum (along with Bangor) dictated specific rubrics for the use of incense during services. According to Sarum and Bangor practice, the priest censed the altar before the Introit began, after he himself had been censed by the deacon. The middle of the altar was censed before the reading of the Gospel, and the thurible and lights attended the Gospel to the lectern. 

During the offertory, the Sarum and Bangor practice required the priest to cense the oblations after offering them, as well as the space between himself and the altar. Following this, the deacon censed the priest, and an acolyte censed the choir. These practices differed from the York rubric, which directed the censing of the altar immediately after the first saying of the Introit and ordered the thurible to be carried around the choir with the Gospel during the singing of the Creed.

## Context within the Breviary Tradition
The Breviary itself evolved from the separation of the Psalter into a choir-book. By the 4th century, psalms were grouped together, a process furthered by monastic practices of reciting the 150 psalms over a week, divided into hours. By the 12th century, the canonical office required several books, including the *Antiphonarium*, the *Psalterium*, the *Collectarium*, and the *Legendarius* (dealing with saints).

Until the Council of Trent, bishops had the power to regulate the Breviary of their own diocese, and monastic communities maintained their own versions. However, Pope Pius V later made the Roman Breviary obligatory in most places, except for those that could prove 200 years of existence. The Roman Breviary eventually superseded almost all local "uses."
