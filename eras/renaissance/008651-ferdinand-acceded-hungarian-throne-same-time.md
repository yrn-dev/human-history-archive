# Ferdinand I

Ferdinand I, an Archduke of Austria and member of the Habsburg dynasty, was a central figure in 16th-century European politics, holding multiple crowns and titles across Central Europe. He is noted for establishing the permanent accession of the Habsburgs to the Bohemian throne and for his subsequent roles as King of the Romans and Emperor.

## Accession to the Bohemian and Hungarian Thrones
Following the death of Louis, Bohemia entered a period of anarchy and disorder, largely because the previous two kings had resided primarily in Hungary. This lack of a strong indigenous government left the throne open to several candidates, including King Sigismund of Poland, the dukes of Bavaria, various German princes, and Bohemian noblemen such as Leo of Rozmital.

Archduke Ferdinand laid claim to the throne as the husband of Anna, the daughter of King Vladislav. On October 23, 1526, a group of twenty-four electors—chosen in equal numbers from the three estates—elected Ferdinand of Habsburg as king. This event marked the beginning of the permanent Habsburg presence on the Bohemian throne. Ferdinand acceded to the Hungarian throne at the same time as he did to that of Bohemia.

## Governance and Religious Policy
Ferdinand's primary objective in Bohemia was to secure the hereditary right of the Habsburg dynasty to the throne. He pursued this goal with obstinacy. In 1541, after a great fire in Prague destroyed state documents, Ferdinand convinced the estates to replace the original charter—which stated he had been elected—with a new charter asserting that he was recognized as king based on the hereditary rights of his wife, Anna. This change caused significant dissatisfaction and contributed to subsequent unrest.

Despite his traditional Habsburg devotion to Rome, Ferdinand exhibited great moderation in religious matters during the early part of his reign. However, he later established the Jesuits in Bohemia to help make the knights and nobles more amenable to the royal will. By the time of his later successes, the power of the cities had been undermined and serfdom had been established, meaning the peasantry was no longer a dangerous force.

## The "Bloody Diet" and Succession
Ferdinand's success in consolidating power was marked by a moment of severity. Following a revolt, four principal leaders—two citizens of Prague and two knights—were sentenced to death. They were decapitated on August 22 in the square outside the Hradcany palace where the estates were meeting. Consequently, this diet became known as the "Krvavy'sneem," or the "bloody diet."

Toward the end of his life, in 1562, Ferdinand ensured the continuation of his dynasty by obtaining the coronation of his eldest son, Maximilian, as king of Bohemia.

## Imperial Roles and Death
Ferdinand's political reach extended beyond Bohemia and Hungary. In 1531, largely due to the influence of his brother, Emperor Charles V, Ferdinand was elected King of the Romans and heir to the Empire. This role involved him deeply in German politics. Following the death of Charles V, Ferdinand became Emperor.

As Emperor, Ferdinand and his two successors paid tribute to the Turk and remained deeply entangled in Hungarian affairs. Because the Austrian line remained obstinately Catholic and focused on its eastern frontiers, it failed to maintain a grip on the north Germans, who were inclined toward Protestantism and indifferent to the Turkish threat. Ferdinand I died in 1564.
