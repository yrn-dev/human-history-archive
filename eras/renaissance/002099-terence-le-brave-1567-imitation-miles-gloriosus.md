# Terence

## Overview and Origins
P. Terentius Afer, known as Terence, was a Roman comic poet who lived approximately between 185 and 159 B.C. As indicated by his cognomen, he was a native of Carthage. He is recognized as one of the most successful authors of the *palliata*, a genre of Roman comedy that treated Greek subjects and imitated Greek originals.

## The Palliata Genre
The *palliata* (named for a specific type of dress) sought its primary inspiration from New Attic comedy. As the style of these plays became more refined, authors became increasingly dependent on their Greek models and avoided using local allusions or "gross seasonings" that would appeal to the coarser tastes of the general public. While the composition of *palliatae* eventually ceased to be common—becoming limited to select circles after roughly a century of cultivation (c. 250-150 B.C.)—the works of Terence and Plautus continued to be performed on stage even after the establishment of the Roman Empire.

## Literary Style and Comparisons
Terence is often compared to other Roman comic poets, such as Plautus and Caecilius Statius. Unlike Plautus, who borrowed plots from Diphilus and Philemon and incorporated national and popular elements, Terence focused on a more refined approach. He tended to eliminate elements from his Greek sources that could not be effectively romanized.

His style and Latinity were highly regarded by later Roman writers. Cicero considered Terence superior to Caecilius Statius in these areas. Horace also contrasted the specific art of Terence with the dignity found in the works of Caecilius.

## Influence and Legacy
The influence of Terence extended into the study of the Latin language. In the 4th century A.D., the Latin grammarian Arusianus Messius (also known as Messus) authored a work titled *Exempla Elocutionum* (referred to by Cassiodorus as the *Quadriga*). This work, dedicated to the consuls Olybrius and Probinus in 395, utilized an alphabetical list of verbs and provided examples from four primary writers: Cicero, Sallust, Virgil, and Terence.

Additionally, the judgment of Terence's work was sought from his contemporaries; for instance, if the account provided by Suetonius is accurate, the poet Caecilius Statius was ordered to listen to a reading of Terence's play *Andria* in 166 B.C. and provide his professional opinion on it.
