# Christian of Anhalt

## Role in Bohemian Religious Politics
Christian of Anhalt was a prominent figure in the early 17th century, recognized as the leader of the German Protestants. During a period of significant religious tension in Bohemia, he became a key contact for King Rudolph. Despite Rudolph's public appearance as a champion of Catholicism, the king engaged in secret negotiations with Christian of Anhalt.

These clandestine dealings occurred around the time of the diet that met on January 20, 1609, a pivotal event in Bohemian history. The court of Spain was aware of these negotiations, and the Bohemian estates also possessed this knowledge. Consequently, the Bohemians were not intimidated by Rudolph's sudden adoption of a truculent attitude, as they knew the king could not rely on aid from Spain.

## Context of the Bohemian Religious Struggle
The political environment in which Christian of Anhalt operated was defined by the demands of the Bohemian estates. In March of the year preceding the 1609 diet, the estates had drafted a document containing twenty-five Articles. These articles sought several religious and political concessions, including:

*   The recognition of the *Confessio Bohemica* as one of the kingdom's fundamental laws.
*   The granting of complete religious liberty to all classes.
*   Equal rights for Roman Catholics and Protestants—a term then used to collectively describe Lutherans, Utraquists, and Bohemian Brethren—to hold all offices of state.
*   Limitations on the ability of the Jesuits to acquire land.
*   Redress for grievances resulting from Rudolph's misrule.

While the estates appointed twelve negotiators, including Count Henry Matthias Thurn, to discuss these matters, Rudolph ultimately stated on March 31 that he would grant no concessions regarding religion.

## The Protestant Landscape in Germany and Anhalt
The leadership of figures like Christian of Anhalt existed within a broader, often divided, German Protestant tradition. In the second half of the 16th century, the Lutheran church faced internal fragmentation between the stricter Lutherans, headquartered at the university of Jena under Matthias Flacius, and the Philippists (followers of Melanchthon) centered in Leipzig and Wittenberg.

While many adopted the *Book of Concord* (1577), it was rejected by several regions, including Anhalt, Hesse, Pomerania, and Denmark. Those who refused the *Book of Concord* largely became Reformed or Calvinistic, often adopting the *Heidelberg Catechism*.

Later, the divided state of German Protestantism contributed to the disasters of the Thirty Years' War. Efforts to unite the confessions occurred through civil authority in various regions. In Anhalt-Dessau, such a union was effected in 1827. These unions generally focused on bringing congregations of different confessions under a single government and discipline rather than unifying doctrine or worship, allowing congregations to choose between the *Heidelberg Catechism* and the *Augsburg Confession*.
