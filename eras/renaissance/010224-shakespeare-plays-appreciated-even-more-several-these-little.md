# William Shakespeare and the Elizabethan Stage

## Influence and Predecessors
The development of Elizabethan drama was significantly shaped by pioneers such as Thomas Kyd. Kyd is regarded as a leader and almost an inventor of the movement of secular drama in England, with his work *The Spanish Tragedy* serving as the earliest specimen of effective stage poetry in English literature. This play, first exercised around 1586, provided a starting date for Elizabethan drama and remained popular throughout the careers of Fletcher, Ben Jonson, and William Shakespeare. Kyd's innovating genius is particularly evident in the bold treatment of scenes of violent crime, an influence marked on all of Shakespeare's immediate predecessors. Specifically, Kyd's relation to *Hamlet* has been noted, and *Titus Andronicus* is seen to exaggerate many of Kyd's characteristics.

Christopher Marlowe also exerted a profound influence. It is considered nearly certain that the better part of the serious scenes in *King Henry VI* are mainly Marlowe's work. Furthermore, Marlowe is regarded as the principal author of the second and third plays under that name, which were first printed imperfectly as *The Contention between the two Famous Houses of York and Lancaster*. While some have attributed *A Taming of a Shrew* to Marlowe, this is viewed as without good reason. Additionally, Fleay conjectured that *Richard III* and *Edward III*, plays usually included in Shakespeare's editions, are at least based on works by Marlowe.

## Dramatic Works and Philosophy
Shakespeare's tragedies, including *Macbeth*, *Lear*, *Othello*, *Hamlet*, the three Roman plays, and the quasi-antique plays *Timon of Athens* and *Troilus and Cressida*, are described as a body of drama apart from anything else in the world. These works introduced a new poetic style, dramatic technique, tragic philosophy, and world of characters. 

The intellectual movement of the time is reflected in these tragedies more than elsewhere, specifically the "new and troublous thoughts on man and conduct" and the freedom of vision stimulated by Montaigne. Florio's 1603 translation of Montaigne's *Essays*, published between the first and second versions of *Hamlet*, is cited as having counted for more than any other book. Shakespeare also appropriated the ancient patterns of heroism and right living found in North's Plutarch. Beyond his plays, the *Sonnets* (published 1609) explore far-wandering thoughts on beauty, truth, good, and evil.

## Performance and Production
During Shakespeare's time, the parts for women were always performed by boys or young men. This practice was mirrored in the Chinese stage, where women were not allowed to perform from the days of Emperor Ch'ien Lung (A.D. 1736-1796).

Richard Burbage (c. 1567-1619) was the preeminent actor of the Elizabethan stage and performed all the great parts in Shakespeare's plays. Burbage was especially famous for his impersonation of Richard III and excelled in tragedy. In 1569, Richard and his brother Cuthbert pulled down a Shoreditch house to erect the Globe theatre, which is famous for its connection to Shakespeare. Shakespeare was a partner in this venture, along with others. Burbage was also a painter; the Felton portrait of Shakespeare is attributed to him.

## Critical Reception and Collectibles
The prestige of first editions of English literary masterpieces has increased immensely. For example, a first folio of Shakespeare from 1623 was sold in 1906. Collectors value such books due to their associations with famous poets or the pedigree of previous book-loving owners.

Critical analysis of Shakespeare's work has varied over time. Otto Ludwig, a German dramatist and critic, showed a fine insight into the creative imagination in his *Shakespeare-Studien* (published 1891), though his enthusiasm for Shakespeare led him to depreciate Schiller. In contrast, Delia Bacon (1811-1859) published *The Philosophy of the Plays of Shakespeare Unfolded* (1857). In this work, she attempted to prove that the plays attributed to Shakespeare were actually written by a coterie of men—including Edmund Spenser, Sir Walter Raleigh, and Francis Bacon—to instill a philosophic system without assuming personal responsibility for it.
