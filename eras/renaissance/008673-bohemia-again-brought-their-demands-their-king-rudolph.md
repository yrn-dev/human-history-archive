# The Reign of Rudolph II in Bohemia

The reign of Rudolph II (1576–1612) was a period of significant religious and political instability in Bohemia, characterized by the king's personal struggles, conflict with his brother Matthias, and the persistent demands of the Bohemian estates for religious liberty.

## Accession and Early Governance
Rudolph succeeded Maximilian II without opposition after the estates of Bohemia recognized him as successor and consented to his coronation. Having been educated in Spain, there were initial expectations that Rudolph would treat Bohemian church reformers with severity. However, the early part of his reign was marked by apathy and indifference toward both matters of state and religious controversy.

Rudolph's personal life was complicated by fits of melancholia and periods of actual insanity. Despite these impairments, he was a great patron of the arts and contributed significantly to the embellishment of Prague, which served as his favorite residence and the center of the Habsburg dominions. By 1600, his mental condition had deteriorated to the point that other Habsburg princes considered the future of the state, as Rudolph had no legitimate descendants.

## Conflict with Matthias
Tensions arose between Rudolph and his younger brother, Matthias, who sought to supplant him by leveraging political and religious controversies. The formal rupture occurred when Rudolph refused to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey that Matthias had concluded while representing his brother in Hungary. Consequently, the Hungarians accepted Matthias as their ruler.

Matthias expanded his influence into Moravia, where the estates—led by Charles, lord of Zerotin—renounced their allegiance to Rudolph. Matthias then invaded Bohemia and invited the estates to join him at Caslav. Unexpectedly, the Bohemians declined these overtures. In March 1608, the estates met at Prague; while they submitted demands regarding ecclesiastical matters to Rudolph, they authorized him to levy troops for Bohemia's defense.

The conflict reached a temporary resolution on June 25, 1608, at Liben. Rudolph formally ceded the government of Upper and Lower Austria, Moravia, and Hungary to Matthias, though Rudolph retained his rights as the king of Bohemia.

## The Diet of 1609 and Religious Demands
Following the settlement with Matthias, the Bohemian estates again brought their demands before the king. Rudolph had previously declined to discuss religious matters while Matthias's troops occupied parts of Bohemia. The diet that convened on January 20, 1609, became a pivotal event in Bohemian history.

The estates sought the recognition of the *Confessio Bohemica* as one of the kingdom's fundamental laws and demanded complete religious liberty for all classes. They requested that Roman Catholics and Protestants—a term then used to collectively describe Lutherans, Utraquists, and Bohemian Brethren—possess equal rights to hold state offices. Additionally, they sought to limit the ability of Jesuits to acquire land and requested redress for grievances resulting from Rudolph's misrule.

These demands were formulated in a document of twenty-five "Articles." Rudolph, viewing the document as illegal, demanded its destruction. The document remained in the possession of Budova, who refused to surrender it. Despite the appointment of twelve negotiators, including Count Henry Matthias Thurn, the discussions were protracted. On March 31, Rudolph stated he could grant no concessions regarding religion.

## Final Years and Dethronement
Rudolph's later years were marked by vacillation. While appearing as a champion of Catholicism, he secretly negotiated with Christian of Anhalt, a leader of the German Protestants. This instability was noted by the court of Spain, leaving Rudolph without their aid and emboldening the Bohemian estates.

The peace of Bohemia was further disturbed in 1611 when Archduke Leopold of Austria, bishop of Passau, invaded the country, likely with Rudolph's connivance. Although Leopold took part of Prague, his army was defeated by troops raised by the Bohemian estates. This raid prompted Matthias to re-occupy Bohemia to protect his own hereditary rights. Under Matthias's instigation, the estates formally deposed Rudolph. Rudolph died a few months after his dethronement, on January 20, 1612.
