# Agreement and Membership in Historical Assemblies

The history of various political, religious, and international bodies is often marked by the pursuit of internal agreement among members to ensure stability, legal consistency, or the maintenance of a shared faith.

## The Bohemian Estates and the Diet of St Wenceslas
In the early sixteenth century, the constitutional development of Bohemia was significantly influenced by the relationship between the monarchy and the estates. During the nominal reign of King Louis, who had been crowned at age three, the Bohemian estates initially refused to allow his guardians—Sigismund, king of Poland, and emperor Maximilian I—any right of interference in Bohemian affairs. This period saw the country ruled almost without control by great nobles, specifically the supreme burgrave, Zdenek Leo, lord of Rozmital.

A pivotal shift occurred at the diet of St Wenceslas in 1517, where members first assembled on September 28. During this meeting, the estates settled long-standing causes of discord. The citizens renounced certain previously claimed privileges, while the other two estates recognized municipal autonomy and sanctioned the citizens' presence at diet meetings. On October 24, it was formally declared that the three estates had agreed henceforth to live together in friendly intercourse, as men belonging to the same race and country.

## Religious Confessions in Bohemia
By 1575, tensions resurfaced between the Bohemian estates and King Maximilian regarding religious recognition and the appointment of ecclesiastical leadership. The Bohemians demanded the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg," a summary of Luther's teaching, and sought the right to appoint members of the consistory, the body ruling the Utraquist church.

Following the king's refusal to recognize the Augsburg confession, a majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—created the *Confessio Bohemica*. This profession of faith was identical to the Augsburg confession on almost all points, with the exception of the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. Regarding this specific sacrament, the Bohemian profession aligned with the views of Calvin rather than Luther, a result of the influence of the Bohemian Brethren. While Maximilian verbally approved the *Confessio Bohemica*, he refused to make the approval public or include it in the kingdom's charters. He did, however, exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the consistory's jurisdiction and allowed them to choose fifteen defenders (five from each estate).

## International Covenants and the League
In the context of international relations, the League established a framework where members pledged to specific behaviors to avoid war. Members agreed to submit disputes to inquiry or arbitration and pledged not to resort to war until three months after an award was issued. Those who complied with an arbitral award were guaranteed that other members would not go to war with them.

The League's members further agreed to the following:
*   The establishment of a permanent court of international justice for advisory opinions or the determination of disputes.
*   Acceptance of the jurisdiction of the Assembly for those who did not submit cases to arbitration.
*   The creation of a permanent bureau of labor to maintain humane conditions for women, men, and children.
*   The maintenance of equitable treatment for commerce and freedom of transit for all member States.
*   The placement of all existing and future international bureaus under the control of the League, provided the parties to the treaties consented.

Members who disregarded the covenant by resorting to war were to be immediately debarred from all intercourse with other members.

## Other Instances of Membership and Discord
Historical records also note instances where membership in a group was characterized by internal conflict or external attack. In ancient Greece, the Pythagoreans faced an attack by enemies who set fire to the house where the members were assembled. Similarly, during an Athenian naval expedition to Eubœa, the crews were described as raw and ill-selected due to reigning dissension and mistrust. Among the crew was Polystratus, a member of the Four Hundred, who was described as a man who had an interest in defeat rather than victory.
