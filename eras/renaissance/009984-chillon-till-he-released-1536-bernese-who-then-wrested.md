# Chillon

The name Chillon appears in the archive's records in two distinct contexts: as a site of political imprisonment in Switzerland and as the name of an individual in the history of the Israelites.

## The Castle of Chillon and François Bonivard

The castle of Chillon served as a place of incarceration for François Bonivard (1493–1570). Born into an old Savoyard family at Seyssel, Bonivard succeeded his uncle in 1510 as the prior of the Cluniac priory of St Victor, located near Geneva. Due to his opposition to the duke of Savoy—who, with the assistance of the bishop of the city, sought to maintain rights as the lord of Geneva—Bonivard became increasingly anti-Savoyard.

Following an initial imprisonment at Gex from 1519 to 1521, during which he lost his priory, Bonivard was seized again by the duke in 1530. He was imprisoned for four years in the underground quarters of the castle of Chillon. His detention was based on political reasons; he did not convert to Protestantism until after his release.

Bonivard was released from Chillon in 1536 by the Bernese, who subsequently wrested Vaud from the duke of Savoy. Upon his release, Bonivard discovered that his priory had been destroyed in 1534. In his later years, he received a pension from Geneva and married four times, though he remained in debt due to extravagances. In 1542, he was officially tasked with compiling a history of Geneva. His manuscript, the *Chroniques de Geneve* (which concludes in 1530), was submitted to Calvin for correction in 1551, though it was not published until 1831. While the work is described as partial and uncritical, it is considered his primary claim to fame. Bonivard also served as the subject of the poem *The Prisoner of Chillon* by Byron, though the legendary account in the poem differs widely from his actual history.

## Chillon in Israelite History

In the accounts of the Israelites, Chillon was the son of Elimelech of Bethlehem, a city within the tribe of Judah. During a period of famine in the country, Elimelech took his wife, Naomi, and their two sons, Mahlon and Chillon, and moved his household to the land of Moab.

While in Moab, Chillon and his brother married Moabite women; Chillon married Orpah. Within a period of ten years, both Elimelech and his sons, including Chillon, died. This left Naomi in a lonesome condition, eventually leading her to return to her own country after learning it was once again in a flourishing condition. While Naomi's daughter-in-law Ruth insisted on accompanying her, Orpah remained behind in Moab.
