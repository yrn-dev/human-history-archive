# Protestant Estates and Religious Conflict in Bohemia

The history of the Bohemian estates is characterized by a prolonged struggle for religious liberty and the preservation of ancient constitutional privileges against the centralizing efforts of the Habsburg dynasty. This conflict involved various Protestant groups—collectively including the Utraquists, Lutherans, and the Bohemian Brethren—and their efforts to secure legal recognition and political equality within the kingdom.

## Religious Demands and the Confessio Bohemica
During the 16th century, the views of Bohemian reformers evolved, with some advanced Utraquists aligning closely with German Lutherans and the Bohemian Brethren gaining influence through the support of powerful nobles. In 1575, during the reign of Maximilian, the Bohemian estates sought the recognition of the "Confession of Augsburg," a summary of Lutheran teaching. They also demanded the right to appoint members of the consistory, the ecclesiastical body governing the Utraquist church.

When Maximilian refused these demands, a majority of the diet—composed of advanced Utraquists and members of the Bohemian brotherhood—drafted the *Confessio Bohemica*. While largely identical to the Augsburg confession, the *Confessio Bohemica* differed regarding the doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper, aligning instead with the views of Calvin, a shift attributed to the influence of the Bohemian Brethren. Although Maximilian verbally approved the document, he refused to make it public or include it among the kingdom's charters. He did, however, exempt advanced Utraquists and Lutherans from the consistory's jurisdiction and allowed them to choose fifteen defenders from the estates.

## Political Struggles under the Habsburgs
The relationship between the estates and the monarchy remained volatile. In the mid-16th century, Ferdinand attempted to raise a Bohemian army to support his brother, but faced significant resistance. Many nobles and cities, particularly those associated with the Bohemian Brethren, refused to provide men. By the end of 1546, Ferdinand was forced to disband his forces. In March 1547, the estates met in Prague without a royal summons—an unconstitutional act—driven by a desire to recover ancient liberties and privileges that Ferdinand had encroached upon by claiming a hereditary right to the crown.

Later, Ferdinand occupied Prague on July 20 with Spanish and Walloon mercenaries. While he treated knights and nobles with forbearance, he dealt severely with the towns, specifically Prague. He revised ancient privileges to confiscate municipal lands and imposed state officials, known as town-captains in Prague and town-judges elsewhere, who exercised nearly unlimited control over municipal affairs. During this period, the Bohemian Brethren were severely persecuted, and their bishop, Augusta, was imprisoned for many years.

## The Diet of 1609 and the Letter of Majesty
A pivotal moment occurred during the reign of Rudolph. In January 1609, a significant diet met in Prague. The estates had previously formulated twenty-five "Articles" demanding that the *Confessio Bohemica* be recognized as a fundamental law of the kingdom and that complete religious liberty be granted to all classes. They further sought equal rights for Protestants and Roman Catholics to hold state offices and requested limits on the Jesuits' ability to acquire land.

Although Rudolph initially viewed these articles as illegal and demanded their destruction, the estates negotiated through twelve representatives, including Count Henry Matthias Thurn. While Rudolph eventually stated on March 31 that he would grant no concessions in religious matters, the period was marked by intense instability and shifting alliances between Rudolph and his brother Matthias.

## Escalation and the Outbreak of Conflict
By 1617, tensions rose again as King Matthias sought to secure the succession for his cousin Ferdinand, Duke of Styria, who was known as a cruel persecutor of Protestants in Styria. Despite this, state officials persuaded the estates to accept Ferdinand as heir. 

The conflict reached a breaking point in December 1617 when the abbot of Brevnov and the archbishop of Prague ordered the suppression of Protestant services in churches located on their domains. This act was a direct infringement of the agreement established when King Rudolph signed the Letter of Majesty. In response, Protestant members of the diet assembled in Prague on May 21, 1618. On May 22, a secret meeting involving Wenceslas of Ruppa, Ulrich of Kinsky, and Count Thurn resulted in a decision to put the most influential of Matthias's councillors to death. On May 23, the Protestant representatives proceeded to the Hradcany palace.

## Later Developments and National Aspirations
Bohemia remained a theater of war during the Thirty Years' War. In 1631, Saxons occupied parts of the country attempting to re-establish Protestantism, and in 1648, Swedish general Konigsmark took part of Prague. Despite Swedish efforts, imperial representatives remained inflexible regarding toleration for Bohemian Protestants.

By the 19th century, the struggle shifted toward national aspirations. In 1845, the estates opposed the government in Vienna, asserting their right to vote on taxes based on the constitution of 1627. By 1847 and 1848, their demands expanded to include more extensive representation for towns at the diet and the introduction of the Bohemian language into all higher schools and government offices.
