# Calvinistic or Particular Baptist Church

The Calvinistic or Particular Baptist Church represents a distinct branch of the Baptist tradition in England, characterized by its adherence to Calvinistic theology and the doctrine of "Particular Redemption." This group emerged as a separate entity from the General Baptists, who held Arminian views and believed in "General Redemption."

## Origins and the Jacob Church
The rise of the first Calvinistic or Particular Baptist Church is associated with the Jacob church in Southwark. Originally an Independent church, the Jacob church later became Baptist. Its membership included several notable figures: William Kiffin (1616–1701), Hanserd Knollys (ca. 1599–1691), Henry Jessey (1601–1663), Praise-God Barbon (ca. 1596–1679), and John Lothropp (or Lathrop, d. 1653).

The Jacob church served as a catalyst for further growth, with six other churches springing from it, five of which were Baptist. However, the first Particular Baptist Church was actually formed in 1633 by a group of members dismissed from the Jacob church because the congregation had grown beyond what was considered proper limits.

## Doctrine and Development
The Particular Baptists differed from the General Baptists primarily in their theological origins; while General Baptists were influenced by Mennonite Anabaptists, Particular Baptists sprang from the Independents. Both groups rejected the baptism of infants, though initially, neither was fully committed to the practice of immersion.

The development of their baptismal doctrine was shaped by disputes over three primary questions:
1. The proper administrator of baptism.
2. The proper subjects of baptism.
3. The proper mode of baptism.

By 1644, the Particular Baptist churches of London, which had grown to seven, published a Confession of Faith. This document noted that they were commonly, though falsely, called "Anabaptist."

## Evolution of Beliefs and Membership
Over time, the Calvinism of the Particular Baptists shifted. In some churches, it became more rigid, approaching or becoming Antinomianism. Conversely, toward the end of the 18th century, many Particular Baptist churches became more moderate in their Calvinism, a change largely attributed to the writings of Andrew Fuller.

Regarding membership and communion, the majority of Baptists historically required baptism for admission. A significant exception existed in churches in Hertfordshire and Bedfordshire influenced by Bunyan, who argued that differences of opinion regarding water baptism should not bar communion. This led to long-continued discussions at the beginning of the 19th century, involving Robert Hall (1764–1831), and the eventual spread of "mixed communion." More recently, some churches have admitted persons professing faith in Christ to full membership even if they do not agree on the ordinance of baptism, citing the right of private judgment.

## Organizational Structure and Unions
In terms of government, Particular Baptists agree with Congregationalists that each separate church is complete in itself, possessing the power to choose its own ministers and establish its own regulations to advance the kingdom of Christ. A small section, known as "Scotch Baptists" in Scotland and the north of England, maintains that a "plurality of elders" or pastors is necessary for complete organization.

The Particular Baptists formed the Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland in 1813. This union is voluntary and does not exert authoritative action over separate churches. In 1891, under the influence of General Baptist leader Dr. John Clifford, the General and Particular denominations united into a single body called "The Baptist Union of Great Britain and Ireland."

## Strict Baptist Churches
Despite the general union, some churches remain separate as "Strict" Baptist churches. These groups are characterized by a rigorous adherence to a type of Calvinistic theology now generally considered obsolete and a strict insistence that baptism is a condition for Christian communion. They are often organized into geographical associations, such as the Metropolitan Association of Strict Baptist Churches and the Suffolk and Norfolk Association of Particular Baptist Churches.

The Strict Baptists are further divided into two rival groups: the "Standard" party and the "Vessel" party, named after their respective organs, the *Gospel Standard* and *Earthen Vessel*. The Strict Baptist Missionary Society, founded in 1860 and refounded in 1897, conducts mission work in South India. As of 1905, this society had 730 church members and 72 workers, with an income of £1146.
