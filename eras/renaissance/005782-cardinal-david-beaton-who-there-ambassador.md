# Cardinal David Beaton

Cardinal David Beaton was a high-ranking official of the Scottish hierarchy during a period of significant religious and political instability in Scotland. He served as a cardinal-archbishop and exerted substantial influence over the Scottish government and the enforcement of religious orthodoxy.

## Political Influence and Relations
Beaton held a position of significant power during the reign of James V, who is described as the "Commons' King." During this time, James V placed himself "into the hands of the Beatons." Following the death of James V, Beaton continued to wield authority, taking possession of the weak regent Arran.

His influence extended beyond Scotland's borders. He served as an ambassador in Paris, where he was encountered by the scholar George Buchanan. Buchanan, who had fled Scotland after being arrested during the persecution of Lutherans in 1539, found Beaton in Paris, which prompted Buchanan to proceed to Bordeaux on the invitation of André de Gouvéa.

## Religious Persecutions and Conflict
As a leader of the Scottish hierarchy, Beaton was a firm opponent of the growing tendency toward freedom in religious profession and the shift toward friendship with England over France. The hierarchy under his influence met these dangers firmly. Beaton is associated with the execution of several individuals for their beliefs: in 1528, Patrick Hamilton was burned, and in 1546, George Wishart was burned.

These actions made Beaton a target of intense hatred. The 3rd Earl of Cassillis, while intriguing with the English, offered to kill Cardinal Beaton to serve the interests of Henry VIII. Similarly, Sir James Kirkcaldy of Grange was a determined opponent of the Cardinal.

## Assassination and Aftermath
The tension surrounding Beaton's leadership culminated in his murder in 1546. Sir James Kirkcaldy of Grange was partly responsible for the assassination, and his son, William Kirkcaldy of Grange, assisted incompassing the murder. Following the killing, certain gentlemen of Fife took possession of Beaton's castle at St Andrews.

The death of the cardinal created an opening for other reformers. John Knox, who had been fleeing from place to place after the execution of George Wishart, joined the men who had slain the cardinal at St Andrews. It was in this environment that Knox began teaching a catechism and "John's Gospel," eventually accepting a public charge of preaching.

## Historical Interpretations
In the historical records regarding George Buchanan's time in Portugal, some biographers previously attributed an attack by the Inquisition against Buchanan and his staff to the influence of the Jesuits, the Franciscans, or Cardinal Beaton. However, records published in 1906 suggest that Diogo de Gouvéa likely urged the Inquisition to attack the staff before the death of André de Gouvéa at the end of 1547.
