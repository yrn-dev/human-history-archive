# Fantastickes

*Fantastickes* is a literary work published in 1626. It is described as a series of short prose pictures of the months.

## Authorship and Publication
The work was written by Nicholas Breton (1545?–1626), an English poet from an old family based at Layer-Breton, Essex. Breton was a versatile writer who produced a variety of miscellaneous prose tracts, satires, and religious and pastoral poems. He was popular among his contemporaries, though he was forgotten by the subsequent generation.

*Fantastickes* was the last work published by Breton. It is believed that the author died shortly after its publication in 1626.

## About the Author
Nicholas Breton was the son of William Breton, a trader who acquired a considerable fortune before his death in 1559. Following William's death, his widow, Elizabeth Bacon, married the poet George Gascoigne. Nicholas Breton was likely born in the parish of St Giles without Cripplegate, at a "capitall mansion house" located in Red Cross Street.

While there is no official record of his university attendance, the diary of the Rev. Richard Madox indicates that Breton was "once of Oriel College" and was present in Antwerp in 1583. In 1593, he married Ann Sutton and started a family.

Throughout his career, Breton enjoyed the patronage of Mary, countess of Pembroke, writing many works in her honor until approximately 1601, when her favor appeared to be withdrawn.

## Literary Style and Beliefs
Breton is characterized as a facile writer. His religious poetry is noted for its sweetness and fluency, reflecting a devout and earnest mind. Although his references to Mary Magdalene and his praise of the Virgin led some to suggest he was a Catholic, his prose writings provide abundant proof that he was an ardent Protestant.

Some of Breton's life is documented through letters signed "N.B." in his work *A Poste with a Packet of Mad Letters* (1603, enlarged 1637). In these letters, he describes a life marked by various hardships, including imprisonment, illness, an "unquiet life," debt, and experiences of war, where he fared hard and slept in cold beds during bitter storms.
