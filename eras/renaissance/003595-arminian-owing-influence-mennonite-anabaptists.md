# Arminianism and the General Baptists

## Overview of Arminianism
Arminianism is a theological system developed and systematized by Simon Episcopius (1583–1643), who followed the teachings of Jacobus Arminius. Episcopius is regarded as the theological founder of the movement, as he expanded upon the principles tentatively put forward by Arminius. The core of Arminianism was defined in 1610 during a conference at the Hague, where followers of Arminius presented a "remonstrance" to the states-general. This document contained five articles, known as the five points of Arminianism.

The Arminian position was a reaction against the irresistibility of grace and the supralapsarian and infralapsarian developments of the doctrine of predestination. Specifically, Arminians held that Christ died for all men rather than only for the elect. Furthermore, they expressed uncertainty regarding whether the elect might fall from grace.

## Conflict with Calvinism
The rise of Arminian opinions led to bitter enmity with Calvinists. In the Netherlands, this theological divide became entangled with political issues. The bourgeois oligarchy of republican tendencies, including Hugo Grotius and Oldenbarnevelt, supported Arminianism, while the partisans of the house of Orange espoused strict Calvinism.

This tension culminated in the Synod of Dort (1618–1619), an assembly of the Reformed Dutch Church with deputies from several regions, including England, Scotland, and Switzerland. The synod was called to decide the differences between the Arminians (Remonstrants) and the Calvinists (Counter-Remonstrants). Simon Episcopius served as the spokesman for thirteen Remonstrant representatives; however, he was refused a hearing. Consequently, Remonstrant doctrines were condemned without a permitted defense. In 1619, Episcopius and the other twelve representatives were expelled from the country and deprived of their offices. The violence of the controversy only began to abate after the death of Maurice, prince of Orange, in 1625.

## Influence on the General Baptists
In England, the Baptist movement split into two distinct sets of churches with different origins and theological foundations. The General Baptists were Arminian, a characteristic attributed to the influence of the Mennonite Anabaptists. In contrast, the Particular Baptists were Calvinist, having sprung from the Independents.

The connection to Mennonite teaching is exemplified by the early modern Baptist movement. John Smith (or Smyth), who broke away from Brownism, emigrated to Amsterdam and came under Mennonite teaching in 1609. He and others, including Thomas Helwys, separated from the Independents and practiced baptism by pouring, following the Anabaptist or Mennonite fashion. This group formed the first English Baptist Church, which published a declaration of faith in 1611. This confession emphasized that baptism should only be administered upon a confession of faith and sins, rejecting the baptism of infants. They also advocated for absolute liberty of conscience, stating that the magistrate should not compel any form of religion or meddle with matters of conscience.

## The Mennonite Connection
The Mennonites were a group of Anabaptists who gathered under the leadership of Menno Simonis after the fall of Muenster. Joining the group in 1537, Simonis used his piety and moderation to control the more fanatical elements of the movement. Following his death in 1561, the Mennonites eventually settled in the Netherlands under the protection of William the Silent, prince of Orange.

While modern Baptists are characterized by the practice of immersion, the Mennonites are noted as Baptists who still practice baptism by pouring or sprinkling.

## Later Developments and Declension
In the decades preceding the "Great Awakening" of 1740–1743, Arminianism was associated with a period of religious declension in some American churches. A "Socinianized Arminianism" was said to have paralyzed evangelistic efforts. For example, the First Church in Providence became Arminian and remained aloof from the evangelism of Whitefield and Edwards. Similarly, the First Church in Newport was rent asunder by Arminianism.

During the Great Awakening, the General (Six Principles) Baptists of Connecticut and Rhode Island increased their membership. However, because the Great Awakening was conducted on Calvinistic principles, the General Baptists did not cooperate heartedly with the movement.
