# Bag-pipe

The bag-pipe is a complex reed instrument of great antiquity that serves as a link between the primitive organ and the syrinx. Its primary function is to provide a reservoir for wind-supply, allowing the performer to regulate sound-producing pressure using their arm.

## Structure and Components
A bag-pipe consists of an air-tight leather bag featuring three to five apertures, each containing a short tube or fixed stock. These stocks serve as air-chambers to protect the reeds and act as sockets for three types of pipes:

1.  **Insufflation Tube:** A simple valved "blow-pipe" used by the performer to fill the bag reservoir.
2.  **Chaunter (Chanter):** The melody-pipe, which always utilizes a double-reed. Depending on the variety, the chaunter may have a cylindrical or conical bore, a bell, lateral holes, and occasionally keys.
3.  **Drones:** Jointed pipes with a cylindrical bore that typically end in a bell. Drones lack lateral holes and are capable of producing only one fixed note.

## European Varieties
Bag-pipes have appeared in various forms across Europe, categorized largely by their method of inflation and structural design.

### German Sackpfeife and Dudelsack
Michael Praetorius described the German *Sackpfeife* (or *Dudelsack*) in five sizes. The *Grosser Bock* (double-bass bag-pipe) features a single long cylindrical drone and a chaunter, both ending in curved ram's horns; its drone is tuned to G, one octave below the chaunter. The *Bock* is similar but pitched a fourth higher in C. The *Schaeferpfeife* utilizes two drones (B flat and F), though Praetorius noted its upper chaunter notes had faulty intonation due to the lack of a thumb-hole. The *Huemmelchen* employs two drones tuned to F and C. The smallest variety, the *Dudey* (treble sack-pipe), has three drones tuned to E flat, B flat, and E flat.

Additionally, Praetorius mentioned a larger version of the *sackpfeife* seen in Magdeburg. This instrument featured two chaunters mounted in one stock, each with one thumb-hole at the back and three holes in front. The right-hand chaunter produced the notes D, E, F, G, A, while the left-hand chaunter produced G, A, B, C, D, allowing the performer to play simple two-part melodies. Its drones were G3 and D4, and the chaunter compass was D4 to D5.

### French and Italian Instruments
Before the *musette*, France, Italy, and the Netherlands used the *cornemuse* and *chalemie*. One variety of *cornemuse* used by shepherds featured a leather bag, a large drone (*gros bourdon*) of 2-1/2 feet, and a small drone (*petit bourdon*) of 1 foot, both tuned to C. Its conical-bore chaunter had eight holes (seven in front, one thumb-hole) with a compass of C to C', which could be extended to E by overblowing. A second type of *cornemuse* was played with the *Hautbois de Poitou* and featured a single drone with a double reed rather than a beating-reed.

The *musette* evolved during the 15th century and was fully developed by the 17th century. Unlike mouth-blown pipes, it is inflated by bellows worked by the arm. It replaced long drones with a compact barrel droner containing four or five drones. These drones used double "hautbois" reeds and could be adjusted via slides called *layettes* to obtain a simple harmonic bass. The *musette* chaunter had a narrow cylindrical bore, a double reed, and eleven holes (four with keys), providing a twelve-note compass from F4 to C6. Later, Martin Hotteterre added a second, shorter chaunter called the *petit chalumeau* (six keys) to accompany the *grand chalumeau* (seven keys).

### Other Regional Types
The *Calabrian bag-pipe* uses a goatskin bag with the hair left on and is inflated via a blow-pipe. It features two drones and two chaunters in one stock; the right-hand pipe includes a key enclosed in a perforated box to cover the fourth hole.

## Historical Origins
The bag-pipe's origins are traced to remote antiquity. While not found on known Egyptian or Assyrian monuments, evidence exists in ancient Persia, Chaldaea, and Greece. The Roman *tibia utricularis* and the ancient Greek *askaulos* belonged to the mouth-inflated class, though it is uncertain if they possessed drones. 

The principle of the drone—a beating-reed sunk into the pipe—was known to ancient Egyptians, as evidenced by a straw beating-reed found in a mummy-case in Turin. The modern Egyptian *arghoul* maintains the drone and chaunter but lacks the bag. In Persia, terracotta figures from the 8th century B.C. discovered at Susa depict musicians playing instruments with a bag under the arm and a hook-shaped chaunter.
