# Bach's Claim to the Title of "the First of Romanticists"

The musical legacy of Johann Sebastian Bach (1685–1750) is characterized by a profound and immediate effect on the history of music whenever his work was encountered by true composers. Despite this, his art was neglected by his younger contemporaries, who viewed it as crabbed and old-fashioned. For a significant period, his work survived only through desultory and unintelligent academic study until it was rediscovered by Mendelssohn.

## Connection to the Romantic Movement
There is a clear connection between the "Romantic" movement in music and the revival of Bach's work. This relationship is so distinct that Bach's claim to the title of "the first of Romanticists" is considered clear. However, the archive notes that the full influence of his entire body of work has hardly begun to show itself.

The depth of this influence was not immediately recognized by all enthusiasts. For example, Schumann died before the editors of the *Bach-Gesellschaft* began to grasp the main features of Bach's designs or find beauty in his ordinary musical language, which some had previously viewed as extravagance.

## The Bach-Gesellschaft and Scholarship
The *Bach-Gesellschaft* spent more than fifty years working to publish Bach's works, including approximately four-fifths of his choral compositions. Because of the bulk of works such as church cantatas, it is suggested that another fifty years might be required to make these works adequately known to the general public. 

Scholarship regarding Bach has evolved over time. While Robert Franz arranged some of Bach's finest works for modern performance, later scholarship demonstrated that a strict adherence to Bach's own scoring offered incomparably greater beauty. Wilhelm Rust, an editor for the *Bach-Gesellschaft* for fourteen years, is regarded as the "Porson of Bach-scholarship" due to his unrivaled insight into Bach's style and his ability to restore priceless harmonies from incomplete texts. His prefaces to the *Bach-Gesellschaft* volumes are considered among the most valuable contributions to the criticism of 18th-century music.

## Influence on Later Composers
Bach's art acted as a subtle and universal force in musical development, even during periods when his musical language seemed forgotten. 

*   **Mozart:** Upon studying manuscript copies of the *Wohltemperirtes Klavier* and various motets provided by Baron von Swieten, Mozart's style developed a depth of harmony and polyphonic richness that increased until his death.
*   **Beethoven:** Beethoven studied accessible works of Bach profoundly and frequently quoted them in his sketch-books. A comparison between Beethoven's 31st variation of the "Thirty-three Variations on a Waltz by Diabelli" and Bach's 25th "Goldberg Variation" reveals a kinship in their most intimate expressions of feeling.

## Musical Characteristics and Design
Bach's melodies are described as characteristic and possessing a vital individuality, similar to a living face with mobile features rather than a static mask. His larger musical designs are compared to a cathedral in the way they react to their changing contents. This individuality ensures that his melodies are greater than any momentary mood, avoiding a "pseudo-classical" quality of reserve.

## Catalog of Works and Authenticity
Bach's output includes a wide array of organ music, such as the *Das Orgelbuechlein* (a planned but partially executed collection of short choral-preludes), 18 larger chorale-preludes, and the "Schuebler" chorales. Other significant works include *Das musikalische Opfer*—comprising two great fugues, puzzle-canons, and a trio—and *Die Kunst der Fuge*, a series of progressive fugues.

The authenticity of some works has been a subject of scholarly debate because Bach's autographs typically only list the composer's name on the outside sheet. This has led to misattributions. For instance, an 8-part motet titled *Lob, Ehr' und Weisheit* was eventually discovered to have been written by a contemporary named Wagner, rather than Bach.
