# French and Paris Measurements and References

## Barometric and Linear Measures
In the field of meteorology and scientific observation, various scales have been employed to measure the height of the barometer. While England and America utilize English inches, and most European countries use the metric system (where a millimetre is one thousandth of a metre, equaling 39.37079 English inches), other specific measures exist. In a few European countries, the French or Paris line is sometimes used, which equals 0.088814 inches. 

Historically, Russia used half-lines until 1869, a measure equaling one twentieth of an English inch, though Russia has since adopted the metric barometric scale. Because different standards are calibrated at different temperatures—the metric scale at 32° Fahr., the old French measure at 61.2° Fahr., and the English measure at 62° Fahr.—observations must be reduced to a single temperature to neutralize inequalities caused by the expansion of the scales due to heat.

Other historical French linear measures include the *toise*. In calculations regarding the size of ancient cities, a French *toise* is sometimes estimated at 600 units (relative to Greek miles). When dividing the cities of Paris and Constantinople into equal squares of 50 French *toises*, Paris contains 1,160 such divisions, while Constantinople contains 850.

## Literary and Artistic Contributions in Paris
Paris has served as a center for significant literary and artistic production. The poet Belleau, described as the "French Herrick," wrote *La Bergerie* (1565-1572), a pastoral work in prose and verse imitating Sannazaro. His works, including the comedy *La Reconnue* and a macaronic poem on the religious wars titled *Dictamen metrificum de bello huguenotico et reistrorum piglamine ad sodales*, were collected in 1578. Belleau died on March 6, 1577, and was buried in the nave of the Grands Augustins at Paris.

In the arts, Paris was the site of publications regarding the Barbizon School of Painters and the work of C. Daubigny. Various texts on Daubigny's oeuvre and contemporary painters and sculptors were published in Paris between 1878 and 1882.

## Military and Strategic Lines
The term "line" also appears in military contexts regarding French defenses and operations. During conflicts with German armies, the "Paris-Verdun line" was a critical strategic area. The French armies between Paris and Verdun, including those under Foch, Dubail, and de Castelnau, maintained resistance against German advances. Specifically, the British army played a decisive role in the military situation around Paris, contributing to a gap of 30 miles in the German line between von Kluck and von Bülow.

Additionally, the French utilized communication lines during retreats. German forces discovered complete telephone systems laid underground, consisting of main cables from peace time supplemented by temporary lines and telephone stations rigged in unsuspected locations. These lines allowed French fire to be directed with high accuracy and rapidity.

## Linguistic and Bibliographic Notes
The French language has varied in style and application. In certain manuscripts, such as those related to Marco Polo, the language used is referred to as French but is noted as not being the "Frenche of Paris." This specific style was characterized by a confusion of tenses, moods, numbers, subject, and object, often incorporating Italian or Oriental words.

Paris has also been a hub for colonial and ethnographic publishing. Various works regarding Dahomey—covering its topography, economics, and the story of the French conquest—were published in Paris between 1885 and 1909. These include *Notre Colonie de Dahomey* (1906), *Le Dahomey* (1909), and the *Bibliographie raisonnee des ouvrages concernant le Dahomey* (1895). Additionally, a *Carte du Dahomey* with a scale of 1:500,000 was published in Paris in 1907.
