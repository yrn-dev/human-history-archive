# Printing and Book Production in 17th Century England

## Printing Quality and Evolution
Printing in 17th century England experienced significant fluctuations in quality. During the second quarter of the century, printing reached its lowest depths. However, a steady improvement followed, which was partially attributed to the adoption of slight modifications to old printing presses. These modifications were first implemented in Holland and subsequently copied by English printers.

Despite poor printing quality in the first half of the century, many English books featured attractive portraits or frontispieces engraved on copper. During this same period, small New Testaments, Bibles, and English prayer-books were frequently adorned with gold or silver thread and gay embroideries in coloured silks.

## Binding and Trade Practices
Binding styles evolved throughout the century. In the second half of the 17th century, the daintiest leather bindings produced in England were those of Samuel Mearne, who imitated the work of the French binder Le Gascon. For trade bindings, sheepskin and rough calf were the most commonly used materials. There was also a gradual shift in the practice of lettering books; titles began to be placed on the back of the book rather than on the fore-edges or sides.

## Book Pricing and Economics
Book prices generally rose during this period due to several factors, including the increase of money, the actions of monopolists, and increased payments made to authors. This trend is evident in the cost of church Bibles, which sold for 10s. in 1541, then rose to 25s., 30s., and finally to 40s. by 1641.

Other notable prices from the era include:
*   **The Shakespeare folio (1623):** Published at £1.
*   **Bishop Walton's polyglot Bible:** Sold to subscribers for £10 in six large volumes, though this resulted in a heavy loss.
*   **Dryden's Virgil:** Published by subscription at £5 5s. This was a handsome book with plates; its high price was primarily due to a desire to befriend or honour the author.
*   **Paradise Lost:** 3s.
*   **The Pilgrim's Progress:** 1s. 6d.
*   **Izaak Walton's Compleat Angler:** 1s. 6d. in sheepskin.

Regarding plays, single plays in quarto cost 6d. each during Shakespeare's time, but this price rose to 1s. after the Restoration.

## Book Formats and Structure
The size of a book is determined by its gatherings or quires. While it is often erroneously believed that these consist of single sheets, the reality varied by format. In books printed on handmade paper, octavos generally consisted of gatherings of eights. 

Folios, however, were arranged differently to reduce the amount of sewing required. The earliest folios were usually arranged in tens (gatherings of five sheets or ten leaves), while English folios in Shakespeare's time were mostly in sixes. Quartos were often made up in eights, though the use of a half-sheet could produce a gathering of only two leaves.

## The Context of Early Modern Drama
The romantic drama produced up to 1642 is characterized by a lack of constructive mastery and moral keeping, though it is noted for its variety of accent, unexpected beauty, and poetry. The lack of artistic conscience and steadfast power in these works was partly due to the conditions of the stage. Playwrights typically sold their rights and wrote rapidly for bread. 

During this era, there was no authors' copyright, and dramas were intended to be seen and heard rather than read. Performances of individual plays were few, and articulate dramatic criticism was largely absent, with the exception of the theory and practice of Ben Jonson and casual instances found in Shakespeare.
