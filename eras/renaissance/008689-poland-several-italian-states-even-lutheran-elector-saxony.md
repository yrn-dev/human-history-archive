# Political and Religious Relations: Saxony, Poland, and the Italian States

## The Elector of Saxony and the Bohemian Conflict
In the early 17th century, the Bohemian crown became vacant following the death of Matthias. On July 31, the Bohemian estates formally deposed Ferdinand, and on August 26, they elected Frederick, the elector palatine, as their king. Frederick and his queen, Elizabeth of England, were crowned at St Vitus's cathedral in Prague. This transition led to continued warfare with Austria in 1619, during which Thurn occupied Moravia and advanced on Vienna before being forced to retreat.

Events shifted in 1620 when Duke Maximilian of Bavaria joined forces with Ferdinand, who had succeeded Matthias as emperor. Ferdinand sought to reconquer Bohemia and received military aid from Spain, Poland, and several Italian states. Notably, the Lutheran elector of Saxony also espoused Ferdinand's cause. Under the command of the duke of Bavaria, Tilly, and Bouquoi, a large imperialist army entered Bohemia in September 1620. Following a series of defeats for the Bohemians, the imperial forces attacked the Bohemian army on November 8 on the plateau of "Bila Hora" (White Hill). The Bohemians were defeated within a few hours, and the imperialists occupied the port of Prague on the Vltava (Moldau) that evening. King Frederick subsequently fled Prague.

As a result of his assistance to Emperor Ferdinand, the elector of Saxony, John George I, conquered Upper and Lower Lusatia in 1620. By the treaty of Prague in 1635, these districts were definitely transferred from Bohemia to Saxony, although the emperor maintained a certain supremacy to guard the rights and privileges of Roman Catholics.

## Lutheranism and the Electorate of Saxony
The Lutheran church in Germany experienced significant internal divisions during the second half of the 16th century. The university of Jena, under Matthias Flacius, served as the headquarters for stricter Lutherans, while Leipzig and Wittenberg were centers for the Philippists, followers of Melanchthon. Efforts to resolve these differences led to the *Torgau Book* (1576) and later the *Book of Concord* (1577), drafted by Nicolas Selnecker of Leipzig, Martin Chemnitz of Brunswick, and Jacob Andreae of Tübingen. The recognition of the *Book of Concord* was primarily due to the efforts of Augustus, the elector of Saxony.

Earlier, in 1521, the elector of Saxony and other important members had left Worms before the presentation of the edict against Luther. This edict threatened the extermination of Luther's sympathizers, though Lutheran opinions continued to spread silently across Germany throughout the latter half of that year.

In more recent history, the Evangelical Lutheran Free Church of Saxony has existed as a separate entity from the State Evangelical United Church, numbering approximately 3,780 members under 15 ordained pastors.

## Saxony and Poland: Personal and Political Unions
The relationship between Saxony and Poland is evidenced by several figures who held titles in both realms. Augustus the Strong served as both the elector of Saxony and the king of Poland. His mistress, Countess Maria Aurora of Königsmark, a member of a noble Swedish family, accompanied him in his diplomatic efforts, including a failed errand to Charles XII of Sweden in 1702.

Later, during the Prussian invasion of Saxony in 1756, the fortress of Königstein served as a place of refuge for Augustus III, the King of Poland. This fortress, located in the kingdom of Saxony on the left bank of the Elbe, had been formally ceded by Bohemia to Saxony in 1459. It was further strengthened by various electors, including Frederick Augustus II, who completed the fortress in its present form.

Additionally, military ties existed between the two regions. Jan Henryk Dombrowski, a Polish general born in the palatinate of Cracow, was brought up in Saxony and served in the Saxon army for several years before being recalled to Poland by the Polish diet in 1791.
