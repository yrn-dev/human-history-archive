# Bohemia and Hungary under Habsburg and Imperial Rule

The historical trajectories of Bohemia and Hungary are characterized by fluctuating levels of autonomy, periods of independence, and complex relationships with the Austrian Habsburg dynasty and external powers.

## Early Governance and the House of Habsburg
In the 16th century, under Emperor Maximilian II (1564–1576), who served as king of both Bohemia and Hungary, a liberal policy was maintained to preserve peace. However, the empire remained a tributary to the Turk, and religious liberty was not permanently established. During this era, various Christian beliefs struggled for mastery in Moravia, Silesia, and Bohemia, while Lutheranism was prevalent in Austria and most of Styria.

The accession of Rudolph II (1576–1612), a fanatical Spanish Catholic, shifted this dynamic. Rudolph encouraged the Jesuits to pursue the counter-Reformation and struggled to assert sovereign power. During his reign, a state of semi-independence existed in Bohemia, while Hungary preferred the Turk over the emperor. This period of internal anarchy led the archdukes to form a compact in 1606, acknowledging Archduke Matthias as the head of the family. Despite this, Matthias (emperor 1612–1619) could not restore order, and by the time of his death, Bohemia was practically independent.

## Conflict and the Thirty Years' War
Ferdinand II (1619–1637), a strong-willed ruler, sought to return Germany to the Catholic faith. Having already suppressed Protestantism in Styria, he was elected king of Bohemia in 1618 and aimed to establish Jesuit rule there. This attempt triggered the outbreak of the Thirty Years' War.

## Constitutional Struggles in the 19th Century
The mid-19th century saw a surge of nationalist and constitutional movements following the fall of Metternich. On March 31, the Hungarian government was forced to accept a new constitution that virtually established Hungary as an independent state. A separate constitution was promised to Bohemia on April 8. The Austrian government's inability to manage these crises was partly due to the necessity of keeping forces in Italy to combat a rising against Habsburg rule.

Following the Italian campaign of 1859, the Austrian empire attempted to move away from absolutism toward a constitutional system. A decree on October 20, 1860, established diets with limited powers, modeled after the ancient diets of Bohemia. While initially welcomed, this hope faded with a February 1861 decree that created a central parliament in Vienna with extensive powers and an electoral system biased toward Germans. The Bohemians eventually left this parliament in 1863, claiming it encroached upon the constitutional power of the diet of Prague.

## The Path to Independence
The constitutional position of Bohemia changed significantly after the Austro-Prussian war of 1866. The resulting treaty signed at Prague on August 23 excluded all Habsburg-ruled lands from Germany, leading to a decline in German influence. While Hungary obtained complete independence, the new constitution of 1867 maintained centralization for the Slavic and German parts of the empire. Bohemians energetically opposed this arrangement.

In 1871, Prime Minister Count Karl Hohenwart attempted to reconcile Bohemia with the empire by recognizing the rights of the kingdom of Bohemia. However, this failed when a government with strong German tendencies took office in 1872. Later, under Prime Minister Count Eduard Taaffe (1879), Bohemian representatives returned to the parliament in Vienna, though they maintained the view that Bohemia, Moravia, and Silesia constituted a separate state under the same sovereign as Austria and Hungary. Taaffe's government subsequently reduced electoral anomalies favoring the German minority and permitted the foundation of a Bohemian university at Prague.

## Final Separation
The ultimate dissolution of these ties occurred following the First World War. Under the terms of the peace treaties, Austria was required to recognize the complete independence of Hungary. Additionally, Austria had to recognize the independence of Jugoslavia and Czecho-Slovakia. The boundaries of Bohemia and Moravia were established to form the border between the new Republic of Austria and Czecho-Slovakia, subject to minor rectifications.
