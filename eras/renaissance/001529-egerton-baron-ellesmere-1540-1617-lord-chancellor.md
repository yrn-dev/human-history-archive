# Egerton, Baron Ellesmere

Sir Thomas Egerton (c. 1540–1617), known as Baron Ellesmere, was a prominent English legal and political figure who served as a high-ranking official under two monarchs. He held the positions of lord keeper for Queen Elizabeth and later served as the lord chancellor for James I.

## Titles and Honors
Throughout his career, Egerton was granted several noble titles. In 1603, he was created baron of Ellesmere. Later, in 1616, he was created Viscount Brackley.

## Judicial Views and Legal Contributions
During the reign of James I, Lord Chancellor Egerton, acting alongside the Chief Baron and two Chief Justices, held a specific legal position regarding juries. He maintained that if a party indicted was found guilty during a trial, the jury should not be questioned. However, he held that if a jury acquitted a traitor or a felon despite manifest proof, they could be charged in the Star-chamber for their partiality in finding a manifest offender not guilty.

Additionally, a work titled *Observations concerning the Office of Lord Chancellor*, published in 1651, is attributed to Lord Chancellor Ellesmere.

## Later Years and Succession
In the early part of 1616, Baron Ellesmere became dangerously ill. During this period, Francis Bacon wrote a detailed letter to the king proposing himself for the office of lord chancellor should the position become vacant. While Bacon appeared to receive a promise of the reversion of the office, the matter was delayed because Ellesmere recovered from his illness.

Sir Thomas Egerton died in 1617. He was the father of John Egerton, the 1st earl of Bridgewater (who died in 1649 and was created earl in 1617). John Egerton's name is associated with the production of Milton's *Comus*. The lineage continued through the 1st earl's great-grandson, Scroop, the 1st duke of Bridgewater (1681–1745), and further to Francis Egerton, the 3rd duke of Bridgewater (1736–1803), who was the originator of British inland navigation.
