# Roger Ascham

## Appointment and Early Career
Roger Ascham served as the Latin secretary to Queen Mary, a position he secured through the influence of Bishop Gardiner. In this role, he was granted a pension of L20 per year. During this period, Ascham had to quietly suppress his Protestantism, although he noted in a communication to Sturm that some individuals attempted to obstruct the "flow of Gardiner's benevolence" due to his religious beliefs. It is suggested that his safety was likely aided by the fact that he had never been in orders.

Prior to this appointment, Ascham had been part of an embassy that visited Venice, Innsbruck, and Louvain. While in Louvain, he observed that the university there was very inferior to Cambridge. During the embassy, Ascham spent four or five days a week reading Greek with the ambassador. Following the embassy's recall upon the accession of Mary, his letters were published in English in 1553 under the title "Report" on Germany.

## Personal Life and Finances
On June 1, 1554, Ascham married Margaret Howe, whom he identified as the niece of Sir R. Wallop (noting that she was certainly not the niece of Henry Wallop, as some have claimed). The couple had two sons. Despite his position, Ascham frequently complained of poverty both during and after this time, suggesting he lived beyond his means. However, like many courtiers of the era, he acquired various lucrative leases of crown and ecclesiastical property.

## Later Career and Educational Work
Following the accession of Elizabeth, Ascham continued his academic pursuits. In 1555, he had resumed studies with Princess Elizabeth, reading the orations of Aeschines and Demosthenes' *De Corona* in Greek. On October 5, 1559, shortly after Elizabeth became queen, Ascham was granted the prebend and canonry of Wetwang in York minster, despite being a layman.

In 1563, Ascham began writing *The Scholemaster*, the work for which he became famous. The impetus for the book occurred during the "great plague" in London in 1563, while the court was situated at Windsor. On December 10 of that year, Ascham dined with other ministers and Sir William Cecil, the secretary of state. Cecil mentioned "strange news" regarding several scholars from Eaton who had run away from school to avoid beating, expressing a desire for schoolmasters to use more discretion in correction than was common. 

This led to a debate among the ministers, with the group divided between those who supported flogging and those who opposed it; Ascham acted as the champion for the anti-floggers. This position was further reinforced when the treasurer, Sir Richard Sackville, informed Ascham that a "fond schoolmaster" had caused him to hate learning through brutality, a loss Sackville lamented now that he had a young son.
