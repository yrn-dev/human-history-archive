# Gascons and Swiss in European Military History

The Gascons and Swiss have historically functioned as significant military forces in European conflicts, often serving as mercenary troops or specialized infantry in campaigns across Italy and France.

## The Swiss Mercenaries
During the period of Italian warfare and politics, the Swiss cantons were recognized as providing the most numerous and best mercenary soldiers in Europe. Their military prowess was noted by figures such as Machiavelli, who was impressed by Swiss victories over the Milanese.

In 1515, the Swiss were involved in the Battle of Marignan, fought on September 13 and 14 on the northern outskirts of the village of Melegnano, near the river Lambro. The Swiss had been secured as supporters by Maximilian Sforza, the duke of Milan, along with the support of the pope, the king of Spain, and the emperor. While the French king, Francis I, attempted to pay the Swiss a large sum to retire to their own country, a fresh corps of mercenaries entered Italy to seek booty and demonstrate their skill against the French gendarmerie—whom they mocked as "hares in armour"—and the Lower Rhine "lansquenets" (Landsknechts).

During the Battle of Marignan, the Swiss advanced in three huge columns. Despite facing French artillery, they reached intersected ground by a stream, which protected them from the French gendarmerie, and attacked the landsknechts. The fighting continued by moonlight until midnight. On the second day, the Swiss attempted to work around the French flanks; one corps targeted the French baggage but was driven back by landsknechts and gendarmerie. Another Swiss attack closed with French infantry bands and "aventuriers" commanded by the engineer Pedro Navarro. Ultimately, a detachment from the Swiss centre was "hunted out" by the French camp.

## The Gascons and French Military Operations
Gascon troops frequently operated alongside French forces in various territorial enterprises. In the late 15th century, Cesare Borgia utilized a military force to subdue turbulent local despots in Romagna to carve out a principality. This army included 4,000 Gascons and Swiss, as well as 300 French lances and Italian troops. With this force, Cesare attacked Imola, which surrendered immediately, and subsequently besieged Forli. The city of Forli, held by Caterina Sforza, eventually surrendered on January 22, 1500.

Gascon infantry also played a role in the Battle of Marignan. Positioned on the extreme right of the French army, the Gascon and French infantry were largely armed with arquebuses.

In other historical contexts, the Gascons are credited with specific military successes. One account notes that a victory often claimed by the Spaniards was actually the result of a sudden onset by the Gascons, who were assisted by the Beaure mountaineers and possibly a few Navarrese.

## Other References
Beyond military history, the term "Gascon" appears in the history of bookbinding. Le Gascon was a master binder who invented "dotted work," a style created by scoring gilding tools to produce dotted lines instead of straight lines. This style was later imitated by Dutch binders Poncyn and Magnus.

The Swiss are also mentioned in the context of national folk costumes. Works by F. Hottenroth and E. H. T. Pingret have documented Swiss local and national costumes. Additionally, in modern demographic records for Los Angeles, Swiss individuals are listed as part of the foreign-born population.
