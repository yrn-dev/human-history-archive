# The Anabaptists

The Anabaptists were a radical anti-ecclesiastical movement that emerged during the spiritual upheavals of the 15th and 16th centuries. This period was characterized by a general revolt against authority, manifesting in the anti-papal reformation of Luther, anti-feudal peasant uprisings, and various movements within reformation areas.

## Core Teachings and Beliefs
Due to intense persecution, the Anabaptists were unable to develop a homogeneous community or adopt a common public confession of faith. However, a few central doctrines were generally held across the movement:
*   **Baptism:** The rejection of infant baptism in favor of the baptism of believers only.
*   **Justification:** The rejection of the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith, which they viewed as one-sided.
*   **The Lord's Supper:** The practice of the breaking of bread, viewed as a way for brothers and sisters in Christ to remember the death of Christ and renew the bond of brotherly love as the foundation for neighborly life.

While some early figures, such as the Zwickau prophets (Marcus Stuebner, Nikolaus Storch, and Thomas Muenzer), emphasized the "inner word"—the belief that divine revelation is received solely and directly from the Spirit of God rather than through the church, preaching, or the Bible—they are not considered typical of Anabaptism. For instance, although Muenzer repudiated infant baptism in theory, he continued the practice and did not insist on the re-baptism of believers.

## The Münster Rebellion
The movement is frequently remembered for the events in Münster, Westphalia, between 1532 and 1535. Following the spread of Anabaptist teachings to Holland by Melchior Hofmann, a "preacher of vengeance" named Jan Matthisson (or Matthyszoon) prophesied the establishment of the kingdom of heaven and the end of the world. Matthisson sent Boekebinder and de Kniper to Münster, where they attempted to realize his ideals. By 1533, all who did not embrace Anabaptism were driven from the town, and Bernt Knipperdolling became burgomaster.

After Matthisson was killed in early 1534, leadership passed to Johann Bockelson (known as John of Leiden). Under his rule, the town became a scene of "grossest licence and cruelty," with some accounts citing the occurrence of cannibalism. John of Leiden proclaimed himself king of the "new Zion" and the successor to King David, practicing polygamy and establishing a theocracy. This government included twelve elders, a structure suggested by the tribes of Israel. 

The city was besieged by the prince-bishop, Franz von Waldeck, and fell on June 25, 1535. John of Leiden and his companions were tortured and executed on January 22, 1536; their bodies were hung in cages from the tower of the Lamberti church.

## Anabaptism in England
Anabaptist views entered England through foreign importations, primarily from the Netherlands. London registers from 1535 mention Dutch Anabaptists, ten of whom were put to death. In 1536, King Henry VIII issued a proclamation directing the clergy to teach that Anabaptist opinions were "detestable heresies." In 1538, four Dutch Anabaptists (three men and one woman) were forced to bear faggots at Paul's Cross, and two others were burnt in Smithfield.

By the end of the 16th century, communities existed in London, Kent, and East Anglia. Notable figures included Robert Cooke and Joan Boucher of Kent, the latter of whom was burnt in 1550 for heretical views regarding the Virgin Mary. During the reign of Edward VI, Bishop John Hooper noted that Anabaptists flocked to his public lectures, causing him "much trouble." These groups often segregated themselves from other men and existed in communities separate from the established Church.

## Relationship to Modern Baptists
While the Anabaptists raised the question of baptism in England, the rise of the modern Baptist churches was wholly independent of them. By 1644, the Particular Baptist churches of London published a Confession of Faith, noting that they were "commonly (though falsely) called Anabaptist." 

The General Baptists were influenced by Mennonite Anabaptists and were Arminian, whereas the Particular Baptists were Calvinist and emerged from the Independents. Both groups rejected infant baptism, though they were initially unpledged to the practice of immersion.

## Legacy and Figures
The archive notes that the Anabaptists are often unfairly remembered solely for the crimes of John of Leiden and the events at Münster. Instead, the sources suggest they should be known by the piety and learning of Hans Denck, as well as the teaching and martyrdom of Hubmaier, Grebel, and Blaurock.
