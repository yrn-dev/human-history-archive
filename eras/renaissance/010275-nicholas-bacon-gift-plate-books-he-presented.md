# Sir Nicholas Bacon's Gift-Plate

The book-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon is recognized as the oldest discovered example of a book-plate in England. It was created as a gift-plate for books that Sir Nicholas Bacon presented to the university of Cambridge.

## Historical Context of Book-Plates
A book-plate, also known as an *ex-libris*, is a printed label used to indicate the ownership of individual volumes. This practice is nearly as old as the printed book itself and serves a similar purpose to the symbolical personal devices or hand-painted armorials found in medieval manuscripts. 

The earliest known examples of these labels are German. Around the year 1480, Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach used hand-painted woodcuts depicting a shield of arms supported by an angel for books presented to the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim. Other early European examples include a French plate from 1529 belonging to Jean Bertaud de la Tour-Blanche, a Dutch plate from 1597 belonging to Anna van der Aa, and an Italian plate attributed to 1622. In America, the earliest known example is a plain printed label from 1679 belonging to John Williams.

## The Bacon Plate and English Styles
Within the history of English book-plates, the gift-plate of Sir Nicholas Bacon is the earliest known instance. While a gorgeous, hand-painted armorial device associated with a folio once owned by Henry VIII exists in the King's library at the British Museum, it is not classified as a book-plate in the modern sense. Following the Bacon plate, the next oldest English example is that of Sir Thomas Tresham, dated 1585.

The Bacon plate is used to exemplify the "early armorial" style, a classification established in 1880 by Lord de Tabley in the *Guide to the Study of Book-Plates*. This style refers to plates created previous to the Restoration. English plates from this early period are characterized by a remarkable simplicity compared to the elaborateness of German examples. They are generally plainly armorial, with decoration typically limited to a symmetrical arrangement of mantling and the occasional use of wreaths or palms.

## Sir Nicholas Bacon
Sir Nicholas Bacon served as the lord keeper and provided patronage to figures such as Sir Thomas Bromley, who would later be appointed lord chancellor upon Bacon's death in 1579. Sir Nicholas was also the father of Francis Bacon (Baron Verulam, Viscount St Albans), the English essayist, statesman, and philosopher born in 1560/1.

## Evolution of the Book-Plate Study
The academic study and collection of book-plates as private tokens of ownership is a relatively modern development, dating back no further than 1875. The field gained significant momentum with the publication of Lord de Tabley's guide in 1880, which categorized various styles including:
*   **Early Armorial:** Exemplified by the Nicholas Bacon plate.
*   **Jacobean:** Including the heavy decorative manner of the early Georgian, Queen Anne, and Restoration periods.
*   **Chippendale:** A style also described as rococo.
*   **Wreath and Ribbon:** Associated with the period of the urn.

Following these developments, collector societies were established in England, Germany, France, and the United States. These societies produced various publications, such as the *Ex-libris Zeitschrift* in Berlin, the *Archives de la societe francaise de collectionneurs d'ex-libris* in Paris, and *The Journal of the Ex-libris Society* in London.
