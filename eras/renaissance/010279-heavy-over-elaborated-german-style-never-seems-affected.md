# The German Style in Book-Plates and Decorative Art

## Origins and Early Development
The history of the book-plate, whether viewed as an accessory to book binding or as a minor work of decorative and symbolical art, begins in Germany. The earliest known examples of these plates are German, and they appeared in great numbers in Germany long before the fashion spread to other nations. These early works are often of high artistic interest.

Between 1503 and 1516, Albrecht Durer engraved at least six plates, some of which were of very important size; he also provided designs for numerous other plates. Other notable plates are attributed to Hans Holbein, Lucas Cranach, and a group known as the Little Masters, which included Jost Amman, Matthias Zundt, the Behams, Virgil Solis, Georg Hupschmann, and Saldorfer.

## Characteristics and Influence
The draughtsmen of this period exerted a lasting influence on German decorative styles that persisted through subsequent centuries into the present day. This influence remained despite the effort for originality in composition by modern designers and the invasion of French and Italian fashions during the 18th and 17th centuries.

A defining feature of this tradition is the "heavy, over-elaborated German style." While Germany was the source from which the fashion of using ornamental book-plates as marks of possession spread, this specific heavy and over-elaborated style never seems to have affected neighboring countries.

## Comparison with English Book-Plates
The English book-plate reflects the prevailing tastes of decorative art from the late Victorian to the late Elizabethan period. Early English examples are remarkably simple and show nothing of the elaborateness found in German styles. These early plates were generally plain and armorial, with decoration usually limited to wreaths or palms and a symmetrical arrangement of mantling.

Following the Restoration, book-plates became a standard accessory for well-ordered libraries. While they maintained simple heraldic arrangements, their appearance changed. They began to use tincture lines and dots based on a method devised in the middle of the century by Petra Sancta, the author of *Tesserae Gentilitiae*, a practice that was adopted across Europe. During this time, the mantling became more elaborate, recalling the periwigs of the era. This particular style was imported from France, and from that point until the dawn of the French Revolution, English book-plate decoration generally followed the ruling French taste with a delay of several years.

## Related Artistic Trends in Germany
Beyond book-plates, German artistic production in other mediums has exhibited distinct characteristics. In the 12th century, there was a widespread outburst of artistic sentiment across western Europe, including western Germany, France, and England, which brought the art of illumination into alignment. This period was characterized by large folios and magnificent initial letters featuring gold, brilliant colors, and a wealth of interlacing branches and foliage containing animal and human life. 

In the production of these boldly ornamented volumes, the work of German and Flemish artists was distinguished from the schools of France and England by a certain heaviness of outline.
