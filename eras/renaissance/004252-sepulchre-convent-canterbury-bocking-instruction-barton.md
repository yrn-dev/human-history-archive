# St Sepulchre's Convent and Elizabeth Barton

## St Sepulchre's Convent
St Sepulchre's was a Benedictine nunnery located in Canterbury. Established around the year 1100, it was one of several monastic foundations in Kent, a region noted for a high number of such institutions due to its fertility, beauty, and connection to early English history. According to archival records, no remains of the nunnery currently exist.

## Elizabeth Barton
Elizabeth Barton, known as "the maid of Kent," was born in 1506 at Aldington, Kent. In her nineteenth year, an illness led to hysteria and religious mania. At the time, she served as a servant for Thomas Cobb, who was the steward of an estate near Aldington owned by William Warham, the archbishop of Canterbury. 

During her recovery, Barton experienced trances that lasted for several days. While in these states, she spoke with a "marvellous holiness in rebuke of sin and vice," leading local people to believe she was inspired. These events were reported by Cobb to the parish priest, Richard Masters, who then informed Archbishop Warham. Barton later confessed during her trial that once she recovered and noticed the admiration of the locals, she was cunning enough to feign these trances to continue her prophecies.

## Admission to the Convent and the Role of Edward Bocking
As Barton's fame grew, Archbishop Warham took action in 1526 by instructing the prior of Christ Church, Canterbury, to send two monks to investigate the case. One of these monks, Edward Bocking, successfully obtained Barton's admission as a nun to St Sepulchre's convent in Canterbury. 

Under the instruction of Bocking, Barton's prophecies became more remarkable. She asserted that she was in direct communication with the Virgin Mary, a claim that attracted many pilgrims.

## Political Influence and Conflict with Henry VIII
Barton's utterances began to target political matters, creating a widespread and profound sensation. She declared that if Henry persisted in his intention to divorce Catherine, he would "die a villain's death" and "should no longer be king of this realm." Her influence was such that she corresponded with Sir Thomas More and Fisher, the bishop of Rochester.

In 1532, while passing through Canterbury on his return from France, Henry is said to have allowed Barton to force herself into his presence. During this encounter, she attempted to terrify the king into abandoning his marriage.

## Confession and Trial
Following the solemnization of the marriage in May 1533, Barton's utterances became increasingly treasonable. She was subsequently examined and confessed before Cranmer, who had succeeded Warham as archbishop in March. Records indicate she was examined on September 25th.
