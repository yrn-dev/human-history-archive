# The Musical Sons of Johann Sebastian Bach

Johann Sebastian Bach belonged to a family of significant importance in music history for nearly two hundred years. While the Bach clan remained in Thuringia for generations, the sons of Sebastian entered a more modern world. Among his children, four sons grew to manhood and established themselves as musicians. These sons were encouraged by their father to cultivate infant art-forms that would eventually gain global prominence, leading some to believe that the work of Johann Sebastian himself had become obsolete.

## Wilhelm Friedermann Bach
The eldest of the sons who became musicians was Wilhelm Friedermann Bach (1710–1784). By common repute, he was the most gifted of the siblings, recognized as a complete master of counterpoint and a famous improvisor and organist. He received his education at Leipzig.

His professional career included an appointment in 1733 as the organist of the Sophienkirche in Dresden, and in 1747, he became the musical director of the Liebfrauenkirche at Halle. However, Friedermann was characterized by dissolute and idle habits, which resulted in a career described as a series of wasted opportunities. He was forced to resign his office at Halle in 1764, after which he led a wandering life. He died in great poverty in Berlin on July 1, 1784.

His compositions, few of which were printed, consist of instrumental works and many church cantatas. His most notable instrumental pieces include fantasias, polonaises, and fugues for clavier, as well as a sestet for horns, clarinet, and strings. The Royal library at Berlin preserves several of his manuscripts.

## Karl Philipp Emanuel Bach
The third son, Karl Philipp Emanuel Bach (1714–1788), was born in Weimar on March 14. He entered the Thomasschule at Leipzig at age ten, after his father had become the school's cantor in 1723. He pursued studies in jurisprudence at the universities of Frankfort on the Oder (1735) and Leipzig (1731), earning his degree in 1738. Shortly after graduating, he abandoned a legal career to devote himself to music.

In 1738, he entered the service of the crown prince of Prussia, becoming a member of the royal household upon the prince's accession in 1740. He became one of Europe's leading clavier-players. His reputation was solidified by two sets of sonatas dedicated to the grand duke of Wuerttemberg (1744) and Frederick the Great (1742). In 1746, he was promoted to *Kammermusikus* and remained in the king's favor for twenty-two years.

His works include:
* **Clavier music:** Nearly two hundred sonatas and solos, including the set *mit veraenderten Reprisen* (1760–1768) and pieces *fuer Kenner und Liebhaber*.
* **Vocal and Orchestral works:** A setting of the *Magnificat* (1749), an Easter cantata (1756), several concerted works and symphonies, and three volumes of songs: *Sing-Oden* (1766), *Oden mit Melodien* (1762), and *Geistliche Oden und Lieder* (1758).

Additionally, he wrote a masterly systematic treatise, *Versuch ueber die wahre Art das Clavier zu spielen*, which reached its third edition by 1780.

## Johann Gottfried Bernhard Bach
The fourth son, Johann Gottfried Bernhard Bach (1715–1739), was born in Weimar and educated in Leipzig. Between 1735 and 1738, he served as an organist at Sangerhausen and Muehlhausen. In 1738, he left his appointment to study law at Jena. He died in 1739 at the age of 24.

## Johann Christoph Friedrich Bach
The ninth son, Johann Christoph Friedrich Bach (1732–1795), was born in Leipzig and studied at the university and the Thomasschule. In 1750, he was appointed *Kapellmeister* at Bueckeburg. He was an industrious composer of opera and church music.
