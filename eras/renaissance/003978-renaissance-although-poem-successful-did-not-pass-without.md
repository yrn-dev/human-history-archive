# Renaissance

The Renaissance was a period characterized by the eager and hasty comprehension of the traditions of ancient life. This era saw a renewed interest in classical learning and the cultivation of polite letters, which manifested in various literary forms, the adoption of antique styles, and a spirit of intellectual modernization across Europe.

## Literary Theory and Prosody
During the Renaissance, it was considered proper to categorize poetry into various classes. In 1586, William Webbe, an early English critic discussing the laws of prosody, identified four usual kinds of verse: lyric, iambic, elegiac, and heroic. However, critics of the 15th and 16th centuries frequently suffered from a confusion of terms that led to considerable error. For instance, the term "heroic" was often used to mean "epic," and there was a lack of clarity regarding the relationship between lyrical content and iambic measure.

## The Renaissance in Spain
In Spain, the Renaissance spirit was reflected in the works of poets such as Cristobal de Castillejo (1490–1556). Castillejo's writing is noted for its ingenious wit, charming simplicity, and an urbane gaiety. His works *Dialogo de la vida de la corte* and *Dialogo de las condiciones de las mujeres* are specifically described as being impregnated with the Renaissance spirit. 

Castillejo's career was also marked by a resistance to the metrical innovations imported from Italy by Garcilaso de la Vega and Boscan. He largely adhered to traditional native forms, such as *coplas de pie quebrado* or *quintillas*, and only utilized the new school of poetry when engaging in caustic parody, as seen in his lines *Contra los que dejan los metros castellanos*.

## The Renaissance in France
The French Renaissance is strongly characterized by works such as the *Heptameron*, written by Marguerite. Constructed similarly to Boccaccio's *Decameron*, the *Heptameron* consists of seventy-two short stories told by a group of ladies and gentlemen delayed by a swelling river during a journey from Cauterets. The work reflects the sensuality of the period, though in a less coarse manner than the works of Rabelais, and possesses a poetical spirit generally absent from *Pantagruel*. 

Marguerite's other contributions include the *Letters* and *Les Marguerites de la marguerite des princesses*, a miscellaneous collection containing farces, mysteries, and spiritual songs. Her later works, including the allegorical poem *Les Prisons* and the piece *Le Navire*, further illustrate the literary output of the era. Additionally, the works of Clément Marot were influential during this time, with important editions published at Lyons in 1538 and 1544.

## The Renaissance in the Netherlands
In Amsterdam, the "modern spirit" of the Renaissance was represented by figures such as Hendrick Laurenssen Spieghel (1549–1612) and Roemer Pieterssen Visscher (1547–1620). Spieghel, a humanist, wrote a didactic epic titled *Hertspieghel* (1614) and a philological exhortation, *Tweespraeck van de nederduytsche letterkunst*, which urged the Dutch people to enrich and purify their language using the "fountains of antiquity."

Roemer Visscher is described as standing at the threshold of new Renaissance literature. While he practiced the "faded arts of the rhetoricians," he was deeply influenced by a spirit of classical learning that was closer to the true antique than previous Dutch efforts. Known to his disciples as the "Dutch Martial," Visscher acted as an amateur poet who encouraged the genius of professional writers and pointed toward the naturalism of the great period.
