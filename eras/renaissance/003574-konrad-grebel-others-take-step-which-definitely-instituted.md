# The Institution of Anabaptism

## Origins in Zurich
The Anabaptist movement proper began in Zurich, where a group including Konrad Grebel (d. 1526), Felix Manz (d. 1527), Simon Strumpf, and Wilheld Reubli (1480-1554) separated from Zwingli. This group proposed the formation of a separate church based on specific religious and social principles. They advocated for a scriptural communism of goods and repudiated the use of force. Furthermore, they asserted that Christians should be independent of worldly tribunals, exercising patience and love toward one another.

## The Rejection of Infant Baptism
The most radical doctrine adopted by this group was the assertion that infant baptism (paedobaptism) was unscriptural. This stance attracted new adherents, such as Hans Broedli, the pastor of Zollikon. However, the refusal to baptize infants and the subsequent move to form a separate church led to condemnation from Zwingli. As a result, several members of the group were banished.

## The Definitive Step of Anabaptism
The act of banishment drove Konrad Grebel, Joerg Blaurock, and others to take the step that definitely instituted "Anabaptism." They performed baptisms upon one another and subsequently partook of the Lord's Supper together. This action went beyond the mere repudiation of infant baptism; it established a new religious community. This community sought to model itself after primitive Christianity, rejecting all traditions and accretions that appeared later than the records of the New Testament, aiming instead to return to a simple church founded on brotherly love.

## Growth and Practice
Following these events, the movement grew with astonishing rapidity. Scholarly figures joined the community, including Hans Denck (ca. 1495-1527) and Balthasar Hubmaier (ca. 1480-1528). In 1525, Hubmaier baptized 300 converts. 

The mode of baptism used by the Anabaptists was not immersion, but rather pouring or sprinkling. For example, Hubmaier used a single bucket to baptize his 300 converts, while Blaurock and Grebel baptized each other and their adherents while kneeling together in an ordinary room.

## Core Beliefs
The Anabaptists maintained two central articles of faith. First, they believed in religious feeling, conscience, or "inner light" as the sole true ground or beginning of religion—a belief that linked them to the Zwickau prophets. Second, they held with equal sincerity that true Christians are like sheep among wolves and must not defend themselves.
