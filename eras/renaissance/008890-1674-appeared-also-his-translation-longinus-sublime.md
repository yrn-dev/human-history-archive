# On the Sublime

*On the Sublime* (Greek: *Peri Hupsous*) is an ancient treatise concerning the sources of elevation in style. The work is ambiguously attributed to Cassius Longinus, dated approximately A.D. 260, though it is considered more likely to have been written by an author in the first century of the common era.

## Literary Analysis and Concepts
The treatise focuses on the nature of "greatness" in literature. In chapter xxxiii, the author examines whether greatness, despite having some attendant faults, is preferable to a flawless merit that exists on a lower level. The author concludes in the affirmative. 

As an example of this principle, the author compares poets and playwrights. He asks who would prefer Ion of Chios over Sophocles in tragedy, or Bacchylides over Pindar in lyric poetry. While the author acknowledges that Bacchylides and Ion are "faultless" and possess a style of "perfect elegance and finish," he regards Bacchylides as a finished poet of the second class—one who avoids glaring faults but fails to reach "loftier heights."

## Social and Political Observations
The text provides a diagnosis of intellectual and mental decline within the Roman world. Writing from the court of a Syrian queen, Longinus laments the degeneracy of his contemporaries, asserting that this state of affairs debased their talents, enervated their courage, and debased their sentiments. 

He employs a metaphor comparing the minds of his contemporaries to children who remain "pygmies" because their infant limbs were too closely confined. He argues that the minds of his era are similarly fettered by the habits and prejudices of a "just servitude," rendering them unable to expand or achieve the "well-proportioned greatness" seen in the ancients. He attributes the superiority of the ancients to the fact that they lived under a popular government, which allowed them to write with the same freedom with which they acted.

## Historical and Literary References
The treatise is cited in various historical and literary contexts:
* **Historical Events:** The work references the historian Timæus the Sicilian, who viewed the defeat of the Athenians as divine punishment for the desecration of the Hermæ, an act attributed primarily to Hermokratês, the son of Hermon.
* **Literary Comparisons:** The text is used to characterize Herodotus as being the most "Homer-like" (*Ὁμηρικώτατος*) or a "zealot of Homer" (*Ὁμήρου ζηλωτὴς*).

## Critical Reception and Style
Historians such as Gibbon have noted that Longinus's own writing style reflects the caution he exercised in his observations. Rather than presenting his sentiments with "manly boldness," he often insinuates them with "guarded caution," sometimes placing his views in the mouth of a friend or appearing to refute them himself.
