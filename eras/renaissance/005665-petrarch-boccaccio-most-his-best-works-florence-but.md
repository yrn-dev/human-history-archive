# Petrarch and Boccaccio

Petrarch and Boccaccio were two prominent figures of a distinct historical age, characterized by a unity of spirit and intellectual production. Together, they are credited with encouraging and planting the rudiments of Greek learning in Italy, although these initial efforts withered and expired before a new flame of study was rekindled at the end of the fourteenth century.

## Boccaccio and the Study of Greek
Boccaccio is recognized as the father of Tuscan prose and is widely known for the *Decameron*, a collection of one hundred novels centered on love and pleasantry. Beyond his popular writing, Boccaccio is noted for his role in restoring the study of the Greek language in Italy. 

In 1360, Boccaccio provided hospitality and lodging to Leo (also known as Leontius Pilatus), a disciple of Barlaam who was traveling to Avignon. Boccaccio persuaded the republic of Florence to grant Leo an annual stipend, allowing him to become the first professor of Greek in the Western countries of Europe. Despite Leo's rustic deportment, gloomy temper, and hideous countenance, he possessed a vast treasure of Greek learning in grammar, philosophy, history, and fable. 

Under Leo's instruction, Boccaccio transcribed a literal prose version of the *Odyssey* and the *Iliad*. This work satisfied the intellectual thirst of Petrarch and was potentially used clandestinely in the following century by the Latin interpreter Laurentius Valla. Additionally, Boccaccio utilized Leo's narratives to create a treatise on the genealogy of the heathen gods, a work of stupendous erudition that featured Greek passages and characters.

## Petrarch's Literary Contributions and Influence
Petrarch is regarded as a lyrical poet of a stature comparable to that of Dante. His influence extended into later English literature; for example, Chaucer followed Petrarch's version of certain narratives in the *Canterbury Tales*. 

Petrarch's life and works have been the subject of later biographical study, notably by the abbé de Sade. One such study describes Vaucluse as a retreat for a hermit, contradicting modern misconceptions that it was a grotto for Laura and a happy lover. Petrarch's Latin works were printed in the sixteenth century at Basil in a volume of 1,250 pages, though the abbé de Sade later called for a new edition of these works.

## The Story of Griselda
Both Boccaccio and Petrarch handled the story of Griselda, a heroine of romance said to have been the wife of Walter, marquis of Saluces or Saluzzo, in the 11th century. While the narrative was treated as history by both men, it is probable that Boccaccio borrowed the story from a Provencal *fabliau*. Boccaccio included the account in the recitations of the tenth day of the *Decameron*, writing it around 1350. 

Petrarch related the story in a Latin letter in 1373. This translation became the basis for much subsequent literature and was printed by Ulrich Zel around 1470. The story was later translated into French as *La Patience de Griselidis* (printed in 1484) and dramatized in 1395. Other dramatizations occurred in 1603 by Henry Chettle, W. Haughton, and Thomas Dekker, and it is said that Ralph Radcliffe wrote a play on the subject during the reign of Henry VIII.

## Artistic Representation
The Florentine painter Angelo Allori (1502–1572), known as Il Bronzino and a pupil of J. da Pontormo, painted portraits of the most famous men of his era, including Boccaccio, Petrarch, and Dante. While most of Bronzino's best works remain in Florence, some examples are located in the National Gallery in London.
