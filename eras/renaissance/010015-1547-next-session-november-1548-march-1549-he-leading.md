# Events and Figures of the Late 1540s

The late 1540s were marked by significant political, religious, and military upheavals across Europe, particularly in England, Ireland, and Scotland, as well as continued Portuguese expansion in the Indies.

## Religious Conflict in England
The accession of Edward VI initiated a period of tension regarding royal supremacy and ecclesiastical authority. Bishop Bonner, who had previously been an active agent in establishing royal supremacy, began to doubt its application when managed by a Protestant council. Bonner, along with Gardiner, proposed a theory that royal supremacy was in abeyance during a royal minority. This position was neither constitutionally nor legally tenable, as both men had sought licenses from the young king to exercise ecclesiastical jurisdiction.

Bonner's resistance to the government led to his commitment to the Fleet following his opposition to a visitation in August 1547. Although released in time to participate in the parliament of November 1547, he remained a prominent figure of opposition. During the session from November 1548 to March 1549, Bonner was a leading opponent of the Book of Common Prayer and the first Act of Uniformity. 

Following the enactment of these laws, Bonner neglected to enforce them. On September 1, 1549, the council required him to maintain at St Paul's Cross that royal authority remained absolute regardless of the king's age. Bonner's failure to comply resulted in a seven-day trial and his subsequent deprivation of his bishopric by an ecclesiastical court presided over by Cranmer. He was sent to the Marshalsea and, after the Protestant faction prevailed over his appeals, his deprivation was confirmed by the council in the Star Chamber on February 7, 1550, with a sentence of perpetual imprisonment.

During this same period, Edmund Grindal (c. 1519–1583) rose within the Protestant establishment. A graduate of Cambridge, Grindal served as a proctor and Lady Margaret preacher from 1548 to 1549. He was selected as a Protestant disputant during the 1549 visitation, likely due to the influence of Ridley. Grindal later became a chaplain to Edward VI and a prebendary of Westminster.

## Civil Unrest and Military Action in Britain
The year 1549 saw significant internal instability in England and Ireland. In Norfolk, Robert Ket, a tanner and holder of the manor of Wymondham, led a rebellion driven by the general policy of protector Somerset and hardships caused by the enclosure of common lands. After a riot in July 1549, Ket established a camp on Mousehold Heath, where he commanded 16,000 men and implemented a system of justice and discipline. Despite a royal offer of amnesty, Ket took possession of Norwich on August 1, 1549. The rebellion was crushed on August 27 at Dussindale by John Dudley, earl of Warwick, who utilized trained soldiery and foreign mercenaries. Robert and William Ket were taken to London and condemned for treason; Robert was executed at Norwich on December 7, 1549.

Simultaneously, the English crown dealt with unrest in other regions. William, 13th Lord Grey de Wilton, who had been a leader at the battle of Pinkie in 1547, provided service in 1549 by suppressing rebellions in the west of England and Oxfordshire.

In Ireland, Sir Edward Bellingham was appointed lord deputy in May 1548. He encountered a disturbed country but successfully freed the Pale from rebels, built forts, and crushed a rebellion of the O'Connors in Leinster. Though described as a headstrong man who quarreled with his council, he was regarded by an opponent as the best man of war ever seen in Ireland. His tenure ended with his recall in 1549.

In Scotland, Sir James Balfour was released from the French galleys in 1549, where he had been condemned in June 1547 along with Knox and others. Following his release, he abjured the reformers and entered the service of Mary of Guise.

## International Figures and Cartography
In the Portuguese Indies, Joao de Castro (1500–1548) served as the fourth viceroy. A humanist and mathematician, Castro achieved significant military success in his final years, including the relief of Diu and the overthrow of Mahmud, king of Gujarat. His popularity was such that he could secure large loans from Goa merchants on the simple security of his moustache.

In Spain, Luis de Avila y Zuniga (c. 1490–c. 1560) wrote a history of the war of the league of Schmalkalden (1546–1547) titled *Commentarios de la guerra de Alemana, hecha de Carlos V en el ano de 1546 y 1547*. First printed in 1548, the work was translated into several languages, though it was criticized for being partial to Emperor Charles V.

The late 1540s also saw contributions to European cartography. Sigismund von Herberstein produced a map of Muscovy in 1549, and Johann Stumpf produced a map of Switzerland in 1548.
