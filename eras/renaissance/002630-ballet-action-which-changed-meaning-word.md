# Ballet d'Action

The *ballet d'action*, also referred to as pantomime ballet, is a specific form of theatrical representation defined by the telling of a story exclusively through gesture and music. In this form, the music is characterized by a stronger emphasis than would be used with the human voice. While dancing is connected to the story, it is more commonly incidental. This specific definition was created because the general French word *ballet* was considered too comprehensive; consequently, a single scene, such as a village festival with its dances, is distinguished from the *ballet d'action* and termed a *divertissement*.

## Etymology and Evolution of the Term
The word *ballet* is derived from the Greek *ballizein* (to dance), passing through the Medieval Latin *ballare* (with *ballator* as a synonym for *saltator*), the Italian *ballare* and *ballata*, and the Old French *ballet* before entering Old English as *ballette* and eventually *ballad*. According to Rousseau, in Old French, *ballet* signified "to dance, to sing, to rejoice," thereby incorporating what have become three distinct modern words: "ballet, ball and ballad." 

Over time, the meaning of "ball" and "ballet" became limited to dancing, while "ballad" became confined to singing. The changed meaning of the word *ballet* is ascribed to the emergence of the *ballet d'action*, which facilitated the introduction of modern ballet.

## Origins and Early Development
The introduction of the *ballet d'action* has generally been attributed to the 15th century. During this era, the splendid courts of Italy sought novel entertainments to celebrate triumphs of arms or the marriages of princes, often employing the utmost skill of machinists. It is supposed that the art of the old Roman *pantomimi* was revived to enhance court-dances. Under the Roman empire, *pantomimi* used mute gestures to represent scenes from Greek tragedies or mythological stories, while a chorus in the background sang *cantica* to describe the action or narrate the fable.

There is a general opinion that this mute pantomimic action—the essence of modern ballet—was carried through court entertainments involving kings, queens, princes, princesses, and courtiers. However, some suggest it may have derived from professional stage dances. Early court entertainments in France and Italy were often masques or masks that included song and declamation.

The earliest modern ballet on record took place in 1489 at Tortona, given by Bergonzio di Botta to celebrate the marriage of the duke of Milan (also identified as Duke Galeazzo).

## Development in France and the Court
Ballet was developed and perfected in France, where it became closely associated with the history of the opera. Catherine de' Medici introduced these entertainments to France, spending large sums to distract her son from state affairs. In 1581, Baltasarini (also known as Beaujoyeulx) composed the "Ballet Comique de la Reyne," a landmark performance where music and dance were arranged for the first time to display coherent dramatic ideas.

The art was embraced by Henry IV, Louis XIII, and Louis XIV, all of whom performed various characters. Richelieu utilized the ballet as an instrument for political expression, such as in the 1641 performance "Prosperity of the Arms of France." Lully further advanced the art by making a science of ballet music composition and insisting on the admission of women as dancers; previously, feminine roles were played by men dressed as women. The first stage of ballet's development ended when Louis XIV became too fat to dance and the court's interest waned.

## Jean Georges Noverre and the Modern Era
Jean Georges Noverre is regarded as the father of ballet dancing as it is known today and the true inventor of the *ballet d'action*. Noverre's central theory was that mere display was insufficient to ensure the life and interest of a ballet. He perfected the art of using pantomime to present a story and insisted that dancing must be the direct expression of a particular attitude of mind.

Other significant figures in the development of the art include Gaetano Apolline Balthazare Vestris, the finest male dancer Europe produced, who collaborated with Gluck on *Iphigenie en Aulide*. In 1750, Vestris performed alongside Madame Camargo in the ballet-opera *Leandre et Hero*; Camargo is noted for introducing short skirts to the ballet.

## Global Spread and Later Trends
In England, ballet arrived later than the opera, not being introduced until the 18th century. During the regency of Lord Middlesex, a *corps* of dancers was formed and a ballet-master was appointed. The first descriptive ballet seen in London was *The Tavern Bilkers* in 1702. The art reached its peak in England during the first half of the 19th century. Notable dancers of this period included Carlotta Grisi, Mdlle Taglioni, Fanny Elssler, Mdlle Cerito, Miss P. Horton, Mdlle Lucile Grahn, and Mdlle Carolina Rosati.

In later years, Kate Vaughan introduced popular "skirt-dancing," though some students of the art view this as a departure from true dancing because the steps became less important than the exhibition of whirling draperies. Her disciples included Miss Letty Lind and Miss Sylvia Grey, while Adeline Genee remained the finest exponent of the classical school.
