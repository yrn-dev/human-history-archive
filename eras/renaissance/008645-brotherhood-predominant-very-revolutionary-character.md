# The Bohemian Brethren

The Bohemian Brethren were a community of reformers in Bohemia whose influence grew significantly through the support of several powerful nobles. The community was indirectly founded by Chelcicky, and the group exerted a substantial impact on the development of Bohemian literature.

## Literary and Linguistic Contributions
The Bohemian Brethren focused their historical and theological writing on the national language, a practice that served to refine and polish the tongue. One of the most significant achievements of the brotherhood was the *Bible of Kralice*, a translation of the Scriptures produced through the joint efforts of several divines of the community; it was first printed in 1593 at Kralice in Moravia.

Other notable figures within the community included Brother Gregory, known as the patriarch of the brotherhood, who wrote extensively on theological matters, including the book *On good and evil priests* and *Letters to Archbishop Rokycan*. Following Brother Gregory's death in 1480, the community experienced discord, which sparked further literary activity. This period produced numerous controversial works by Augusta (a bishop of the community), as well as Brothers Lucas, Blahoslav, and Jaffet. Additionally, the young priest Jan Bilek wrote an account regarding the captivity of Bishop Augusta.

The brotherhood also produced historical works, though many were destroyed in the 17th century during a period when nearly all anti-Roman books in Bohemia were eliminated. Surviving fragments include portions of Blahoslav's *History of the Unity* (the history of the brotherhood) and biographies of Peter and William of Rosenberg written by the historian Wenceslas Brezan. The most prominent writer associated with the brotherhood was John Amos Komensky, also known as Comenius (1592-1670). Among his 142 works, many were written in his native language, most notably the allegorical tale *Labyrinth of the World*.

## Political Influence and Revolutionary Character
By the mid-16th century, the Bohemian Brethren strongly sympathized with the Protestants of Germany. This alignment created significant obstacles for Ferdinand when he attempted to raise a Bohemian army to support his brother. Although Ferdinand used the pretext that Bohemia was threatened by the Turks to persuade the estates to vote for a general levy, many nobles and cities—specifically those belonging to the community of the Bohemian Brethren—refused to send men. Furthermore, many of the soldiers who did arrive were Protestants who sympathized with their German co-religionists, leading the Bohemian army to refuse to cross the Saxon frontier. By the end of 1546, Ferdinand was forced to disband these forces.

In March 1547, the estates met at Prague without a royal summons, an act described as unconstitutional. This assembly was characterized as having a very revolutionary character, driven largely by the predominant influence of the knights and nobles belonging to the Bohemian Brotherhood, as well as representatives from the town of Prague. The members of the estates sought to recover the ancient liberties of Bohemia, which they believed Ferdinand had encroached upon by curtailing old privileges of the land and claiming a hereditary right to the crown. This revolutionary sentiment was further intensified upon the arrival of news regarding the elector of Saxony's victory at Rochlitz.
