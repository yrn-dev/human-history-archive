# Succession to the Bohemian Throne under Matthias

The question of succession to the Bohemian throne during the reign of Matthias was a central point of political and religious tension, primarily driven by the king's lack of children and the competing interests of the Roman Catholic Church and the Bohemian Protestants.

## The Selection of Ferdinand
Because Matthias was childless, the issue of who would succeed him to the Bohemian throne became a pressing concern. The king sought to secure the succession for his cousin, Ferdinand, the duke of Styria. Ferdinand was characterized as a cruel persecutor of Protestants in Styria and a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome. Despite these traits, state officials in Bohemia used "not very scrupulous means" to persuade the estates to accept Ferdinand as the heir. This effort was successful, and Ferdinand was crowned at Prague on June 17, 1617.

## Influence and Religious Tension
Following Ferdinand's recognition as heir, the policies of Matthias became more pronouncedly ultramontane. The king's councillors, all of whom were adherents of the Church of Rome, openly hoped that the Catholic Church would regain its ancient hold over Bohemia. 

This shift created significant friction with the Bohemian Protestants. Led by Count Thurn—one of the few nobles who had refused to vote for Ferdinand's recognition—the Protestants viewed a conflict as inevitable. They concluded that it was more advantageous to confront the weak Matthias than to wait and encounter his more fanatical successor, Ferdinand.

## The Role of Melchior Klesl
Melchior Klesl, an Austrian statesman and ecclesiastic who served as the originator and director of Matthias's policy, attempted to manage these tensions. Klesl, a convert to Roman Catholicism, sought to strengthen the emperor's position by establishing peace between Catholics and Protestants. 

When the question of imperial succession required prompt attention, Klesl advised that the matter be shelved until an arrangement with the Protestant princes could be reached. While Klesl desired to keep the empire within the house of Habsburg and preserve the dominance of the Roman Catholic Church, his cautious approach displeased Ferdinand and Archduke Maximilian. The latter believed Klesl was hostile to Ferdinand's candidature. Despite this, Klesl's influence over Matthias remained strong until June 1618, when he was seized by order of the archdukes and imprisoned at Ambras in Tirol.

## Escalation to Conflict
The tension reached a breaking point in December 1617 due to a relatively minor incident. The abbot of Brevnov (Braunau) and the archbishop of Prague ordered the suppression of Protestant religious services in churches located on their domains. This action was a direct infringement of the agreement between the Utraquist and Romanist estates signed when King Rudolph had signed the Letter of Majesty.

In response, the defenders invited all Protestant members of the diet to meet in Prague. They assembled on May 21, 1618, and decided to proceed in full armour to the Hradcany palace to present their complaints to Matthias's councillors. On May 22, a secret meeting was held by a more advanced party—including Wenceslas of Ruppa, Ulrich of Kinsky, and Thurn—where they decided to put the most influential of Matthias's councillors to death. On May 23, the representatives of the Bohemian Protestants proceeded to the Hradcany.
