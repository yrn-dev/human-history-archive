# Basil III and the Capture of Smolensk

## Accession and Internal Consolidation
Basil III. Ivanovich (1479–1533) was the son of Sophia Palaeologa and Ivan III. He succeeded his father as the tsar of Muscovy in 1505. Described as a crafty prince possessing the tenacity of his race, Basil III. worked to incorporate the remaining independent principalities into Muscovy. Between 1517 and 1523, he annexed the domains of the princes of Syeversk and Ryazan after accusing them of conspiracy against him and seizing their persons. Earlier, on January 24, 1510, Basil III. targeted Pskov, the last free republic of old Russia. He deprived the city of its assembly-bell and charter—both of which were sent to Moscow—and installed tsarish governors to rule the territory.

## Conflict with Poland and the Loss of Smolensk
Basil III. expanded his influence by taking advantage of the difficult position of Sigismund of Poland. In 1512, Basil III. captured Smolensk, which served as a great eastern fortress for Poland. This military success was achieved primarily through the assistance of Prince Michael Glinsky, a rebel Lithuanian who provided Basil III. with engineers and artillery sourced from western Europe. 

The capture of Smolensk represented the first serious injury Muscovy inflicted upon Poland. Due to the exigencies he faced, Sigismund was eventually compelled to acquiesce in the surrender of the fortress in 1522.

## Relations with the Tatars and the Volga
Basil III.'s efforts against the Tatars were generally successful, though not without difficulty. In 1519, he was forced to buy off Mahommed Girai, the khan of the Crimea, while the khan was positioned under the very walls of Moscow. However, toward the end of his reign, Basil III. successfully established Russian influence on the Volga. This culminated in 1530 when he placed the pretender Elanyei on the throne of Kazan.

## Titles, Legacy, and Succession
Basil III. was the first grand-duke of Moscow to adopt the double-headed eagle of the East Roman empire and the title of tsar. In 1526, he married his second wife, Helena Glinska. From this marriage, he had a son named Ivan, who succeeded him as Ivan IV.
