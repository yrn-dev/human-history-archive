# Portuguese Overseas Settlements and Trade

## Early Expansion and Papal Division
During the late 15th century, the Portuguese engaged in extensive overseas enterprises, pushing southward and eastward. In 1497, Vasco da Gama sailed from Lisbon, rounding the Cape to reach Zanzibar and Calicut in India. To manage the competition for new territories, the Pope divided the new continent of America between Spain and Portugal in 1494. Under this settlement, Portugal was granted Brazil and all territories east of a line located 370 leagues west of the Cape Verde islands.

By 1515, Portuguese ships had reached the Moluccas and Java. The Portuguese established and fortified trading stations throughout the coasts of the Indian Ocean, including possessions in Goa and Mozambique. Other long-term possessions included Macao in China and a portion of Timor.

## Settlements in Asia and the Indian Ocean
The Portuguese established various strategic outposts across Asia, often utilizing "factories" for trade. In Macao, the Portuguese were permitted to erect factories on the peninsula in 1557. Jesuit missionaries settled there, and a bishopric was constituted by Gregory XIII in 1580. A senate was organized in 1583, and Jeronimo de Silveira served as the first royal governor in 1628. While the Portuguese remained largely under Chinese control for centuries—with mandarins insisting on authority over European criminals—Governor Ferreira do Amaral ended this arrangement in 1849, though he was assassinated on August 22 of that year.

In the Gulf of Cambay, the Portuguese established a presence in Damaun. The town was sacked and burnt by the Portuguese in 1531, but after being retaken in 1558, they created a permanent settlement and converted a mosque into a Christian church. The territory of Damaun proper was conquered in 1559, and the area of Nagar Havili was later ceded to them by the Mahrattas in 1780 as indemnification for piracy.

In other regions, the Portuguese obtained possession of the Bahrein islands in 1507, though they were driven out by Shah Abbas in 1622. In the Banda Islands, Antonio D'Abreu annexed the territory in 1512, but the Portuguese were eventually expelled by the Dutch in the early 17th century.

## The Island of Bombay
The island of Bombay did not prosper under Portuguese rule. Land was distributed through a system called *aforamento*, where fiefs were granted to individuals or religious corporations—specifically the Jesuits and Franciscans in the northern districts—in exchange for quit-rents or military service. The intolerance of this rule hindered the growth of the settlement, which had a population of only 10,000 by 1661.

The English recognized Bombay's value as a naval base, leading to the battle of Swally (1614-1615) and an attempt to capture the island in 1626. In 1661, Bombay became British property as part of the dowry of the infanta Catherine of Portugal upon her marriage to Charles II. However, the British did not actually occupy the island until 1665, facing significant opposition from the Portuguese and religious orders. In 1668, the British crown transferred the island to the East India Company for an annual payment of £10.

## British Trade and Factories
The East India Company established its first settlement in the Bombay presidency in 1618 with a factory at Surat, protected by a charter from Emperor Jahangir. By 1687, Bombay was the head of all Company possessions in India, though it became subordinate to Calcutta in 1753.

In other regions, the British attempted to establish trade factories. In 1608, they built a factory on Wai in the Banda Islands, which was demolished by the Dutch. Later, the British held Run and Wai, but were again expelled by the Dutch. In Japan, the English maintained a factory at Hirado. This factory was closed voluntarily, and the English left their stores in the care of a local feudatory. When the English attempted to reopen business in 1673 via the merchantman *Return*, the Japanese authorities refused, stating that because the King of England had married a Portuguese princess, British subjects could not be permitted to visit Japan.
