# English Poets

The history of English poetry is characterized by a diverse array of forms, from the early Northumbrian verse of the early medieval period to the complex stanzas of the Renaissance and the intense lyricism of the nineteenth century.

## Early English Poetry and the Old English Period
One of the earliest figures in English poetry is Cædmon, a herdsman of Streanæshalch. While his name is not explicable by Old English—likely being the British *Cadman*—he is associated with the *Dream of the Rood*. This poem is Northumbrian and predates the date of Cynewulf. Although some have attempted to link the poem to the Ruthwell Cross via the name "Cadmon" inscribed on its top-stone, this reading has been contested.

Later developments in English poetry saw the emergence of writers like Edmund Spenser. Spenser is noted for his "artificial" diction drawn from dialect, false analogy, and middle English, which creates a sense of beauty. His chief technical contribution was the nine-line stanza (*ababbcbcC*), developed by adding an alexandrine to the eight-line stave of Chaucer's *Monk's Tale*. While his work *The Faerie Queene* is praised for its melody, phrasing, and pictorial allegoric trappings, it is noted for a breakdown in structure after the first two books. In terms of certainty of style among prolific English poets, Spenser is considered to have no equals except for Shelley, Milton, and Chaucer.

## The Elizabethan Age and the Renaissance
The late sixteenth century saw the introduction of Italian licenses into English poetry. Barnfield, a juvenile poet, produced *The Affectionate Shepherd*, a work characterized by coloured touches of nature and picturesqueness. In January 1595, Barnfield published *Cynthia, with certain Sonnets*. The poem "Cynthia," a panegyric on Queen Elizabeth, is one of the earliest extant examples of the Spenserian stanza outside of *The Faerie Queene*. Barnfield's sequence of twenty sonnets, which preceded the publication of Shakespeare's sonnets by fourteen years, celebrates a young man with the initials J. U. or J. V. These works show the influence of Drayton's *Idea*.

## The Epigrammatic Tradition
The epigram is a diminutive form that various English poets have utilized. While some early efforts, such as those by Henry Parrot (1613) and Robert Crowley (1550), are considered worthless in form, others achieved nobility. Ben Jonson provided noble examples in his *Underwoods*, and both Herrick and Spenser wrote poems properly classed as epigrams. Other poets who were successful in this form include Dryden, Cowley, Waller, Prior, Parnell, Swift, Addison, Johnson, Goldsmith, and Young. Alexander Pope is the writer most identified with the epigrammatical spirit in English literature. In the modern era, few have followed Pope's footsteps; Landor might have held a distinct reputation as an epigrammatist had he not written his best minor poems in Latin.

## The Brontë Sisters and Later Poetry
In the nineteenth century, the Brontë sisters contributed significantly to English literature. Emily Brontë is regarded as a great poet; her works "Last Lines" and "Old Stoic" are described as perhaps the finest achievements of poetry given to English literature by any woman. In contrast, Charlotte Brontë is considered undistinguished as a maker of verse, although her novels *Villette* and *Jane Eyre* contain passages of magnificent pure poetry. Anne Brontë, while primarily known for her novels, also maintains a place in most religious communities as a hymn writer.

## Literary Criticism and Biography
The study of English poets has been shaped by various critics and biographers. Stopford Augustus Brooke (1832–), an English divine and man of letters, wrote extensively on the subject, including *Theology in the English Poets—Cowper, Coleridge, Wordsworth, Burns* (1874), *The Poetry of Robert Browning* (1902), and *Tennyson, his Art and Relation to Modern Life* (1894).

The art of recording the lives of poets also evolved. Early biography was often rhetorical and artificial. A shift occurred with Mason's *Life and Letters of Gray* (1774), which pioneered the use of familiar correspondence to illustrate a writer's real character. This approach was further improved upon by Boswell in his 1791 *Life of Dr Samuel Johnson*, which is cited as one of the most interesting examples of biography in the English language. Other notable early biographical works include the *Lives of the Poets* (1753), mainly written by Robert Shiels under the name of Theophilus Cibber, and Dr. Johnson's *Life of Savage* (1744).
