# Jakob Ayrer

Jakob Ayrer (?-1605) was a German dramatist. While little is known regarding his early life, it appears he arrived in Nuremberg as a boy and eventually attained the position of imperial notary. He died in Nuremberg on March 26, 1605.

## Literary Works
Ayrer produced a wide array of written works. His dramatic output was printed in Nuremberg in 1618 under the title *Opus Theatricum*. This collection consists of thirty comedies and tragedies, as well as thirty-six *Singspiele* and *Fastnachtsspiele* (Shrovetide plays). 

Beyond his plays, Ayrer wrote a rhymed *Chronik der Stadt Bamberg*, which was edited by J. Heller and published in Bamberg in 1838. He also produced a translation of the Psalms, though this work remained unpublished.

## Dramatic Style and Influences
In the realm of drama, Ayrer is considered virtually the successor to Hans Sachs. However, his work was significantly influenced by the *Englische Komoedianten*—troupes of English actors who visited the continent during the 17th century and the close of the 16th century. These troupes brought the repertory of the Elizabethan theatre to the continent.

From these English actors, Ayrer adopted the character of the clown and learned to incorporate spectacular effects and sensational incidents to enliven his dramas. Despite these foreign influences, his plays are regarded as being hardly more dramatic in the true sense than those of Hans Sachs, and they are considered inferior to Sachs' work in terms of poetic qualities.

## Relationship to Other Works
The plots of two of Ayrer's comedies, *Von der schoenen Sidea* and *Von der schoenen Phoenicia*, were evidently derived from the same sources used for Shakespeare's *Tempest* and *Much Ado about Nothing*.

## Publications and Editions
The work *Ayrers Dramen* was edited by A. von Keller and published by the Stuttgart Lit. Verein between 1864 and 1865. Other related references in the archive include J. Tittmann's *Schauspiele des sechzehnten Jahrhunderts* (1888), L. Tieck's *Deutsches Theater* (1817), and A. Cohn's *Shakespeare in Germany* (1885), the latter of which contains a translation of two of Ayrer's plays.
