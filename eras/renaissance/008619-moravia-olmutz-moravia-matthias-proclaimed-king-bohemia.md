# Matthias and the Proclamation of the King of Bohemia at Olmutz

The proclamation of Matthias as king of Bohemia at Olmutz was a pivotal event resulting from a complex intersection of religious conflict, papal intervention, and shifting political alliances in the 15th century. This event occurred during the reign of George of Podebrad, the only Hussite king of Bohemia.

## The Reign of George of Podebrad
The early part of King George's reign was characterized by prosperity and a firm alliance with Matthias Corvinus, the king of Hungary. Through the influence of Matthias, George was crowned by the Romanist bishop of Waitzen. George's primary support base consisted of the towns and the smaller nobility. However, over time, Romanist nobles became hostile toward the king. This internal friction led George into a protracted struggle with the papal see, which ultimately caused the failure of his broader ambitions, including an attempt to obtain supremacy over Germany.

In 1452, following unsuccessful negotiations with Rome, George assembled the estates at Prague. He declared his commitment to the communion in both kinds and stated his willingness to risk his crown and life to defend his faith. This declaration further embittered the Romanist party in Bohemia. By 1465, many nobles of the Roman religion formed a confederacy against George at a meeting held at Zelena Hora (Grunberg).

## The Intervention of Matthias of Hungary
The conflict escalated when Pope Paul II provided moral support to the confederates. The Pope excommunicated George of Podebrad and released all Bohemians from their oaths of allegiance to him. Under this papal influence, King Matthias of Hungary deserted his former alliance with George and began supporting the league of Zelena Hora.

A period of desultory warfare ensued. While George was initially successful, the tide turned when Matthias invaded Moravia and captured Brunn, the capital of the country. Following this military success, a meeting of the Catholic nobles of Moravia and Bohemia was convened at Olmutz in Moravia. On May 3, 1469, Matthias was proclaimed king of Bohemia.

## Succession and the Treaty of Olmutz
Despite the proclamation, George of Podebrad achieved some successes over Matthias in the following year. The war was temporarily halted by George's death in 1471. George had previously intended to establish a national dynasty, but in his later years, he offered the succession to the Bohemian throne to Vladislav, the son of Casimir, king of Poland, in hopes of obtaining aid against Matthias.

Following George's death, the Bohemian nobility were divided; many supported the claim of Matthias, who had already been proclaimed king. However, on May 27, 1471, Prince Vladislav of Poland was elected at Kutna Hora. This election represented a victory for the national party and reflected strong anti-clerical sentiment, as Matthias was an unconditional adherent of Rome, whereas Vladislav's envoys promised to maintain the Compacts.

A struggle for the throne continued between Vladislav and Matthias until 1478. The conflict was resolved by a treaty concluded at Olmutz. Under the terms of this treaty, Bohemia was secured for Vladislav. Matthias was permitted to retain the "lands of the Bohemian crown"—specifically Lusatia, Silesia, and Moravia—for the remainder of his lifetime, after which they were to be restored to Bohemia.

## Later Historical Context of Matthias
The name Matthias appears in later Bohemian history in a different context involving the House of Habsburg. In the early 17th century, a struggle broke out between Rudolph and his younger brother Matthias. Matthias used political and religious controversies to supplant his brother, specifically following Rudolph's refusal to sanction a peace treaty with Turkey that Matthias had concluded as a representative in Hungary.

Matthias's forces entered Moravia, where the estates renounced their allegiance to Rudolph. Matthias then invaded Bohemia and reached Liben, near Prague. On June 25, 1608, Matthias reached an understanding with Rudolph. Rudolph formally ceded the government of Upper and Lower Austria, Moravia, and Hungary to Matthias, though Rudolph retained his rights as king of Bohemia. Upon the death of this Matthias in March of a subsequent year, the Bohemian crown became vacant, eventually leading to the election of Frederick, elector palatine, and further warfare with Ferdinand, Matthias's successor as emperor.
