# English Churchmen and Ecclesiastical Traditions

The history of the English church is characterized by a variety of roles, from high-ranking officials and architects to the development of distinct theological and disciplinary factions. These figures and movements have shaped the administrative, physical, and spiritual landscape of the Established Church.

## Notable Church Figures
Throughout English history, various individuals have held significant positions within the church hierarchy. Athelm, an English churchman who is said to have been a monk at Glastonbury, was elevated in 909 to become the first occupant of the see of Wells; he later became the archbishop of Canterbury in 914. 

In the 17th century, the church was utilized as an instrument of obedience by King Charles I. To achieve this, the King appointed Laud as the Archbishop of Canterbury and head of the Church of England. Laud is described as an aggressive high churchman and a strong believer in "divine right," who viewed the role of the archbishop as being very much that of a priest.

In the 16th century, Sir Nicholas Bacon (1509–1579) served as lord keeper of the great seal under Queen Elizabeth. While Bacon was a legal and political figure rather than a member of the clergy, he was instrumental in securing the archbishopric of Canterbury for his friend, Matthew Parker. Bacon's own sympathies were with the Protestants.

## Architectural Contributions
The physical environment of the English church was influenced by figures such as William Butterfield (1814–1900). An English architect educated in Worcester, Butterfield specialized in Gothic architecture and was a keen churchman intimately associated with the English church revival. He was prominent in the work of the Cambridge Camden Society, focusing on the improvement of church art and furniture. 

Butterfield's notable ecclesiastical works include St Augustine's in Canterbury (1845), All Saints' in Margaret Street, London (1859), and St Alban's in Holborn (1863). He also designed Keble College, Oxford (1875) and the new part of Merton College, Oxford (1864). His architectural style was marked by original views on color, utilizing the natural colors of materials, which some critics described as garish.

## Ecclesiastical Factions and Terminology
The English church has seen the emergence of various designations to describe differing views on faith, discipline, and authority.

## High Churchmen and Ritualists
The term "High Churchman" has been applied to those who hold a high view of the sacramental system, the episcopacy, and the exclusive authority of the Established Church. In the 19th century, the Tractarian movement modified this sense, with High Churchmen exalting the sacramental system and the idea of the Catholic Church over the exclusive authority of Scripture and the Establishment. 

A subset of this group, known as "ritualists," sought the revival of pre-Reformation ritual. While "High Churchman" and "Ritualist" are often wrongly used interchangeably, many High Churchmen are not Ritualists, though there is a tendency toward that direction. Additionally, some are categorized as "old-fashioned High Churchmen" or the "high and dry" type, a designation for those preceding the Oxford Movement.

## Low and Broad Churchmen
In contrast to the High Church tradition, the "Low Churchman" became equivalent to the "Evangelical." This movement, associated with the name of Simeon, emphasizes the necessity of personal "conversion." 

Another group, the "Broad Churchmen," emerged as a replacement for the earlier term "Latitudinarian." Latitudinarians were those prepared to concede significant latitude in matters of faith and discipline. Broad Churchmen similarly minimize the value of orthodoxy, instead placing their stress on the ethical teaching of the Church.
