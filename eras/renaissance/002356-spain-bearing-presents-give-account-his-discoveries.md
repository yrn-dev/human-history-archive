# Spanish Exploration and Global Expansion

## Rise to Global Prominence
Spain's ascent to world power was sudden and memorable. For centuries, the corrugated and infertile peninsula had been divided, characterized by a perpetual conflict between its Christian population and the Moors. This internal fragmentation mirrored the political states of Britain, France, and Italy. Historically, Spain had been a poor country—a condition that persisted into the modern era—with wealth primarily limited to its mines.

However, Spain achieved unity just in time to benefit from the discovery of America. For one century, Spain dominated the world through a monopoly on American silver and gold. This expansion was stimulated by the growth of knowledge regarding Asiatic civilization and wealth, as well as Mongolian inventions such as paper and the compass. The discovery of America was itself a consequence of Turkish conquests. This shift in European interest moved the center of history from the Mediterranean and the Alps toward the Atlantic fringe, eventually followed by the expansionist efforts of England, France, and Holland.

## Exploration of the Americas
Spain claimed the largest portion of the new realms, acting as the "mistress" of two-thirds of America. Among the leading figures of this era was Vasco Nunez de Balboa (c. 1475–1517). Born a gentleman (*hidalgo*) in Jerez de los Caballeros, Balboa initially struggled as a farmer in Hispaniola, where he accumulated oppressive debts. In 1510, he secretly joined an expedition led by Enciso by hiding in a cask of victuals. 

After finding the settlement of San Sebastian in ruins, Balboa proposed sailing for Darien on the Gulf of Uraba. There, he founded the town of Sta Maria de la Antigua del Darien. After deposing Enciso and sending him to Spain, Balboa conquered the surrounding region, gaining the friendship of native chiefs through kindness and just dealing. During these excursions, the cacique Comogre informed Balboa of gold in Peru and an ocean on the other side of the mountains, leading Balboa to be recognized as the discoverer of the Pacific.

Other Spanish efforts extended into the south-western United States. Francisco Vasquez de Coronado (c. 1500–c. 1545), appointed governor of New Galicia in 1539, led an expedition in February 1540 to find the "Seven Cities of Cibola" following reports by Fray Marcos de Niza. While Coronado captured the cities, the expected fabled wealth was absent. His expedition resulted in the first trustworthy account of the Zuni pueblos, the first description of the prairie plains and bison, and the discovery of the Grand Canyon of the Colorado by Garcia Lopez de Cardenas. Coronado's forces also penetrated as far as central Kansas in search of an Eldorado called "Quivira" before returning home in 1542.

## Pacific Discoveries and Territorial Shifts
Spanish exploration also reached the Eastern seas. Magellan discovered an archipelago on March 6, 1521, which he called *Islas de las Velas Latinas* ("Islands of the Lateen Sails"). The crew named them *Islas de los Ladrones* ("Islands of the Thieves") due to the inhabitants' propensity for theft. In 1668, these islands were renamed *Las Marianas* in honor of Maria Anna of Austria, the widow of Philip IV of Spain. 

Spain's dominion in the Pacific eventually diminished. In 1899, Spain sold the Caroline and Pelew Islands, as well as the entire Marianas archipelago (excluding Guam), to Germany for £837,500.

## Military and Political Context
Spain's military capabilities were shaped by the development of gunpowder. By the end of the fourteenth century, the use of artillery in sieges and battles by land and sea was familiar to Spain, as well as to England, France, Italy, and Germany. This technology utilized a mixture of charcoal, sulphur, and saltpetre to expel stone or iron balls at destructive velocities.

Politically, the peninsula had faced significant upheavals. In 711, a Moslem army of Berbers and Arabs crossed the Straits of Gibraltar, defeating the West Goths. This invasion was aided by Spanish Jews, who were descendants of the Roman side of an old feud with the Carthaginians. By 720, Islam had reached the Pyrenees and pushed into France before being halted by the kingdom of the Franks.
