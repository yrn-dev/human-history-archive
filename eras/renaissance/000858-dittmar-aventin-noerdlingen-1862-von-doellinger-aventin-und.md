## The Aventine in Rome
The Aventine is identified as a location in Rome associated with both ancient burials and early Christian architecture.

### Ancient History
According to the records of Plutarch, the body of Tatius was buried honorably on Mount Aventine. His burial site is located near an area referred to as the *Armilustrium*. Tatius had been slain by the relatives of ambassadors from Laurentum, who had been murdered by Tatius's own relatives—a crime Tatius had previously hushed up, leading to a variance between him and Romulus.

### Early Christian Structures
The Aventine is also the site of significant early ecclesiastical architecture. At S. Saba, a small and very early church without aisles was discovered beneath the floor of the existing basilica. This discovery provides confirmation of the theory that early Christian buildings, erected in the 2nd or 3rd century A.D., likely began as oblong interiors terminated by an apse before side aisles were added after the time of Constantine to accommodate increasing numbers of the faithful.

Additionally, the church of S. Sabina on the Aventine is noted for its doors of rich wood, which are elaborately carved with scriptural subjects.

## Other References
The archive contains a separate entry for "aventurine" (or "avanturine"), which is a variety of quartz containing scales of iron-oxide or mica. This mineral is found primarily in the Ural Mountains and is cut for ornament at Ekaterinburg. While most is yellow or reddish brown, a green variety containing chrome-mica or fuchsite is highly valued by the Chinese and is said to occur in India's Bellary district. A related variety known as Sun-stone (aventurine felspar) is found principally in south Norway at Tvedestrand.
