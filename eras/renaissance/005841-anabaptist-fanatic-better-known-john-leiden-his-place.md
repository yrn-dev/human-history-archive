# John of Leiden

Johann Beukelsz (also known as Bockelszoon, Buchholdt, or Johann Bockelson), better known as John of Leiden, was a Dutch Anabaptist leader who established a theocratic rule in Münster during the 16th century. Born around 1508 as the illegitimate son of Bockel, the burgomaster of Soevenhagen, he was apprenticed as a tailor. His early life included four years spent in London pursuing his trade. Though he possessed little formal education, he had some literary faculty and wrote plays.

## Rise to Power
Buckholdt became influenced by the opinions of Thomas Münzer and eventually joined the Anabaptist movement in September 1533 under the leadership of Johann Matthysz (Matthyszoon), a baker from Haarlem. On January 13, 1534, Buckholdt arrived in Münster acting as an apostle for Matthysz. Described as fluent and good-looking, he possessed a winning personality and strong organizational skills. He gained the confidence of Bernt Knipperdollinck, a revolutionary cloth merchant and burgomaster, and married Knipperdollinck's daughter.

Following the death of Matthysz in 1534, Buckholdt succeeded him as prophet. He further expanded his household by adding Matthysz's widow to his number of wives.

## The Theocracy of Münster
Under Buckholdt's leadership, the town of Münster became a center for the realization of Anabaptist ideals, though it eventually devolved into a scene of cruelty and gross licence. He initially organized a new constitution for the city based on a theocracy, appointing twelve elders—a number suggested by the tribes of Israel. However, Buckholdt soon superseded these officers, proclaiming himself king of the "new Zion" and the successor to King David.

His rule was characterized by arbitrary power, severity, and pomp. He practiced polygamy, following what some sources describe as the "evil example" of King David. During the period between 1532 and 1535, the insurgents in Münster attempted to implement a form of religious communism. As the city was besieged by the prince-bishop, a state of insanity reportedly ran rife in the town, with some accounts claiming that cannibalism occurred.

## Fall and Execution
The rule of John of Leiden ended on June 25, 1535, when Münster was retaken by its prince-bishop, Franz von Waldeck. Following the city's surrender, Buckholdt was subjected to many indignities and was tortured horribly. He was executed on January 22, 1536. To serve as a witness to the world that order and decency had been restored to Münster, his mutilated body and those of his companions were hung in cages from the tower of the Lamberti church.

## Legacy and Historical Context
The actions of John of Leiden and the "revelry of Muenster" have left a lasting negative impression on the memory of the Anabaptist movement. Historians note that the persecution of Anabaptists deprived the movement of noble leaders who preached non-resistance, which in turn provoked others toward an attitude of vengeance. This shift culminated in the horrors seen in Münster under leaders like Jan Matthisson and John of Leiden.

In contrast to the fanaticism of Leiden, other Anabaptist figures are remembered for their piety and gentle learning, such as Hans Denck, Grebel, Hubmaier, and Blaurock. Following the fall of Münster, the remaining Anabaptists were gathered under the leadership of Menno Simonis in 1537, whose moderation helped check the turbulence of the more fanatical elements of the sect.
