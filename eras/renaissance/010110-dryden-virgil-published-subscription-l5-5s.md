# Dryden's Virgil

## Publication and Cost
Dryden's translation of Virgil was published by subscription at a price of L5:5s. The work was produced as a handsome book and was ornamented with plates. The high price of this volume, as well as other subscription books of the era, was primarily driven by a desire from subscribers to befriend or honour the author. This practice of publishing poetry by subscription at very high prices proved lucrative for Dryden, a trend that was later followed by Pope and Prior.

## Literary Context and Style
John Dryden is described as the master of expression for his time. His translations of Virgil, along with parts of Lucretius, Boccaccio, and Chaucer (the latter in *Fables*, 1700), served to finalize his command of the heroic couplet, which he utilized for oratory and higher kinds of appeal. 

Dryden's work on Virgil and Lucretius also reflected the influence of the *pedestris sermo* (epistolary and critical style) of Horace and the secondary forms of Latin poetry, specifically the satirists and Juvenal. These influences were further commended by Boileau, the chief modernizer of these forms.

## The Author's Career
Dryden's path to his consummate poetical style was gradual. For twenty years, he practiced his couplet through various forms, including heroic tragedy, dramatic prologues, epilogues, and panegyric verse. Early in his career, he wrote panegyrics for Cromwell and later for Charles; a primary example is the *Annus Mirabilis* (1667), which celebrated the naval victory, the Fire, and the Plague. 

Until 1680, Dryden supported himself mainly through his plays. He is also noted for his prose, specifically the *Essay on Dramatic Poesy* (1668), a dialogue regarding the rivalries between the French and Elizabethan drama, and between rhyme and blank verse. His skill in versification and diction reached full power in his dedications, rhymed arguments, translations, and satires. Notable satirical works include *Mac Flecknoe*, *The Medal*, and *Absalom and Achitophel* (1681–1682). Additionally, he wrote *Religio Laici* (1682) and *The Hind and the Panther* (1687), which presented Protestant and Papistical arguings, respectively.

## Other Translations and Works
Beyond Virgil, Dryden is associated with a translation of Plutarch. This version was performed by many different hands and is considered of unequal merit; while some "Lives" are rendered in an idiomatic and racy English, others are viewed as falling short of the standard set by Sir Thomas North. In later years, this version was re-edited by A.H. Clough.

Dryden also contributed significantly to the Restoration drama. Between 1660 and the reign of Queen Anne, prologues and epilogues—often written in heroic couplets—were used to provide a literary finish to plays. Dryden's contributions to these were often brilliantly finished exercises in literary polemic. In 1672, he published "Defence of the Epilogue; or An Essay on the Dramatic Poetry of the Last Age" to defend an epilogue he had written for *The Conquest of Granada*.
