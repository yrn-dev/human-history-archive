# Cesare Borgia

Cesare Borgia (1476–1507), the duke of Valentinois and Romagna, was a prominent figure among the adventurers of the Italian Renaissance. The son of Pope Alexander VI and Vanozza dei Cattanei, he was born in Rome while his father served as cardinal. Following his father's elevation to the papacy in 1492, Cesare was created cardinal a year after being named archbishop of Valencia. He was the favorite son of Alexander VI, who practiced extensive nepotism on his behalf.

## Early Life and Ecclesiastical Career
During the early years of his father's pontificate, Cesare led a profligate life at the Vatican. In January 1495, he accompanied Charles VIII as a hostage for the pope's good behavior during the conquest of Naples, though he escaped at Velletri to return to Rome. 

Cesare soon became known for his violence. In 1497, his brother Giovanni, the duke of Gandia, was murdered; this act was likely committed by Cesare. While some suggested the motive was a rivalry over Donna Sancha (wife of the pope's youngest son, Giuffre) or hinted at incestuous relations with their sister Lucrezia, it is more probable that Cesare envied Giovanni's position as he sought to trade his ecclesiastical titles for a secular career.

In July 1497, Cesare served as papal legate in Naples to crown Frederick of Aragon. He attempted to marry Carlotta, the daughter of the king of Naples, but was refused by both the king and the princess, who viewed him as "a priest, the bastard of a priest." Consequently, in August 1498, Cesare renounced the priesthood with the pope's permission.

## Conquests and the Duchy of Romagna
In October 1498, Cesare traveled to France as papal legate to Louis XII to secure a bull annulling the king's marriage to Jeanne of France. In return, Cesare received military assistance and the duchy of Valentinois. He married Charlotte d'Albret, sister of the king of Navarre, in May 1499.

With French support, Cesare sought to carve a principality in Romagna. Using an army of Italian troops, 4,000 Gascons and Swiss, and 300 French lances, he established headquarters at Cesena. He captured Imola and besieged Forli, forcing the surrender of Caterina Sforza on January 22, 1500. In October 1500, he returned to Romagna with 10,000 men, leading to the flight of Giovanni Sforza of Pesaro and Pandolfo Malatesta of Rimini. Although Astorre Manfredi of Faenza surrendered in April 1501 on the promise of safety, Cesare broke his word and sent him to Rome to be outraged and killed.

Cesare eventually became the duke of Romagna, extending his power to Camerino and Urbino. He was characterized as morose, silent, and unsympathetic, possessing an insatiable avidity and a total lack of scruple.

## The Conspiracy of La Magione and the Fall
Cesare's power was challenged by the conspiracy of La Magione, involving the Orsinis, several deposed princes, and his own captains, including Oliverotto da Fermo, G.P. Baglioni, and Vitellozzo Vitelli. While the papal troops were defeated and risings broke out, the promise of help from the French king forced the confederates to negotiate.

Cesare outmaneuvered his rivals by decoying the *condottieri*—including Vitelli, Oliverotto, and Paolo Orsini—into his house at Senigallia, where he had them arrested.

His downfall was precipitated by a simultaneous fever that struck both him and his father in the summer of 1503. Pope Alexander VI died on August 18, 1503, while Cesare was still incapacitated. This coincidence ruined him, as his enemies rose up and his dominion began to collapse. After the brief papacy of Pius III, Giuliano della Rovere was elected as Julius II on November 1, 1503. Although Julius II initially showed no ill-will, he insisted that Cesare's territories be restored to the church. Julius II eventually had Cesare arrested, ending his power.

## Influence on Machiavelli
Cesare Borgia served as a primary model for Niccolò Machiavelli's political ideals. In October 1502, Machiavelli was sent as an envoy to Cesare's camp in Romagna, where he observed the duke's "intricate intrigues," including the murder of his disaffected captains. Machiavelli admired Cesare's audacity, diplomatic prudence, self-reliance, and his use of fraud and cruelty. He specifically noted Cesare's success in substituting native troops for mercenaries and his firm administration of conquered provinces.

In his work *The Prince*, Cesare appears as a representative figure of the perfect prince. Machiavelli later viewed the historical Cesare as "ruined and contemptible" when he saw him in Rome, separating the actual man from the idealized "Valentino" of his political writings.
