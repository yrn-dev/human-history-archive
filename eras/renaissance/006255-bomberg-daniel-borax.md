# BOMBERG, DANIEL AND BORAX

## Daniel Bomberg
Daniel Bomberg was a prominent Christian printer of Hebrew books who operated primarily in Venice from 1516 until his death in 1549. His work is credited with introducing a new era to Hebrew typography. Among his most significant achievements was the publication of the *editio princeps* of the rabbinical Bible between 1516 and 1517, which featured the Hebrew text alongside rabbinical commentaries. Additionally, Bomberg produced the first complete edition of the Talmud from 1520 to 1523.

## Borax: Chemical Properties and Forms
Borax, also known as sodium biborate or sodium pyroborate ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7$), is a substance that appears in commerce in two primary forms: "common" or prismatic borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot10\text{H}_2\text{O}$) and "jewellers'" or octahedral borax ($\text{Na}_2\text{B}_4\text{O}_7\cdot5\text{H}_2\text{O}$). While alchemists previously used the term "borax" in a vague manner, modern chemistry defines it specifically.

Prismatic borax crystals belong to the monoclinic system and closely resemble pyroxene in their angles and habit. These crystals are transparent to opaque and typically white, though they may appear greenish, bluish, or greyish. They possess a hardness of 2-2.5 and a specific gravity between 1.69 and 1.72. A notable optical characteristic of borax is the striking crossed dispersion of its optic axes; the plane of the optic axes for blue light is inclined at 2 degrees to that of red light, and the angle between the axes is 3 degrees greater for red light.

## Natural Deposits and Mineralogy
Borax is widely distributed as a natural product. In Tibet, deposits have been known since early times; crude material was exported to Europe as *tincal*. Tibetan deposits include *sohaga* (impure borax) in the lake-plain of Pugha on the Rulangchu, and *chu tsale* (water borax) at the lakes of Rudok and Tengri Nor. 

In North America, large masses of crystallized borax are found in the bed of Borax Lake near Clear Lake, California, as well as in San Bernardino county. In these locations, isolated crystals weighing a pound and reaching 7 inches in length have been discovered. Other major commercial sources of boron compounds include the Atacama desert in South America—specifically in the province of Atacama in northern Chile—and the Mohave desert on the borders of Nevada and California. Other natural occurrences are found in Peru and Transylvania.

It is believed that the boron in these salt lakes was supplied by solfataras and hot springs of volcanic origin, similar to those that charge the lagoons of Tuscany with boric acid.

## Production and Preparation
The primary supply of borax is derived from the boric acid of Tuscany. This is processed by fusing the acid in a reverberatory furnace with half its weight of sodium carbonate, then extracting the resulting mass with warm water. Alternatively, boric acid can be added gradually to sodium carbonate dissolved in lead-lined steam-heated pans and concentrated until crystallization occurs. Borax can also be prepared by fusing naturally occurring calcium borate with soda ash, followed by water extraction and concentration.

The specific hydrate produced depends on temperature. The pentahydrate (octahedral borax) is deposited from supersaturated aqueous solutions at high temperatures, or when a solution with a specific gravity of 1.246 is cooled to between 79°C and 56°C. Below 56°C, the decahydrate (ordinary borax) is deposited.

## Industrial and Scientific Applications
Borax is utilized in several industrial processes, including glass-making, the glazing of linen, and the glazing of pottery. In soldering and fluxing, octahedral borax is preferred over the prismatic form because it fuses more easily and is less likely to split when heated. Because solder only adheres to untarnished metal, borax is heated on the metal surface with a soldering iron to remove the superficial film of oxide. When fused, borax can dissolve many metallic oxides to form complex borates.

In chemistry, borax is used to demonstrate hydrolytic dissociation. Because boric acid is a weak acid, adding a large volume of water to a concentrated borax solution (which has been turned red with acetic acid) will cause the litmus to turn blue. This occurs because the dilution produces sodium hydroxide, a strong base that creates an excess of hydroxyl ions.

In metallurgy, borax is used as a flux for the "dry" or fire assay of lead ores. One such flux composition consists of 40.6% potassium carbonate, 31.3% sodium bicarbonate, 15.6% borax, and 12.5% flour.
