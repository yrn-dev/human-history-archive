# Early Engraving and Engravers in England

Engraving is an art form historically associated with the concept of a furrow created by digging or cutting. Derived from a common Teutonic form meaning "to dig" (such as the Old English *grafan*), the term originally included carving and sculpture in old English literature. In modern application, it is restricted to the creation of original pictures via incised lines on metal plates (line-engraving), the use of acid to corrode lines (etching), the roughening of metal surfaces (mezzotint), or the cutting of wood surfaces to leave lines in relief (wood-engraving).

## Historical Development and Techniques
Historically, the woodcut is the earliest of the four primary varieties of engraving. While line-engraving is now considered practically obsolete, etching and mezzotint have recently gained prominence. Technical handling differs across these mediums; for instance, a line engraver does not think or see in the same manner as an etcher or a mezzotint engraver.

The quality of an engraving is determined not only by the artist but also by the "state" (the stage of the plate's creation) and the fineness of the impression. Artists may also take "proofs" from the plate, which can be touched up in various stages.

## Engraving in English Book Illustration
Illustration emerged coincidentally with the invention of printing. While early illustrated books from Italy, Germany, France, and the Netherlands are well-documented in various bibliographies, the later English copper-engraved book illustrations have been imperfectly appreciated. A primary source for this specific subject is Colvin's *Early Engraving and Engravers in England* (1905). Additionally, the work of Bewick has been described by Austin Dobson, and Martin Hardie's *English Colour Books* (1906) describes English books featuring coloured illustrations, a style that has seen a sudden increase in fashion.

## The Rise of Wood-Engraving and Journalism
In England, the development of illustrated journalism around 1832 significantly influenced the national art. While French illustrators utilized lithography, English illustration became increasingly identified with wood-engraving. This shift was driven by the demand for affordable, easy-to-produce illustrations for magazines and books with large circulations.

This evolution led to a transition in the relationship between the artist and the engraver. Initially, the engraver's translation of a painting or wash-drawing into "tint" often prioritized the individuality of the engraver over the artist. However, as artists began drawing directly onto the wood, a "school of the 'sixties" emerged, focusing on facsimile or partial-facsimile engraving. This alliance between designer and engraver flourished until improved mechanical appliances allowed artists to once again dissociate themselves from the craft of the engraver.

## Engraving in Finance and Counterfeiting
Engraving has played a critical role in the production of bank-notes, utilizing two primary systems: line-engraving (where lines are cut into copper or steel plates for plate-printing) and relief-engraving (where lines stand above the plate, as in wood-engraving, for surface-printing). Plate-printing provides a sharper impression, though it was historically believed to be easier to photograph due to the body of ink transferred to the paper.

Early English bank-notes were simple printed forms on water-marked paper for large amounts (L40 and upwards), and forgeries were rare. However, when small notes of L1 and L2 were introduced at the end of the 18th century, forgery became rife; in one single year, 352 persons were convicted of the crime in England. To combat this, engravers introduced emblematical figures, views, and engine-turned ornaments. 

The advent of photography provided counterfeiters with a dangerous tool for reproducing complicated vignettes. In 1856, Henry Bradbury introduced anti-photographic printing using multiple colours to confuse photographic reproduction. Because early copper plates wore out quickly, resulting in a mix of sharp new notes and blurred old ones, the public became accustomed to variations, which further assisted forgers.

## Bibliographic Records of Engraving
Various histories of engraving exist to document the art. In England, W.Y. Ottley published *Early History of Engraving* in two volumes in 1816 and initiated a series of notices on engravers and their works. Other general works on the subject include William Gilpin's *Essay on Prints* (1781), J. Maberly's *The Print Collector* (1844), W.H. Wiltshire's *Introduction to the Study and Collection of Ancient Prints* (1874), and F. Wedmore's *Fine Prints* (1897). Modern iconography has been further advanced by photographic reproduction processes.
