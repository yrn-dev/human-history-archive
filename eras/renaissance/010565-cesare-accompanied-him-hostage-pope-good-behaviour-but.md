# Cesare Borgia

Cesare Borgia (1476–1507), the Duke of Valentinois and Romagna, was the son of Pope Alexander VI and Vanozza dei Cattanei. Born in Rome while his father served as a cardinal, Cesare became the favorite son of the Pope, who practiced extensive nepotism on his behalf. Following his father's elevation to the papacy in 1492, Cesare was appointed archbishop of Valencia and, a year later, was made a cardinal. During the early years of the pontificate, he lived a profligate life at the Vatican.

## Early Career and Transition to Secular Life
In January 1495, when Charles VIII departed Rome to conquer Naples, Cesare accompanied him as a hostage to ensure the Pope's good behavior; however, he escaped at Velletri and returned to Rome. By 1497, Cesare began to exhibit the violence for which he would later be known. When his brother, Giovanni, the Duke of Gandia, was murdered in 1497, the deed was attributed to Cesare. While some suggested the motive was a rivalry over the affection of Donna Sancha (wife of the Pope's youngest son, Giuffre) or hinted at incestuous relations with their sister Lucrezia, it is considered more probable that Cesare envied his brother's position and wished to secure his father's full favor as he contemplated a secular career.

In July 1497, acting as papal legate, Cesare crowned Frederick of Aragon as king in Naples. Seeking a wealthy marriage, Cesare desired to marry Carlotta, the daughter of the King of Naples, but was refused by both the king and the princess, who viewed him as "a priest, the bastard of a priest." Consequently, in August 1498, Cesare received permission from the Pope and the cardinals to renounce the priesthood "for the good of his soul."

## Diplomatic Missions and the Conquest of Romagna
On October 1, 1498, Cesare traveled to France as a papal legate to Louis XII to deliver a bull annulling Louis's marriage to Jeanne of France. In return, Cesare received military assistance for his own goals and the duchy of Valentinois. After failing once more to win Carlotta of Naples, he married Charlotte d'Albret, the sister of the king of Navarre, in May 1499.

Pope Alexander VI then tasked Cesare with subduing turbulent local despots in Romagna to carve out a principality. Establishing headquarters at Cesena, Cesare utilized an army of 300 French lances, 4,000 Gascons and Swiss, and Italian troops. He captured Imola and besieged Forli, forcing the surrender of Caterina Sforza on January 22, 1500. After a brief return to Rome in February 1500—where he was named *gonfaloniere* of the church and participated in carnival festivities and bull fights—he returned to Romagna in October 1500 with 10,000 men. 

During this campaign, Pandolfo Malatesta of Rimini and Giovanni Sforza of Pesaro fled, allowing their cities to open to Cesare. In April 1501, Astorre Manfredi surrendered Faenza on the promise that his life would be spared; however, Cesare broke this word, and Manfredi was sent to Rome, where he was outraged and killed. Following these successes, the Pope created Cesare the Duke of Romagna.

## Political Ideal and the Conspiracy of La Magione
Cesare's reputation grew as a terror to Italy due to his insatiable avidity, lack of scruple, and cruelty. He was described as morose, silent, and unsympathetic. His power was later challenged by the conspiracy of La Magione, where deposed princes, the Orsinis, and his own captains—including Vitellozzo Vitelli, Oliverotto da Fermo, and G.P. Baglioni—plotted against the Borgias. Although papal troops were defeated and risings occurred in Romagna and Urbino, the promise of help from the French king frightened the confederates into terms.

Cesare eventually outmaneuvered his rivals. He decoyed the *condottieri*—including Vitelli, Oliverotto, and Paolo Orsini—into his house at Senigallia, where he had them arrested. These intricate intrigues were observed by Niccolò Machiavelli, who was sent as an envoy to Cesare's camp in October 1502. Machiavelli developed a strong admiration for Cesare's combination of diplomatic prudence, audacity, and the adroit use of fraud and cruelty. Machiavelli later used Cesare as the model for his political ideal of the perfect prince, referring to him as "Valentino."

## Decline and Fall
Cesare returned to Rome in early 1503 to help reduce the last rebel Orsinis. In the summer of 1503, both Cesare and his father were seized by fever. Pope Alexander VI died on August 18, while Cesare was still incapacitated. This coincidence led to Cesare's ruin. His enemies rose against him, and the deposed princes in Romagna sought to regain their lands. After attempting to intimidate the cardinals with his bravos and Don Michelotto, Cesare was forced to leave Rome in September.

The subsequent election of Pius III did not stop the collapse of Cesare's dominion; the lords of Pesaro, Rimini, and Piombino, as well as Guidobaldo, Duke of Urbino, regained their territories. Only Cesena remained faithful. Following the death of Pius III, Giuliano della Rovere was elected as Pope Julius II on November 1. Although Julius II initially showed no ill-will, he insisted that Cesare's territories be restored to the church. After attempting to use Cesare to keep the Venetians out of Romagna, Julius II had him arrested, ending Borgia's power and forcing the surrender of his castles.
