# Orlando Inamorato

The *Orlando Inamorato* is a significant work of Italian literature, recognized as the first example of the romantic epic that served as a model for later works, including Ariosto's *Orlando Furioso*. The poem was composed by Boiardo, who did not live to see its completion.

## Composition and Patronage
The *Orlando Inamorato*, along with nearly all of Boiardo's other works, was composed for the amusement of Duke Hercules and his court. Although written for the Duke, the poem was not composed within the precincts of the court. Boiardo typically retired to his estates, such as Scandiano, to devote himself to his writing.

Boiardo incorporated elements of his personal surroundings into the work. According to the accounts of Tiraboschi, Mazzuchelli, Vallisnieri, and Castelvetro, the author included descriptions of the agreeable environs of his chateau within the poem. Furthermore, it is stated that Boiardo derived the names of several of his heroes—including Agramant, Sacripant, Gradasse, and Mandricardo—from the names of his own peasants. He believed the uncouthness of these names made them appropriate for Saracen warriors.

## Literary Influences and Structure
The subject matter of the *Orlando Inamorato* is derived from the *Fabulous Chronicle* of the pseudo-Turpin. However, the detailed plot bears little resemblance to that source, with the exception of the presence of principal warriors such as Oliver, Roland, and Charlemagne.

Some writers, including Mazzuchell and Gravina, have asserted that Boiardo used Homer's *Iliad* as his model. According to this view, the poem acts as a reflex image of the *Iliad*, with the character of Angelica occupying the role of Helen and the city of Paris being besieged in a manner similar to the city of Troy.

## Publication History
Following Boiardo's death, the poem was printed in Scandiano the following year under the superintendence of his son, Count Camillo. While the title of the book does not include a date, a Latin letter from Antonia Caraffa di Reggio prefixed to the poem is dated the kalends of June 1495. A second edition was printed in Venice before the year 1500, and the work was subsequently reprinted in Venice twice during the following twenty years.
