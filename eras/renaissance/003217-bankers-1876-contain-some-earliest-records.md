# Early Banking and Financial Instruments in England

## Early Proposals for Banking
The establishment of banking in England is documented in state papers included in F. G. Hilton Price's *Handbook of London Bankers* (1876). One of the earliest records is a petition from November 1581 sent to Queen Elizabeth by Christopher Hagenbuck and his partners. Written in the original Italian, the petition proposed a "method and form" to institute an office that would bring in a very large sum of money annually without expense to the Queen. Hagenbuck claimed this office would provide the Queen, the State, and the people with necessary funds, maintain abundance in the country, and remove "extreme usuries" that were devastating the people and the monarchy.

In exchange for this plan, Hagenbuck requested 6% of the total money received by the office for twenty years. Queen Elizabeth agreed to a grant of 4% for a twenty-year term, a grant confirmed under the great seal and signed by Francis Walsingham. However, no further action appears to have followed this agreement. It is suggested that Hagenbuck may have become familiar with the idea of banking through commerce with Venice, specifically the Banco della Piazza di Rialto. Similar proposals were recorded in state papers in 1621, 1622, 1662, and 1666, though none were put into practice.

## The Role of Goldsmiths
During the reign of Charles II, goldsmiths played a significant role in early banking. The *London Directory* of 1677 lists goldsmiths who maintained "running cashes." Of the firms listed in 1677, five were still operating in 1876. Three of these—or their direct descendants—continued business beyond that date: Hoare & Co. of Fleet Street, Child & Co. of Temple Bar, and Martin & Co. of Lombard Street (as Martin's Bank, Ltd.). Other firms, such as Willis, Percival & Co. and Barnetts, were absorbed after 1876, with Barnetts being taken over by Lloyds Bank in 1884 and the second by the Capital and Counties in 1878.

Some goldsmiths conducted extensive business. Edward Blackwell, an eminent goldsmith and banker during Charles II's reign, served a prestigious clientele including the king, the queen mother Henrietta Maria, James, duke of York, the prince of Orange, Samuel Pepys, the Goldsmiths' Company, and the East India Company. Sir John Houblon, who later became the first governor of the Bank of England, also held an account with Blackwell. Although Blackwell was ruined by the closing of the exchequer in 1672, his grandsons eventually became partners in Child's Bank after his son married into the family of Sir Francis Child.

## Cheques and Legal Frameworks
A cheque is defined as a bill of exchange drawn on a banker that is payable on demand. While most laws regarding bills payable on demand apply to cheques, specific rules exist because of the relationship between the banker and the customer. Legally, a person with a bank account is entitled to draw on it using cheques, though the right to overdraw requires a specific agreement. 

A banker's authority to pay a customer's cheque can be terminated by a notice of the customer's death or a countermand of payment. To prevent fraud and ensure safe transmission via post, a system of "crossing" was developed. A "general" crossing consists of two parallel lines with the words "& Co." written between them, meaning the cheque cannot be paid over the counter and must be presented by a banker. A "special" crossing includes the name of a specific banker, restricting payment to that particular institution. Additionally, cheques may be marked "not negotiable."

Legislative recognition of crossing began with the 19 and 20 Vict. c. 95, which was amended in 1858. A consolidating and amending act was passed in 1876, which was later repealed and re-enacted with slight modifications in the Bills of Exchange Act 1883 (Sec. 76 to 82).

## Deposit Accounts and Banking Relations
A deposit account—whether on fixed notice or at call—is viewed legally as a debt due from the banker to the depositor rather than a fiduciary relation. While a banker may honor a cheque drawn against a deposit account on call by relying on a right of set-off or lien, the customer has no legal right to enforce such payment. 

Deposit receipts provided by bankers are exempt from stamp duty, even if they include undertakings regarding interest and principal payments. These receipts are not considered negotiable instruments. However, if a deposit note includes a cheque, the banker cannot refuse payment if the cheque is presented and all required formalities are met.
