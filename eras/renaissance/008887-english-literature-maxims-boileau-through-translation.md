# The Influence of Boileau's Maxims on English Literature

Nicolas Boileau-Despreaux (1636–1711) was a French poet and critic who established a well-defined position in French literature as the first individual to reduce its versification to rule and emphasize the value of workmanship for its own sake. His critical work, particularly his maxims and precepts, exerted a significant influence on English literature, primarily through translations and imitations.

## L'Art poétique and its Principles
One of Boileau's masterpieces, *L'Art poétique*, published in 1674, was written in imitation of Horace's *Ars Poetica*. This work served as a code for future French verse, occupying a position in French literature parallel to that of its Latin prototype. In this text, Boileau did more than establish rules for poetic language; he provided a careful analysis of various kinds of verse composition and the specific principles governing each.

The work is divided into four books. The first and last books focus on general precepts, primarily advocating for the great rule of *bon sens*. The second book addresses the satire, epigram, ode, elegy, and pastoral, while the third book focuses on epic and tragic poetry. While these rules are considered of value, they have been noted for a tendency to render poetic efforts too mechanical or to hamper them.

## Influence on English Literature
The maxims of Boileau reached English literature through two primary channels: a translation revised by Dryden and a "magnificent imitation" found in Alexander Pope's *Essay on Criticism*. Through these mediums, Boileau's influence on English letters was strong, although less durable than his impact on French literature.

Boileau's role as the chief modernizer of the *pedestris sermo* (critical and epistolary style) and the secondary forms of Latin poetry—specifically the satirists and Juvenal—further commended these styles to English writers. During the period between 1660 and 1700, France exerted a literary empire over Northern and Southern lands. In England, this influence was most pronounced in the social forms of prose and verse, specifically affecting the maxim, epigram, criticism, satire, and comedy.

Beyond his theoretical maxims, Boileau's mock heroic poem *Le Lutrin* (1674) provided a model for Alexander Pope's *The Rape of the Lock*, though the English work is regarded as superior in its richness of imagination.

## Critical Perspectives and Technical Advice
Boileau is described as a great, though not infallible, critic in verse, though he is not considered a great poet. He is credited with destroying the exaggerated reputations of mediocrities of his era, despite occasional faults in judgment.

In terms of technical execution, Boileau used his intelligence to address the dangers of purely descriptive poetry, which he believed risked being frigid, sterile, or lacking intensity. He advised poets to be "riches et pompeux" (rich and pompous) in descriptions to display elegance and to use numerous figures to create "riante image" (cheerful images). He specifically mocked authors who provided "abondance stérile" (sterile abundance) by including unnecessary details.

## Biographical Context
Born in Paris on November 1, 1636, Nicolas Boileau was the fifteenth child of Gilles Boileau, a parlement clerk. Educated at the College de Beauvais and the Sorbonne, he initially studied theology before turning to law and being called to the bar on December 4, 1656. He eventually abandoned the legal profession due to its chicanery and devoted himself to letters following his father's death in 1657.

His early works included *Satires du Sieur D....* (1666) and his *Epitres*, which were more polished and mature in thought than his satires. The *Epitres* earned him the favor of Louis XIV, who granted him a pension of 2,000 livres after Boileau recited some unprinted lines in honor of the monarch.
