# Clovis Eve and French Bookbinding

## Contributions to Bookbinding
Clovis Eve was a master binder in France during the 16th and 17th centuries. He is credited with the invention of a specific decorative style known as "fanfare." This style is characterized by a delicate tracery applied over the boards of a book, which is further detailed with spirals of leafy stems.

## Contemporaries and Related Styles
The work of Clovis Eve existed alongside other influential masters of French binding. Nicholas Eve, for example, bound the *Livre des Statuts et Ordonnances de l'ordre du Benvist Sainct Esprit* for Henry III; this specific volume is described as being made of brown morocco with gold tooling and featuring the arms of the King of France.

Another significant figure of the era was Le Gascon, who invented "dotted work." This technique involved scoring small gilding tools—specifically arabesques and curves—so that the resulting impressions appeared as dotted lines rather than straight lines. Le Gascon's style was widely imitated, most notably by the 17th-century Dutch binders Magnus and Poncyn in Amsterdam. Florimond Badier also worked in a style very similar to Le Gascon, though unlike Le Gascon, Badier sometimes signed his work.

Additionally, the Padeloup le Jeune family, active during the 17th and 18th centuries, were known as excellent workmen. Their bindings frequently featured inlaid designs and borders of lace-like gold tooling referred to as "dentelle," and often included small tickets bearing the binder's name.

## Other Notable Binders
Beyond the French masters, other significant binders are recorded in the archive. Samuel Mearne, who died in 1683, served as the royal binder to Charles II. Mearne invented the "cottage style" of decoration, which involves running an inner rectangle parallel to the book's edges and breaking the upper and lower lines outwards to create the outline of a gable roof. This style remained in use into the modern era, appearing on the Bible used by Edward VII for his coronation oath. Mearne was also known for painting the fore-edges of books so that the artwork remained invisible until the pages were fanned out.
