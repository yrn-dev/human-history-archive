# Vegetius and the Evolution of the Buccina

The works of Vegetius, specifically *De re militari*, served as a significant reference for the understanding of ancient musical instruments during the 15th and 16th centuries. This is particularly evident in the German translations of his texts published in the cities of Ulm and Augsburg.

## German Translations and Musical Terminology
The relationship between the ancient Roman buccina and later European brass instruments was recognized in Germany through two specific translations of Vegetius. One translation was published at Ulm in 1470, and another was published at Augsburg in 1534. These texts demonstrate the linguistic and technical evolution of the instrument by equating the buccina with the trumpet and the trombone.

The 1470 Ulm translation states, "Bucina das ist die trumet oder pusan," which translates to "the bucina is the trumpet or trombone." The 1534 Augsburg translation further clarifies the mechanical nature of the instrument, describing the buccina as "die trummet die wirt ausz und eingezogen," meaning "the trumpet which is drawn out and in." These translations establish a clear connection between the buccina and the derivation of the German word for trombone, *Posaune*, as well as the word *Sackbut*.

## The Buccina in Context
The buccina is identified as the ancestor of both the trombone and the trumpet. In its military form, it was a more advanced instrument than its prototype, the *buccina marina*. The *buccina marina* was a primitive trumpet shaped like a conical shell, often featuring a spiral twist, and was frequently referred to in poetry as *concha*. This primitive version is often depicted in the hands of sailors or Tritons.

In contrast, the military buccina is distinguishable from other Roman instruments, such as the tuba and the cornu, on Trajan's column. While the buccina and cornu both originally possessed a characteristic "C" curve, this specific shape was lost in Europe after the fall of the Roman Empire as the art of bending metal tubes gradually disappeared. Although the instrument survived in name and principle through the Middle Ages, the curve was never recovered.

## Comparative Translations
Other translations of Vegetius provide different perspectives on the instrument. A French translation by Jean de Meung, published in Paris in 1488, renders a passage from chapter iii. 5 by distinguishing between the *trompe* and the *buisine*. According to this translation, the *trompe* is "longue et droite" (long and straight), while the *buisine* is "courte et reflechist en li meisme si comme partie de cercle" (short and reflected in itself like part of a circle).

## Historical and Geographic Context
The cities where these translations were published, Ulm and Augsburg, were prominent "free towns" in Germany. During the 14th and 15th centuries, these wealthier cities operated practically as *imperia in imperio*, ruling their people without outside interference and forming alliances known as *Städtebünde*. Both Ulm and Augsburg were categorized as Swabian free cities.

Augsburg, in particular, was a center of significant activity during the period these translations were produced. In the early 1470s, books printed in Augsburg were noted for their abundant illustrations. The city also served as a site for major political and religious events, such as the diet of Augsburg, where the Reformer Luther appeared before Cardinal Cajetan.
