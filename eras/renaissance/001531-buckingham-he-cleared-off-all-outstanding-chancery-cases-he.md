# Francis Bacon and the Chancery Court

## Appointment and Early Tenure
Francis Bacon attained a significant peak in his professional ambition when he was appointed to the position of first law officer in the kingdom and accredited minister to his sovereign. Following the resignation of Ellesmere on March 5, 1616/7, the great seal was bestowed upon Bacon on March 7, granting him the title of lord keeper. Two months after this appointment, Bacon took his seat in the chancery court with great pomp, delivering an opening discourse described as weighty and impressive.

Bacon approached his new responsibilities with great vigour. His efficiency in the role was evidenced by the fact that, in less than a month, he was able to report to Buckingham that he had cleared all outstanding chancery cases. At this time, Bacon appeared to be in a secure and prosperous position, maintaining the best possible terms with both the king and the king's favourite, Villiers.

## Relationship with Villiers and Buckingham
Bacon had acquired great favour with Villiers and sought to guide the young favourite's conduct through his advice. In letters addressed to Villiers, Bacon analyzed the various relations a man in such a position must maintain and prescribed suitable courses of action for each. 

Notably, Bacon provided specific counsel regarding the treatment of judges. He urged Villiers not to interpose himself, via letter or word, in any cause that was currently pending or likely to be pending in a court of justice. Bacon advised that Villiers should prevent others from doing so and should dissuade the king from intervening in cases due to the importunity of friends or individuals. Bacon warned that such interference perverts justice; even if a judge possessed the undaunted courage and justice to remain unswayed, such actions would still leave a "taint of suspicions and prejudice" behind.

## Charges of Influence and the Case of Dr. Steward
Despite the advice Bacon had provided, Buckingham developed a habit of writing letters to Bacon to recommend the causes of various suitors. While many of these letters appear to be mere courtesies—suggesting no illegal intention to sway a judge's opinion—one specific case involving Dr. Steward cast suspicion on these interactions.

The notes from Buckingham regarding Dr. Steward's case were such that they could arouse doubts. The subsequent course of the action appeared to be exactly accommodated to Dr. Steward, who had received strong recommendations. While there is no explanation from Bacon himself regarding this isolated case, some interpretations suggest that Bacon allowed a just initial judgment on the case to be set aside, reopening the entire matter in obedience to Buckingham's request.

## The Chancery Division and Legal Context
Within the broader legal framework of the archive's records, the chancery division is noted for its role in the administration of insolvent estates. Under the Judicature Act 1875, an insolvent estate may be administered in the chancery division, though the law of bankruptcy continues to regulate the order in which assets are divided among creditors. Specifically, the rules regarding the rights of secured and unsecured creditors, the valuation of future and contingent liabilities, and provable debts and liabilities are imported from bankruptcy law into the chancery division's administration.
