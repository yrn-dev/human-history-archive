# Francis Bacon

Francis Bacon was an English statesman, philosopher, and legal official whose career spanned the reigns of Elizabeth I and the subsequent monarchy. His life was marked by significant political ascent, a prolific output of philosophical works, and a controversial legal downfall.

## Political Career and Public Service
Bacon's political connections began early; as early as 1588, he appears to have become acquainted with the Earl of Essex, a favorite of Queen Elizabeth. By the end of 1591, Bacon served as the Earl's confidential adviser, working diligently in his service alongside his brother, Anthony. In February 1593, Bacon took a seat in Parliament representing Middlesex. This particular session of the House was summoned primarily to address the discovery of one of the many popish plots that occurred during Elizabeth's reign.

During his time in the House of Commons, Bacon was involved in critical deliberations regarding state necessities and subsidies. In one instance, he opposed a conference with the House of Lords because the Lords' proposal to discuss supply infringed upon the privileges of the Commons. While Bacon approved of a proposed triple subsidy, he argued against the short four-year period proposed for its collection, suggesting instead that money be raised by imposition or levy to avoid public discontent. Although his motion failed and the subsidy passed unanimously, his actions—though intended to serve the queen—caused deep and lasting offense, leading to accusations that he was seeking popularity.

In later years, Bacon sought the office of lord chancellor. In early 1616, while Thomas Egerton, Baron Ellesmere, was dangerously ill, Bacon wrote to the king proposing himself for the vacancy. Although he received a promise of the reversion of the office, Ellesmere recovered. Consequently, Bacon proposed becoming a privy councillor to strengthen his position as an adviser to the king, taking his seat at the council board on June 9, 1616. During this period, he also became acquainted with the king's new favorite, Sir George Villiers.

## Philosophical Works
Bacon produced a wide array of philosophical writings, ranging from minor tracts to elaborate systems. Among his most significant works are *The Advancement of Learning*, which was later expanded into *De Augmentis* and serves as the first part of the *Instauratio*, and the *Novum Organum*.

His minor philosophical writings include several important pieces:
*   ***Valerius Terminus*** (composed circa 1603): A brief outline of the first two parts of the *Instauratio* that provides insight into the development of his system.
*   ***Temporis Partus Masculus***: A fragment noted for an arrogant and offensive style, where the term *masculus* refers to the fruit-bearing power of the new philosophy.
*   ***Redargutio Philosophiarum*** (composed circa 1608 or 1609): An oration containing details that later appeared in the *Idola Theatri* of the *Novum Organum*.
*   ***Cogitata et Visa*** (1607): A work written in sonorous Latin containing the substance of the first book of the *Organum*.
*   ***Descriptio Globi Intellectualis***: A text that classifies the sciences and serves as an intermediate work between the *Advancement* and *De Augmentis*.

## Legal Controversies and Style
Bacon's tenure as a legal official ended in severe condemnation. He was accused of corruption, specifically regarding the acceptance of bribes. Bacon himself confessed that some of his acts amounted to corruption and were worthy of condemnation, distinguishing between different ways bribes could be given. However, he explicitly denied the deliberate sale of justice, asserting that he never had a reward in mind when pronouncing a sentence. 

Evidence suggests that in several cases, Bacon's judgments were given against the parties who had provided bribes; in the case of Lady Wharton, it appears he accepted a present knowing his opinion would be adverse to her cause. Furthermore, records indicate that no attempt to reverse his numerous decrees was successful, supporting his plea that he did not willfully pervert justice.

Contemporaries noted the peculiarities of Bacon's writing style. His friend Sir Tobie Matthews described him as a man of rare and diverse knowledge who possessed a "felicity of expressing it all in so elegant, significant, so abundant, and yet so choice and ravishing a way of words, of metaphors, of allusions."
