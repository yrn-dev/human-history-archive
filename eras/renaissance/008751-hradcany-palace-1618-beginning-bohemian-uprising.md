# Hradcany Palace and the Bohemian Uprising of 1618

The Hradcany palace in Prague served as a central site of political conflict during the onset of the Bohemian uprising in 1618. The events at the palace were the culmination of escalating religious and political tensions between the Bohemian Protestants and the supporters of the House of Habsburg.

## Political and Religious Context
The instability leading to the uprising began with the succession to the Bohemian throne. King Matthias, who was childless, sought to secure the succession for his cousin, Ferdinand, Duke of Styria. Ferdinand was recognized as a cruel persecutor of Protestants in Styria and a fanatical adherent of the Church of Rome. Despite this, Bohemian state officials used "not very scrupulous means" to persuade the estates to accept Ferdinand as heir. He was crowned in Prague on June 17, 1617.

Following this, the policy of King Matthias became more pronouncedly ultramontane. The king's councillors, all adherents of the Church of Rome, expressed hope that the Catholic Church would soon regain its ancient hold over Bohemia. This alarmed Bohemian Protestants, led by Count Thurn—one of the few nobles who had refused to recognize Ferdinand as heir. The Protestants believed a conflict was inevitable and decided it was more advantageous to confront the weak Matthias than the more fanatical Ferdinand.

## The Precipitation of Conflict
The immediate catalyst for the uprising occurred in December 1617, when the abbot of Brevnov and the archbishop of Prague ordered the suppression of Protestant religious services in churches located on their domains. This action was a direct infringement of the agreement established when King Rudolph signed the Letter of Majesty.

In response, the defenders of the Protestant faith invited all Protestant members of the diet to meet in Prague. They assembled on May 21, 1618, and resolved to proceed to the Hradcany palace in full armour to present their complaints to the councillors of Matthias.

## The Events at Hradcany Palace
On May 22, 1618, a secret meeting was held by members of the "more advanced party," including Ulrich of Kinsky, Wenceslas of Ruppa, and Count Thurn. During this meeting, it was decided that the most influential of Matthias's councillors should be put to death.

On May 23, the representatives of the Bohemian Protestants proceeded to the Hradcany palace. During the ensuing events, several government officials were thrown from the windows of the palace. Among those thrown from the windows was William Count Slavata (1572-1652), a faithful servant of the house of Habsburg. Slavata later detailed these events in his memoirs, *Pamety*, published in 1637.

## Aftermath and the Thirty Years' War
The uprising at the Hradcany palace marked the beginning of a broader conflict known as the Thirty Years' War (1618-1648). This war was a devastating struggle for political advantage involving Bohemians, Swedes, and Germans. 

The period following the uprising eventually led to the downfall of Bohemian independence in 1620. This era also saw a decline in the national tongue, which by the 18th century had almost vanished as a written language. Historians of the period include Paul Skala ze Zhore, an official for the "winter king" Frederick of the Palatinate, and William Count Slavata, who remained a loyalist to the Habsburgs.
