# Poets and Mythic Verse

The development of mythic poetry in England and the broader classical tradition is characterized by the adaptation of ancient models, the exploration of amorous situations, and the influence of Italian Renaissance licenses.

## Elizabethan Mythic Poetry
Between 1588 and 1598, during the early sonnet-vogue, several significant amorous mythologic tales in verse appeared. These included *Scillaes Metamorphosis* by Lodge, *Hero and Leander* by Marlowe, *Endimion and Phoebe* by Drayton, and the works of Shakespeare, specifically *Venus and Adonis* and *The Rape of Lucrece*. These poems generally describe love situations from an external perspective, with the exception of Marlowe, who is noted for achieving a "more than Sicilian perfection."

The works of Shakespeare in this genre exhibit different focuses: in *Venus and Adonis*, the poet is less enamored with love itself than with the poses and tones of lovers and the gallant motion and beauty of animals. In *The Rape of Lucrece*, the focus shifts to a spectator's interest in the gradations of indignation, shame, and lust. A common trait among these pieces is virtuosity, defined as the delight of the executant in their own brilliant cunning.

These poets were influenced by earlier works. Shakespeare owed something to Lodge, and Drayton to Marlowe. The broader tradition of amorous mythologic tales derived from contemporary Italy and originally from Ovid. Ovid's *Metamorphoses* (available in Golding's version from 1555-1557) provided tales, decorations, and descriptions, while his *Heroides* served as a model for Boccaccio and Chaucer regarding the self-anatomy of plaintive or tragic sentiment.

## Influence and Imitation in the Renaissance
The influence of *Venus and Adonis* extended to other poets, such as the juvenile Barnfield. In *The Affectionate Shepherd*, Barnfield utilized the license introduced from Italy during the Renaissance, pouring his fancy and colored touches of nature into conventional outlines. While successful, the poem faced moral censure. Barnfield is noted for his picturesqueness, specifically his descriptions of "speckled flower call'd sops-in-wine," "fine ruff-footed doves," and the desire to catch the "long-bill'd woodcock" by the glimmering of starry light.

Barnfield's later work, *Cynthia, with certain Sonnets* (published January 1595 and dedicated to William Stanley, the new earl of Derby), is an example of the earliest study of both Shakespeare and Spenser. *Cynthia* is a panegyric on Queen Elizabeth and is likely the earliest extant example of the Spenserian stanza outside of *The Faerie Queene*. The accompanying twenty sonnets, which celebrate a young man with the initials J. U. or J. V., precede the publication of Shakespeare's sonnets by fourteen years and are closer to them in manner than any other Elizabethan works. These sonnets also show the influence of Drayton's *Idea*, which also contained amatory sonnets addressed to a young man.

## Classical and Bucolic Traditions
In the Greek tradition, Bion was a bucolic poet born at Phlossa near Smyrna who flourished around 100 B.C. and lived in Sicily. His work is characterized by an exaggerated sentimentality and overstrained reflection, lacking the truthfulness to nature found in Theocritus. Bion's longest and best work is the *Lament for Adonis*, which refers to the first day of the festival of Adonis, where the death of Aphrodite's favorite was lamented. This serves as an introduction to Theocritus's *Adoniazusae*, which celebrates the reunion of Adonis and Aphrodite on the second day.

The figure of Adonis owes his renown to contemporary sovereigns and the Alexandrine poets. In other Greek poetry, such as that of Stesichorus, the character of Daphnis the herdsman illustrated the destroying force of Aphrodite. Similarly, Hesiod and Stesichorus attributed the conduct of Klytæmnêstra and Helen to the anger of Aphrodite, caused by Tyndareus's failure to sacrifice to her.

## Mythological Interpretations
Different historical and literary perspectives have interpreted these mythic figures. In the *De Astrologiâ* printed among the works of Lucian, the author rejects the literal meaning of myths, suggesting instead that figures like Æneas were not the sons of deities but men born under favorable planetary influences, such as Venus.

In Egyptian accounts, the temple of Proteus at Memphis contained a chapel dedicated to "Venus the Stranger," which some conjecture was Helen. According to Herodotus, Paris brought Helen to Proteus, who reproached him for his perfidy in stealing his host's wife. Proteus eventually restored Helen and her treasures to Menelaus after the Trojan War.
