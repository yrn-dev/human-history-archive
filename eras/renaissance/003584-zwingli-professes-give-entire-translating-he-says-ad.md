# Huldrych Zwingli

Huldrych Zwingli was a Swiss reformer and a central figure in the religious and political upheavals of the early 16th century. His work in Zürich and his interactions with other reformers of the era, most notably Martin Luther, defined the early development of the Protestant movement in Switzerland and Germany.

## Theological Conflicts and the Marburg Colloquy
Zwingli was involved in a fierce controversy with Martin Luther regarding the true doctrine of the sacrament of the Lord's Supper. This disagreement was a significant obstacle to a general union of evangelical Christians in the empire. While Luther was a patriotic German, Zwingli sought confederations between South German cities and Swiss cantons, a vision that Luther believed would weaken the empire. Furthermore, Luther viewed Zwingli as a thorough democrat—a contrast to Luther's own lack of trust in the "common man"—and suspected that friends of Zwingli had helped organize a Peasants' War to link social revolution with religious awakening.

These tensions culminated in a conference at Philip of Hesse's castle at Marburg. The proceedings began on October 1 with conferences between Zwingli and Melanchthon, and Luther and Oecolampadius. The primary discussion was conducted almost entirely between Luther and Zwingli before Duke Ulrich of Württemberg and the landgrave. Zwingli attacked the "ubiquity" of the body of Christ, while Luther attacked Zwingli's exegesis of the words of the institution.

The resulting *Articles of Marburg* showed that the parties agreed on most doctrinal points, including the Trinity, the personality of Christ, faith, justification, the Scriptures, baptism, good works, confession, government, tradition, and infant baptism. However, no agreement was reached on whether the true body and blood of Christ are corporeally present in the bread and wine. Despite this failure to reach a mental rapprochement, the articles recommended that adherents of each doctrine display Christian charity toward one another.

## Relations with Anabaptists
Zwingli was a vehement opponent of the Anabaptists. In 1527, he published the second part of his *Elenchus contra Catabaptistas*, which contained what is likely the earliest confession of faith of any Baptist community. Zwingli claimed to translate this confession *ad verbum* into Latin to show the world that the articles were "stolid, audacious, impious," and "fanatical." 

In his detailing of these seven articles, Zwingli joined the fourth and fifth articles together to create a section regarding "the avoiding of abominable pastors in the church" (*Super devitatione abominabilium pastorum in Ecclesia*). This was despite the fact that the fourth article contained nothing about pastors and the fifth contained nothing about abominations. Zwingli noted in a marginal comment that while some copies he received read this way, other copies maintained them as two separate articles. During this period, the Protestant Council of Zürich instituted a cruel persecution of the Anabaptists, even as the Council itself feared persecution from Romanists.

## Influence and Succession
Zwingli's influence extended to other reformers and later figures. John Hooper, who later became the bishop of Gloucester and Worcester, encountered Zwingli's works while living in England. These writings, along with commentaries on St Paul's epistles by Heinrich Bullinger, led Hooper to take refuge on the continent and travel to Zürich, where his Zwinglian convictions were confirmed.

Zwingli's life ended following the battle of Cappel on October 11, 1531, in which he fell. He was succeeded as the chief pastor of Zürich on December 9, 1531, by Heinrich Bullinger. Bullinger, a Swiss reformer born in 1504, had heard Zwingli in Zürich in 1527 and accompanied him to a disputation at Berne the following year. Bullinger continued the work of the reformation in Switzerland, eventually authoring the second Helvetic Confession in 1566.

## Biblical Scholarship
In the realm of textual criticism, Zwingli was among the earliest reformers—alongside Calvin and Luther—to question or deny the coeval origin of consonants and vowels in the Hebrew texts of the Old Testament. This position stood in contrast to later Protestant scholars of the Swiss school, such as the Buxtorfs, who adopted different views during the period of Protestant scholasticism.
