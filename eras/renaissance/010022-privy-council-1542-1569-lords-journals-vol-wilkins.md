# Privy Council (1542-1569)

The Privy Council served as the primary council of state or government during the Tudor period, specifically from the later years of Henry VIII and continuing under the Stuarts. During the subsequent Commonwealth period, the body assumed the name of the council of state.

## Role and Administration
The Privy Council functioned as the central administrative body of the realm. Its powers were exercised through various committees, which historically handled responsibilities that are now managed by different government entities. For instance, a committee of the lords of the council was previously responsible for duties now performed by the Board of Trade and the secretary of state for foreign affairs.

The formal direction of the Privy Council was not always managed by a permanent office. Until 1679, the council was directed by the lord keeper of the seal, the lord chancellor, or a particular court official. While a special lord president of the council was appointed during the reign of Charles I, the office remained unfilled during the following reign.

## Membership and Personnel
Membership in the Privy Council included various officers of state and military figures. For example, Sir Edward Bellingham, a soldier who fought in France and served as an English member of parliament, was a member of the privy council before being sent to Ireland in May 1548 to serve as lord deputy. 

Under Queen Elizabeth, Francis Knollys served as a privy councillor. Knollys, a knight and member of parliament who had entered the service of Henry VIII before 1540, also held the position of vice-chamberlain of the royal household. Despite being an active Puritan and frequently expressing frank objections to Elizabeth's foreign policy, Knollys maintained the queen's favor.

## Relationship with the Cabinet
Historically, the Privy Council was distinct from what was then termed a "cabinet council." In the 17th century, the term "cabinet" was often used as a reproach to describe a select body of royal favorites who were believed to possess more of the king's confidence than the Privy Council at large. This was contrasted with the broader Privy Council, which acted as the official government body. For example, in 1640, a committee of state consisting of Laud, Strafford, Cottington, and a few others was referred to enviously in court as the "Cabinet Council" and reproachfully as the "Juncto."

## Archival Records and Sources
The activities and proceedings of the Privy Council during the mid-16th century are documented in several official sources. The *Acts of the Privy Council* provide records for the period of 1542-1569, with specific excerpts available for the years 1542-1547. These records, along with the *Lords' Journals* (vol. i) and Wilkins' *Concilia*, serve as primary authorities for historians researching the era. Other official sources that complement these records include the Journals of the Commons and Lords, D'Ewes' Journals, and the *Letters and Papers of Henry VIII*.
