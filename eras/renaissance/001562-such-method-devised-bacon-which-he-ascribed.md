# Francis Bacon and the Baconian Method

Francis Bacon (1561–1626), also known as Baron Verulam and Viscount St Albans, was an English essayist, statesman, and philosopher. Born on January 22, 1560/1, at York House in the Strand, London, he was the youngest son of Sir Nicholas Bacon and a daughter of Sir Anthony Cooke. Due to delicate health, Bacon likely received much of his early instruction at home before entering Trinity College, Cambridge, in April 1573. During his time at university, Bacon concluded that the results and methods of the sciences as they were then taught were erroneous. While he maintained a reverence for Aristotle, he despised the prevailing Aristotelian philosophy, viewing it as fruitless and useful only for disputation. This led him to believe that philosophy required a new method and a true purpose.

## The Inductive Method and the Organum

Bacon devised a method to which he ascribed mechanical simplicity and absolute certainty. This system of induction was designed to move away from the "natural method" pursued by unassisted human reason. The process of inductive investigation into the "form" of a nature involves several specific steps based on the principle that a form is always present when the nature is present, absent when the nature is absent, and varies in degree as the nature increases or decreases.

To implement this, Bacon proposed the creation of three types of tables:
1. **Table of Essence and Presence**: A list of instances where the nature is present.
2. **Table of Absence in Proximity**: A list of instances where the nature is absent, focusing on those most akin to the instances of presence.
3. **Table of Degrees or Comparison**: A list of instances where the nature is present in varying degrees, either in different subjects or within the same subject.

Following the formation of these tables, the most critical part of the method is the process of exclusion or rejection. By eliminating the non-essential, Bacon believed one could infallibly detect the cause or form. Because he recognized that these conditions might be impracticable, he devised "adminicula" (helps), which included the Rectification of Induction, Prerogative Instances, and Limits of Investigation, among others.

## Natural History and the New Philosophy

Bacon emphasized that his *Organum* could not significantly advance the "Instauration of the Sciences" without a complete "Natural History." He viewed the *Organum* and the *History* as correlative sides of a true philosophy. To Bacon, a true natural history consisted strictly of observations, connections, canons, and instances. In a letter to Jean Antoine Baranzano, he asserted that a store of instances must be procured or the business given up, even if the description of these instances were to fill six times as many volumes as Pliny's *History*.

## Critical Reception and Scientific Value

The value of Bacon's method has been subject to significant debate. Macaulay admitted the process was accurate but denied its efficiency, arguing that analyzing a natural operation does not make it more efficacious. More formidable objections suggest that science has not actually followed the Baconian method and that no single discovery can be definitely ascribed to his rules. 

Logicians have argued that Bacon's method was based on a false conception of causal relations, treating scientific problems as matters of co-existence and assuming each phenomenon has only one cause. Critics note that the true scientific procedure relies on the deductive method—framing a hypothesis and testing it via verification—a process Bacon is hardly said to have recognized.

Despite these flaws, Bacon is regarded as a leader in the reformation of modern science and, alongside Descartes, an inaugurator of modern philosophy. His influence is attributed to his eloquence and his ability to organize a new mode of thinking into a connected system at a time when scholasticism and church authority were decaying. His fundamental insistence on the critical analysis of experience and the rejection of rash generalization remains valuable.

## Influence and Legacy

Bacon's influence extended into psychology and ethics. The principle in Locke's *Essay* that ideas are products of reflection and sensation is found in the first aphorism of the *Novum Organum*. In ethics, his inductive method is seen as a forerunner to the empirical method and utilitarianism, which applies the experimental method to human conduct. However, it is noted that Hobbes, who served as Bacon's secretary, made no critical reference to Bacon or Baconian induction in his works.

In popular estimation, Bacon was often credited with mechanical discoveries. While he described a method for constructing a telescope, research indicates he likely did not possess the instrument. He was acquainted with the principles of spectacles and burning-glasses were common, but little can be accurately ascribed to him in these fields. Additionally, he believed in the philosopher's stone, astrology, the doctrine of signatures, and the squaring of the circle.

## Other Figures Named Bacon

The archive also contains records of Delia Bacon (1811–1859), the sister of Leonard Bacon. A teacher and author of *Tales of the Puritans*, she is primarily remembered for *The Philosophy of the Plays of Shakespeare Unfolded* (1857). In this work, she attempted to prove that the plays of Shakespeare were actually written by a coterie of men, including Francis Bacon, Sir Walter Raleigh, and Edmund Spenser, to communicate a philosophic system.
