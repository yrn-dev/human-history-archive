# Bohemia

## Accession of the Habsburg Dynasty
Following the death of King Louis in 1526, Bohemia entered a period of anarchy and disorder, as previous monarchs had primarily resided in Hungary and the country lacked a strong indigenous government. Several candidates sought the throne, including King Sigismund of Poland, German princes, and Bohemian noblemen such as Leo of Rozmital. On October 23, 1526, a group of twenty-four electors, chosen equally from the three estates, elected Archduke Ferdinand of Austria (Ferdinand I) as king. Ferdinand laid claim to the throne as the husband of Anna, daughter of King Vladislav. This event marked the permanent accession of the Habsburg dynasty to the Bohemian throne.

## Religious and Political Tensions under Ferdinand I
Although Ferdinand I was devoted to Rome, he initially showed moderation in religious matters. His primary objective was to establish the hereditary right of his dynasty to the crown. In 1541, after a great fire in Prague destroyed state documents, Ferdinand persuaded the estates to replace the original charter—which stated he was king by election—with a new charter claiming he was recognized as king due to the hereditary rights of his wife, Anna. This action caused significant dissatisfaction.

By the mid-16th century, the religious landscape of Bohemia had shifted. The Bohemian Brethren grew in influence through the support of powerful nobles and sympathized with German Protestants. Some advanced Utraquists also held views similar to German Lutherans. These tensions complicated Ferdinand's attempts to raise a Bohemian army to support his brother. In 1546, Ferdinand used the pretext of a Turkish menace to persuade the estates to vote for a general levy. However, many nobles and cities, particularly those associated with the Bohemian Brethren, refused to send men. The soldiers who did arrive, many of whom were Protestants, refused to cross the Saxon frontier, forcing Ferdinand to disband the forces by the end of the year.

## The Revolt of 1547 and the "Bloody Diet"
In early 1547, Ferdinand again called for an army, but only Romanists and retrograde Utraquists obeyed. The majority of Bohemians sought to recover ancient liberties that Ferdinand had encroached upon by claiming hereditary right to the crown and curtailing old privileges. In March 1547, the estates met in Prague without a royal summons. This assembly was dominated by the Bohemian Brotherhood and representatives of Prague, taking on a revolutionary character.

Following the success of the royal forces, Ferdinand established the Jesuits in Bohemia to make the knights and nobles more amenable to his will. On August 22, a diet was held where four principal leaders of the revolt—two citizens of Prague and two knights—were sentenced to death and decapitated outside the Hradcany palace. This event became known as the "Krvavy'sneem" or "bloody diet." In 1562, Ferdinand secured the coronation of his eldest son, Maximilian, as king of Bohemia to ensure the succession.

## Succession and the Path to the Thirty Years' War
Under Emperor Maximilian II (1564–1576), a liberal policy maintained peace, though various Christian beliefs continued to struggle for mastery in Bohemia, Silesia, and Moravia. This changed with the accession of Rudolph II (1576–1612), a fanatical Spanish Catholic who encouraged the Jesuits to pursue the counter-Reformation. During Rudolph's reign, Bohemia existed in a state of semi-independence.

The situation deteriorated further under Matthias (1612–1619), who was unable to restore order. Because Matthias was childless, he sought to secure the succession for his cousin, Ferdinand, Duke of Styria, who was known as a cruel persecutor of Protestants. Despite the efforts of state officials, the Bohemian Protestants, led by Count Thurn, opposed this. Conflict was precipitated in December 1617 when the abbot of Brevnov and the archbishop of Prague suppressed Protestant services in churches on their domains, violating the Letter of Majesty. On May 22, 1618, Protestant members of the diet proceeded to the Hradcany palace to lodge complaints, and a secret meeting led to the decision to put Matthias's most influential councillors to death.

## The Battle of the White Hill and Habsburg Absolutism
Following the death of Matthias, the Bohemian estates formally deposed Ferdinand on July 31, 1619, and elected Frederick, Elector Palatine, as king. However, Ferdinand, now emperor, sought to reconquer the territory with aid from Spain, Poland, several Italian states, and the Duke of Bavaria. On November 8, 1620, imperial forces defeated the Bohemian army at the Battle of the White Hill (Bila Hora). King Frederick fled Prague the following morning.

The aftermath of the battle marked a turning point for Bohemia. On June 21, 1621, the leaders of the national movement were executed, and the lands of those who participated in the movement were confiscated and given to foreigners and imperial army veterans. Catholicism was re-established through "terrible cruelty," and those refusing the Roman creed were expelled. 

In 1627, Ferdinand published a decree suppressing Bohemia's ancient free constitution. The new system proclaimed the crown's heredity in the House of Habsburg and added the clergy as a primary estate. The diet was stripped of legislative power, and while the estates could still vote on taxes, they were forbidden from attaching conditions to those grants. Additionally, the German language was granted equal right with Bohemian in law courts and government offices.
