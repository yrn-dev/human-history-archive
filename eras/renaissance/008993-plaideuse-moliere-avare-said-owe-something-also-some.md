# Molière and Associated Literary Influences

Molière was a prominent French dramatist whose work and professional life were intertwined with various contemporaries, actors, and literary sources. His plays often utilized specific motifs and were influenced by the comedies of his era.

## Literary Influences and Works
Molière's play *L'Avare* is said to owe something to the comedy *La Belle Plaideuse*, written by the French poet and priest François le Metel de Boisrobert (1592–1662). Boisrobert, a former lawyer at the bar at Rouen and a favorite of Cardinal Richelieu, was one of five poets who implemented Richelieu's dramatic ideas. 

In *L'Avare*, a central plot element is Harpagnon's casket. The casket, a small box or coffer used for storing items of value such as money, jewels, or papers, plays an important part in the narrative. Other works of the period also featured such receptacles, including the stories of Bluebeard and the trial of the poisoner marquise de Brinvilliers.

Additionally, Molière authored the *Impromptu du Versailles* (1663), which is a miniature comedy in prose. This work serves as an example of the "impromptu," a term sometimes used to describe short dramatic sketches.

## Professional Collaborations and the Theatre
Molière formed l'Illustre Theatre in 1643 with the Bejart family. This included Madeleine Bejart (1618–1672), a tall, handsome blonde and an excellent actress who specialized in soubrette parts. Molière wrote several roles specifically for her, including Dorine in *Tartuffe*, Marotte in *Les Precieuses ridicules*, and Lisette in *L'Ecole des maris*.

Molière also worked with Michel Baron (1653–1729), a talented actor whom Molière secured for his company with the king's permission. Although Baron briefly withdrew from the company due to a misunderstanding with Molière's wife, he rejoined in 1670 and remained until Molière's death.

## Personal Life and Armande Béjart
In 1662, Molière married Armande Gresinde Claire Elizabeth Bejart (1645–1700), whom he had directed in her education since 1653. Their marriage was marked by jealousy and instability; they separated after the birth of a daughter in 1665 and only met at the theatre until 1671, when they were reconciled. 

Armande was a talented actress who first appeared on stage in 1663 as Elise in *Critique de l'ecole des femmes*. Molière wrote several roles to showcase her talents, including:
* Elmire in the first performance of *Tartuffe*
* Celimene in *Le Misanthrope* (described as her own highly-finished portrait)
* Angelique in *Le Malade imaginaire*
* Lucile in *Le Bourgeois gentilhomme* (which also contains a portrait of her in Act iii., sc. 9)

Following Molière's death, Armande leased the Theatre Guenegaud and combined the residue of her company with players from the Theatre du Marais to form the *troupe du roi*.

## Literary Circles and Contemporaries
Molière was part of a literary circle that met at the *Pomme du Pin* and the *Moulon Blanc*. These gatherings included Antoine Furetiere, Chapelle, Racine, and Boileau. Boileau was a constant friend to Molière and supported his interests, expressing his admiration for the dramatist in the second satire (1664) and in stanzas addressed to him in 1663.

Later literary figures also referenced Molière. The English novelist Lawrence Sterne, known for *Tristram Shandy*, is noted as having taken his literary *bien* from a wider variety of sources than Molière did.
