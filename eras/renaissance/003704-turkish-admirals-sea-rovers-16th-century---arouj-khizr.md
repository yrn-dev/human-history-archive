# Turkish Admirals and Sea Rovers of the 16th Century: Arouj and Khizr

The term "Barbarossa," meaning "Redbeard" (from the Latin *barba*), was a nickname applied by Christians to a family of 16th-century Turkish sea rovers and admirals. This family included Arouj, his brother Khizr (also known as Khair-ed-Din), and Khair-ed-Din's son, Hassan. While some historians, such as Captain Walsin Esterhazy, suggested the name was a mispronunciation of "Baba Arouj," contemporary Arab chronicles explicitly state that the name Barbarossa was applied to Khair-ed-Din. It is generally believed the nickname referred to the family's tawny or red beards.

## Origins and Early Life
The family was founded by Yakub, a Roumeliot likely of Albanian descent, who settled in Mitylene following its conquest by the Turks. Yakub worked as a skipper and coasting trader. He had four sons born after 1482: Elias, Isaak, Arouj, and Khizr. While Khizr became a potter and Isaak a trader, Elias and Arouj pursued careers as sea rovers.

During an engagement with a galley belonging to the Knights of Saint John based at Rhodes, Elias was killed and Arouj was captured. After being ransomed by a Turkish pasha, Arouj returned to the sea. He initially served the Mamelukes in Egypt, but during the conflict between the Mamelukes and Sultan Selim I, he relocated to Tunis.

## Activities in North Africa
In northern Africa, Arouj found employment as a mercenary due to the constant conflicts among Berber princes. He supplemented this by conducting piratical raids against Christian trade. He was eventually joined in Tunis by his brother Khizr, who adopted the name Khair-ed-Din, and later by their brother Isaak.

Arouj and Khair-ed-Din collaborated with exiled Moors from Granada—who had been driven into exile by the Catholic sovereigns of Spain in 1492—to conduct raids on the Spanish coast. The brothers also sought to increase their power by fighting for, or murdering and supplanting, native African princes. They established their headquarters on the island of Jerba in the Gulf of Gabes. In 1512, they attempted to seize Bougie from the Spaniards, though they were beaten off.

## Establishment of Turkish Power
The Spanish responded to the piratical attacks by conquering coast towns including Tunis, Algiers, and Oran. Arouj eventually fell in battle against the Spaniards in 1518. Following his death, Khair-ed-Din appealed to the Turkish sultan, Selim, who provided him with troops. 

In 1529, Khair-ed-Din succeeded in driving the Spaniards from a fort located on a rocky island in front of Algiers. This victory established the foundation of Turkish power in the region. From 1518 until the death of Uluch Ali in 1587, Algiers served as the primary seat of government for the beylerbeys of northern Africa, who exercised rule over Algeria, Tunisia, and Tripoli.

## Later Governance of North Africa
Following the period of the beylerbeys, the region was governed from 1587 to 1659 by Turkish pashas sent from Constantinople for three-year terms. However, a military revolt in Algiers in 1659 rendered these pashas nonentities. From that point forward, while the African cities remained nominally part of the Turkish empire, they functioned as anarchical military republics that lived by plunder and elected their own rulers.

These communities were part of a broader history of Barbary pirates who infested the Mediterranean and, for a time, the ocean. While piracy had existed in the region since the decline of the Roman Empire and the Mahommedan conquest, the "savage robber powers" of the Barbary pirates specifically arose in the 16th century, peaked in the 17th century, and declined throughout the 18th century. These pirate communities, which relied entirely on plunder for survival, were finally extinguished around 1830 following the French conquest of Algiers.
