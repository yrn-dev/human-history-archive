# The History of the Book-Plate (Ex-Libris)

The book-plate, also known as the *ex-libris*, is a printed label used to indicate the ownership of individual volumes. The practice is nearly as old as the printed book itself and serves a similar purpose to the hand-painted symbolical or armorial personal devices found in medieval manuscripts, mirroring the relationship between the printed page and the work of a scribe.

## Origins and Early Development in Germany
The history of the ornamental book-plate as a mark of possession began in Germany. The earliest known examples of book-plates are German, and they appeared in great numbers in this region long before the fashion spread to other countries. These early works are often of significant artistic interest.

According to Friedrich Warnecke of Berlin, the oldest movable *ex-libris* are certain hand-painted woodcuts depicting a shield of arms supported by an angel. These were pasted into books presented to the Carthusian monastery of Buxheim around the year 1480 by Brother Hildebrand Brandenburg of Biberach.

Germany's early dominance in the field is further evidenced by the contributions of renowned artists. Between 1503 and 1516, Albrecht Dürer engraved at least six plates, some of which were of very important size, and provided designs for many others. Other notable plates are attributed to Hans Holbein, Lucas Cranach, and a group known as the Little Masters, which included Jost Amman, Virgil Solis, the Behams, Matthias Zundt, Saldorfer, and Georg Hupschmann. The decorative styles established by these draughtsmen influenced German design for subsequent centuries, persisting despite the introduction of French and Italian fashions during the 18th and 17th centuries. While the "heavy, over-elaborated German style" did not affect neighboring countries, Germany remained the source from which the fashion of ornamental book-plates spread.

## International Spread and Chronology
Following the German origins, the use of book-plates appeared in other nations at various intervals:

*   **France:** The most ancient discovered *ex-libris* in France belongs to Jean Bertaud de la Tour-Blanche and is dated 1529.
*   **Holland:** A plate belonging to Anna van der Aa dates to 1597.
*   **Italy:** An example has been attributed to the year 1622.
*   **England:** An early example is a gift-plate used by Sir Nicholas Bacon for books he presented to the university of Cambridge.
*   **America:** The earliest known American example is a plain printed label from 1679 belonging to John Williams.

## Evolution in France
In France, the adoption of the movable *ex-libris* followed a different trajectory than in Germany. Prior to the 17th century, book-owners generally preferred the more luxurious habit of stamping the cover of a book with a personal device, which rendered the use of labels superfluous.

It was not until the 17th century that the movable *ex-libris* became tolerably common in France. By the middle of that century, the *ex-libris* proper became "quite naturalized," resulting in a numerous collection of examples that are, as a rule, very handsome. 

France is also the origin of the term *ex-libris* when used as a substantive. While it is now the recognized term for a book-plate across the entire continent, the words appeared in the personal tokens of other nationalities only long after they had become a recognized inscription on French labels.
