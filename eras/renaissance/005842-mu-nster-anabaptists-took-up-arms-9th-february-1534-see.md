# The Münster Anabaptists

The Münster Anabaptists represented one of the most vigorous and notorious radical anti-ecclesiastical movements of the 16th century. Their activities in the city of Münster culminated in the establishment of a theocratic regime characterized by severe rule and eventual violent collapse.

## Origins and Rise in Münster
The movement in Münster was influenced by various reforming figures. In 1532, Bernhardt Rothmann, the pastor of the city church of St Lamberti, led a reforming movement. By May 1533, Rothmann had been brought to a rejection of infant baptism by a refugee named Heinrich Roll. On September 8, 1533, Anabaptism was proclaimed in the city by a journeyman smith, and by January 1, 1534, Roll began preaching Anabaptist doctrines from a city pulpit.

During this period, Bernt Knipperdollinck, a prosperous cloth-merchant and leader of the democrats, rose to prominence. Knipperdollinck had a history of eccentricity and political activism, including helping to break the prison of Tonies Kruse in 1527 and leading a surprise attack on negotiating nobles at Telgte in December 1532. By February 1534, Knipperdollinck was appointed burgomaster.

## The Influence of Jan Matthysz and John of Leiden
The radicalization of the Münster community was driven by the influence of Jan Matthysz (also known as Matthyszoon), a master-baker and Anabaptist prophet from Haarlem. Matthysz preached a message of vengeance, prophesying the establishment of the kingdom of heaven and a speedy end to the world. He dispatched emissaries, including de Kniper and Boekebinder, to Münster to realize these ideals. Under this influence, all individuals who did not embrace Anabaptism were driven from the city in 1533.

On January 13, 1534, Johann Buckholdt (also known as Beukelsz, Bockelszoon, or John of Leiden) arrived in Münster as an apostle of Matthysz. Buckholdt was the illegitimate son of the burgomaster of Soevenhagen and had previously been a tailor and a playwright. Described as good-looking and fluent, he won the confidence of Knipperdollinck and married the merchant's daughter.

## The Theocracy and the Rule of John of Leiden
The Münster Anabaptists took up arms on February 9, 1534. Following the death of Matthysz later that year, Buckholdt succeeded him as prophet. He expanded his household by adding Matthysz's widow to his wives and organized a new constitution for the city. This theocratic structure included twelve elders, a number suggested by the tribes of Israel.

Buckholdt eventually superseded the elders and officers, declaring himself king of the "new Zion." His reign was marked by arbitrary rule, severity, and pomp. The city became a scene of cruelty and "grossest licence."

## Fall and Execution
The city of Münster was besieged and eventually retaken on June 25, 1535, by its prince-bishop, Franz von Waldeck. Following the city's fall, Buckholdt was subjected to many indignities. He was cruelly executed on January 22, 1536. As a final indignity, his body and those of his companions were hung in cages from the tower of the Lamberti church.

## Context within Anabaptism
The events at Münster are often contrasted with the broader Anabaptist movement. While some Anabaptists held general doctrines—such as the baptism of believers only, the practice of breaking bread to renew brotherly love, and the rejection of the Lutheran doctrine of justification by faith as one-sided—the Münster episode was a result of the loss of noble leaders who had preached non-resistance. The "horrors of Muenster" are viewed as a culmination of an attitude of vengeance. Historians note that the movement should be better remembered through the piety and learning of figures such as Hans Denck, or the martyrdom of Grebel, Hubmaier, and Blaurock, rather than the crimes of John of Leiden.
