# Rome

## Medieval Perceptions of Julius Caesar
In the middle ages, the history of Julius Caesar remained relatively stable compared to other ancient legends, as the general facts of his life were well known and the works of Lucan were regularly studied in schools. However, a common historical error persisted during this era, with Caesar being regarded as the first emperor of Rome. In the popular imagination, he came to represent the glory of Rome and was eventually viewed as a pillar of the Church. This transition is evident in the French pseudo-historic romance *Les Faits des Romains* (c. 1223), which depicts Caesar receiving a bishopric. 

Other medieval works further expanded on his life. Around 1240, Jehan de Tuim wrote a prose *Hystore de Julius Cesar* based on the commentaries of Caesar and the *Pharsalia* of Lucan, which included a romanticized account of Caesar's meeting with Cleopatra. This work was later turned into verse by Jacot de Forest as the *Roman de Julius César*. Another prose compilation, *Les Fails des Romains* (c. 1225), focused on the twelve Caesars but concluded with the murder of the dictator. Additionally, a *Mistaire de Julius César* was reportedly performed at Amboise in 1500 before Louis XII.

## The Rise and Career of Cesare Borgia
Cesare Borgia (1476–1507), the son of Pope Alexander VI and Vanozza dei Cattanei, was born in Rome. He was the favorite son of the pope, who practiced extensive nepotism on his behalf. Initially created archbishop of Valencia and later a cardinal, Cesare led a profligate life at the Vatican. In 1495, he accompanied Charles VIII as a hostage for the pope's behavior but escaped at Velletri. In 1497, the murder of his brother Giovanni, the duke of Gandia, was attributed to Cesare, possibly due to rivalry over Donna Sancha or a desire to secure his father's sole favor as he sought a secular career.

After receiving permission to renounce the priesthood in 1498, Cesare served as papal legate to Louis XII of France. In exchange for providing a bull to annul Louis's marriage to Jeanne of France, Cesare received military assistance and the duchy of Valentinois. He married Charlotte d'Albret in May 1499 after being rejected by Carlotta of Naples.

## Conquests in Romagna
With French support and an army of Italian, Gascon, and Swiss troops, Cesare sought to carve a principality in Romagna. He established headquarters in Cesena and captured Imola and Forli; the latter was held by Caterina Sforza, who surrendered on January 22, 1500. In October 1500, with an army of 10,000 men and Venetian support, he took Rimini and Pesaro. He captured Astorre Manfredi of Faenza in April 1501, but despite promising to spare his life, Cesare sent him to Rome to be outraged and executed.

Cesare's expansion continued with the conquest of Camerino and Urbino, leading the pope to create him duke of Romagna. His reputation was one of insatiable avidity, cruelty, and a lack of scruples. His power was briefly threatened by the conspiracy of La Magione, where deposed princes, the Orsinis, and captains such as Vitellozzo Vitelli, Oliverotto da Fermo, and G.P. Baglioni conspired against the Borgias. After French help frightened the confederates into terms, Cesare lured Vitelli, Oliverotto, and Paolo Orsini to Senigallia, where he had them arrested.

## The Fall of Cesare Borgia
The decline of Cesare's power began in 1503 when both he and his father were seized by fever. Pope Alexander VI died on August 18, 1503, while Cesare was still incapacitated. This coincidence led to his ruin, as enemies in Romagna and the Orsinis in Rome rose against him. When Cesare attempted to intimidate the cardinals using Don Michelotto and his bravos, they refused, and he was forced to leave Rome in September.

The subsequent election of Pius III saw Cesare's dominion collapse; the lords of Piombino, Rimini, and Pesaro regained their lands, and Guidobaldo returned to Urbino with Venetian aid. Following the death of Pius III on October 18, 1503, Giuliano della Rovere was elected as Julius II on November 1. Although Julius II initially showed no ill-will, he insisted that Cesare's territories be restored to the church. After attempting to use Cesare to block Venetian intervention in Romagna, Julius II had him arrested, ending Borgia's power.

## Other Historical Figures in Rome
Rome served as a center for various political and religious disputes. In 313, an imperial commission presided over by Melchiades, the bishop of Rome, investigated a dispute involving the Donatists. The case, which continued through a synod in Arles in 314 and a final hearing in Milan in 316, ultimately decided in favor of Caecilian.

In the 19th century, Rome remained a focal point for Italian nationalism. Cesare d'Azeglio, an officer in the Piedmontese army, served as a special envoy to the Vatican upon the return of Pope Pius VII. His son, Massimo Taparelli, Marquis d'Azeglio, lived in Rome as a painter before becoming a statesman. Massimo later spent time in Rome during the papacy of Pius IX, where he and others hoped for an Italian confederation under papal auspices.
